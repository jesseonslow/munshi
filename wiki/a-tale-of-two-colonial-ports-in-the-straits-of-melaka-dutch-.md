# A tale of two colonial ports in the Straits of Melaka: Dutch Melaka and English Penang

Nordin Hussin's 2002 comparative study examines Dutch Melaka (1641–1795) and English Penang (from 1786) as colonial port-towns in the Straits of Melaka, arguing that despite their different metropolitan origins and administrative traditions, both settlements shared the fundamental structural characteristics that defined colonial urbanism in the region. Drawing on the theoretical framework of colonial city studies, Hussin demonstrates that the differences between the two were matters of degree rather than kind, and that both grew within the same overarching scheme of European colonial port-town development.

## Summary

Hussin's article applies the comparative colonial city framework—drawing on the work of Anthony King, David Simon, Ronald Horvath, and others—to two specific port-towns in the Straits of Melaka. The central analytical problem is whether the Dutch and British colonial projects produced fundamentally different urban forms, or whether they were variations on a common colonial template. Hussin argues for the latter: both Melaka and Penang functioned as entrepôt settlements with small European populations occupying the apex of a rigid social hierarchy, physically segregated from the indigenous and Asian majority.

The article traces how each settlement developed its spatial and social architecture in response to its metropolitan model. Penang, established by the East India Company in 1786, was shaped by the segregation patterns already established in British India, where European populations were clustered into distinct enclaves. In Melaka, the Dutch VOC's approach to social organization in the eighteenth century placed less emphasis on rigid spatial segregation, though the European community remained clearly demarcated by skin colour, social position, and administrative authority. The physical infrastructure of each town—forts, churches, stone buildings—served as markers of Western colonial presence within a predominantly non-European urban landscape.

Hussin uses primary and secondary sources to illustrate how the built environment reflected these social arrangements. The comparison of fortifications is particularly instructive: Melaka's Portuguese-Dutch fort was a substantial complex that housed government buildings and facilities for the VOC's servants and governor, while Penang's Fort Cornwallis was a small, poorly constructed square masonry structure that never achieved its intended defensive purpose. This contrast in military architecture mirrors the broader difference in strategic priority: Melaka sat at the heart of the VOC's spice trade network and required a more formidable defensive posture, while Penang, with no serious external threats, never justified the investment in a strong fortification.

### Key Findings

- Fort Cornwallis in Penang was built in 1786 as a square masonry structure with exterior sides measuring 500 feet, constructed by a local Chinese contractor; it was never completely finished and was in a deplorable state by the 1790s, with parts of the walls having fallen down (p. 93).
- The Melakan fort, by contrast, was a larger and more grand complex that housed government buildings and facilities for the VOC's servants and governor, reflecting the higher strategic priority of the Dutch settlement (p. 93).
- Penang's fort had no ditch or natural boundary separating it from the town, and no clear boundary existed between the fort and adjacent town areas; the administration was unable to rebuild or expand it partly because the cost of purchasing adjacent lands from wealthy European merchants was prohibitive (p. 93).
- The Dutch in eighteenth-century Melaka placed less emphasis on spatial segregation of the European population than the British in Penang, where the model was directly influenced by the segregated urban patterns of British India (p. 93).
- Both towns shared the common colonial feature of a small European population occupying the top of the social scale, clearly demarcated by skin colour, superior social position, and administrative responsibility, living in isolated communities segregated from the major part of the town's population (p. 93).

### Conclusion

Hussin's definitive takeaway is that the differences between Dutch Melaka and English Penang were not fundamental and did not negate the shared characteristics that identified both as colonial port-towns. Both reflected the same scheme of colonial urbanism—European dominance, entrepôt function, physical segregation, and the imposition of Western architectural markers—and grew within the same structural logic, even as their specific expressions varied according to metropolitan tradition and strategic context.

## Context

- The article draws on the Straits Settlements Factory Records (G/34) as its primary archival source, supplemented by a broad secondary literature on colonial city theory, Dutch and British colonial administration, and the specific histories of Melaka and Penang.
- The study contributes to the historiography of colonial urbanism in Southeast Asia by applying the comparative colonial city framework (King, Simon, Horvath) to a Straits of Melaka case study, bridging the gap between theoretical literature on colonial ports and the specific urban histories of these two settlements.
