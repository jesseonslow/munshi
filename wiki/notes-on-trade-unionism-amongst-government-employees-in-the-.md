# Notes on trade unionism amongst government employees in the Federation of Malaya, 1948–1957

R.N. Jackson's 1970 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the development of trade unionism among government employees in the Federation of Malaya from the onset of the Emergency in 1948 to independence in 1957. Jackson argues that, despite the fragmentation of the movement into over one hundred small unions, government employees' unions constituted the most financially robust and structurally stable segment of the Malayan trade-union movement throughout this period, outperforming both the private sector and the rubber-estate unions in accumulated funds per member and organisational continuity.

## Summary

Jackson's central concern is to establish the place and significance of government employees' unions within the broader Malayan trade-union landscape during a decade of political transition. The starting point is the collapse of Communist-dominated unions at the beginning of the Emergency, which left the movement bifurcated into private-sector and government-employee unions. Jackson demonstrates that government employees were far more likely to organise than their private-sector counterparts, and that their unions recovered from the 1948 shock faster than any other sector, with membership returning to pre-Emergency levels by late 1951 and continuing to grow steadily thereafter.

The article then turns to the structural question of why a single employer—the Government—was represented by over one hundred separate unions rather than a small number of large ones. Jackson identifies two dominant patterns: unions defined by department, function, geography, and race (such as state-level teachers' unions or district labourers' unions), and unions based in Kuala Lumpur around specific federal departmental grades. He attributes this fragmentation to grass-roots origins, the social satisfactions of office-holding in small bodies, and the practical ease of local communication. Despite official encouragement from the Trade Union Adviser's department and a British expert adviser to consolidate into national unions, the movement resisted amalgamation, preferring federations that preserved individual union identity.

The final substantive section traces the development of Whitley Council machinery, modelled on the British system, from an interim joint council in 1946 through to two National Whitley Councils (for monthly-paid and daily-paid staff) established in 1953, complete with arbitration procedures. Jackson draws on annual reports of the Trade Unions Registry, census data, Ministry of Labour reports, and the papers of the Malayan Trade Union Council to construct a quantitative and institutional portrait of a movement that was, by 1957, a major and stable pillar of Malayan labour organisation.

### Key Findings

- In-benefit membership of government employees' unions grew from 27,392 in 1948 to 51,572 in 1957, while the number of active unions trebled from 48 to 129; unionisation among eligible government employees rose from 25% to 36% (pp. 129–130).
- Accumulated funds of government employees' unions exceeded those of all other unions combined in every year except 1948, reaching $826,522 by March 1957, with funds per member rising steadily from $4 to $10, compared to a fluctuating $2–$4 for all other unions (pp. 133–134).
- In 1953, forty of the government employees' unions had fewer than one hundred members, and only one union (railwaymen) exceeded two thousand members, illustrating the extreme fragmentation of the movement (p. 135).
- Two National Whitley Councils—one for monthly-paid staff and one for daily-paid staff—were established in April 1953 following the advice of a British expert, with arbitration procedures accepted by both councils by the end of that year (pp. 138–139).
- At the Malayan Trade Union Council delegates conferences, Indian delegates predominated among government employees' unions: 62 of 118 delegates in 1950 and 30 of 50 in 1957, reflecting the over-representation of Indians in government employment (24% of government employees versus 11% of the total population) (pp. 136–137).
- Seven federations of government employees' unions were formed between 1948 and 1955, but by 1957 the Registrar of Trade Unions noted resignedly that federations had not, as expected, given way to national unions (pp. 140–141).

### Conclusion

Jackson's definitive takeaway is that the trade unions of government employees, though structurally fragmented and resistant to the consolidation that officials and advisers urged, constituted the financial backbone and most stable component of the Malayan trade-union movement between 1948 and 1957. Their combination of high unionisation rates, growing membership, substantial reserve funds, and institutional integration through Whitley Councils made them a major force in the country's labour landscape on the eve of independence.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Annual Reports of the Registrar of Trade Unions, the 1947 and 1957 Census Reports, Ministry of Labour Annual Reports, and the conference papers of the Malayan Trade Union Council, all held in the public record of the Federation of Malaya.
- As a preliminary study published in a learned society journal, the article provides a foundational quantitative baseline for the history of Malayan labour relations during the Emergency and independence period, a topic that has since attracted considerably more scholarly attention.
