# Minority voices and dominant structures: the case of Amos Yee

## Abstract

The sacralization of the death of Singapore’s ‘founding father’, Lee Kuan Yew, and the strong unification of the community during the week of national mourning that followed gave rise to a degree of friction between the dominant response and counter-voices. During this period a teenager named Amos Yee posted a YouTube video insulting Lee Kuan Yew and Christianity, and was subsequently arrested. This article examines the reactions by Singaporeans and critics from abroad, and proffers an interpretation of the episode that goes from issues of bad parenting to state victimization. Yee is both a ‘floating signifier’—bearing different meanings to different people—and a symbol of Singapore’s progress and associated challenges. The juxtaposition of the dominant rituals and the rupture caused by Yee suggests a future where increased minority representation and alternative voices may be expected in Singapore.

## Summary

This article examines the intersection of state ritual and dissent in Singapore following the death of Lee Kuan Yew in March 2015. Chen frames the week-long national mourning period as a moment of "collective effervescence" in which the state sacralized Lee's death as that of a "founding father" and "ancestor," reinforcing power relations between government and citizenry through organized rituals. The rupture came when 16-year-old Amos Yee posted a YouTube video insulting Lee and Christianity, triggering his arrest and a sharply polarized public response. Chen's core argument is that Yee functions as a "floating signifier" — his case carries different meanings for different audiences — and that the juxtaposition of dominant mourning rituals with Yee's counter-voice signals an evolving political culture in which minority and dissenting voices are increasingly asserting themselves in the public sphere.

Chen draws on Raymond Williams's concept of "structures of feeling" to explain how the mourning period produced a unique social experience that was neither purely top-down imposition nor purely organic expression, but a convergence of state apparatus and popular sentiment. He parallels the mourning rituals with Lily Kong and Brenda Yeoh's analysis of National Day parades as instruments of state hegemony, arguing that the memorialization of Lee simultaneously served as an opportunity for the state to mobilize nationalist pride and demonstrate logistical and military competence. The article draws on the State Funeral Organising Committee's official e-book, contemporaneous media coverage from Singaporean and international outlets, and public responses to Yee to reconstruct both the dominant narrative and the counter-narratives it provoked.

### Key Findings

- Approximately 450,000 people attended the lying-in-state at Parliament House, and over 1.2 million visited 18 condolence centres across Singapore during the mourning week (pp. 125–126).
- The State Funeral Organising Committee deployed approximately 40,000 public officers and 1,000 volunteers; 500 military personnel from the 3rd Battalion of the Singapore Guards erected 300 tents overnight to shelter mourners at the Padang (pp. 126–128).
- 170 foreign dignitaries representing 27 countries, regions, and organizations attended the state funeral on 29 March 2015 (p. 126).
- Yee was charged on three counts, found guilty of two (deliberate intent to wound religious feelings and use of profanity), and sentenced to four weeks' jail, backdated to account for time in custody, resulting in immediate release (p. 131).
- More than 20 police reports were filed against Yee by members of the public (p. 131, n. 27).
- A Change.org petition calling for Yee's release received close to 5,000 signatures (p. 132, n. 35).

### Conclusion

Chen concludes that the Amos Yee case is at once a rupture during a moment of ritual focus and a significant inflection point in Singapore's evolving political culture. The case demonstrates that the paternalistic political culture of earlier generations — characterized by trust in a government that makes most decisions for the country — is giving way to increasing political involvement and more widespread public criticism, even if systemic change will need to come from sources other than Yee himself.

## Context

- Primary sources include the State Funeral Organising Committee's official e-book *3:18 The Rally Call* (2016), contemporaneous reporting from *The Straits Times*, *Channel News Asia*, and *New York Times*, and a transcript of Yee's YouTube video.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Singaporean political culture by applying anthropological frameworks (Durkheim, Williams, Lévi-Strauss) to a contemporary event, bridging ritual theory and political analysis in a way that extends Kong and Yeoh's earlier work on state spectacle.
