# Sultan Abdul Jalil Shah III

Sultan Abdul Jalil Shah III (r. 1699–1720) was the second Sultan of Johor, succeeding his predecessor Sultan Mahmud II in September 1699 and reigning until his death at Kuala Pahang in late 1719 or early 1720. His accession was not by blood relationship but by the constitutional custom whereby the *Bendahara* (chief minister) assumed the throne when the ruling line died out without a direct heir [[1, pp. 29–30]](#ref-1). Although Wilkinson characterises the period as "a dark page of Malay History," it is of genealogical significance because it marks the origin of five Malay dynasties: Lingga, Singapore, Trengganu, Pahang, and Johore [[1, p. 28]](#ref-1).

## Accession and Early Reign

Abdul Jalil had served as *Bendahara Paduka Raja* under Sultan Mahmud II, succeeding his father Abdul Majid in that office [[1, p. 29]](#ref-1). When Mahmud was killed in August 1699 at Kota Tinggi, Abdul Jalil was installed on the throne on 3 September 1699, not by virtue of any familial relationship—as is sometimes claimed by the *Bendahara*'s descendants—but *ex officio*, in accordance with the custom that "if a ruling house dies out kingship goes to the *Bendahara*" [[1, pp. 29–30]](#ref-1).

Captain Hamilton, who knew Abdul Jalil personally, described him as "a religious bigot and a weak man, submissive in all things to a masterful younger brother" [[1, p. 29]](#ref-1). That brother, Raja Indera Bongsu, was elevated to the title of *Yamtuan Muda* (crown prince), a rank of considerable authority. In 1715 the *Yamtuan Muda* signed a treaty with the Dutch in which the Sultan was not even mentioned, a fact that Wilkinson takes as evidence of the younger brother's arrogance [[1, p. 30]](#ref-1).

## The Minangkabau Invasion and Flight

On 21 March 1717, a Minangkabau force under Raja Kechil, a pirate-king of Siak of uncertain origin, attacked and captured Johor [[1, p. 30]](#ref-1). The Bugis Annals attribute the defeat to internal discord, stating that "there was no harmony between the people and the nobles who had oppressed the people, altered old customs and done much wrong" [[1, p. 30]](#ref-1). The *Yamtuan Muda* was killed in the fighting, shot by Raja Kechil's men in a coppice, and received the posthumous name *Marhum di-Kayu* [[1, p. 30]](#ref-1).

Abdul Jalil fled to the East Coast and met Captain Hamilton at Trengganu in 1719 [[1, p. 30]](#ref-1). A peace was subsequently patched up between him and Raja Kechil on the understanding that the Sultan would give one of his daughters in marriage to the pirate-king. The arrangement was initially made with the eldest daughter, Tengku Erang, but Raja Kechil later changed his mind and demanded the youngest, Tengku Kamariah [[1, p. 30]](#ref-1). The princesses, offended and questioning the genuineness of Raja Kechil's claims to princely rank, decided he was not a fit husband. The Sultan, his women, and children attempted to flee back to the East Coast but were caught off Kuala Pahang; the Sultan was killed and the women and children taken to Riau, where Raja Kechil married Tengku Kamariah by force [[1, p. 30]](#ref-1).

## Legacy and Documentation

Abdul Jalil is known posthumously as *Marhum Kuala Pahang* [[1, p. 31]](#ref-1). The Bugis History records that he reigned twenty-two lunar years, nineteen of which were peaceful, a figure Wilkinson notes tallies exactly with the established chronology [[1, p. 30]](#ref-1). His son became Sultan Sulaiman Badr-al-alam Shah in 1722, continuing the Johore line [[1, p. 31]](#ref-1).

The principal sources for this period are Netscher's *De Nederlanders in Djohor en Siak*, the Bugis History (*Silasila Melayu dan Bugis*, 1911) as translated by Overbeck, and the unpublished *Hikayat Johor* held in the Batavian Society's library at Weltevreden [[1, p. 28]](#ref-1). Wilkinson's 1931 article in the *JMBRAS* represents the first systematic cross-referencing of these three traditions with Dutch treaty documents to produce an integrated genealogy of the five dynasties descended from this period [[1, pp. 28]](#ref-1). Wilkinson explicitly disclaims finality, stating that "further evidence will come by degrees" and that the article aims merely to "sum up what we know at present" [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1).
