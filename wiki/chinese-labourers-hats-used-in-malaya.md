# Chinese labourers’ hats used in Malaya

C.A. Gibson-Hill (1952) provides the first systematic description of the five distinct patterns of Chinese bamboo labourers' hats imported into Malaya, documenting their construction, regional origins in Kwang-tung, Fu-kien and Hainan, and their distribution across the Malay world and beyond. The article addresses a conspicuous gap in the ethnographic record: while the author's own 1951 paper had covered Malay hats and dishcovers, the Chinese hats—far more prevalent in western Malaya and the only kind normally seen in Singapore—had never been described even at their point of origin (p. 33).

## Summary

Gibson-Hill identifies five patterns reaching Malaya: one from the Canton district of Kwang-tung, three from the Swatow district of Kwang-tung and adjacent south Fu-kien, and one from Hainan Island. All share a common structural plan of two plaited bamboo lattice frames enclosing layers of bamboo leaves, bound tightly at the rim with bamboo strips, and typically finished with a plaited cap at the peak of the crown. They diverge in the number of base strands (four for the Swatow/Fu-kien group, six for the Canton and Hainan hats), the manner in which the free edge of the frames is finished, and overall proportions (pp. 35–36).

The article extends beyond cataloguing to explore the geographical and historical diffusion of these hats. Gibson-Hill examines a parallel form described by Das Gupta (1950) in North Bengal, arguing that its nearest affinities in detail are with the Swatow patterns, and speculates on possible routes of transmission—either through Chinese labourers brought to Assam for the tea industry established in the 1820s, or through older Indian-Chinese contacts in the Amoy/Swatow region evidenced by the spirit-medium cults found there (pp. 36–39). He also considers the influence of the Canton hat on the Tumpassuk Dusun hat of North Borneo and the obsolete *toering* of the Cape Malays, which appeared in South Africa early in the nineteenth century and was replaced by the fez after 1861 (p. 34).

The descriptions are grounded in Gibson-Hill's direct examination of examples in Singapore and Malaya, supplemented by photographic evidence of labourers wearing these hats in southern Kwang-tung. A brief appendix describes two locally made hat forms from the Bangkok district of Thailand, which Gibson-Hill contrasts with the Chinese import patterns to highlight the structural difference between a single rigid frame with sewn covering and the dual-frame construction characteristic of the Chinese hats (p. 46).

### Key Findings

- Five distinct patterns reach Malaya: Cantonese (Canton district), two Teochew (Swatow district), one Hokkien (Amoy district, Fu-kien), and one Hylam (Hainan Island); all are imported through Hong Kong (p. 33).
- The Cantonese hat is the most stoutly constructed, about 8–9 inches high with a 20–24 inch brim diameter, selling in Singapore for $1 to $1.25; the Teochew hat (No. 2) is the most popular, about 7 inches high with an 18 inch brim, selling for 60–80 cents (pp. 39, 41).
- The framework starts from four strands twisted into a cross (Swatow/Fu-kien hats) or six strands leaving a hexagonal centre (Canton and Hainan hats), a distinction that divides the five into two natural groups (p. 36).
- Two Teochew families in Malacca began local manufacture of the No. 2 pattern after the recent war, producing coarser work at 75 cents retail—the only known attempt at local production (pp. 33, 42).
- The Hylam hat from Hainan is about 6½ inches high with a 14–15 inch brim diameter, used almost exclusively by boatmen and fishermen in Malaya, and was giving way to the trilby by 1952 (pp. 45–46).
- The Bengali hat described by Das Gupta (1950) from Rangpur closely matches the Swatow No. 2 pattern, differing only in lacking the plaited crown cap and having two additional bamboo hoops for reinforcement (p. 36).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive historical takeaway is that these hats represent a distinctive Chinese craft tradition that has been exported widely across the region but has never been successfully replicated by non-Chinese peoples in terms of construction method, despite the availability of suitable materials locally. The remarkable uniformity of finish in Malayan examples indicates they are made specifically for export, and the nearest parallels outside China—the Bengali hat, the Dusun hat, and the Cape Malay *toering*—suggest either direct Chinese influence or shared ancestral forms whose transmission routes remain uncertain (pp. 34, 36–39).

## Context

- Primary evidence: direct examination of hat examples in Singapore and Malaya, photographs of labourers in southern Kwang-tung, and comparison with the British Museum collection (pp. 36, 39).
- Historiographical contribution: first published description of these hat patterns, filling a gap left by the author's own 1951 study of Malay hats; the appendix on Bangkok hats is likewise noted as the first published description of those forms (pp. 33, 46).
