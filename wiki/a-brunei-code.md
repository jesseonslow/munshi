# A Brunei code

## Abstract

In the early 1900s the zoological staff of the Selangor Museum regularly collected birds in mountainous parts of the Thai-Malay Peninsula, giving hill stations a prominent role in Malaysian ornithology. H. C. Robinson published a synopsis of the Selangor Museum’s collections of mountain birds in 1909, drawing connections to the work of other prominent European and American bird collectors. The Selangor Museum made a large donation of specimens to the Liverpool Museums in 1914, and several of these mountain birds are now in the World Museum, National Museums Liverpool. An examination of the provenance of these specimens reveals the synergy between the growth of bird collections from the peninsula, the transition to bird ‘watching’, and the development of some of these hill stations into enduringly popular resorts.

## Summary

This article traces the provenance of sixty bird specimens from the Selangor Museum that were donated to the Liverpool Museums in 1914 and now reside in the World Museum, National Museums Liverpool. Wilson uses these specimens as a lens to examine how the zoological staff of the Selangor Museum—under curator Herbert Christopher Robinson from 1903—systematically collected birds from the mountains of the Thai-Malay Peninsula, establishing the scientific foundations for what would become Malaysia's most popular hill-station birdwatching destinations.

The narrative is organised around the twelve mountain localities listed in Robinson's 1909 synopsis, "The birds at present known from the mountains of the Malay Peninsula." For each locality, Wilson identifies which specimens survive in Liverpool, who collected them, and when. The collectors were largely anonymous indigenous men referred to in the *Journal of the Federated Malay States Museums* only as "the Dyak Collectors," though one—Charles Ulok, formerly of the Sarawak Museum—is identifiable from other records. European staff including Robinson, Cecil Boden Kloss, and Eibert Carl Henry Seimund accompanied them on expeditions. The article draws on specimen labels, museum annual reports, Robinson's published synopses, and contemporary accounts by FMS geologist John Brooke Scrivenor to reconstruct the fieldwork.

A central argument is that the synergy between scientific collecting, the establishment of government hill stations for civil servants' recuperation, and the subsequent development of these sites into tourist resorts created a self-reinforcing cycle: museum collectors documented the birds, their publications (especially Robinson's 1928 *The Birds of the Malay Peninsula*, Vol. II: *The Birds of the Hill Stations*) popularised the localities, and the growing tourist infrastructure in turn sustained their ornithological reputation into the present day.

### Key Findings

- Approximately 300 bird specimens were donated from the Selangor Museum to the Liverpool Museums in 1914; 60 of these are mountain birds now in World Museum (p. 127).
- The Selangor Museum employed at least two full-time indigenous collectors, referred to only as "the Dyak Collectors" in the *Journal*; Charles Ulok, formerly of the Sarawak Museum, is identified as one of them and died of dysentery in 1920 while on duty at Kuala Tahan (pp. 123, 136).
- The 1905 FMS Museums expedition to Gunung Tahan (the highest peak in Peninsular Malaysia) was partly funded by the British Museum; specimens were divided between that museum and the Selangor Museum, with seven birds from the expedition now in World Museum (p. 134).
- Robinson's 1928 field guide *The Birds of the Malay Peninsula* was described by Frederick Nutter Chasen as having "stimulated an interest in birds throughout the country that has added more to our knowledge of life-histories in one decade than in the previous fifty years" (p. 135).
- By 1931, Scrivenor reported weekend "invasions" of the hill stations by European residents, confirming the transition from scientific collecting sites to popular resorts (p. 136).
- The Selangor Museum was demolished in 1959; most of its bird collections were transferred to the Raffles Museum (now the Lee Kong Chian Natural History Museum, Singapore) in 1926, with additional holdings at NHMUK and the American Museum of Natural History (pp. 136–137).

### Conclusion

Wilson's definitive takeaway is that the colonial-era scientific collecting programme of the Selangor Museum was not merely an academic exercise but the direct precursor to the hill-station birdwatching culture that persists in Peninsular Malaysia today. The transition from specimen collecting to live bird recording—epitomised by Chasen's observation logs and Robinson's semi-popular field guide—was mirrored by the physical development of the same mountains into enduring tourist resorts, creating a continuous thread from 1900s museum expeditions to contemporary eBird records.

## Context

- Primary archival collections examined: World Museum, National Museums Liverpool (NML-VZ vertebrate zoology collection), with cross-references to the Lee Kong Chian Natural History Museum (Singapore), NHMUK, and the American Museum of Natural History.
- The article contributes to the historiography of colonial natural history in British Southeast Asia by centring the role of unnamed indigenous collectors and by linking museum provenance research to the social history of hill-station development.
