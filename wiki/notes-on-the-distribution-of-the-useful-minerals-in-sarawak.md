# Notes on the distribution of the useful minerals in Sarawak

Tom Harrisson published this article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1976. It addresses the historiographical problem of early European term usages for the peoples of Borneo and the wider Southeast Asian archipelago, arguing that the seemingly distinct ethnonyms "Ngaju" (an upriver Kalimantan people) and "Bajau" (the sea-nomads of the Sulu Sea and northern Borneo) are in fact early variants of the same term referring to a single, fluid population that shifted between land and sea roles.

## Summary

The article's central argument is that the ethnonyms Ngaju and Bajau — and their many early European spellings (Byajo, Beaju, Biajo, Badjoo, Baju, etc.) — should not be treated as referring to separate peoples. Harrisson traces the term through 17th- and 18th-century European travel accounts, beginning with Beeckman's 1718 *A Voyage to and from the Island of Borneo*, which describes "Byajo" as fierce, warlike inlanders of the southwest Bornean coast who were simultaneously at sea in large prows, raiding and trading. The critical evidence is that Beeckman's "Byajo" are clearly the same people as the later "Ngaju" of the Kahayan River in Kalimantan — an upriver, agricultural, pagan population — yet in 1714 segments of them were already operating as sea-faring pirates off the coast, a duality that European observers failed to reconcile (pp. 1–3).

Harrisson extends the argument well beyond Borneo, drawing on Annandale and Robinson's 1903 account of the Orang Laut Kappir of Trang (southwest Thailand), Worsley's identification of "Baijini" boats in pre-Cook Australia, and the modern Sabah Bajau who have become rice-farmers and horsemen in the Mount Kinabalu foothills. He proposes that these sea-nomads were once part of a much wider "nation" stretching from the Bay of Burma through the peninsula and archipelago, whose members fluidly shifted between agricultural, jungle-collecting, and maritime roles. The advent of Islam in the 15th–16th centuries and European colonial administration from the 16th century onward progressively froze these fluid populations into fixed categories, a process that steam and diesel power finalised on the sea (pp. 6–8).

To test his hypothesis, Harrisson turns to oral tradition, specifically Clifford Sather's 1975 publication of a Bajau Laut narrative from Semporna about a culture hero, Pitun Depah, who demonstrates full mastery of rice cultivation despite the Bajau Laut being recorded as "not a rice-cultivating people." Harrisson reads the story's emphasis on rice — both in the hero's insatatable consumption as an infant and in his later agricultural exploits — as a symbolic expression of an ancient ambivalence between sea and land, suggesting that in earlier times the Bajau could plant as well as fish with equal competence (pp. 10–11).

### Key Findings

- Beeckman's 1718 account records the first known European encounter with "Byajo" boats at sea off the southwest Bornean coast; the Europeans initially mistook them for Banjareens before recognising them by dress and language, and the Byajo discharged "a shower of their poison'd Arrows" (p. 1).
- The term appears in at least seven distinct spellings across the 17th–20th centuries in Kalimantan alone (Byajo, Biajo, Beaju, Beadje, Viadje, Ngadju, Ngaju), with additional variants in Sabah (Lutao, Badjoo, Baju, Bajou, Bajau), Brunei, the Sulu Sea, the South China Sea, and Australia (Baijini) (p. 7).
- Annandale and Robinson (1903) documented two Orang Laut cemeteries at Chau Mai, Trang — one in a limestone cave containing hollowed-log coffins, the other in a rock shelter with pottery and Chinese porcelain — confirming that these sea-nomads maintained terrestrial burial practices and had only within the preceding decade begun settling on islands (p. 5).
- By 1975, the largest single group identifying as "Bajau" in Sabah were skilled rice-farmers and horsemen in the Mount Kinabalu foothills around Kota Belud, a dramatic transformation from the coastal fisherman-pedlars recorded by St. John in 1862 (p. 6).
- Sather's (1975) Semporna narrative of Pitun Depah shows the hero consuming a whole barrel of rice daily as an infant and later demonstrating complete mastery of planting, harvesting, and threshing, despite the Bajau Laut being recorded as "not an agricultural people" who mainly obtain vegetable food in the form of cassava by barter (pp. 10–11).
- Harrisson tabulates the term's usage across six geographic areas spanning the 17th through 20th centuries, showing continuous but variable application that defies fixed ethnic or racial categorisation (p. 7).

### Conclusion

Harrisson's definitive takeaway is that modern classificatory categories — whether "racial," linguistic, or ecological — have over-simplified a historically fluid human reality. The Ngaju and Bajau are not separate peoples but represent different positional emphases (inland versus maritime) within what was once a single, elastic population network. He urges scholars to resist the temptation to classify pre-literate peoples into fixed boxes and instead to use oral tradition as the one available form of native history capable of recovering these older, more complex patterns of movement and role-shifting.

## Context

- Primary sources drawn upon include Beeckman's 1718 voyage account (copy in Sarawak Museum Reference Library), Annandale and Robinson's 1903 *Fasciculi Malayensis* article (National Library, Singapore), and Sather's 1975 *Brunei Museum Journal* publication of a Semporna Bajau narrative.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its explicit challenge to the then-dominant tendency in Southeast Asian ethnography to treat sea-nomads as a fixed, specialised "type" (following Sopher 1965), arguing instead for a dynamic, time-depth model in which land and sea roles were interchangeable within a single population.
