# History, literature and social change: Harun Aminurrashid’s independence novel Panglima Awang

Nathan Porath's 2011 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the *Hikayat Patani*—a six-book manuscript composed between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries—as a mimetic text that encodes the kingdom of Patani's power relationships with Ayutthaya and Johor within the hierarchical Indic political order of pre-modern Southeast Asia. Porath argues that the text simultaneously affirms Patani's cultural similarity with Ayutthaya (even while narrating rebellions against it) and fractures its resemblances with Johor, revealing that Patani's political prestige was derived from its position within the Ayutthaya mandala rather than from any ethnic affinity with other Malay kingdoms.

## Summary

The article applies René Girard's theory of mimetic desire and Simon Harrison's concept of "fracturing resemblances" to the first book of the *Hikayat Patani*, the principal source for the early history of the kingdom. Porath treats the text not as a straightforward historical chronicle but as a symbolically political-cultural document whose narrative structure encodes power relationships. The first book, which Bradley identifies as originally oral in composition, is concerned with Patani's relations with two kingdoms: Ayutthaya to the north and Johor to the south. The text's mimetic logic is paradoxical: it affirms similarity with Ayutthaya even as it narrates Patani's rebellions against that kingdom, while it goes to great lengths to fracture any resemblance with Johor at every narrative juncture (pp. 45–48).

Porath traces the political history encoded in the text, from the founding of Patani by inland rajas from Kota Maligai, through the conversion to Islam (which he interprets as a social contract between ruler and realm rather than a simple religious event), to the devolution of power to the *bendahara* of Sai and the eventual appropriation of the throne by Kelantanese dynasts. The two rebellions against Ayutthaya—the first by Sultan Mudhaffar Syah in the 1560s, the second under Queen Raja Ungu in the 1630s—are reinterpreted not as proto-nationalist secessionist acts but as betrayals within a shared Indic framework of mimetic kingship. The first rebellion, in particular, is read as an attempted usurpation of the Ayutthaya throne during the crisis of Burmese conquest, not as Patani asserting independence from Siam (pp. 48–62).

The article's central interpretive move is to show that the *Hikayat Patani* was composed as a charter for the viability of the Patani *kerajaan*, and that one precondition for that viability was the maintenance of hierarchical order with Ayutthaya. The text's refusal to moralize against Ayutthaya—unlike other Malay *hikayats* such as the *Sejarah Melayu*, which deny resemblance with Siam—suggests that the scribes recognized Patani's political identity as fundamentally constituted by its relationship to the northern kingdom. The ethnic identity question is raised in the text only in relation to Johor, and only to fracture it (pp. 62–63).

### Key Findings

- The *Hikayat Patani* manuscript was written in 1839 and comprises six books composed at different periods between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries, following the end of the original founding dynasty and during the kingdom's decline under Kelantanese kings (p. 46).
- Sultan Mudhaffar Syah's attack on Ayutthaya (c. 1564) is confirmed by the Luang Prasoert chronicle, which records the sultan riding one of King Cakkraphat's white elephants in the palace plaza; van Vliet provides the motive as the perceived weakness of the Siamese king during the Burmese wars (pp. 55–56).
- The key Malay sentence "apa bicara bendahara karena kita hendak pergi ke Ayutia dan Beracau itu pun tiada orang lain dari kita dan dari pada sebuah negeri baiklah dua" is argued to encode the Ayutthaya-Burmese war context: "that king is not different from us" affirms mimetic identity, while "rather than one country, two is better" references the Burmese demand that Ayutthaya and Pegu become "one great golden country" (pp. 56–57).
- Queen Raja Ungu rejected her Thai royal title *Phra Nang Chaou Ying* (pronounced locally as *raja nang chayang*) and instead adopted the Islamic title Paduka Syah Alam, severing Patani's ties with Ayutthaya in a manner unprecedented in the kingdom's history (p. 60).
- King Prasat Thong mobilized 60,000 men for the army that attacked Patani in the 1630s, headed by the Governor of Ligor, Okya Kalahom, the Pra Klang, and Rabisit (a Muslim official); the Dutch in Batavia agreed to send armed ships but arrived too late (p. 61).
- The *Hikayat Patani* states that the first time the governor of Ligor and Siam had attacked Patani was during the second rebellion, indicating a radical change in the Patani-Ayutthaya relationship between the first and second rebellions (p. 61).

### Conclusion

Porath concludes that the *Hikayat Patani* is best understood as a charter text that encodes the conditions necessary for Patani's viability as a *kerajaan*: the maintenance of peace and hierarchical order with Ayutthaya, and the deliberate fracturing of cultural resemblance with Johor. Patani's political identity was constituted within the Ayutthaya mandala, not within the Malay-speaking world, and the text's mimetic affirmations of similarity with Siam—even amid rebellion—reveal that the kingdom's prestige and self-conception were derived from its position in the Indic hierarchical order. Reading the text through a modern ethnicist lens would fundamentally misread its political logic (pp. 62–63).

## Context

- The article draws on the Teeuw and Wyatt (1970) edition of the *Hikayat Patani*, the Thai version translated by Baker et al. (2005) from van Vliet's papers, the Luang Prasoert chronicle (Cushman 2006), and the *Sejarah Melayu* (Brown 1952), alongside secondary works by Bougas, Bradley, Suwannathat-Pian, and Horstmann.
- Historiographically, the article contributes a structural-interpretive reading of the *Hikayat Patani* that departs from both nationalist and ethnicist framings, positioning the text within the broader scholarship on Indic mandala politics and mimetic theory in Southeast Asian state formation.
