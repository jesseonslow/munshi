# Adat Kuala Pilah. J. J. Sheehan and Abdul Aziz bin Khamis

J. J. Sheehan and Abdul Aziz bin Khamis published this systematic codification of the adat (customary law) governing the Kuala Pilah district of Negri Sembilan in 1936, during the British colonial period when the state's traditional institutions were being formally documented and increasingly subordinated to statutory law. The work presents the living adat as agreed upon by the Penghulu and Lembaga councils and approved by the Yang di-Pertuan Besar, capturing a transitional moment in which customary authority coexisted with—and was progressively constrained by—colonial administrative structures.

## Summary

The article is structured as a comprehensive code of customary law for the Kuala Pilah district, organized around the six luak (Johol, Ulu Muar, Jempol, Terachi, Gunong Pasir, and Inas) and the hierarchical relationship between the Ke-Adilan (the Yang di-Pertuan Besar), the Undang (Penghulu Luak), the Lembaga (clan heads), and the Orang Empat Istana (Four Palace Officers). It distinguishes between two adat systems: Adat Perpateh, followed by the general Malay population organized into twelve suku (clans), and Adat Temenggong, followed by the royal lineage and their ancient retainers (Ayer Kaki and Ayer Kaki Lengkongan). The authors explicitly note that the text was compiled from deliberations of the Penghulu and Lembaga councils and that no additions or alterations were made to the rules, language, or proverbs as agreed by those councils (p. 191).

The most substantive chapters deal with marriage, inheritance, and land tenure. Marriage customs are detailed with specific bride-price (mas kahwin) schedules that vary by luak, by the status of the bride (waris versus other suku), and by whether the marriage is consensual or by elopement (bersalahan). The inheritance rules reflect the matrilineal logic of Adat Perpateh: customary land (tanah pesaka) descends through the female line, children belong to the mother's suku upon divorce or death, and a woman without daughters may "pull" (menarek) a female relative into her lineage to continue the pesaka. The article also documents the ongoing tension between adat and sharia, particularly regarding mosque governance, where the Kathi's authority was asserted over the traditional adat-based selection of religious officers (pp. 218–219).

The appendix, written by the Tengku Besar Sri Menanti, provides critical historical corrections that challenge some of the main text's claims. Most significantly, it demonstrates that Luak Inas was never genuinely subordinate to Luak Johol, despite appearances created by a marriage alliance between Dato' Johol Gubah and a Waris of Inas, and that the Penghulu Tanah Mengandong never possessed the sweeping judicial powers attributed to them in the main text's proverbs (pp. 221–223). The appendix also clarifies that the Pegawai 99, though numbered in the hundreds by 1936, were originally a small corps of royal attendants whose titles were granted by the Ke-Adilan (p. 204).

### Key Findings

- The Malay adat population of Kuala Pilah numbered approximately 30,000, holding 25,000 acres of customary land (tanah pesaka) and 13,000 land grants, with Luak Ulu Muar the largest at 16,000 people and 13,000 acres (p. 194).
- Bride price (mas kahwin) for a waris anak dara in Luak Johol was $40, while bersalahan (elopement) fines for the same category reached $120; for a janda waris the figures were $20 and $60 respectively (pp. 205–207).
- The Penghulu's marriage payment (tanda pesaka) was $14 (sabahara), while the Lembaga's was $7; the kepala mayat (corpse-head payment) upon an Undang's death was $14 to the Ke-Adilan, and $14 to the Penghulu upon a Lembaga's death (pp. 195–196, 215).
- Sedekah mati (death charity) was graded by rank: an Undang paid $1.50, his wife $1.50, his parents $0.75 each, his in-laws $0.50 each; a Lembaga and his wife each paid $0.50 (p. 214).
- The district contained 43 mosques (23 in Ulu Muar, 8 in Jempol, 8 in Johol, 3 in Terachi, 1 in Gunong Pasir), and the former Kathi of Sri Menanti, Haji Ibrahim, confirmed that the Kathi held sole authority over mosque establishment and officer appointment (p. 225).
- The Pegawai 99, originally a small corps of royal attendants, had grown to over 300 by 1936, though the Lembaga insisted they remained subject to local adat jurisdiction rather than being free of it as the Orang Empat Istana argued (pp. 204–205).

### Conclusion

The authors present the adat of Kuala Pilah as a living, self-regulating system that, while fundamentally stable in its core principles ("not yellowed by turmeric, not stretched by heat, not rotted by rain"), was undergoing measurable erosion at its margins—evidenced by the informal sale of pesaka land, the relaxation of kerbau-slaying protocols, and the shift from adat to sharia in mosque governance. The definitive historical takeaway is that the adat functioned as a parallel legal order whose authority derived from communal consent (muafakat) rather than statutory mandate, and whose continued viability depended on the willingness of its officers to enforce sanctions that were increasingly costly and socially awkward in a colonial cash economy.

## Context

- J. J. Sheehan was a British colonial administrator (M.C.S.) serving in the Kuala Pilah district; the work was produced under the patronage of the Yang di-Pertuan Besar and with the editorial assistance of the Tengku Besar Sri Menanti, reflecting a collaborative colonial-indigenous documentation effort typical of interwar MBRAS publications.
- The article draws on the 1931 district census, land office records, and oral testimony from the Dato' Ulu Muar, and represents one of the most comprehensive district-level adat codifications produced in Negri Sembilan during the interwar period.
