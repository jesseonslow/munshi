# The Mentra traditions

D.F.A. Hervey published this collection of Mentra oral traditions in 1882, recording narratives communicated by Batin Pa' Inai, who claimed authority as head of all the Batins of the Mentra tribes and who had resided in Johol for approximately fifteen years (p. 189). The work presents a cosmogonic and genealogical corpus that positions the Mentra as the primordial aboriginal stock from which all other indigenous peoples of the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra derive, linking the creation of the natural world to the political foundations of Minangkabau and the Jelebu–Johol region.

## Summary

The traditions open with the primordial figures Mertang and Belo, whose actions generate the basic features of the world: Mertang takes his house to Tanah Bangun, leaving behind the first dog; Belo, so tall that the sea reaches only his knees, raises the sky to clear the way for his pestle (p. 190). Mertang marries his youngest sister, from whom the Mentra descend, while Belo marries the other sister without issue. When Mertang's descendants multiply to an alarming degree, Tuhan Dibawah turns half of mankind into trees; later, when the problem recurs, Belo persuades Tuhan Dibawah to institute death in the manner of the banana plant—leaving children behind—replacing the earlier condition in which men merely waxed and waned with the lunar cycle (p. 190).

The second major cycle concerns To' Entah, son of Mertang and the first Batin, who engineers the separation of day and night by deceiving the female sun into swallowing her husband (Bintang Tunang, the evening star) and child, after having tricked the moon into holding them in her mouth (pp. 190–191). The enraged sole sun then withdraws to the opposite side of the heavens, vowing to devour the moon at every conjunction—the origin of eclipses. In a parallel episode, To' Entah's first experience of thirst after cooking a monkey leads him to discover water in a jelotong stump; the lelabi (a great turtle) that emerges with a vast body of water pursues him, and it is only the collective effort of the kanchil (the smallest deer) that destroys the creature, the released water becoming the sea (pp. 191–192).

The final section transitions from cosmogony to political genealogy. To' Entah travels to Pagar-ruyong in Sumatra; his son To' Terjeli returns to settle in Jelebu and fathers eight Batins who disperse to Klang, Jelebu, Johor, Pagar-ruyong, Siam, Minangkabau, Stambul, and Moar (p. 192). To' Terjeli also establishes the first Penghulus, including the notable To' Mutan Jantan, a woman, at Kwala Moar, whose memory is preserved in the long hair of the Dato' of Johol (pp. 192–193). The first Raja is identified as Salengkar Alam of Bukit Guntang Penyaring in Ulu Menangkabau, and the name "Menangkabau" itself is etymologised from a race of golden-horned buffaloes that vanished into a hole behind the Raja's house (p. 193). The text closes with the legend of Khatib Malim Seleman, who pursues a fair-haired Princess into a giant bamboo and, after failing with a parang, patil, and pahat, succeeds with a kachip (betel-nut clippers); both then disappear at Bukit Peraja in Ulu Jempol, where they are said to remain invisible to this day (pp. 193–194).

### Key Findings

- The Mentra are explicitly identified as the original aboriginal stock; all other indigenous tribes are described as "merely varieties" of this group, some of whom "perhaps have turned Malay" (p. 194).
- Death is not primordial but a later institutional change: men originally cycled with the moon, and Belo's banana-plant analogy secured divine approval for mortality as a population-control mechanism (p. 190).
- The tri-solar cosmogony (husband, wife, child) and To' Entah's deception explain both the day/night division and the periodicity of solar eclipses as the sun's ongoing wrath (pp. 190–191).
- The kanchil, the smallest of the deer kind, defeats the lelabi through collective action, and the released water becomes the sea; the kanchil's reward is the leaves of the kledek yam (p. 192).
- To' Terjeli's eight sons establish a pan-regional Batin network spanning the Malay Peninsula, Siam, Minangkabau, and even "Stambul" (Istanbul), suggesting a mythic claim to universal indigenous sovereignty (p. 192).
- The succession of the Penghulu at Kwala Moar passes through the female line, beginning with To' Mutan Jantan, a woman, and the Dato' of Johol's waist-length hair serves as a living mnemonic of this matriarchal origin (pp. 192–193).

### Conclusion

Hervey's definitive historical takeaway is that the Mentra constitute the ur-people of the Malay world: their cosmogonic acts (raising the sky, instituting death, creating day and night, releasing the sea) and their genealogical dispersal (through To' Terjeli's eight sons) provide a single explanatory framework for both natural phenomena and the political geography of the peninsula and western Sumatra. The Batin's closing assertion—that all aboriginal tribes are merely varieties of the Mentra—functions as an ethnographic claim of primacy, positioning the Jelebu–Johol Mentra as the ancestral source of Malay, Minangkabau, and other indigenous identities.

## Context

- The informant, Batin Pa' Inai (originally Kolor of Tanah Taseh, Jelebu), was a self-styled political authority claiming jurisdiction over all Mentra Batins; his fifteen-year residence in Johol suggests a displaced or itinerant leadership role typical of the Batin class in the late nineteenth century.
- Hervey's brief observation that "some of these legends somewhat resemble German stories on the same subjects" (p. 189) reflects the comparative-folklore methodology prevalent in Victorian-era ethnography, though no specific German parallels are identified.
