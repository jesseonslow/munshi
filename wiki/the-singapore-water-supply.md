# The Singapore water supply

P.H. Kratoska's 2016 Editor's Note in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* presents a curated selection of Colonial Office records tracing Singapore's water supply from the 1850s through the early twenty-first century. The piece, which accompanies Prof. Martha Kaplan's article in the same issue, argues that the colony's water infrastructure was shaped by a persistent tension between imperial fiscal restraint and the public health imperatives of a rapidly growing tropical port city.

## Summary

The note assembles six primary-source excerpts from the Colonial Office (CO273) series to illustrate how Singapore's water supply evolved from a matter of private philanthropy to a state-managed utility. In the 1850s and early 1860s, the Government of India explicitly declined responsibility for waterworks, viewing them as beyond the scope of imperial expenditure in a "wealthy city" expected to "find their own water supply." The project was initiated only after the merchant Tan Kim Sing donated $13,000 as a private contribution, and even then the India Office insisted that "no superfluous expense should be incurred" (pp. 144–145).

The 1861 report by the Senior Surgeon for the Straits Settlements provides a vivid account of water scarcity during a five-month drought, describing water boats drawing from the Ballestier and Bras Basah Canals—sources rendered "utterly unfit for drinking" by leper hospital waste and convict-line refuse. The surgeon linked impure water to cholera and dysentery among the poorer classes and attributed declining rainfall to deforestation of the island's hills (p. 145). Following the 1867 transfer of the Straits Settlements from the India Office to the Colonial Office, the administration became more willing to treat water as an essential public work, and the Thompson (now Macritchie) Reservoir was planned in 1863 to serve a population then consuming roughly one million gallons per day (pp. 145–146).

The most dramatic episode documented is the 1902 drought, when the reservoir level fell to 13 feet—barely above the 11-foot minimum for pure water extraction—and the Governor reported that only one month's supply could be guaranteed. In 1905 the Governor formally rejected a mainland supply from Johore as "prohibitive" in cost and strategically inadvisable for a "commercial and strategic base" to depend on a territory outside immediate British administration. It was not until the 1920s and 1930s that Johore sources were developed, with the first reservoir completed in 1929 and a second in 1931, connected via the causeway constructed during that decade (pp. 146–147).

### Key Findings

- The Government of India refused to sanction waterworks in 1861, stating that "wealthy cities like Singapore are expected to find their own water supply"; the project proceeded only after Tan Kim Sing's private donation of $13,000 (p. 144).
- The Thompson (Macritchie) Reservoir was planned in 1863 to dam a stream in a valley of not less than 425 acres, with the bund site 11 feet above high water mark (p. 145).
- By 1869, daily consumption had risen to approximately 1.5 million gallons; by 1902 it reached 5 million gallons, with a projected 9 million by 1911 (pp. 145–146).
- The 1902 drought reduced the reservoir to 13 feet (minimum for pure water: 11 feet), forcing the cutoff of all harbour ship supply except in emergencies and the reopening of previously sealed wells (p. 146).
- The 1905 rejection of a Johore supply cited the need for a costly syphon or elevated aqueduct across the strait, plus a further fourteen-mile pipeline across the island, and the strategic objection to dependence on a non-British territory (p. 146).
- Singapore's Public Utilities Board reported daily consumption of 430 million gallons in June 2016, illustrating the scale of growth from the 1860s baseline (p. 147).

### Conclusion

Kratoska's compilation underscores that Singapore's water security was never a straightforward engineering problem but a product of shifting imperial administrative priorities, public health crises, and geopolitical calculations about strategic self-sufficiency. The eventual turn to Johore in the 1920s—precisely the dependency the 1905 Governor had deemed unacceptable—reveals how demographic and commercial growth ultimately overrode the strategic caution that had governed earlier policy.

## Context

- The primary sources are drawn from the Colonial Office series CO273 (Straits Settlements), held at The National Archives, Kew, spanning 1861–1905.
- The note serves as a documentary supplement to Martha Kaplan's article in the same issue and points readers to Brenda S.A. Yeoh's *Contesting Space in Colonial Singapore* (1996; reissued 2003, pp. 175–190) for a comprehensive treatment of the topic.
