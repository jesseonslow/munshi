# Minor reports concerning Negritos in Northern Pahang

Rodney Needham published this article in 1976 in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, recording fragmentary ethnographic observations gathered during an unsuccessful two-week search in May 1955 for nomadic Negritos on the Gunong Tahan massif in northern Pahang. The overarching thesis is that even the most minor and uncertain field reports, when set alongside the sparse existing literature, can yield decisive evidence of the distribution, economy, and social organisation of forest-nomadic Negrito groups in a region that had been poorly documented up to that point.

## Summary

Needham frames the article with a methodological reflection on the value of "minor reports" to later researchers: observations that seemed trivial to their original authors may prove decisive to subsequent scholars working with different questions. He then turns to the specific problem that motivated his 1955 fieldwork. G. de G. Sieveking had reported in 1954 that purely nomadic Negritos existed in the Kenyam river region, a tributary descending the southeastern flanks of the Gunong Tahan. Needham, accompanied by J. A. Hislop (Superintendent of the King George V National Park) and two Siwang trackers, set out to locate them. The search covered the Kenyam, the Kenyam Kechil, and the high ridges between them, but the party found only abandoned camps and old tuber-diggings, no living Negritos, and no recent signs of their presence.

The article's substantive contribution lies in the corroborating evidence Needham assembled from local Malays, Siwang, and Semelai informants, which collectively confirmed that the Kenyam people were indeed Negritos (Bateg). Physical descriptions (black skin, tightly curled hair), a small vocabulary list, and—most decisively—a set of kinship terms that align with those recorded by Schebesta and Evans for other Negrito groups all converge on this identification. Needham further establishes that the Negritos' economy rested entirely on hunting and wild-tuber collection, that they laid exclusive territorial claims to the areas they exploited, and that they shared all food equally within the group. He argues that, despite Evans's generalisation that Negritos prefer river valleys and foothills rather than high ground, the people on the Gunong Tahan massif were in fact forest nomads who had been progressively driven up the steep flanks of the mountain by the encroachment of Malays and other groups on the upper Tembeling and Jelai.

A further line of argument concerns the question of whether one or two distinct groups occupied the massif. Hubback's earlier report on the Tanum (Tanom) people described them as "not pure Negritos, but a mixture," whereas Needham's own informants described the Kenyam people as unambiguously Negrito. Needham considers both possibilities—that there were two physically distinguishable groups, or a single group with mixed somatic composition—and notes that the Kenyam group, which absolutely avoided contact with riverine peoples, would have needed some communication with kindred groups (such as those trading with a Malay village on the upper Kechau) in order to acquire the iron cutting tools essential to their survival.

### Key Findings

- Schebesta (1927) listed the Menri Negritos at approximately 400 souls in south-eastern Kelantan and northern Pahang; Williams-Hunt's 1952 census recorded 382 Negritos in Ulu Kelantan, 559 in Lipis (Pahang), and 161 in Kemaman (Trengganu) (pp. 182–183).
- The abandoned camp on the Kenyam ridge contained the remains of four lean-to shelters, estimated at 15–20 occupants; the camp west of the Kenyam Kechil contained two shelters, estimated at 8–10 occupants. Begng's estimate of the maximum viable group size was about 10 families or 50 individuals (p. 191).
- The *wau* tuber, the most common staple, takes only two months to grow to a size large enough for consumption; most tubers must be eaten the same day they are dug or they turn black and become inedible (pp. 188–189).
- Linguistic comparison: the Bateg word *petom* (stream, water) is assimilable to *beteu* in the Skeat and Blagden comparative vocabulary; *lahu* (sky) matches the Negrito *lahu* recorded by Skeat; kinship terms *tak* (grandfather), *yak* (grandmother), *e* (father), *nak* (mother), *banhup* (sibling), *kesiu* (husband), and *kenih* (wife) correspond to terms in both Schebesta's and Evans's Negrito vocabularies (pp. 186–187).
- The 1955 search lasted a little over two weeks, covering the Kenyam, Kenyam Kechil, and the high ridges between them; the most intensive single day's march (8:15 a.m. to 5:45 p.m.) along the western watershed of the Kenyam Kechil yielded only five or six *wau* tubers and no recent Negrito signs (pp. 189–190).
- No case of marriage between Bateg and Malays was known to local informants; the Siwang stated they did not marry the Negritos and that "they do not want to either," though Ogilvie reported at least one Bateg married into the Siwang group in the ulu Dong before the war (p. 187).

### Conclusion

Needham's definitive historical takeaway is that the fragmentary reports and the few direct physical evidences (abandoned shelters, old tuber-diggings) together confirm the presence of forest-nomadic Negritos on the Gunong Tahan massif in the mid-1950s, a finding that contradicts Evans's categorical statement that Negritos are not mountaineers. The people were almost certainly driven to the higher, more inaccessible ground by the advance of Malays and others onto the upper Tembeling and Jelai, and their extreme isolation—avoiding even the Semelai and Siwang—testifies to their status as among the "wildest" aboriginal groups in Malaya. The postscript, noting that Dr. Kirk Endicott and Dr. Geoffrey Benjamin had subsequently visited the Tahan and Tanum groups respectively, lends the article a retrospective historical value: the circumstances of these people had already changed greatly by the time of publication, making Needham's 1955 observations a baseline record of a way of life in the process of disappearing.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on Needham's own 1955 field notes (originally a confidential report to the Operations Research Unit, G.H.Q., Far Eastern Land Forces) and on the secondary ethnographic literature of Schebesta, Evans, Williams-Hunt, and Ogilvie; no new archival collections were consulted for the 1976 publication.
- Historiographically, the piece fills a specific gap in the record for the upper Tembeling region and provides a dated baseline against which the post-Emergency and post-national-park transformations of Negrito life in northern Pahang can be measured.
