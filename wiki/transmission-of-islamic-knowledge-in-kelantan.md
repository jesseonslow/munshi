# Transmission of Islamic knowledge in Kelantan

Muhamad Ali's 2006 article surveys the transmission of Islamic knowledge in Kelantan across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, arguing that local *ulama*—not traders or foreign missionaries—were the primary agents of Islamization in the state. The article traces how scholarly networks linking Kelantan to Mecca, Egypt, Patani, and South Asia produced generations of teachers who disseminated Islamic learning through *surau*, *pondok*, and *madrasah*, and how this educational infrastructure eventually became entangled with modernist reform and political activism.

## Summary

Ali's central argument is that the Islamization of Kelantan was a process driven by teacher–pupil networks rather than by commercial or royal channels. Before the nineteenth century, Islamic knowledge in Kelantan was confined largely to Quranic recitation in mosques and *surau*, with the Sultan's court as the primary patron of religious learning. The pivotal shift came when local students returned from Patani and Mecca bearing broader curricula in *fiqh*, *tauhid*, *tasawwuf*, and Arabic grammar, and established *pondok* as semi-independent centres of learning. The *pondok* system, which Ali traces to Haji Abd. Samad (Tok Pulai Chondong) in 1820, was hierarchical, orally oriented, and rooted in memorization of classical Arabic texts. Teachers held both religious and social authority, and their personal example—piety, dress, demeanour—functioned as a form of transmission in itself.

The early twentieth century brought two major transformations. First, the establishment of the Majelis Ugama dan Isti'adat Melayu in 1915 marked the bureaucratization of Islamic education, creating a state-recognized class of *ulama* and *imam* and founding the Madrasah Muhammadiah, which offered a modernist curriculum in Malay, English, and Arabic. Second, reformist ideas from Egypt (Muhammad Abduh, Rashid Rida) and India (Shah Waliyullah al-Dahlawi, Darul Uloom Deoband) entered Kelantan through returning graduates such as Tok Kenali and Tok Khurasan, introducing *tafsir*, Hadith sciences, and calls for *ijtihad* over *taqlid*. These reformist currents were disseminated not primarily through journals—though *Pengasuh* and *Al-Hidayah* played a role—but through *madrasah* teaching and mosque sermons.

By the 1950s, the boundary between religious education and political engagement dissolved. Reformist *ulama* who had long argued that Islam governed all aspects of life began to enter party politics, culminating in the formation of PAS in 1951 and its electoral victories in Kelantan. Ali argues that this political turn was not a rupture but a continuation of the same process: the *ulama* who had transmitted Islamic knowledge through *pondok* and *madrasah* now transmitted it through governance, with figures like Nik Abdul Aziz Nik Mat embodying the fusion of educator and politician.

### Key Findings

- Haji Abd. Samad (Tok Pulai Chondong) is recognized as the first *ulama* to introduce the *pondok* system in Kelantan in 1820, converting his land into a *waqf* for students; by 1880 his students numbered approximately 500 (p. 6).
- The Majelis Ugama dan Isti'adat Melayu was established in December 1915 with twelve members, four of whom were recognized *ulama*; it founded the Madrasah Muhammadiah, whose Malay stream began with 310 pupils and English stream with 140 pupils in 1917 (pp. 10, 14).
- Tok Kenali (1869–1933), a student of Ahmad Patani, returned from Mecca and Egypt in 1908 and reportedly had about 300 students from across the peninsula, Sumatera, Patani, and Cambodia; he served as chief editor of *Pengasuh* from July 1918 and *Al-Hidayah* from June 1923 (p. 9).
- In 1973, Kelantan had 54 *pondok* with approximately 3,152 pupils, demonstrating the survival of the *pondok* tradition alongside *madrasah* institutions (p. 14).
- Madrasah al-Diniyatul Bakariah in Terusan, Pasir Tumbuh, was founded in 1948 with some 700 students, 4 *ulama*, and 50 teaching assistants; Darul Ulum al-Ahmadiyah in Kolam, Bachok, was established in 1950 with some 300 pupils from Kelantan, Trengganu, Kedah, Pahang, Singapore, and Thailand (p. 14).
- PAS gained control of Kelantan in the 1959 general election, regained it in 1978, and has held power continuously since 1990 under Nik Abdul Aziz Nik Mat, who studied at Deoband (India), Lahore (Pakistan), and al-Azhar University (Cairo) before returning to Kelantan (p. 17).

### Conclusion

Ali concludes that the transmission of Islamic knowledge in Kelantan followed a distinct trajectory later than in Sumatera or Sulawesi, with significant scholarly networks only emerging in the nineteenth century. The *ulama*—local rather than foreign—were the decisive agents of Islamization, and the institutional forms they created (*surau*, *pondok*, *madrasah*) proved durable enough to survive into the post-independence era. The ultimate historical takeaway is that in Kelantan, Islamic education, reformist thought, and political power became progressively fused, producing a distinctive model in which the *ulama* class assumed direct governance—a process Ali frames as the "Kelantanization" of Islam.

## Context

- The article draws on a range of secondary sources including the edited volume *Kelantan: Religion, Society and Politics in a Malay State* (Roff, ed., 1974), Abdul Halim Ahmad's *Pendidikan Islam di Kelantan* (1982), and Nik Abdul Aziz bin Haji Nik Hassan's essays on Kelantanese *ulama*, alongside more recent works by Riddell (2001) and Roff (2003).
- The study contributes to the historiography of Islamic education in the Malay world by shifting focus from the well-studied seventeenth- and eighteenth-century networks of Ar-Raniri, al-Sinkili, and al-Makassari to the later, locally centred networks of Kelantan, and by explicitly linking the *pondok*–*madrasah* educational tradition to the political rise of PAS.
