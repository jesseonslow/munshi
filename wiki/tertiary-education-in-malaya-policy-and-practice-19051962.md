# Tertiary education in Malaya: policy and practice, 1905–1962

Lee Kam Hing and Ahmat Adam (1990) revisit C.A. Gibson-Hill's 1956 puzzle over the Acehnese Order of the Golden Sword conferred on Sir Stamford Raffles, resolving it through the newly discovered Malay letter of Sultan Jauhar Alam Syah dated 27 April 1811. The authors argue that the award was a straightforward diplomatic exchange motivated by the Sultan's desire for British support following the seizure of his ship the *Hydroos*, and that Raffles' decision to incorporate it into his 1817 coat of arms reflected both its status as his first royal decoration and his strategic commitment to Jauhar Alam Syah's legitimacy in the Acehnese succession crisis.

## Summary

The article addresses a gap left by Gibson-Hill, who in 1956 could find no documentary record of the circumstances surrounding Raffles' Acehnese decoration. The breakthrough comes from a Malay manuscript in Jawi script, discovered in a British attic and translated by Ahmat Adam, in which Sultan Jauhar Alam Syah explicitly confers the title *Sri Paduka Orangkaya Berpedang Emas* on Raffles. The letter was delivered by the envoy Tengku Nakhoda Abdul Rahman when Raffles was in Melaka preparing for the Java expedition, and a copy was forwarded to Lord Minto as "enclosure No. 7" in Raffles' despatch of 11 June 1811, now held in the Raffles-Minto Collection at the India Office Library (pp. 77–79).

The authors reconstruct the diplomatic context to explain the Sultan's motivation. The *Hydroos*, a vessel purchased by Jauhar Alam Syah but still registered to a Danish citizen, had been seized and sold by Penang authorities in 1808 as enemy property during the Napoleonic Wars. The Sultan's repeated appeals for its return went unanswered, and British suspicions that Aceh harboured French naval interests—evidenced by the 1809 detention of Lt. Col. Leon de la Houssaye at Benkulen—had further strained relations. Raffles' letter of early 1811, which briefed the Sultan on impending British military moves against Java, therefore represented a significant shift in tone and must have encouraged the Sultan to reciprocate with a decoration befitting the agent of the Governor-General of Bengal (pp. 80–81).

The article then turns to the question of why Raffles singled out this particular award for his coat of arms when he had received more prestigious decorations from Javanese rulers. The authors offer a composite explanation: it was his first award from any monarch (his British knighthood came only in 1816); Aceh held historical significance as the site of the first EIC treaty with an Asian power in 1602; and, most importantly, the inclusion in 1817 was a public statement of Raffles' political position in the Acehnese succession dispute, where he backed Jauhar Alam Syah against the Penang-backed pretender Syed Hussein Aidid (pp. 81–85).

### Key Findings

- The Sultan's letter is dated 27 April 1811 (Hijri 1226, third of Rabiul-akhir) and was written by Pocut Kaya son of Muhammad Hassan; it is a single folio measuring 47 cm × 57.6 cm in Jawi script with a black seal (pp. 86–88).
- The *Hydroos* was seized by Penang in 1808; the cargo was later returned but the ship itself was sold, and the Sultan's appeals to both Penang and Bengal for compensation were unsuccessful (p. 80).
- Raffles' despatch to Lord Minto of 11 June 1811 references "enclosure No. 7," which is positively identified as the same Acehnese letter, now in the Raffles-Minto Collection, Vol. 6, India Office Library (p. 78).
- The three Panglima Sagis declared the overthrow of Jauhar Alam Syah in 1814 and invited Syed Hussein Aidid of Penang to the Acehnese throne; Syed Hussein had obtained loans of $50,000 (July 1815) and $30,000 (August 1816) from the Penang government (pp. 82–83).
- Raffles proposed in 1810 a British resident in Aceh supported by 100 sepoys, with costs borne by the Acehnese, to manage port duties and prevent French naval use of Acehnese ports (p. 83).
- The first treaty of friendship between the English East India Company and an Asian power was signed with Aceh in 1602 under Sultan Ala'uddin Riayat Syah (p. 82).

### Conclusion

The authors conclude that the Order of the Golden Sword was neither trivial nor suspicious but a legitimate diplomatic gesture within a broader exchange of goodwill, and that Raffles' decisive support for Jauhar Alam Syah in the 1819 mission to Aceh—ending the civil war and producing the long-advocated treaty with the British—was the practical outcome of the relationship the decoration symbolised (p. 85).

## Context

- Primary archival sources: Raffles-Minto Collection (India Office Library, London), Straits Settlements Factory Records (India Office Library), and the newly discovered Malay manuscript translated by Ahmat Adam.
- The article represents a historiographical correction to Gibson-Hill's 1956 speculative reconstruction, replacing conjecture with documentary evidence and situating Raffles' Acehnese policy within the broader strategic competition between Penang-based commercial interests and the Governor-General's imperial calculus.
