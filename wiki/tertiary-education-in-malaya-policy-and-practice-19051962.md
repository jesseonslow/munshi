# Tertiary education in Malaya: policy and practice, 1905–1962

K.G. Tregonning, a historian who served as Raffles Professor of History at the University of Malaya from 1953, published this retrospective in 1990 tracing the development of tertiary education in Malaya from the founding of the Straits Settlements Medical School in 1905 to the separation of the University of Malaya into two independent institutions in 1962. His central argument is that British colonial laissez-faire attitudes to higher education perpetuated racial divisions and squandered opportunities for using the university as a unifying national institution, while the Japanese occupation—despite its brutality—broke the colonial inertia that had stifled educational progress.

## Summary

Tregonning opens by drawing a parallel between the British government's hands-off approach to education in Britain and its colonial counterpart in the Straits Settlements. The Medical School (later King Edward VII College of Medicine) was established in 1905 largely through the pressure of prominent Straits Chinese leaders, particularly Tan Jiak Kim, and began with just twenty-three students in buildings formerly part of a female lunatic asylum (p. 1). When the Methodist Mission sought to establish an Anglo-Chinese college in 1914, the government recoiled, fearing the American-style, technically oriented curriculum it proposed would undermine British prestige and its elitist educational philosophy (p. 2). The government's alternative, Raffles College, was deliberately diminished under Winstedt's directorship and opened only in 1928 as a teacher-training institution that "failed utterly to satisfy the aspirations for higher education held by so many of the community" (p. 2).

The Japanese occupation, Tregonning argues, was not an entirely negative period in Malayan educational history. By insisting on a common language and emphasising technical education, the Japanese "stirred the stagnant waters" and stimulated national thought considerably, making any resumption of colonial inertia suspect (p. 3). The post-war Carr-Saunders Report of 1947 recommended merging KE7 and Raffles College into a full university, and the University of Malaya was established on 8 October 1949 with three faculties and 645 students (p. 4). Tregonning, who joined in 1953, describes the institution as unique in possessing a fully residential, multi-racial, multi-ethnic student body drawn entirely from Malaya and Singapore, and he documents the remarkable quality of the early student cohort, many of whom went on to become prominent national leaders and scholars (pp. 5–10).

The critical failure, Tregonning contends, was the University Executive's inability to appreciate the pace of political change. After the 1955 elections and the Razak Report, a rushed migration of first-year Arts students to Kuala Lumpur preceded any clear planning. The Aitken Commission of 1957 recommended a two-division university under central control, a structure Tregonning considers short-sighted and unfortunate, effectively making the Malayan division "a little brother of Singapore" (p. 12). The separation in 1962—Kuala Lumpur becoming the University of Malaya and Singapore the University of Singapore—was the logical correction to this flawed arrangement and marked "a watershed in the history of tertiary education in Malaya" (p. 13).

### Key Findings

- The Straits Settlements Medical School opened in 1905 with 23 students in former asylum buildings; by 1947 it had 265 students and 11 chairs, and had been recognised by the Medical Council of Great Britain since 1916 (p. 1).
- The Methodist Mission's 1914 proposal for an Anglo-Chinese college with courses in engineering, accounting, and tropical agriculture was rejected because it was "completely alien to the British concept of a University" and threatened British prestige (p. 2).
- The University of Malaya began in 1949 with 645 students (395 Medicine, 168 Arts, 82 Science); by 1953/54 total enrolment had grown to 954, with a gender ratio of four men to every woman (pp. 4–5).
- In 1954/55 the undergraduate population comprised 61% Chinese, 13% Indian, 11% Ceylonese, 10% Malay, and 3% Eurasian, all of Malayan or Singaporean origin (p. 6).
- The Aitken Commission's 1957 recommendation of a two-division university under central administration was accepted and enacted by Act in 1958, a structure Tregonning identifies as the principal institutional failure of the period (pp. 11–12).
- The separation into the University of Malaya (Kuala Lumpur) and the University of Singapore took effect in 1962, which Tregonning calls "a watershed in the history of tertiary education in Malaya" (p. 13).

### Conclusion

Tregonning's definitive takeaway is that the University of Malaya, despite the dedication of its younger staff, played a vital nation-building role that was never fully recognised or supported by its own leadership or by the governments concerned. The absence of a clear, communicated sense of purpose—of what the university was for in the context of emerging independent nations—meant that the institution drifted rather than drove, and that the political implications of its structure were consistently misjudged by those in authority (pp. 13–14).

## Context

- Tregonning's account draws on his own fifteen years at the university and on H.E. Wilson's *Social Engineering in Singapore: Educational Policies and Social Change 1819–1972* (Singapore University Press, 1978) for primary-source quotations (p. 2).
- Several of the early student theses Tregonning cites were subsequently published in JMBRAS or the *Journal of South-East Asian History*, which he founded in 1960, linking this article directly to the Society's own archival and publishing record (pp. 8–10).
