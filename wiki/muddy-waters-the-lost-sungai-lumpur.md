# Muddy Waters: The Lost Sungai Lumpur

## Abstract

The name of Malaysia’s capital, ‘Kuala Lumpur,’ is commonly translated as ‘muddy confluence,’ here referring to the junction of the Klang and Gombak Rivers. However, alternative explanations of this name’s origins have long been proposed. Based on a study of Malay toponymic traditions, close-readings of historical records and maps, and a topographical analysis of local rivers and streams, there is evidence that the city’s name derives from a lost ‘Sungai Lumpur’ (Mud River). A stream by that name is mentioned in several historical records, including an 1824 account by John Anderson, an 1872 sketch map, and recollections of the Selangor Civil War (1867–74). However, neither colonial nor modern maps refer to any local stream as ‘Sungai Lumpur.’ Our analysis suggests that this is because this stream was renamed by the British as the ‘Treacher Valley Stream’ which runs through the heart of the city, although it is now channelised and partly covered over. Nevertheless, we propose that resurrecting the Sungai Lumpur allows us to reframe Kuala Lumpur’s history by highlighting its marginalised indigenous and ecological roots, while offering opportunities to reconnect the modern city with this lost riparian heritage.

## Summary

This article investigates the etymology of "Kuala Lumpur" by arguing that the city's name derives not from the confluence of the Klang and Gombak Rivers, as is commonly assumed, but from a now-lost tributary stream called Sungai Lumpur. The authors draw on four nineteenth-century references to a "Sungai Lumpur" or "Sungei Lumpoor"—including John Anderson's 1824 survey account, an 1872 sketch map of Selangor, a Malay informant's recollections of the Selangor Civil War recorded by J.C. Pasqual, and E. Macfadyen's 1916 note—and combine these with a systematic topographical analysis of five candidate streams joining Sungai Klang near the original Pangkalan Lumpur landing stage.

The core argument is that the Treacher Valley Stream, which runs through the heart of modern Kuala Lumpur and is now largely channelised and covered, is the physical remnant of the Sungai Lumpur. The authors demonstrate this by scoring each candidate stream against eight criteria (naming status, size, swamp presence, bank position, settlement history, relative location, distance from town, and proximity to Tangga China), of which the Treacher Valley Stream matches most strongly. They further reconstruct the metonymic transfer by which the stream's name passed to the mukim (Mukim Lumpur, later renamed Mukim Kuala Lumpur when the upper reaches were absorbed into Mukim Ampang), then to the landing stage (Pangkalan Lumpur), and finally to the growing town itself.

The article situates this toponymic recovery within a broader critique of how colonial cartography and developmental urban planning erased indigenous and ecological place names. The authors argue that the disappearance of "Sungai Lumpur" from the map was facilitated by the British renaming of the stream, the resettlement of the Temuan community from Bukit Nanas in 1897, and the channelisation of the watercourse into a monsoon drain.

### Key Findings

- John Anderson's 1824 account lists "Sungei Lumpoor" as the 23rd and final tributary village on Sungai Klang, noting that "most" tin was obtained there and that Pahang was "one day's journey" from the settlement (p. 61).
- The 1872 sketch map labels a watercourse "R. Lumpor" joining "R. Hulu Klang" at "Port Q. Lumpor," passing "Pancallan Battu" to "Port Qualla Klang"—a configuration that has been misread as referring to Sungai Gombak (p. 63).
- The Treacher Valley Stream is 5 km long with a 600 ha catchment, its kuala located approximately 1.1 miles upstream from Pangkalan Lumpur on the Klang's east bank, matching the distance and positional criteria given in Pasqual's informant account (pp. 72–73).
- By 1897, the Temuan population at Bukit Nanas had been reduced to about 30 people and was resettled to Bukit Lanjan; the stream was subsequently labelled "Treacher Valley Stream" on the 1929 town map after Resident W.H. Treacher (1892–96) (pp. 76, 81).
- The 1911 *Malay Mail* letter by "C.W.H." is the first recorded report that the Sungai Lumpur had become unidentifiable on local maps, urging readers to locate it "before all trace of its existence is lost" (p. 81).
- A 10-metre storage tunnel uncovered by Kuala Lumpur City Hall in 2014 beneath Bukit Nanas is interpreted as part of an underground labyrinth dug during the Selangor Civil War, its mouth only about 150 metres from the Sungai Lumpur's kuala (p. 79).

### Conclusion

The authors contend that resurrecting the name Sungai Lumpur reframes Kuala Lumpur's founding narrative away from the conventional account of tin miners arriving in 1856 and toward recognition of the area's pre-existing Temuan habitation, indigenous toponymic traditions, and ecological character. They propose practical steps—including signposting the stream's subterranean course, creating a waterfront heritage path to its kuala, and identifying the Temuan burial ground near Bukit Nanas—as means of reconnecting the modern city with its lost riparian heritage and remedying the historical invisibility of its indigenous inhabitants.

## Context

- The study draws on archival maps held at the National Archives of Singapore (1883, 1889, 1923, 1929, 1945, 1957, 1964 sheets), Arkib Negara Malaysia (1884 mining plan), and the Nationaal Archief in The Hague (1681 Claarbout report), supplemented by Temuan oral history interviews conducted in 2024–25.
- Historiographically, the article intervenes in the long-running debate over Kuala Lumpur's name (citing Sheppard 1972, Gullick 1955/2000, Durai Raja Singam 1939/1962, and Abdur-Razzaq Lubis 2023) by providing the first systematic topographical elimination of candidate streams and a detailed reconstruction of the metonymic transfer chain from stream to mukim to town.
