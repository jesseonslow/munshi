# Foreigners in the Achehnese court, 1760–1819

Lee Kam Hing's 1970 article examines the role of non-Acehnese foreigners—Chuliah traders, European adventurers, and private merchants—in the Achehnese court between 1760 and 1819, arguing that their presence was both a symptom of the collapse of traditional Acehnese governance and a persistent diplomatic irritant for British authorities in Bengal and Penang who feared uncontrolled foreign influence in a strategically located state. The study demonstrates that the seemingly peripheral Articles 6 and 7 of the Sriduli Treaty of 1819, which excluded other European powers and Americans from fixed residence in Aceh, were in fact the culmination of decades of anxiety over precisely this problem.

## Summary

The article traces a pattern in which successive Acehnese Sultans, weakened by the eclipse of the *orang-besar* (officers of state) and the rise of autonomous *ulubalangs* (territorial chiefs) and *Sagis* (leaders of the three main political divisions), turned to non-Acehnese outsiders for military protection and commercial expertise. From the Madras Firm's sepoys garrisoning the palace in the 1760s, through the Chuliah *shahbander* Poh Salleh's domination of the court in the 1780s, to the European adventurers who surrounded Sultan Johar Allum Shah in the 1810s, each generation of foreigners exploited the Sultan's vulnerability for personal gain while simultaneously deepening the political fragmentation that made their services necessary. The *ulubalangs*, already resentful of the Sultan's diminished authority, found in the Sultan's reliance on foreigners—particularly Europeans whose presence was viewed as un-Islamic—a rallying cry for open rebellion, culminating in the deposition of Johar Allum on 15 October 1814.

British authorities in Bengal and Penang occupied an ambivalent position throughout this period. They were alarmed by the disorder in Aceh because it disrupted the pepper and beetlenut trade and threatened the security of the Bay of Bengal, yet they consistently refused to commit military resources to internal Acehnese affairs. Missions by Giles Holloway (1771), Charles Desvouex (1772), Henry Botham (1784), David Campbell (1810), and I. Lawrence (1811) all failed to secure a British factory or resident, partly because the Sultan preferred the more pliable private traders and partly because the Chuliah community actively obstructed British overtures. The article argues that the Sultan's recruitment of European advisers was not merely a matter of desperation but a conscious strategy to create a diplomatic *rapport* with the British, who had become the dominant power in the region after the founding of Penang in 1786. When the Sultan's requests for a commercial treaty or subsidiary force went unanswered, he had no recourse but to retain his European advisers as informal intermediaries.

The Sriduli Treaty of 1819, negotiated by Raffles and Coombs, resolved the immediate succession dispute between Johar Allum and the Penang claimant Syful Allum Shah, but its deeper purpose—revealed by the instructions issued to the negotiating mission—was to institutionalise British control over the foreign element in Aceh. Article 6 excluded all other European powers and Americans from fixed residence; Article 7 empowered the British resident to object to any British subject's continued stay. Together these clauses were designed to replace the uncontrolled network of private adventurers with a single accountable British agent. The treaty was rendered moot within five years by the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824, which relinquished all British interests in Sumatra.

### Key Findings

- The Madras Firm stationed approximately 150 sepoys to garrison the Sultan's fort in Aceh beginning in 1766, an arrangement that London condemned as an unauthorised military alliance with a native state (pp. 6–7).
- The Chuliah *shahbander* Poh Salleh, who dominated the court after 1781, maintained a personal guard of 100 sepoys and effectively ran the state while Sultan Muhammed Shah "occupied himself with the pleasures of the palace" (p. 10).
- Captain Huatt, a Fleming, commanded an Acehnese vessel with a crew of 150 and was assisted by British private trader William Lesle and twenty to thirty other Europeans in a small naval force created to enforce revenue collection on the Pedier coast (p. 11).
- By 1814, Cuthbert Fenwick controlled the Sultan's royal seal, the household budget, and the dispatch of official correspondence to Penang, to the extent that the Sultan was often ignorant of the contents of letters sent in his own name (pp. 18–19).
- Henry Botham's 1784 report recommended that 300 British troops would be sufficient to topple Poh Salleh if assisted by the *ulubalangs*, a proposal that was rejected by the Supreme Government (p. 11).
- The Sriduli Treaty of 22 April 1819 contained nine articles; Articles 6 and 7 specifically excluded subjects of all other European powers and Americans from fixed residence in Aceh and empowered the British resident to object to any British subject's continued stay (pp. 3, 23–24).

### Conclusion

Lee Kam Hing concludes that the foreign element in the Achehnese court was not an aberration but a structural feature of a state in which the traditional administrative hierarchy had collapsed and the Sultan's authority had become dependent on external military and commercial support. The British response—oscillating between strategic interest and administrative reluctance—ultimately produced the Sriduli Treaty, whose exclusionary clauses represented an attempt to channel and control the very phenomenon that had made Aceh a source of diplomatic difficulty for nearly sixty years.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the *Sumatra Factory Records* and *Straits Settlement Factory Records* (India Office Library), supplemented by published sources including J. Anderson's *Acheen* (1840) and W. Marsden's *A History of Sumatra* (1783).
- Historiographically, the study redirects attention from the standard diplomatic narrative of the Sriduli Treaty to the micro-politics of individual foreigners in the Achehnese court, arguing that Articles 6 and 7—previously dismissed as peripheral—were in fact central to British strategic calculations in the region.
