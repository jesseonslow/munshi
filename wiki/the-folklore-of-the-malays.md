# The folklore of the Malays

W. E. Maxwell published "The Folklore of the Malays" in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1881, drawing on his observations among the Perak Malay peasantry during the early period of British administrative consolidation in the Straits Settlements. His central thesis is that the external uniformity of Malay Muhammadanism conceals deep strata of earlier indigenous and Brahmanic belief, and that the folklore of the peasantry—its legends, proverbs, and ritual observances—preserves these older religious layers in a form that no amount of Islamic conversion has fully erased.

## Summary

Maxwell's article is structured as a thematic survey of popular beliefs rather than a systematic ethnography, moving through birds, houses, auspicious timing, celestial phenomena, animals, and miscellaneous customs. The unifying argument is one of religious layering: just as the Buddhists of Ceylon still propitiate demons and the Burmese retain the Nat cult beneath their Theravada practice, so the Malay peasant who prays the *salat* may simultaneously invoke Hindu gods against evil spirits or beat a wooden platter to ward off the Spectre Huntsman. Maxwell treats this not as mere "pagan survival" but as a living, vital force that "colour[s] or corrupt[s]" the alien faith grafted over it, following the principle he attributes to Logan on the persistence of indigenous religious thought.

The most developed section concerns the *hantu pemburu* (Spectre Huntsman), whose legend Maxwell records in full from Perak informants. The narrative—a man who hunted a phantom mouse-deer until his head became fixed to his back and a tree grew from his throat—Maxwell explicitly parallels with the European Wild Huntsman of the Hartz, arguing for a common Aryan origin transmitted through Sumatra. This comparative framing is the article's most ambitious historiographical move: it positions Malay folklore not as isolated curiosity but as part of a broader Indo-Malay cultural substrate, with the Batak *birik-birik* bird and the Sanskrit-derived vocabulary serving as corroborating evidence of the same transmission route.

The remaining sections—house superstitions, *langkah* (auspicious timing), the rainbow, sunset, animal beliefs, and miscellaneous omens—function as illustrative specimens rather than sustained argument. Maxwell's method is essentially that of the colonial resident: he records what he has witnessed or been told, offers a brief comparative note (the bear-and-tiger story as a local Panchatantra variant, the crocodile myth as a Muhammadan appropriation of an older sacred-reptile belief), and moves on. The article closes with an explicit methodological plea: that English observers should treat Malay Muhammadanism as "an accident" and study the people as a continuous Asiatic race from Sumatra to Borneo, rather than as a distinct "Muhammadan" category.

### Key Findings

- The Spectre Huntsman legend is fixed to Katapang in Sumatra; the hunter's four named dogs are Si Langsat, Si Kumbang, Si Nibong, Si Pintas, and Si Aru-Aru, and his weapons include the blow-pipe *Timiang Balu*, the spear *Lankapuri*, and the blade *Singha-buana* (pp. 14–17).
- The bird *birik-birik* (a nocturnal flier associated with the Spectre Huntsman) is called by the identical name by the Bataks of Sumatra, and in Batak creation legends the origin of mankind is ascribed to *Putri-Orta-Bulan*, daughter of *Batara-Guru*, who descended with a white owl and a dog (p. 18).
- The seven planets (*bintang tujoh*) used in *langkah* calculations are enumerated as *Shems* (sun), *Kamr* (moon), *Marik* (Mars), *Utarid* (Mercury), *Zahrat* (Venus), *Mustari* (Jupiter), and *Zahal* (Saturn), with tables assigning each to specific hours of the week (p. 21).
- The Sanskrit phrase *senja kala* (*sandhya kala*) is employed in Malay to describe evening twilight, and the word *bujang* is identified with Sanskrit *bhujangga* ("dragon"), the latter confirmed by the snake-spirit *Si Bujang* of Gunong Pondok, Perak (pp. 21, 28).
- The crocodile creation myth involves *Putri Padang Gerinsing* moulding the creature from clay on a betel-nut palm sheath, with sugar-cane joints as backbone and rind peelings as ribs; it was cursed by *Siti Fatima*, daughter of the Prophet, who drove nails into its jaws to serve as teeth (pp. 24–25).
- The universal Malay preference for buffalo over ox meat is identified as a residual Hindu taboo against cow-flesh, a prejudice that persists among a population governed by Islamic dietary law (p. 22).

### Conclusion

Maxwell's definitive takeaway is that the "actual religion" of the Malays is a palimpsest: indigenous animism, Brahmanism, and Muhammadanism are superimposed rather than successive replacements, and the deepest layer remains the most psychologically potent. He urges the colonial reader to abandon the reductive category of "the Muhammadan Malay" and to recognise the Asiatic Malay as a continuous people whose folklore, from Sumatra to Borneo, preserves a shared pre-Islamic substrate that is the true key to their character.

## Context

- Maxwell was a British colonial official resident in Perak; the article reflects the administrative-ethnographic impulse of the Straits Settlements civil service to document "native" beliefs as a complement to governance, and its comparative framework (Aryan origins, Panchatantra parallels) is shaped by the racial-linguistic theories current in 1880s British scholarship.
- The primary evidence is Maxwell's own field observation and the testimony of Perak Malay informants, including *Pawangs* (medicine-men); no archival or manuscript sources are cited beyond a passing reference to Logan's *Dawn of History* and his own earlier publications on Malay proverbs in the same journal.
