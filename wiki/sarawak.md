# Sarawak

### Scope and Historical Definition

Sarawak is a territory on the northwestern coast of Borneo, encompassing the catchments of the Sarawak, Samarahan, and Lundu rivers, and extending along the coast to the island's northwesternmost tip, Tanjung Datu [[1, pp. 1–2]](#ref-1). Its political history spans from the appointment of Sultan Tengah Manga as the first Sultan of Sarawak in 1599 by his elder brother, Sultan Abdul Jalilul Akbar of Brunei, through the two-century period of local and contested rule following his assassination in 1641, to the establishment of the White Rajah dynasty under James Brooke in 1841, and finally to the cession of the territory to Britain in 1946 [[1, pp. 2]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2), [[2, p. 119]](#ref-2). The territory's position at the junction of the spheres of influence of Brunei and the neighbouring sultanate of Sambas in what is now Indonesian Kalimantan gave it a distinctive and often contested political character, with control varying over the centuries and between different parts of the territory [[1, pp. 14–15]](#ref-1).

### Pre-Brooke History and the Sambas Connection

The conventional narrative of continuous Brunei suzerainty over Sarawak from 1641 to 1842 has been challenged by Ib Larsen's 2012 study, which integrates previously underutilised Sambas-side primary sources—including the *Silsilah Krajaan Sambas*, a 1903 typewritten copy of a record made by Sultan Umar Aqam Uddin I (r. 1708–32), family-held manuscripts, and materials from the Indonesian National Archives and Leiden—to demonstrate that the neighbouring sultanate of Sambas exercised considerably stronger influence on the territory than has been generally credited [[1, pp. 1]](#ref-1), [[3]](#ref-3). Sultan Tengah Manga's son, Radin Suleiman, became the first Muslim ruler of Sambas, and the question of whether he and his successors continued to exercise authority over Sarawak from their new base remains unresolved, though the evidence points strongly in that direction [[1, pp. 2–3]](#ref-1).

Several pieces of evidence support this thesis. Francis Valentyn's map of Borneo, printed in Amsterdam around 1725, clearly places the western part of Sarawak under "T Koninkryk van Sambas" (Sambas Kingdom), and a mid-nineteenth-century map by J. Rapkin (1851) still shows Kuching within the Sambas sultanate [[1, pp. 3–4]](#ref-1). In 1811, the British ship *Commence* was burned by "Pangeran Samewda of Sarawak, a relative of the sultan of Sambas," and the British navy responded by bombarding Sambas in 1812, implying that the British were in no doubt about the alliance between the two territories [[1, pp. 6–7]](#ref-1). In 1831, the Dutch resident in Sambas formally proposed to the East Indian government that Sambas should acquire Lundu, Sematan, Sarawak, and Sadoud from Brunei, with the stated purpose of stopping rampant salt smuggling [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). During the 1836 uprising against the Pengiran Mahkota, the rebel *datu* sent a message to the Dutch Assistant Resident in Sambas stating that they wished to come under Dutch rule, and the Sultan of Sambas supplied the *datu* with arms and ammunition [[1, pp. 8–9]](#ref-1).

The discovery of antimony ore at Siniawan up the Sarawak River in the early nineteenth century provided a potential new source of income for the declining Brunei sultanate and prompted the Sultan to send the Pengiran Mahkota to Sarawak in 1826 to re-establish Brunei control and exploit the deposits [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). The Malay *datu* did not accept the Pengiran Mahkota's supremacy, and the subsequent 1836 uprising was, in Walker's assessment, "a struggle between the Malay elites from Sambas, Brunei and Sarawak for control of the antimony" [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1). Only when James Brooke interfered in the conflict on the Brunei side from 1841 and finally quelled the uprising did relations between Sarawak and Brunei become clearly settled [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1).

### Governance under the White Rajahs

The administrative system of Sarawak during the reign of the second White Rajah, Charles Brooke (r. 1868–1888), was not a simple top-down colonial imposition but a complex, compromise-driven system that depended fundamentally on the maintenance of native authority structures and the personal qualities of individual European officers [[4, p. 95]](#ref-4). Doering's analysis, drawing on Brooke's own published writings and Sarawak Gazette outstation reports from 1880 to 1883, demonstrates that the formal structure of four divisions, each with a Divisional Resident, was far less rigid in practice than official descriptions suggested [[4, p. 102]](#ref-4). The District Officer's role was all-encompassing—judicial, fiscal, military, and diplomatic—and his success depended less on formal authority than on personal skill, local knowledge, and the good will of the community [[4, p. 102]](#ref-4).

The Supreme Council, founded in 1855 at Earl Grey's suggestion, operated under a rule that European members should never outnumber native ones; by the turn of the century it was considered the sole legislative authority in Sarawak [[4, pp. 99–100]](#ref-4). The Council Negri, created by Charles Brooke in 1865, comprised thirty to fifty members and met roughly every three years [[4, p. 100]](#ref-4). In the 1880s, Doering estimates approximately twenty-five to thirty European officers in the entire Sarawak Civil Service, with a third based in Kuching; at stations such as Betong, Pusa, Tuban, Kabong, Sarebas, Balleh, and Kanowit, the entire staff was native [[4, p. 102]](#ref-4). The opium farm was the government's greatest single source of revenue at one point and remained very important in the 1880s, while the Captain China controlled gambling, pawn-broking, and some monopolies within strictly regulated Chinese settlements [[4, p. 105]](#ref-4).

The relationship between the Brooke raj and the British Colonial Office was characterised by a persistent structural anomaly. The 1888 protectorate treaties recognised Sarawak's independence while assigning Britain complete external responsibility, creating a framework that limited the Government's power to interfere in internal affairs [[5, p. 29]](#ref-5). Tarling's 1970 study, drawing extensively on Colonial Office papers at the Public Record Office, London, demonstrates that the raj was structurally "stronger than it seemed": the Brooke family's entrenchment in British public opinion and society, combined with the limited powers conferred by the treaty, made it politically impossible for the Colonial Office to increase its control over Sarawak by any means short of the Raja's own voluntary concession, which never came [[5, pp. 25–28]](#ref-5). The Rajah's abrupt annexation of the Limbang river from Brunei in 1890, which the British Government did not compel him to reverse, became a persistent source of friction and a symbol of the limits of British authority [[5, pp. 29]](#ref-5).

During the final years of Charles Brooke's life, the correspondence with his Sarawak Treasurer, F. H. Dallas, reveals a man simultaneously managing the detailed administration of a tropical state from Chesterton Lodge in Gloucestershire, pursuing territorial expansion in Borneo, and maintaining the persona of an English country squire [[6, p. 4]](#ref-6). His acquisition of the Lawas district from the British North Borneo Company in December 1904 was explicitly framed as "a stepping stone to get Brunei," and his conviction that Brunei was a "garden of weeds" that needed to be "swept away" drove his expansionist ambitions [[6, p. 16]](#ref-6). The domestic drama surrounding the succession was equally prominent: Charles had little confidence in his eldest son Vyner, and by 1912 the family had fractured to the point where Charles issued a proclamation recognising his second son Bertram as "part of the Government of the State" [[6, p. 22]](#ref-6).

### Economic and Ethnographic Dimensions

The economic life of Sarawak in the late nineteenth century was shaped by the sago trade, the opium farm, and the exploitation of forest and mineral resources. Denison's 1872 journal, recording a twenty-six-day voyage along the north-west Borneo coast, documents the commercial tensions arising from the encroachment of Chinese traders—many of them Sarawak-based—into the Baram river system, where they were paying Kayan headhunters $40 per pikul for gutta, undercutting the established Brunei Malay middlemen who had long monopolised the trade between the interior Kayans and the coast [[7, pp. 177–178]](#ref-7). The Oya Trading Company's sago mill at Oya used a 14-horsepower engine; sago tree trunks, thirty to forty feet long, were sold at thirty cents per three-foot cut, yielding roughly half a *pasu* of sago per cut [[7, p. 185]](#ref-7). Birds' nests from the Baram were estimated at fifty pikuls per annum, valued at $10,000, and Brunei traders' profits on Baram trade had fallen from 400 per cent to 100 per cent due to Chinese competition [[7, pp. 178–179]](#ref-7).

The ethnographic record of Sarawak's indigenous populations is substantial. Mulder and Hewitt's 1911 account documents two Milano spiritual ceremonies—the Payun, a healing rite centred on a rattan swing lasting eight days and timed to the full moon, and the Plato, a post-mortem communication with a departed spirit—arguing that both traditions share a deep ancestral root with Sea Dayak manangism [[8, pp. 171–181]](#ref-8). The Payun was presided over by one or more *Bayoh*, spiritual practitioners of either sex who claimed special power in the spirit world, and the essential and only sacred element was the rattan swing; if the rattan snapped during the ceremony, it was interpreted as the presence of an angry spirit and the patient invariably died within a short time [[8, pp. 174]](#ref-8). Denison's journal provides a parallel ethnographic survey of the Milano, Kayan, Dayak, Murut, and Dusun peoples, including accounts of Milano religious practice, the persistence of head-hunting, and the contrasting customs of the Muruts and Dusuns of northern Borneo [[7, pp. 181–184]](#ref-7).

The natural history of Sarawak attracted the attention of colonial scientific institutions. Moulton's 1913 field report documents a brief joint collecting expedition to Mt. Poi—the only granite mountain and highest peak in Sarawak Proper—undertaken by the Raffles Museum, Singapore, and the Sarawak Museum, which yielded several species of biogeographical significance, particularly Bornean mountain endemics previously known only from the far eastern peaks of Kina Balu and Penrissen [[9, pp. 1–12]](#ref-9). The expedition collected approximately eight mammal species, forty bird species, three snakes, one lizard, several frogs, myriapods, land molluscs, and a modest insect assemblage over six days on the mountain [[9, p. 4]](#ref-9). The discovery of a 24-inch stone image of Ganesa at Limbang in 1921, sent to the Sarawak Museum by Resident F. F. Boult, drew expert assessments from Prof. N. J. Krom of Leiden University, who suggested a sixth or seventh century date, and Sir John Marshall, Director-General of Archaeology in India, who proposed a later date of approximately the thirteenth century on the grounds that the image's posture matched Javanese examples from the age of Brambanan and Singasari [[10, p. 211]](#ref-10).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Sarawak reveals a shifting of analytical priorities across more than a century of publication. The earliest contributions, such as Denison's 1872 journal and Mulder and Hewitt's 1911 ethnography, reflect the colonial-era interest in trade, administration, and indigenous custom, written from positions of colonial authority and offering the perspective of the administrative insider [[7, p. 173]](#ref-7), [[8, p. 171]](#ref-8). Moulton's 1913 and 1922 notes, produced in his capacity as Curator of the Sarawak Museum, represent the formal inter-museum collaboration between Singapore and Sarawak that characterised early twentieth-century colonial natural history, relying on a network of European specialists for taxonomic identification [[9, p. 1]](#ref-9), [[10, p. 210]](#ref-10).

A significant historiographical shift occurred in the 1960s and 1970s. Doering's 1966 article moved the analytical focus from the romantic founding narrative of James Brooke to the administrative mechanics of Charles Brooke's reign, using outstation-level evidence to challenge the simplified three-tiered model of Brooke governance that had prevailed in earlier secondary literature [[4, p. 95]](#ref-4). Tarling's 1970 study made a further contribution by shifting the focus from the Brooke rulers themselves to the British administrative machinery that interacted with them, demonstrating that the constraints on imperial action were as much a product of London's political culture and public opinion as of the treaty framework or the Rajas' own agency [[5, p. 25]](#ref-5). The Harrissons' 1968 report on the Magala cave system at Niah introduced systematic archaeological methodology—C-14 dating, sherd reconstruction, and spatial scatter analysis—to the study of Sarawak's prehistory, establishing the site as a key reference point for understanding the Neolithic and Metal Age populations of the Subis mountain region [[11, pp. 148–151]](#ref-11).

More recent work has continued to challenge established narratives. Reece's 2008 study of Charles Brooke's correspondence with Dallas, drawn from a previously underutilised private collection at The National Archives, Kew, revealed the Rajah's remote governance practices, his expansionist ambitions, and the domestic tensions surrounding the succession [[6, pp. 4]](#ref-6). Larsen's 2012 article, drawing on Sambas royal and family archives and field visits to the sultanate, demonstrated that the "missing" two centuries of Sarawak governance are better understood through the lens of Sambas influence rather than as a simple continuation of Brunei administration [[1, pp. 1]](#ref-1), [[12]](#ref-12). Sheppard's 1991 commemorative account of the Patriots' Memorial in Kuching contributed to the underexplored history of Sarawak's anti-cession resistance and its relationship to the broader Malayan independence movement, documenting the 1946 Cession Bill, the assassination of Governor Sir Duncan Stewart in 1949, and Tunku Abdul Rahman's final public act in laying the foundation stone in 1990 [[2, pp. 119–126]](#ref-2). Together, these studies illustrate a trajectory from descriptive colonial reporting toward critical, source-based historiography that interrogates the assumptions of earlier scholarship.
