# Between the Malay peasants and a beautiful theory: romanticism and the imperialist agenda in Hugh Clifford’s early fiction

Mohamed Rashidi Pakri's 2004 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the tension between romantic sympathy and imperialist ideology in the early fiction of Sir Hugh Clifford (1866–1941), the British Resident of Pahang and prolific writer of Malayan subjects. Pakri argues that Clifford's short story collections and novel simultaneously express genuine affection for Malay peasant life while advancing the colonial justification for British intervention in the Malay States.

## Summary

Pakri traces a discernible evolution across Clifford's early works—from *In Court and Kampong* (1897) through *Studies in Brown Humanity* (1898), *In a Corner of Asia* (1899), to the novel *A Prince of Malaya* (1926)—in which the author's stance shifts from what Clifford himself called "archaeological studies" drawn from life toward an explicit advocacy of British civilizing mission. The central dilemma Pakri identifies is structural: Clifford loved the Malay peasants and their culture while simultaneously serving as an agent of the empire that was dismantling that very culture. His prefatory remarks across the collections reveal this contradiction in its rawest form—he describes his "brown friends" as "very real and very lovable" while declaring himself "a firm believer in the necessity for the intervention of Great Britain in Malaya" (pp. 107–108).

Pakri demonstrates how the imperialist agenda operates beneath the surface of seemingly sentimental narratives. In "A Tale of a Theft," the depiction of a slave-debtor's imprisonment under the Malay aristocracy functions as an indictment of native governance that implicitly demands British correction. In "Umat," the loyalty of a blind fisherman to his white employer encodes a message of Malay dependency on the colonial ruler. In "A Daughter of the Muhammadians," the villagers' inability to comprehend a leper's wife's devotion is framed as evidence of the "unregenerate" state of the Malay mind, requiring the superior judgment of the European Resident (pp. 110–113).

The longest section of the article is devoted to *A Prince of Malaya*, in which Pakri reads the character of Raja Saleh as a literary argument against Malay self-government. Saleh's denationalization in England, his failure to reintegrate into Malay society, and his ultimate death while seeking guidance from the British Resident Jack Norris collectively construct a narrative in which the Malay prince is "unfitted by training to be a Malay raja, unsuited by nature to be an Englishman" (p. 120). Pakri notes that Clifford's own friend Joseph Conrad criticized the novel for its "immaturity or rather superficialness in character," yet the work's political logic—that the Malays require perpetual British tutelage—remained intact and underpinned half a century of colonial rule (pp. 120–121).

### Key Findings

- Clifford's prefatory statement in *In Court and Kampong* (1897) frames British intervention as compressing "six long centuries" of European development into "twenty years of revolutions in facts and ideas" for the Malay race (p. 109).
- The short story "A Tale of a Theft" depicts a prison in which a slave-debtor named Talib is held without food or ventilation; Clifford's narrator explicitly contrasts this with Singapore's "churches, and its ballrooms, its societies for the prevention of cruelty" to demand immediate British action (pp. 110–111).
- In *A Prince of Malaya*, the protagonist Raja Saleh spends five years in England before returning to Malaya, where he is posted as a tax collector in Kuala Buyong—a task his followers denounce as "coolie work" and "ta' patut" (not befitting) for a royal scion (pp. 117–118).
- Saleh's death occurs when he kills a Bengali guard at the camp of British Resident Jack Norris and is shot by other guards; Clifford's narrator describes him as "unfitted by training to be a Malay raja, unsuited by nature to be an Englishman" (p. 120).
- Clifford returned to Malaya in 1927 at age sixty-one to serve as Governor of the Straits Settlements, but departed in 1929, citing his wife's health; Pakri suggests the real cause was his recognition that the Malays he remembered no longer existed (pp. 122–123).
- Joseph Conrad's 1898 criticism of Clifford's work identified "a certain immaturity or rather superficialness in character" and unrealistic conversation, a charge Pakri concedes applies particularly to *A Prince of Malaya* (p. 120).

### Conclusion

Pakri's definitive takeaway is that Clifford was a "complete imperialist romantic" who could not escape the ideological framework of his civil service position even while genuinely loving his subjects. His fiction romanticizes the Malay peasant while simultaneously constructing the evidentiary case for colonial rule: the peasants are noble but helpless, the aristocracy is corrupt, and only British intervention can resolve the contradiction. The article closes with the irony that when Clifford finally returned to Malaya in 1927, the people he had idealized in print had changed beyond recognition, and the "spell" of the East that had sustained his writing for decades had broken.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on Clifford's own published texts (prefaces, short stories, and the novel) as primary sources, supplemented by secondary scholarship from Gailey, Savage, Wicks, Fernando, and Gullick.
- Pakri's contribution is historiographical: he situates Clifford's fiction within the broader debate over the literary representation of colonial subjects in Southeast Asia, connecting Clifford's work to the wider tradition of "Asia in Western fiction" identified by Winks (1990) and to the specific Malayan context of the "noble savage" trope used to justify the protectorate system.
