# Sultan Mansur Shah of Terengganu

Sultan Mansur Syah of Terengganu (r. 1741–1793) was the ruler of the Malay state of Terengganu whose fifty-year tenure made him the dominant figure in eighteenth-century Malay politics. As a close relative of the Johor court — son-in-law of Sultan Sulaiman and cousin to the Johor royal house — he served as the acknowledged leader of the anti-Bugis faction in the Riau-Johor polity and was a central actor in the prolonged struggle over the succession to the Johor throne. Contemporary European observers described his government in admiring terms, noting that "his government is very well controlled for Malays" and that "he is feared and loved by his people." [[1, pp. 78]](#ref-1)

## Role in the Johor Succession and Riau Politics

Sultan Mansur was conferred the title *Yang di Pertuan Kecil* (a junior royal designation functioning as heir apparent) in 1736 or possibly earlier, a title apparently instituted because the Bugis had assumed the traditional *Yang di Pertuan Muda* (crown prince) designation for themselves. [[1, pp. 99–100]](#ref-1) By 1746 the Dutch were referring to him as heir, and from that time until 1759 he took a leading part in the government of Johor-Riau, becoming increasingly influential as Sultan Sulaiman grew weaker. [[1, p. 99]](#ref-1) On a number of occasions he represented his father-in-law in negotiations with the Dutch and with the Bugis; in 1757 Sultan Sulaiman asked to be excused from further conferences and appointed Sultan Mansur to act in his name, promising to honour any commitment the Terengganu ruler might make. [[1, p. 100]](#ref-1)

The return of the Bugis and their resumption of power in Riau in 1759 confirmed Sultan Mansur's exclusion from the Johor succession. After the deaths of Sultan Sulaiman and Raja Abdul Jalil in 1760, Daeng Kemboja engineered the installation of a child ruler and had himself appointed regent with complete authority over the Johor government. [[1, p. 100]](#ref-1) Sultan Mansur returned to Terengganu that year, but in his correspondence with the Dutch he always made plain that he regarded the Bugis as interlopers and never ceased to distinguish them from "the Malay race." [[1, p. 100]](#ref-1) In 1777 he asked the Dutch for help to regain his position in Riau, basing his appeal on the fact that his wife was Sultan Sulaiman's daughter. [[1, p. 100]](#ref-1)

## Diplomatic Engagements and the Anti-Bugis Campaign

Sultan Mansur's foreign policy was conducted primarily through the Dutch East India Company (VOC) and English country traders. The Dutch, though unable to secure a monopoly of Terengganu's pepper, maintained amicable relations with a ruler they regarded as one of the most influential in the area and a potential ally against the Bugis. [[1, p. 82]](#ref-1) Governor Abraham Couperus (1788–1795) considered him an "old and wise king" and refused to believe he could be involved in a rumoured attack on Melaka. [[1, p. 82]](#ref-1)

His relationship with the English was equally significant. English country traders had been frequent visitors to Terengganu since the early eighteenth century, and some exerted considerable influence on Sultan Mansur, who singled out several as his particular favourites. [[1, p. 83]](#ref-1) In 1788 he sought the advice of Captain James Glass when problems developed in his relations with Siam, and it was largely due to Glass's persuasion that the Terengganu ruler decided to send missions to Bengal and Penang in another attempt to negotiate a treaty with the English Company. [[1, p. 83]](#ref-1) From 1786 until his death in 1794, Francis Light, Superintendent of Penang, corresponded extensively with Sultan Mansur, and their exchanges make up the bulk of the existing English material dealing with Terengganu. [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1)

Sultan Mansur's military efforts against the Bugis included two combined attacks on Linggi, the Bugis stronghold. In 1756, acting as Sultan Sulaiman's delegated representative, he met Governor Dekker of Melaka and, according to the Dutch records, assumed the role of mediator, persuading the Raja of Selangor to come to a conference at Tanjung Keling to discuss the possibilities of peace. [[1, pp. 94–95]](#ref-1) The Dutch, rather than Sultan Mansur, pressed for an immediate attack on Linggi, and it was Governor Dekker who made the decision to seize the initiative. [[1, p. 95]](#ref-1) In 1765 Sultan Mansur married his daughter to the displaced Siak prince Raja Ismail, regarded by many as a legitimate claimant to the Johor throne, and two years later made overtures to Sultan Muhammad Ali of Siak in the hope of gaining additional support against the Bugis. [[1, p. 101]](#ref-1) He initiated an attack on a Bugis fleet in the Singapore Straits in 1767 and, despite the failure of this expedition, continued his struggle against the Bugis for another twenty years. [[1, p. 101]](#ref-1)

## Legacy and Documentation

The principal challenge in reconstructing Sultan Mansur's life lies in the absence of any known Malay source originating from Terengganu itself. [[1, p. 78]](#ref-1) The court almost certainly maintained some kind of official record, and Raja Ali Haji refers to a *Sejarah Pihak Terengganu*, but no such text has to date been identified. [[1, pp. 78–79]](#ref-1) The *Tuhfat al-Nafis*, long treated as the principal source for this period, systematically distorts Sultan Mansur's character and actions through selective adaptation of its underlying texts, presenting him as a treacherous and cowardly antagonist to the Bugis in order to legitimise the Bugis-descended community's political position in Riau. [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1) Barbara Watson Andaya's source-critical study demonstrates that the *Tuhfat*'s four principal source texts — the *Peringatan Negeri Johor*, the *Siarah Selangor*, the *Karangan Engku Busu*, and the *Siarah Siak* — none of which purports to represent the Terengganu point of view, were selectively quoted and altered to conform with a pro-Bugis narrative. [[1, pp. 87–92]](#ref-1) Raja Ali Haji's expanded version goes further still, inserting invented dialogue and implications of guilt absent from the source texts, ensuring that readers would be left in no doubt as to the person responsible for the troubles which beset Riau in the latter half of the eighteenth century. [[1, pp. 96–98]](#ref-1) The Dutch VOC records, by contrast, portray Sultan Mansur as Sultan Sulaiman's loyal representative and a capable diplomat, and no Dutch document mentions any of the alleged plots attributed to him in the *Tuhfat*. [[1, pp. 94–96]](#ref-1)
