# Marriage and divorce in Johore among Malay-Muslims during the Japanese occupation, 1942–1945

Abu Talib Ahmad's 1998 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines marriage and divorce patterns among Malay-Muslims in Johore during the Japanese occupation (1942–1945), drawing on previously neglected records of the state's Ecclesiastical and Education Department and the Office of the Chief Qadhi. Ahmad argues that the sharp rise in divorce rates was a manifestation of the loosening of traditional and religious bonds—particularly among women—driven by a deepening socio-economic crisis and social transformation, and that this process of emancipation was already well underway from the economically turbulent 1930s rather than being a product of Japanese-era education as commonly supposed.

## Summary

The article addresses a conspicuous gap in the occupation literature: the near-total absence of attention to the social and family life of Malay-Muslims under Japanese rule. Ahmad's central intervention is to demonstrate that the high incidence of divorce, its causes, and the circumstances of women divorcees reveal a significant loosening of traditional constraints on Malay-Muslim women that predated and outlasted the occupation itself. He challenges the prevailing scholarly narrative that attributes Malay women's post-war emancipation to the egalitarian nature of wartime Japanese education, arguing instead that the process was already advanced by the early 1930s and was accelerated by the occupation's economic devastation and the new social freedoms it inadvertently created.

The evidential basis is the monthly returns compiled by district Qadhis under the Malayan Military Administration's new bureaucratisation of the Religious Department—a system imposed from December 1942 that required detailed reporting on marriages, divorces, reconciliations, court cases, and mosque attendance. These records, though incomplete due to wartime destruction, non-compliance by understaffed district offices, and the changing priorities of the Japanese administration as the war turned, provide the only systematic quantitative data on family life among Malay-Muslims in Johore for the period. Ahmad supplements this with population census data (1931 and mid-1942), inflation figures, and qualitative evidence from court files, Qadhi correspondence, and lecture reports to construct a narrative of how economic collapse, male absence, and new social patterns converged to destabilise the family institution.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to the response—or rather the failure of response—by the religious elite. Ahmad documents how the Qadhi's courts processed divorce petitions (predominantly filed by women alleging abandonment and failure to provide *nafkah*), how the Religious Department's lecture circuits were co-opted into Japanese propaganda, and how even the most forward-thinking officials, such as the Muar Qadhi Haji Ismail Haji Yusof, could only offer belated and largely unimplemented proposals by 1945. The article thus situates the family crisis within the broader context of the Japanese appropriation of Islamic institutions for political purposes, which simultaneously undermined the religious elite's capacity to address social ills.

### Key Findings

- The state-wide divorce percentage in Johore rose from 37.5% in 1932 and 34.4% in 1936 to 48.3% (Batu Pahat), 56.9% (Kluang), and 76.2% (Mersing) in 1944, and reached 52.7% (Batu Pahat) and 47.6% (Muar) by mid-1945, before falling to 29.9% (1951) and 21.3% (1952) in the post-war period (pp. 72–75).
- Johore's rice allocation was cut from 6,000 tons in November 1942 to 2,700 tons by June 1943 and further to 1,320 tons by early 1945, while Singapore food price indices (base November 1942 = 100) climbed to 2,421 by May 1944, illustrating the severity of the economic crisis driving marital breakdown (pp. 82–83).
- The minimum age of marriage for Malay-Muslim women was 15, as confirmed by a mid-1944 Syariah Court ruling that nullified a marriage on the grounds that the bride "had not reached the age of 15" and had not yet experienced menstruation; divorce petitioners' ages ranged from 14 to 30 (pp. 75–76).
- The average period of alleged husband abandonment cited in divorce petitions was between 6 months and 3 years, with some cases extending to 4–10 years; the quickest divorce case processed took only 9 days (Hajah Masriah Haji Kadri, Batu Pahat, June 1944) (pp. 79–80).
- In October 1944, Segamat district recorded a divorce rate of 119% (more divorces than marriages in a single month), and Pontian reached 115% in November 1944, indicating extreme family instability at the peak of the crisis (p. 76).
- The Muar Qadhi's radical 1945 proposal to require divorcing husbands to pay triple *mut'ah* (compensation) or face imprisonment in the magistrates' court was never implemented, and by mid-1945 the issue had been entirely displaced by Malay-Chinese racial violence in Muar and Batu Pahat (pp. 86–88).

### Conclusion

Ahmad concludes that the occupation period confirmed a correlation between economic crisis and the erosion of traditional family structures among Malay-Muslims, and that the religious elite—despite their nominal role as guardians of socio-religious tradition—were rendered ineffective by the Japanese appropriation of Islamic institutions for propaganda purposes. The loosening of traditional bonds, particularly for women, was a structural process that the war accelerated but did not originate, and which the religious bureaucracy was neither institutionally equipped nor politically permitted to reverse.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: Records of the Pejabat Qadhi Besar Johor and Pejabat Agama Islam dan Pelajaran Johore, held at the National Archive of Malaysia, Johore/Malacca Branch, Johor Baharu (indexed in Roman Malay across 7 and 4 volumes respectively), supplemented by the 1931 census report and wartime British intelligence price data (pp. 64–66).
- Historiographical contribution: The article fills a significant lacuna in the Japanese occupation literature by providing the first systematic quantitative study of family life among Malay-Muslims in a specific state, and it challenges the dominant narrative attributing Malay women's emancipation to Japanese-era education by demonstrating that the process was already advanced from the 1930s (pp. 63–64).
