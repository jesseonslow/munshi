# Indonesians in Malaya

A.B. Ramsay, a retired Malayan Civil Service district officer, published this ethnographic survey in 1956, offering a district-by-district account of the distribution, economic roles, and social characteristics of Indonesian immigrant communities across the Malay Peninsula. Written in the immediate aftermath of independence, the article argues that the 1947 Census had failed to adequately distinguish Indonesian groups from indigenous Malays, and that vernacular education, military service, and shared anti-Chinese sentiment would soon render the distinctions moot.

## Summary

The article proceeds geographically, tracing Indonesian settlements from Singapore through Johore, Malacca, Selangor, Perak, Province Wellesley, Kedah, and the East Coast States. Ramsay identifies and characterises each group—Boyanese, Bugis, Javanese, Banjarese, Jambi Malays, Menangkabau, and Achenese—recording their preferred economic niches (coconut, padi, rubber, retail trade), their spatial relationship to indigenous Malay riverine kampongs, and their distinctive social temperaments. The narrative is grounded in his own administrative experience: personal anecdotes about a solitary Bugis hawker in Kemaman, a Menangkabau currency speculator called "Habshi," and a Javanese headman, Dato Muskam, who exercised quasi-magical authority over hundreds of rubber smallholders in Kuala Selangor.

A recurring analytical thread is the contrast between coastal and inland settlement patterns: Javanese and Banjarese dominate the coastal flats in padi and coconut, while Sumatrans have pushed further inland to rubber and orchard cultivation. Ramsay also notes the administrative problem posed by these communities—the *pengulus* system, which drew exclusively from indigenous Malays, was ill-suited to areas where Indonesians predominated, making the semi-official *Ketua* headman arrangement a practical necessity.

### Key Findings

- The 1947 Census is judged unreliable for Indonesian populations because enumerators recorded anyone superficially Malay-looking as Malay, and Sumatran immigrants self-identified as Malay unless pressed (p. 119).
- The Bugis of Kukub and Senggarang, west coast of Johore, were the only compact Bugis group in Malaya; during the Stevenson Restriction scheme they smuggled rubber to Sumatra and killed the European commander of a preventive launch (pp. 119–120).
- The Banjarese form a solid block in the Krian padi areas of Perak, from which they have spread south to Tanjong Karang and Sungei Manik; they cultivate exclusively with the *tajak* (short heavy scythe) and maintain padi nurseries on floating rafts (pp. 120, 122–123).
- The Menangkabau hold a near-monopoly on Malay retail trade, including the manufacture and sale of songkok and embroidered slippers, and are described as the most financially and politically ambitious of all Indonesian groups (p. 123).
- The Kinnersley brothers, originally from Ceylon, are credited with providing the initial impetus for Sumatran immigration to the Kajang area of Selangor as pioneer coffee and rubber planters (p. 120).
- The *Ketua* system of semi-official headmen is presented as an administrative necessity wherever Indonesians predominate, since *pengulus* were without exception drawn from indigenous Malays (p. 124).

### Conclusion

Ramsay's definitive observation is that while the Indonesian groups remain distinguishable to those with intimate administrative contact, the combined pressures of vernacular schooling, intermarriage, and political ferment will produce "a pretty complete degree of assimilation" within a generation, making the distinctions he records a matter of historical rather than living interest.

## Context

- The author was a retired M.C.S. district officer with direct administrative responsibility over several of the communities described; the article reads as a personal ethnographic record compiled from decades of field contact rather than from archival sources.
- The piece was received by the Society in May 1955 and published in 1956, placing it in the transitional period immediately after Malayan independence, when such colonial-era administrative knowledge was being rapidly superseded.
