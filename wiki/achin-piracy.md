# Achin piracy

S.G.H. Leyh, a senior official in the Colonial Secretariat at Singapore, published this article in 1927, presenting the first-person field report of D.F.A. Hervey—the first cadet appointed to the Straits Settlements Civil Service—recording the 1868 British expedition to investigate the plundering of a Penang-registered schooner by Achinese at Kerti Creek on the north coast of Sumatra. The document serves as a rare administrative and diplomatic account of how the Straits Settlements Government enforced protection of British commercial interests against local piracy in the Acheen littoral during the early colonial period.

## Summary

The article opens with a brief prefatory note by Leyh identifying the incident and providing biographical context for Hervey, before reproducing his detailed narrative of the expedition. The core problem addressed is one of jurisdictional ambiguity: the plundering of a British-registered vessel by subjects of a local Achinese Raja fell into a grey zone between outright piracy (which would have warranted naval punishment) and a local criminal matter subject to indigenous law. The British response was calibrated to extract compensation and a nominal prisoner without provoking open conflict with a polity that maintained its own military capacity and internal legal codes.

Hervey's account traces the expedition's route from Singapore through Penang to Diamond Point, Telok Samawi, and finally Kerti Creek, documenting the diplomatic encounters at each stop. At Telok Samawi, the British delegation was received by the Raja (younger brother of the Sultan of Acheen), who offered a 21-gun salute and provided limited witness testimony. At Kerti, the negotiation was more adversarial: the Raja initially denied responsibility, then offered a payment of $595 and the delivery of the responsible Panglima, but subsequently reneged on the arrangement, attempting to substitute a cargo of betel-nut for the cash payment. The matter was resolved only after the British fired warning shots from H.M.S. *Perseus*, after which the Raja's representative delivered the money and a substitute prisoner (the Panglima's elder brother, the Panglima himself having disappeared).

The narrative is informed entirely by Hervey's direct observation and the documentary trail of the Colonial Secretary's Office (cited as C.S.O. "P.R. Receipt" numbers), giving it the character of an official dispatch enriched with ethnographic detail on local weapons, social hierarchy, and the political dynamics between the rival Rajas of Kerti and Telok Samawi.

### Key Findings

- The British schooner *Good Fortune* (34 tons burthen, registered Penang, owned by Neou Tean How) was plundered on 27 April 1868 off Kerti Creek by three boats carrying 15–20 Achinese each, with losses valued at $5,000 in money and goods (p. 316).
- The expedition departed Singapore on 29 June 1868 aboard the S.S. *Peiho* with H.M.S. *Perseus* and H.M.S. *Grasshopper*, and returned to Singapore on 14 July 1868 after a fortnight's absence (pp. 317, 322).
- The Raja of Kerti ultimately paid $595 as compensation and delivered a prisoner (the Panglima's elder brother, the Panglima himself having fled); the British accepted this as sufficient given the weakness of their evidentiary position (pp. 321–322).
- The local punishment for all forms of stealing, robbery, and plundering in the Acheen coast was death by decapitation, as stated by both the Rajas of Kerti and Telok Samawi (p. 320).
- The Raja of Telok Samawi claimed an army of 16,000 against Kerti's 20,000, and the stated cause of their impending war was the Raja of Kerti's refusal to acknowledge Telok Samawi's authority as a "second Sultan" (pp. 319–320).
- The population of Kerti was stated at 100,000, though Hervey estimated the true figure was likely no more than half that (p. 323).

### Conclusion

Hervey's report demonstrates that British enforcement of anti-piracy norms in the Acheen littoral in the 1860s operated through a pragmatic blend of diplomatic pressure, limited show of naval force, and acceptance of partial satisfaction—prioritising the recovery of a modest sum and a symbolic prisoner over the imposition of British legal standards on a polity that maintained its own sovereign justice system. The episode underscores the limits of colonial authority in peripheral zones where the Crown's reach depended on the cooperation of local rulers who retained genuine military and judicial autonomy.

## Context

- D.F.A. Hervey was the first cadet of the Straits Settlements Civil Service (appointed 24 May 1867); this report was written during his early service and published posthumously by Leyh in 1927 with the permission of the Colonial Secretary, Straits Settlements (p. 316).
- The article draws on Colonial Secretary's Office "P.R. Receipt" files (Nos. 785/1868, 1017/68, 1301/1868) held in the Straits Settlements archives, and represents a contemporary administrative record rather than a retrospective scholarly analysis.
