# Succession to the Perak Sultanate

Khoo Kay Kim's 1983 article examines the succession system of the Perak Sultanate from its 16th-century origins through the 1980s, arguing that the throne was never governed by primogeniture but by an elective practice that, from 1826, incorporated a rotation among three royal families descended from Sultan Ahmaddin. The article demonstrates how British intervention from 1874 onward fundamentally restructured this system, concentrating succession power in the hands of the reigning Sultan while nominally preserving the older arrangement.

## Summary

Khoo challenges the long-standing British official claim that Malay succession followed primogeniture, a misconception he argues was instrumental in justifying colonial expansion. Drawing on a succession chart of the first 19 rulers, he shows that only five succeeded their fathers, and that the pattern before the 19th century revealed a tendency for brothers to succeed one another rather than a father-to-son line. The so-called "rotation system" is dated precisely to the 1826 reshuffle accompanying the Low Treaty, when descendants of three sons of Sultan Ahmaddin—Sultan Abdul Malek Mansur Shah, Raja Inu, and Raja Abdul Rahman—were arranged to provide successive candidates for the Sultanate. Khoo emphasizes, however, that this rotation was never rigidly observed: there was no automatic promotion from one royal office to the next, and the holder of any title could be bypassed if deemed unsatisfactory.

The bulk of the article traces the intricate negotiations between the British colonial administration and the Perak royalty from 1874 to 1948, using Colonial Office correspondence to reveal how succession was effectively managed as a political instrument. The 1909 decision by Lord Crewe and Sir John Anderson to bar Sultan Abdullah's descendants from the major royal offices, its subsequent modification in 1915, and its complete lifting in 1920 form the central narrative arc. Khoo shows how individual assessments of princes—Raja Chulan, Raja Alang Iskandar, Raja Abdul Aziz, Raja Haji Ngah Abu Bakar—were weighed against dynastic claims, the wishes of the reigning Sultan, and British strategic interests. The article also documents how the system evolved after Sultan Idris's reign, with the rotation effectively narrowing to descendants of a single branch (Sultan Abdul Malek Mansur Shah) and the family of the exiled Sultan Abdullah remaining perpetually at the fringe.

The article's evidential base rests heavily on Colonial Office files, personal correspondence from High Commissioners and Residents, and Malay-language sources including the Maxwell translation of a native Perak history. Khoo also incorporates testimony from members of the Perak royal family, notably Raja Razman's 1953 remarks on the titles and succession practice, to ground his analysis in indigenous perspectives.

### Key Findings

- Of the first 19 rulers of Perak (from c. 1528), only five succeeded their fathers; the dominant pre-19th-century pattern was fraternal succession, not primogeniture (p. 8).
- The three-family rotation arrangement was established in 1826 following the Low Treaty, involving the descendants of Sultan Abdul Malek Mansur Shah, Raja Inu, and Raja Abdul Rahman, sons of Sultan Ahmaddin (1792–1806) (pp. 10–11).
- In 1909, Lord Crewe and Sir John Anderson decided that no member of the exiled Sultan Abdullah's family should be eligible for succession; this ban was notified privately to Sultan Idris and not made public until 1915 (pp. 13–14).
- The ban was completely lifted in 1920, and Raja Chulan (son of the exiled Sultan Abdullah) was conferred the title of Raja Di Hilir, a position he held until his death in 1933 (pp. 21–22).
- Between 1920 and the 1980s, no member of Sultan Abdullah's family reached the office of Raja Muda or Raja Bendahara; of the five rulers who reigned after Sultan Idris, four were descendants of Sultan Idris himself (p. 25).
- Since 1871, no Raja Muda has been bypassed in the succession to the Sultanate, indicating that the office had become effectively the heir-apparent position by the 20th century (p. 26).

### Conclusion

Khoo's definitive takeaway is that British administration, over approximately 70 years, transformed Perak's succession from a system in which the higher strata of the ruling class collectively controlled power into one where the reigning Sultan's wishes were decisive. The British preserved elements of the 1826 arrangement to maintain legitimacy, but in practice created a new tradition of royal patronage that concentrated authority in the palace. The rotation among the three original families was effectively abandoned after Sultan Idris, replaced by a narrower circulation among his own descendants and those of the exiled Sultan Abdullah—a family that, despite the lifting of the ban, never fully re-entered the inner circle of succession.

## Context

- The article draws extensively on Colonial Office files (C.O. 273/410, C.O. 273/426, C.O. 273/444, C.O. 273/473, C.O. 717/4, C.O. 717/99, C.O. 717/140) and on the Maxwell translation of a native Perak history, providing a level of documentary detail on succession negotiations not previously available in the secondary literature.
- Historiographically, the article corrects the earlier British administrative narrative (represented by Winstedt and Wilkinson) that treated the rotation system as a fixed constitutional norm, demonstrating instead that it was a flexible political arrangement continuously renegotiated by both the colonial government and the Perak royalty.
