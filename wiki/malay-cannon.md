# Malay cannon

G.C. Woolley, writing in 1947 for the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, surveys the origins, typology, and cultural functions of Malay cannon, arguing that these weapons represent a localized adaptation of European and Asian metallurgical traditions to the specific defensive and naval requirements of the Malay Archipelago. The article situates Malay cannon-making within a broader framework of cross-cultural transmission, tracing the probable 15th-century introduction of gunpowder technology from either China or the West and examining how distinct regional centres developed characteristic forms and ornamentation.

## Summary

Woolley opens by establishing the broad chronology of gunpowder and artillery in the world, from its Chinese origins through its European adoption in the 14th and 15th centuries, before narrowing to the Malay context. His central argument is that Malay cannon are unlikely to predate the 15th century, and that their manufacture was stimulated by the arrival of European and Asian ships carrying ordnance, combined with local metal-working traditions. He identifies several centres of production—Brunei, Palembang, Atcheh, Menangkabau, and Trengganu—and associates each with a dominant external influence: Chinese in Brunei, Arab in Palembang and Atcheh, and possibly Hindu in Java (pp. 35–36).

The article then turns to typology, explaining why the swivel or pivot gun became the dominant Malay form: the small calibre, light weight, and ease of mounting on boat bulwarks or fort walls made it far more practical than the large-calibre wheeled carriages of European fortifications. Woolley describes the distinctive breech construction—a hollow tube of roughly six inches rather than the European round knob—into which a wooden spike could be fitted for elevation, and notes the variety of decorative treatments, from twisted fluted barrels to dragon- or crocodile-mouthed muzzles associated with Brunei (pp. 36–37).

A distinctive feature of the article is its treatment of cannon as economic objects. Woolley documents their use in Borneo as a form of currency, valued at approximately $25–$30 per pikul, their role in legal fines and dowry payments, and their practical advantages as a store of wealth over livestock. He closes with a brief note on the fate of a specific Brunei cannon formerly held at Government House, Sandakan, which had disappeared—likely melted down by the Japanese during the occupation (p. 37).

### Key Findings

- Good cannon were being made in Pahang by 1600 A.D., and a casting mould dated to the 14th–15th century was discovered on the Tembeling (p. 35).
- In Brunei, the metal for cannon was obtained from selected Chinese brass 'cash' coins, confirming direct Chinese material influence (p. 36).
- Hamilton recorded 'culverins' in a fort at Atcheh in 1702, providing a dated European eyewitness account of Malay cannon in situ (p. 36).
- In Borneo, cannon served as a stable currency at approximately $25–$30 per pikul and were used to satisfy legal fines and as part of dowry ('Brian') payments (p. 37).
- The swivel gun's breech featured a hollow tube of roughly six inches (not the European round knob), accepting a wooden spike for elevation—an aiming mechanism Woolley compares favourably to the elevating screw of a modern machine gun (p. 36).
- A Brunei cannon formerly in Government House, Sandakan, and property of the North Borneo Government, had disappeared by 1947, presumably melted down as scrap by the Japanese (p. 37).

### Conclusion

Woolley's definitive takeaway is that Malay cannon represent a successful indigenous adaptation of imported technology to local conditions: the swivel form, the distinctive breech design, and the regional decorative traditions all reflect a pragmatic response to the demands of small-boat warfare and fort defence in the Archipelago, rather than mere imitation of European models. The article also implicitly laments the loss of surviving specimens, noting that many had already been melted down for gongs or other brassware even before the Japanese occupation.

## Context

- Woolley was a British colonial administrator in North Borneo; the article draws on his direct familiarity with local collections and the fate of government-held artefacts during the Japanese occupation.
- The study contributes to the early 20th-century historiography of Malay material culture by integrating European travel accounts (Marsden, Hamilton, St. John) with local ethnographic evidence, and by foregrounding the economic rather than purely military significance of cannon in Bornean society.
