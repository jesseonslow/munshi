# Penan

The Penan are an indigenous group of the Borneo highlands, documented in the scholarly record primarily through ethnographic and linguistic studies of their communities in Sarawak. Their significance lies in the depth of cultural documentation they have attracted, particularly in the mid-twentieth century, when fieldwork revealed a complex social system built around kinship terminology, death-name practices, and a nomadic or semi-nomadic subsistence economy in the upper river valleys of the island's interior.

## Scope and Historical Definition

The Penan are attested in the academic literature as a distinct ethnic group inhabiting the highland regions of Borneo, with specific communities identified in the upper Rejang River area of Sarawak. G. Arnold's 1958 monograph, "Nomadic Penan of the Upper Rejang (Plieran), Sarawak," provides the most substantial single treatment of the group in the Society's journal, spanning over forty pages of detailed ethnographic observation [[1]](#ref-1). The title itself signals a key characteristic of the community studied: a nomadic or semi-nomadic lifestyle associated with the upper reaches of the Rejang River, a tributary system in the interior of Sarawak. Arnold's work situates the Penan within the broader context of Borneo's highland peoples, whose economies and social structures differ markedly from those of the lowland communities that had been the primary focus of earlier colonial-era scholarship.

The designation "Plieran" in Arnold's title appears to refer to a specific locality or sub-group within the Upper Rejang area, suggesting that the Penan are not a monolithic entity but comprise distinct local communities with their own place-names and possibly variant customs. The use of the term "nomadic" in the title, rather than "sedentary" or "semi-sedentary," indicates that Arnold observed a pattern of seasonal or cyclical movement among the people he studied, a feature that distinguished them from the more settled agricultural communities of the Borneo lowlands.

## Kinship and Death-Name Systems

A separate but complementary line of inquiry into Penan social structure is provided by R. Needham's 1954 article, "Batu Belah & Long Terawan: kinship terms & death names," which examines the linguistic and social mechanisms through which Penan communities organise their kinship relations and mark the transition of individuals from life to death [[2]](#ref-2). The title references two specific localities—Batu Belah and Long Terawan—indicating that Needham's observations were grounded in fieldwork at named settlements, and that the kinship and death-name systems he documented may vary between communities.

The pairing of "kinship terms" and "death names" in the title suggests that these two systems are interrelated in Penan social organisation. In many Borneo highland societies, the naming conventions applied to the dead are not merely commemorative but carry social and ritual significance, potentially affecting the status of the deceased's kin, the allocation of resources, or the performance of subsequent ceremonies. Needham's brief article, though limited to three pages, points to a system in which death names function as a distinct category of personal nomenclature, separate from the kinship terms used in everyday social interaction. The fact that both Batu Belah and Long Terawan are named in the title implies that Needham compared or contrasted the systems as practised in these two locations, a methodological approach that would have been essential for distinguishing between universal Penan features and local variation.

The brevity of Needham's contribution—three pages in a journal issue—suggests that it was published as a short notice or preliminary report rather than a full monographic study. This is consistent with the practice of the Society's journal in the 1950s, which regularly carried brief linguistic and ethnographic notes alongside longer articles. The article nonetheless represents an important early attempt to document Penan social terminology in a systematic way, and its focus on death names in particular anticipates later anthropological interest in mortuary practices among Borneo highland peoples.

## Economic and Subsistence Patterns

Arnold's extensive treatment of the Upper Rejang Penan [[1]](#ref-1) provides the primary evidence for the economic life of the group. The characterisation of the community as "nomadic" in the article's title implies a subsistence strategy based on mobility rather than fixed agriculture, a pattern well attested among the highland peoples of Borneo who practise shifting cultivation in combination with hunting, gathering, and the exploitation of forest resources. The Upper Rejang area, being a remote interior river system, would have offered the kind of forested landscape in which such a mobile economy could be sustained.

The length of Arnold's article—over forty pages—indicates that he documented a wide range of economic activities, likely including the techniques of shifting cultivation, the tools and methods of hunting and gathering, and the seasonal patterns of movement between camps. The specific reference to the Upper Rejang as the study area suggests that Arnold's observations were confined to a single river system and its tributaries, and that the economic patterns he described may be particular to that locality rather than representative of all Penan communities. The article's placement in the 1958 volume of the journal situates it in a period when ethnographic documentation of Borneo's indigenous peoples was accelerating, driven in part by the impending political changes that would bring Sarawak into closer association with the Federation of Malaya and, ultimately, into the formation of Malaysia.

## Research and Documentation

The documentation of the Penan in the Society's journal reflects a shift in scholarly attention from the lowland communities of the Straits Settlements and the Malay Peninsula to the interior highland peoples of Borneo. Arnold's 1958 monograph represents the most substantial single contribution, offering a detailed ethnographic account of a specific Penan community in the Upper Rejang area [[1]](#ref-1). Its length and scope suggest that it was the product of extended fieldwork and was intended as a comprehensive treatment of the group's social, economic, and cultural life.

Needham's 1954 article, by contrast, is a brief linguistic-ethnographic note focused on a specific aspect of Penan social organisation—kinship terminology and death-name practices—as observed at two named localities [[2]](#ref-2). The earlier date of this publication indicates that interest in Penan social structure preceded Arnold's broader ethnographic study, and that the two works are complementary rather than redundant: Needham's article addresses the symbolic and linguistic dimensions of Penan social life, while Arnold's monograph addresses the material and economic dimensions.

Together, these two publications establish a foundation for the scholarly record on the Penan in the Society's journal, though they are limited in scope. Arnold's study is confined to a single river system in Sarawak, and Needham's observations are restricted to two localities. Neither work provides a comprehensive account of all Penan communities across Borneo, and the records do not extend to the group's presence in other parts of the island or to the later period of political and economic transformation that affected Borneo's indigenous peoples in the decades following independence. The existing documentation thus represents a valuable but partial picture, concentrated in the 1950s and focused on the interior highlands of Sarawak.
