# The ‘Indian Association Movement’ in peninsular Malaysia: the early years

Khoo Kay Kim's 1992 article traces the origins of the "Indian Association Movement" in peninsular Malaysia from the 1890s through the early 1920s, arguing that the English-educated urban Indian community—long overshadowed by plantation labour studies—was socially, religiously, and politically active well before the rise of mass nationalism. The study demonstrates that the movement was inspired by the Indian Association of Calcutta (founded 1876) and that its early failures in Penang and Taiping gave way to a more durable institution in Ipoh, the Kinta Indian Association, which became the principal centre of Indian nationalist activity in the Kinta Valley by the early 1920s.

## Summary

The article challenges the popular belief that the first Indian Association in Malaya was founded in 1906, establishing instead that the Indian Association, Penang, was formed in 1892 and lasted barely three years before collapsing due to internal disagreements. A Taiping branch was opened in September 1894, and a successor body, the Penang Hindu Association, was formally registered in November 1904. Though named "Hindu," it admitted Indian Christians and barred sectarian discussion; its activities centred on lectures, a small library, and football. By early 1906, however, a more politically ambitious faction—led by V. Chelliah Pillai, P. D. Nambyar, S. Augustine, and V. V. Krishnier—circulated a proposal for a new "Indian Association" that would unite both Indian and Ceylonese residents, apparently ignoring or deliberately disavowing the earlier bodies.

The pivotal figures of 1906 were P. K. Nambyar, a Cambridge-educated barrister and former Registrar of the Madras High Court who had settled in Penang in 1904, and Rev. W. Raju Naidu, a Telegu Methodist pastor newly arrived in Taiping. Naidu's impassioned speeches on the partition of Bengal, the Swadeshi movement, and the arrest of Surendranath Banerjea gave the movement its political edge. On April 2, 1906, a meeting at the Methodist Episcopal Church in Taiping unanimously resolved to form an Indian Association and elected Nambyar as President; on April 7 the body was designated "The Indian Association of the FMS." A parallel Indo-Ceylonese Association was formed in Penang on April 9, also with Nambyar as President. An Ipoh branch was inaugurated on June 9, 1906, at the Kinta Chinese Club, with over 90 attendees of "all castes, sects and creeds."

Despite this initial energy, the Penang and Ipoh associations both collapsed within months. The Taiping association survived but lost its political character by late 1906, and by 1911 its presidency had passed to Shaik Nunna Meah, a Muslim, with the newspaper proprietor Syed Abu Hassan Burhan urging members to seek government assistance for a better building—a marked shift from the earlier nationalist tone. Khoo attributes the early failures to the absence of a sense of urgency among urban Indian immigrants whose livelihoods were not threatened, the difficulty of sustaining enthusiasm without the continuous presence of charismatic leaders, and possibly the reluctance of a predominantly Hindu community to be led by a Christian pastor.

The Kinta Indian Association (KIA), formally registered on April 28, 1911, proved more resilient. After a period of lethargy, it was revived in November 1914 through the efforts of Dr. Dhanasamy, T. V. Ponnuswami, S. Gulam Mohidin, and others. Within six months it had recruited 78 new members, raised $464 in donations, acquired a 171-book library, and by 1916 operated tennis and badminton courts. It purchased an acre of land near Sungai Pari in 1920 with its $2,000 savings and patron donations of $500 each from Chidabaram Chettiar, S. L. S. Chettiappa Chettiar, and Louis Thivy, J.P. The KIA also engaged in social welfare: it dispatched volunteers during the 1918 influenza epidemic, collected famine relief for Bengal in 1920, and remitted Rs. 12,000 to the Ramakrishna Mission in Madras in 1924 for flood victims.

### Key Findings

- The first Indian Association in peninsular Malaysia was founded in Penang in 1892 (not 1906 as commonly believed) and lasted approximately three years; it opened a Taiping branch on September 8, 1894 (pp. 3–4).
- The Indian Association of the FMS was formally constituted in Taiping on April 7, 1906, with P. K. Nambyar as President and Rev. W. Raju Naidu as Vice-President; its initial membership was approximately 40 (pp. 9–11).
- The Ipoh branch, inaugurated on June 9, 1906, attracted over 90 attendees but began with only twelve members besides the President and failed to survive the first year (pp. 13–15).
- The Kinta Indian Association was formally registered on April 28, 1911; by 1916 it had raised $341 in donations plus $468 in surplus funds, and by 1920 had accumulated $2,000 to purchase an acre of land near Sungai Pari (pp. 18–20).
- Malayan Political Intelligence Bureau reports from 1922 identified Ipoh as the main centre of Indian nationalist activity in the peninsula, noting that "almost all educated Southern Indians and Ceylonese of the clerical and commercial classes exhibit a strong sympathy with Indian Nationalist aims" and that V. Raju, Honorary Secretary of the KIA, was a recipient of rings bearing Gandhi's portrait for distribution (p. 21).
- The KIA published its own official paper, the *Indo-Malayan Review*, in 1933, in which it explicitly disavowed political and religious functions—a statement Khoo notes was "for the consumption of the authorities" (p. 20).

### Conclusion

Khoo concludes that the 1906 Indian Association movement was a deliberate attempt to replicate, in the Malayan context, the function of Surendranath Banerjea's Indian Association of Calcutta as a vehicle for creating public opinion among the educated middle class. The early failures in Penang and Taiping were attributable to the absence of sustained leadership and a community not yet sufficiently politicised to sustain nationalist fervour. It was only through the "accidental convergence" of persistent individuals in Ipoh—Sengalraya Naidu, T. V. Pillai, and S. Gulam Mohidin—that a durable institution emerged. A truly all-Malayan body of the kind the 1906 founders envisioned did not materialise until the Central Indian Association of Malaya (CIAM) was established in 1936.

## Context

- The article draws heavily on contemporary newspaper reports (*The Perak Pioneer*, *The Straits Echo*, *The Pinang Gazette*, *The Malay Mail*, *The Malaya Tribune*) and Colonial Office political intelligence files (C.O. 273/517, C.O. 273/518), supplemented by G. Palanivel's unpublished typescript on the KIA.
- The study contributes to the historiography of urban Indian communities in Malaya by shifting focus away from plantation labour and toward the English-educated professional and clerical classes, and by establishing a direct genealogical link between the 1906 associations and the later CIAM.
