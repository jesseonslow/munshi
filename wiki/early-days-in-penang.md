# Early days in Penang

Revd. Keppel Garnier's 1923 article "Early Days in Penang" reconstructs the founding and formative decades of the settlement (1786–1820s) from the perspective of a local clergyman with direct access to parish registers and community memory, arguing that early Penang was a close-knit, socially vibrant European community that defied the conventional narrative of colonial life as lonely exile.

## Summary

Garnier opens with the landing of Lieutenant Gray and the marines on 17 July 1786 and Captain Francis Light's arrival the following day, drawing on Light's own accounts to narrate the rapid establishment of the Fort, the Bazar, and the formal taking of possession on 11 August 1786. The early growth is sketched through the arrival of Captain James Scott and the clearing of Glugor, the establishment of Ayer Itam, and the swift influx of Chinese shopkeepers and Malay inhabitants. The article then turns to the central administrative conflict of the 1790s: the friction between the EIC government under Major Macdonald and the independent European planters who had accumulated land and wealth. Macdonald's attempt to investigate their land titles and the resulting standoff is presented as a recurring tension between Company authority and private commercial interest, with the planters described as "a thorn in a side of Government" who bore "a close resemblance to their brothers and cousins who were living similar cheery lives on the plantations in the West Indies" (p. 7).

The latter half of the article shifts decisively to social history, using church registers (baptisms, marriages, burials at St. George's Church) and the 1811 travel journal of James Wathen as its primary evidentiary base. Through these sources Garnier reconstructs the domestic and social world of the European community: the Raffles family with Stamford, his wife Olivia, and their sisters; the Bannerman, Caunter, Carnegy, and Cousens families; and the career of W. E. Phillips, who served as Acting Governor and later Governor while simultaneously performing the functions of chaplain. The article culminates in Wathen's two-month visit in 1811, which provides a contemporary outsider's eye on the settlement's physical appearance, its European tradesmen, its postal arrangements, and its domestic life.

### Key Findings

- By 1792, Light reported a population of 10,000 (7,000 "inhabitants," 1,000 Company's servants, 1,500 Malays, and 1,500–2,000 transient strangers); by 1794 the figure had risen to 25,000 (p. 6).
- Within one year of the founding, 60 Chinese families operated shops in the Bazar, which Light described as "already pretty extensive" (p. 6).
- The EIC commercial career ladder in Penang progressed from Writer to Factor (after 6 years), to Junior Merchant (years 9–11), to Senior Merchant (from year 12), a system that blurred the line between government official and private trader (p. 7).
- A regulation issued circa 1811 required all servants to give one month's notice before quitting employment, with penalties of a fine not exceeding 10 Spanish dollars and imprisonment not exceeding one month (p. 12).
- The double wedding of 30 June 1818 united W. E. Phillips to Janet Bannerman (daughter of Governor Colonel Bannerman) and Lieutenant Henry Burney to Janet Bannerman (daughter of the Rev. James Patrick Bannerman, niece of the Governor), both ceremonies performed at the newly built Church of St. George the Martyr (p. 9).
- James Wathen's 1811 account records that European hands still performed trades including printing, tavern-keeping, hairdressing, coachmaking, watchmaking, cooperage, and shipwrighting, and that the Governor's coachman and valet were English (p. 11).

### Conclusion

Garnier's definitive takeaway is that the early European community in Penang was neither the isolated exile nor the purely commercial outpost that later popular imagination had made it. It was a small, intermarried, family-oriented society in which "one finds that many of one's preconceived ideas of life in the East in those days have to be revised" (p. 10). The rapid demographic growth from a handful of marines to 25,000 inhabitants within a decade of Light's death, driven by the confidence of "Asiatics of all races" in the government, is presented as the ultimate vindication of Light's founding vision.

## Context

- Garnier was an Anglican clergyman in Penang writing from within the community he described; his access to St. George's Church registers and his familiarity with the families named (several of whom were still living in 1923) give the article the character of a local antiquarian sketch rather than a detached scholarly study.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its use of parish registers and Wathen's 1811 journal as social evidence for a period otherwise thinly documented in official EIC records, offering a domestic and cultural counterpoint to the administrative narrative.
