# Hikayat Sultan Bustaman

H. Overbeck published this detailed synopsis of the *Hikayat Sultan Bustamam* in 1931, presenting a Malay literary text of Indian origin that narrates the legendary spread of Islam across a fictionalized India. Overbeck's overarching purpose is twofold: to facilitate the tracing of the text's Indian source and to contribute to the still-sparse scholarly knowledge of the legends woven around Islam's conquest of the subcontinent.

## Summary

Overbeck's article is primarily a literary publication rather than a conventional historical study. His brief introduction establishes the text's provenance: the *Hikayat Sultan Bustamam* is not an original Malay composition but a translation from Hindustani, rendered into Jawi by a figure identified in the Penang editions as "Dato' Saudagar Puteh." The author situates the work within the broader tradition of Indian political literature, noting its author's evident command of the *Nitisatra* (the science of politics) and drawing a parallel to Visakhadatta's *Mudraraksha* in the way the story's viziers manipulate kings toward state interests without scruple about means (pp. 35–36). Overbeck also identifies a kinship between this text and the *Hikayat Ganja Mara*, which he had previously published, suggesting a common source he was unable to trace.

The synopsis itself runs to nearly 90 pages and follows the hero Bustamam, son of Sultan Yahya of Sematrani and the village girl Siti Selamih, from his childhood under a Zahid Sufi through his adventures in Bederani and Tahta Yemen, where he ultimately converts the sun- and fire-worshipping kingdoms of Khairani to Islam. The narrative is structured around the political dilemma of Maharaja Beniasin of Bederani, whose daughter Seri Maharaja Puteri is forced by the overlord Kerbabahur of Kaladesa to marry the brutal giant Dandam Bachtiar. Bustamam intervenes, defeats Bachtiar and the Brahman Jakni, and is ultimately crowned sovereign of Khairani. Overbeck flags the text's 507-page folio length and laments that his condensation can only give "a very feeble reflection" of the original's beauty (p. 36).

The historical problems Overbeck addresses are modest but significant for their time. He notes that the story was already circulating in Acehnese literature before the first known printed edition (1895), as evidenced by Snouck-Hurgronje's account in *The Acehnese* (vol. II, pp. 143–155), and that the Malays treat the text with a degree of veneration unusual for popular literature, reading a *Jampi* (incantation) over it before opening the book (p. 35). He also observes that the author's evident respect for the king of Gujerat may point to a Gujarati or nearby origin for the story, which would explain its transmission into Malay literary culture given the well-established commercial and cultural influence of Gujarat on the Malay world (p. 36).

### Key Findings

- The *Hikayat Sultan Bustamam* is a translation from Hindustani into Jawi by "Dato' Saudagar Puteh"; three lithographed editions are known: Penang A.H. 1312 (1895), Penang A.H. 1317 (1900), and Singapore A.H. 1332 (1915), the last being Overbeck's copy (p. 35).
- The text is 507 pages in folio format; Overbeck's synopsis condenses this into approximately 87 pages of narrative (p. 36).
- Dr. C. Snouck-Hurgronje records an Acehnese version ("Hikayat Sulutan Boseutaman") in *The Acehnese*, vol. II, pp. 143–155, gathered mainly in 1892, predating the first printed Malay edition (p. 35).
- The Malay text contains an Arabic genealogy of the kings of Sematrani, tracing Sultan Yahya al-Ansari back through Ishak ibn Ja'far Madani to the Ansari lineage, with a historical narrative explaining the dynasty's founding through the intervention of "Sultan Kebir Shah of our country" (p. 117).
- The story's political framework draws on the *Nitisatra* tradition; the viziers operate with the pragmatic sagacity characteristic of the *Mudraraksha*, guiding kings toward dynastic interests without moral scruple about means (pp. 35–36).
- Overbeck was unable to trace the names of persons or countries in the Malay text in the Index to *The Arabic History of Gujerat* (ed. Sir E. Denison Ross, London 1910–1928), leaving the Indian original unidentified (p. 36).

### Conclusion

Overbeck's definitive takeaway is that the *Hikayat Sultan Bustamam* represents a valuable but underexplored window into the Indian legendary tradition surrounding Islam's conquest of the subcontinent, a body of literature about which "very little is known" at the time of writing. He argues that publishing the synopsis serves a dual purpose: it may enable future scholars to locate and study the Indian original, and it preserves a text of considerable literary merit that deserves a new critical edition of the Malay version.

## Context

- Overbeck's primary source is a single lithographed copy (Singapore, A.H. 1332) acquired from a Malay bookshop; he had no access to a manuscript and could not consult the two earlier Penang editions held at the Koloniale Bibliotheek, The Hague (p. 35).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malay-Indian literary exchange by documenting a specific case of textual transmission from Hindustani to Jawi and by identifying the *Nitisatra* as the intellectual framework underlying the narrative's political philosophy.
