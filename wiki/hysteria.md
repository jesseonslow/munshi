# Hysteria

## Hysteria

The term *hysteria*, in its broadest clinical and anthropological usage, has long served as a catch-all category for psychogenic conditions characterised by sudden outbursts of uncontrolled behaviour, vocalisation, and motor disturbance. In the Malay Peninsula and Borneo, two such conditions—*latah* and *amok*—have attracted sustained scholarly attention, particularly for their distinctive cultural expressions and their apparent parallels with abnormal mental phenomena observed among geographically distant peoples. The comparative study of these conditions, as documented in the early literature of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, reveals a field of inquiry that straddled clinical medicine, ethnography, and the emerging discipline of cross-cultural psychology, with scholars debating whether such phenomena were culturally specific or represented universal expressions of a common underlying psychological mechanism.

### Scope and Historical Definition

The Malay condition of *latah* is a psychogenic disorder, predominantly affecting women, in which the sufferer exhibits sudden, involuntary outbursts of profanity, screaming, and sometimes aggressive or self-endangering behaviour, typically triggered by suggestion, startle, or social provocation. *Amok*, by contrast, is characterised by a sudden episode of violent, purposeless aggression, often culminating in the sufferer's death or capture, and is not specifically linked to a triggering stimulus in the same way as *latah*. C. Boden Kloss, in a brief comparative note, drew attention to the Eskimo condition *piblokto*, as described by Robert Peary in *The North Pole*, which he noted presented a superficial resemblance to *latah* in its female manifestations: adult Eskimo women would scream, tear off their clothing, and run naked on the ice in extreme cold, sometimes to the point of freezing to death if not restrained [[1, p. 254]](#ref-1). Peary also recorded an instance in which a *piblokto* sufferer killed a companion [[1, p. 254]](#ref-1). Kloss argued, however, that despite the surface similarity, *piblokto* was not triggered by suggestion or shock and was therefore more akin to *amok* than to *latah* [[1, p. 254]](#ref-1). He closed with the observation that both Eskimos and Malays were Mongoloid peoples, despite the vast geographic separation between the Arctic and the equatorial belt [[1, p. 254]](#ref-1).

J. O'May, writing in the same year, extended the comparative frame further, arguing that the abnormal mental phenomena among North Siberian peoples such as the Yakuts were "notably similar" to those among Malays and "still more similar" to those among the Dayak peoples of Borneo [[2, p. 382]](#ref-2). He identified the Malay *pawang* (a traditional healer or spirit-medium) and the Dayak *manang* (a ritual specialist and healer) as essentially equivalent to the Central Asian *shaman*, a point he attributed to Archdeacon Perham, who had made the same observation in 1887 [[2, p. 382]](#ref-2). O'May's contribution was to situate *latah* within a broader typology of psychogenic and possession states that transcended any single cultural context.

### The Question of Profanity and Linguistic Outbursts

A distinctive feature of *latah* that has drawn particular scholarly attention is the sudden eruption of profane, obscene, or otherwise socially inappropriate language from the sufferer's mouth. O'May addressed this feature directly, contending that such outbursts were not peculiar to the condition but were observable in a range of analogous states: among Yakut shamans, in Batak spirit possession, among the insane generally, and even among normal individuals under the influence of nitrous oxide or in hypnagogic states [[2, p. 382]](#ref-2). To reinforce his point, O'May cited an anecdote from Dr. T. B. Hyslop illustrating the extraordinary vocabulary that could emerge from a sub-conscious state, thereby supporting his conclusion that the linguistic outbursts of *latah* admitted of a general psychological explanation rather than being specific to the Malay context [[2, p. 383]](#ref-2). This argument positioned *latah* not as a culturally bounded anomaly but as one expression among many of a universal psychological mechanism in which the suppression of socially taboo material is temporarily lifted under conditions of heightened suggestibility or altered consciousness.

### Comparative Frameworks and Racial Taxonomy

The comparative literature on *latah*, *amok*, and their Arctic and Siberian analogues was shaped by the racial and classificatory assumptions prevalent in early twentieth-century anthropology. Kloss's closing remark—that both Eskimos and Malays were Mongoloid peoples—illustrates how such comparisons were often framed within a racial taxonomy that sought to explain cross-cultural similarities through shared biological ancestry rather than through independent cultural development or universal psychological processes [[1, p. 254]](#ref-1). O'May's approach, while also comparative, leaned more heavily toward a psychological rather than a racial explanation, emphasising the functional equivalence of the *pawang* and *manang* to the *shaman* and the general psychological account of the linguistic phenomena [[2, pp. 382–383]](#ref-2). The tension between these two explanatory frameworks—racial affinity versus universal psychology—was a recurring feature of the comparative literature on psychogenic conditions in the region, and it reflected broader debates within anthropology about the relative weight of biology and culture in shaping human behaviour.

## Research and Documentation

The documentation of *latah*, *amok*, and their comparative analogues in the early volumes of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society's journal reflects a period of active scholarly engagement with psychogenic conditions in the Malay world. The two notes by Kloss and O'May, both published in 1923, represent a moment in which the Society's contributors were actively seeking to place local phenomena within a global comparative framework. Kloss's note, though brief, introduced the Arctic parallel and raised the question of whether *latah* and *amok* were distinct conditions or points on a single spectrum of psychogenic disturbance. O'May's note, slightly longer and more analytically developed, pushed the comparison further into Siberia and Borneo and offered a psychological explanation for the most culturally salient feature of *latah*—its profanity. Together, these contributions illustrate a shift in the Society's literature from purely descriptive ethnography toward a more interpretive and comparative approach, one that sought to identify the underlying mechanisms common to diverse cultural expressions of abnormal mental states. The reliance on secondary sources—Peary's travel narrative, Perham's earlier observation, Hyslop's clinical anecdote—also indicates that the field was still in an early stage, with direct clinical or ethnographic study of *latah* and *amok* in the Malay context yet to be fully developed within the Society's own publications.
