# Edward Blundell

Edmund Blundell (1811–1870s) served as Governor of the Straits Settlements from 1855 to 1859, a post he attained after thirty-four years of continuous service to the East India Company in the region. His appointment was widely regarded as a just reward for his long career, and the community at large shared in the satisfaction of his promotion, for very few Englishmen still living in the Straits at that time could be as closely identified as he was with the development of the Settlements and the rise of their prosperity [[1, p. 134]](#ref-1). However, his Governorship ended in what Turnbull describes as a fate "partly of his own making and largely the outcome of his very virtues," as his loyalty to the Supreme Government of India and his respect for tradition prevented him from adjusting his outlook to the rapidly changing conditions in the Straits [[1, p. 134]](#ref-1).

### Career and Administrative Context

Blundell had come to Penang as a civil servant in 1821 and had acted temporarily as Governor on several occasions prior to 1855 [[1, p. 134]](#ref-1). His appointment was long anticipated; it had been expected as far back as 1843, and there was a great public outcry when Colonel Butterworth was appointed from India instead [[1, p. 134]](#ref-1). By the time of his Governorship, however, the Straits Settlements had transformed from small trading posts into cosmopolitan cities with large and expanding populations requiring careful administration and the enforcement of a definite, common and impartial code of law [[1, p. 135]](#ref-1). Blundell's difficulty lay in the structural inadequacy of the governance system: since 1830, when the Penang Presidency was abolished and the Settlements were demoted to the status of a mere Residency subordinate to the Bengal Presidency, the Governor had been at once the highest legislative, judicial and executive authority, yet in practice was a middle-grade civil servant lacking the substance of power [[1, pp. 135–136]](#ref-1). The normal checks and balances necessary for good government were therefore lacking within the administration itself [[1, p. 136]](#ref-1).

### The Maxwell Dispute

The crisis of Blundell's Governorship centred on a bitter quarrel with Sir Benson Maxwell, the Recorder of Penang, triggered by the illegal detention and transfer of a young Malay woman named Meh to the court of the Rajah of Kedah [[1, p. 134]](#ref-1). The incident arose from a robbery at a Chinese pawnshop in Telok Ayer Tawar, Province Wellesley, on the night of 11 August 1858, committed by a gang of more than twenty Malays, several of whom were related to the Kedah royal family [[1, p. 140]](#ref-1). The Deputy Commissioner of Police, Robertson, who was Blundell's son-in-law, sent Meh to Kedah as a decoy for her husband, in contravention of the law [[1, pp. 141–143]](#ref-1). When Maxwell learned of the detention, he demanded an immediate enquiry, but Blundell, persuaded by the Resident Councillor Lewis that the matter was trivial, dismissed the complaint [[1, pp. 144–145]](#ref-1). The dispute escalated into mutual imputation of base personal motives, with Blundell accusing Maxwell of feigning a desire to expose the truth to mask personal vindictiveness, and Maxwell accusing Blundell of shielding his son-in-law from legal accountability [[1, pp. 147–148]](#ref-1).

### Resignation and Aftermath

Blundell submitted his resignation, citing old age and failing eyesight, though the quarrel with Maxwell was the last overwhelming blow [[1, p. 151]](#ref-1). Lord Canning accepted the resignation without reservations and acquitted Blundell entirely of the corrupt acts and motives charged against him [[1, pp. 151–152]](#ref-1). When Blundell left Singapore in August 1859, only a handful of close friends and officials saw him off; there were none of the farewell dinners or receptions which were the normal due of departing Governors [[1, p. 152]](#ref-1). He was shortly afterwards reduced to partial blindness through cataract of one eye [[1, p. 152]](#ref-1). The subsequent investigation, presided over by his successor Colonel Sir Orfeur Cavenagh, confirmed that the police had acted irregularly and illegally in their treatment of Meh, but attributed the failures to Lewis's lazy supervision rather than to Blundell's deliberate misconduct [[1, pp. 153–157]](#ref-1). Turnbull argues that the quarrel, while personal in its intensity, exposed the bankruptcy of the old-fashioned government of the Straits Settlements as an appendage of India, and showed that the time had come when a more orderly administration was required [[1, p. 161]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The principal scholarly treatment of Blundell's Governorship remains Constance M. Turnbull's 1957 article in the *JMBRAS*, which reconstructs the conflict between Blundell and Maxwell in considerable detail from primary archival sources including the Straits Settlements Records in the Raffles Library, Singapore, the printed *Proceedings of the Investigation* of October 1859, and contemporary newspapers [[1, p. 162]](#ref-1). Turnbull's approach is distinctive in that she uses the personal quarrel as a lens to expose structural and constitutional problems in the colonial administration, rather than treating it merely as a biographical episode. Her article also recovers the voices of subordinate officials and local inhabitants who were otherwise marginalised in official narratives of the period. The article sits within a broader historiographical tradition of the Society's journal examining the transition of the Straits Settlements from Company rule to Crown colony, a process that Blundell's tenure both reflected and accelerated.
