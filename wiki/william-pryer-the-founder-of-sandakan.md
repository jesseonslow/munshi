# William Pryer, the founder of Sandakan

K.G. Tregonning's 1954 article reconstructs the life and administrative career of William L. Pryer, the first Resident of the East Coast of North Borneo and the founder of Sandakan, drawing heavily on Pryer's own preserved diary and the British North Borneo Company's papers. The article argues that Pryer's personal energy, sympathetic understanding of indigenous peoples, and willingness to use force when necessary established the foundations of British authority in the region during its most precarious early years (pp. 35, 50).

## Summary

The article traces Pryer's arrival in Sandakan Bay in February 1878, when he was deposited there by Baron von Overbeck as a placeholder while Overbeck and Alfred Dent hastened to London to float the Chartered Company. Tregonning uses Pryer's diary to show how, in his first months, the young former bookkeeper built authority through personal engagement with local chiefs, investigation of the slave trade, and the collection of customs dues. The narrative highlights the tensions between the new Company's claims and existing Sulu sovereignty, particularly the confrontation with W.C. Cowie over the re-hoisting of the Sulu flag and the Spanish naval incursion of September 1878, which Pryer defused through a combination of armed native show of force and diplomatic pressure (pp. 35–42).

The second phase covers the founding of Elopura (later Sandakan) after the burning of Pryer's original village in June 1879, and the rapid growth of the settlement as a port for jungle produce, timber, and birds' nests. Tregonning documents Pryer's role in attracting settlers, organizing a police force, and managing the complex interplay of Sulu, Bajau, Bugis, and Chinese communities. The article also covers the failed Chinese migration scheme of 1882–83, the brief gold boom on the Segama River, and the eventual rise of tobacco as the Company's financial saviour (pp. 42–48).

The final phase addresses Pryer's post-Charter career as an agricultural entrepreneur and his absence from Sandakan during the Mat Salleh crisis of 1895, which Tregonning implies Pryer's presence might have averted. The article closes with Pryer's death at sea in January 1899 and a comparison of his achievement to that of Singapore's founding (pp. 48–50).

### Key Findings

- Pryer landed at Kampong German, Sandakan Harbour, on February 11, 1878, with a staff of two (an African and a West Indian) and instructions from Overbeck to cultivate friendly relations and introduce changes only gradually (pp. 35–36).
- The Spanish warship *Eldorado* entered Sandakan Harbour on September 4, 1878, demanding the hoisting of the Spanish flag; Pryer's show of armed native resistance and diplomatic pressure caused the Spaniards to withdraw without returning (pp. 41–42).
- Pryer's original village was burnt to the ground on June 15, 1879, with losses including $3,000 worth of rattans belonging to Toongal and $1,000 to Hadji Basoh; he relocated to Booly Sim Sim at the harbour mouth and named the new site "Elopura" (p. 42).
- By 1891, Sandakan had grown to a population of 6,350 (p. 48).
- The Segama gold boom, confirmed by an official report in 1886, collapsed by September 1887 when no reef was found and the alluvial deposits were exhausted (p. 47).
- Pryer died at sea on January 7 or 8, 1899, at the age of sixty, and was buried at Suez (p. 49).

### Conclusion

Tregonning's definitive takeaway is that Pryer's achievement—establishing peace, law, and prosperity in a remote and dangerous corner of Borneo without the backing of the Royal Navy or a major imperial institution—deserves recognition comparable to that of Singapore's founding. He credits Pryer's sympathetic understanding of indigenous peoples and personal courage as the primary factors that made British North Borneo a stable colony, and insists that "much of the credit" for the territory's peaceful development "must go to William Pryer" (p. 50).

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the British North Borneo Company's Papers, transferred to the Colonial Office upon the Company's liquidation after World War II, and on Pryer's own diary, made available through the courtesy of Sir Thomas Lloyd and Sir Douglas Malcolm (p. 35, n.1).
- Tregonning was compiling a history of the Chartered Company (1881–1941) at the time of writing, and this article serves as a biographical precursor to that larger work; he also notes the intention to publish the diary of Pryer's wife, Mrs. Ada Pryer, in a future issue of the Journal (p. 48, n.25).
