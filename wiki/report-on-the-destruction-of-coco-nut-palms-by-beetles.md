# Report on the destruction of coco-nut palms by beetles

Henry N. Ridley, Director of Gardens and Forests for the Straits Settlements, published this report in 1889 to address the escalating destruction of coconut plantations in Singapore by two beetle species. Writing from the Surveyor General's Office, Ridley provides a detailed entomological and agricultural account of the elephant beetle (*Oryctes rhinoceros*) and the red weevil (*Rhynchophorus ferrugineus*), arguing that while absolute extermination is impossible, systematic regulatory and practical measures can reduce damage to negligible levels (pp. 1–11).

## Summary

Ridley distinguishes sharply between the two pest species in both their biology and their mode of attack. The elephant beetle is destructive in its adult form: it flies at night to the base of a leaf-stalk, burrows into the heart of the cabbage, and nibbles so deeply that it either severs the growing point or creates a wound through which rain enters and sets up fatal decay. A single attacked tree becomes a magnet for repeated visits, with Ridley recording two or three beetles removed daily from the same palm despite attempts to repel them with carbolic acid and salt (pp. 2–3). The grub of this species, by contrast, is harmless and even beneficial, feeding on rotting wood and vegetable refuse to convert it into soil. Ridley found as many as forty full-grown larvae in three feet of a rotten palm-stem, and notes that a single decaying coconut tree can breed over a thousand beetles before it is fully consumed (pp. 1–2).

The red weevil operates on the opposite principle: it is the grub, not the adult, that causes damage. The female uses a remarkably long ovipositor to deposit eggs deep into the soft pithy wood at the base of the cabbage, and the resulting footless, cylindrical larva burrows transversely through the growing tissue. Detection is far more difficult than with the elephant beetle, as the grub works entirely internally; the first visible sign is usually the withering and fall of the central shoot, or in severe cases the exudation of a shiny, sour-smelling liquid (pp. 5–6). Ridley refutes the popular belief that grubs breed in mangrove mud, noting that plantations near mangrove swamps in the interior are typically free from attack, and that the grubs are physiologically unsuited to that habitat (p. 2).

On the question of control, Ridley advocates a combination of practical and legislative measures. Practically, planters should employ men with barbed iron or copper wire spears to extract adult beetles from their burrows, plug holes with rag dipped in weak carbolic acid, and burn fallen leaves and husks in large night-fires to attract and kill the insects. Legislatively, he calls for compulsory burning of all dead coconut trees, prohibition on storing stems for use as bridges or posts, and regulation of tan-works and sugar-factories whose vegetable by-products provide breeding ground (pp. 4–7).

### Key Findings

- A single rotten coconut palm-stem can sustain the breeding of over 1,000 elephant beetles; the grubs also develop in manure pits, cow dung, tan bark, crushed sugar-cane, and leaf mould, making any accumulation of decaying vegetable matter a potential breeding site (pp. 1–2).
- The elephant beetle attacks a wide range of palm species in the Botanic Gardens—including *Cocos plumosa*, *Corypha gebanga*, *Phoenix dactylifera*, *Elœis guineensis*, and *Borassus flabelliformis*—but never the betel nut, gomuti, sago palm, or slender-stemmed palms; Ridley even observed one gnawing a cycad, evidently mistaking it for a palm (p. 2).
- Trees in the neighbourhood of towns are most vulnerable to attack, while small isolated patches around Malay villages in the interior are usually free from damage, even when situated near mangrove swamps (p. 2).
- The red weevil grub, when full grown, reaches a length of just over two inches and is a thick, fleshy, cylindrical, opaque white larva with no feet or antennæ; it moves by the aid of thickened segments and burrows transversely through the tree (p. 6).
- A considerable proportion of palms attacked by the red weevil recover, provided no grub bores through the growing point or sets up decay in the heart of the cabbage; only when the central bud is destroyed is the tree irrecoverably lost (p. 6).
- The practice of trimming and cleaning the fibrous sheath of young palms, recommended by some planters, is counterproductive: Ridley cites evidence from Ferguson's *All About the Coco-nut Palm* (Colombo) showing that on one property more than one-third of the original plants perished within ten years of trimming, at a rate of three trees weekly, until the practice was abandoned (p. 5).

### Conclusion

Ridley's definitive historical takeaway is that the beetle problem is fundamentally a problem of landscape management rather than of entomology. Because decaying vegetable matter is ubiquitous and will always sustain some beetle population, absolute eradication is impossible; however, if every coconut owner is compelled to burn dead trees immediately, if no one is permitted to store stems or accumulate refuse in proximity to estates, and if industrial by-products such as tan bark and sugar-cane waste are regulated, then the breeding base can be reduced so far that the damage becomes "infinitesimal" and the pest "almost eradicated" (pp. 7–8). The report thus functions as both a scientific monograph and a policy brief, urging the colonial administration to enact binding regulations on all landholders, however small their holdings.

## Context

- Ridley held the dual appointment of Director of Gardens and Forests for the Straits Settlements, placing him at the intersection of colonial botanical science and agricultural policy; the report was issued from the Surveyor General's Office, Singapore, and accompanied by plates of both beetle species (pp. 9–11).
- The report was published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 20, 1889), a venue that at this period served as a primary channel for disseminating practical entomological and agricultural knowledge to the planter class in the Straits Settlements and the wider Malay Archipelago.
