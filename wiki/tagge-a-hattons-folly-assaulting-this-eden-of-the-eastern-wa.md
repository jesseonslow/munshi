# Tagge, A. Hatton’s folly: assaulting “This Eden of the eastern wave” (Sir James Brooke describing Borneo, 1838

Anne Tagge's 1997 article reconstructs the circumstances surrounding the death of Frank Hatton, a twenty-year-old British mineral explorer killed in North Borneo on 1 March 1883, and argues that his story—along with the broader pattern of European imperial presumption in the Borneo region—supplied direct material for Joseph Conrad's Malayan novels. The piece situates Hatton's ambiguous death within the administrative and commercial framework of the British North Borneo Company and traces its thematic resonance through *Almayer's Folly*, *Lord Jim*, and *Heart of Darkness*.

## Summary

Tagge begins by establishing that Conrad, waiting in Singapore in March 1883 for the inquiry into the *Palestine* disaster, would have encountered news of Hatton's death and the subsequent publication of his diaries and memoir in 1885–86. The article then dissects the inquest testimony held at Elopura (Sandakan) on 4 March 1883, revealing significant discrepancies among the accounts of Hatton's native servants and his white companion, the Australian goldminer Andrew Beveridge. The official version—that Hatton's Winchester rifle caught in jungle creepers and discharged accidentally while he pursued a wounded elephant at dusk—collides with details such as the nearest vine being four feet from where Hatton lay, the absence of any evidence the gun was cocked, and the fact that the natives' own guns had been unloaded after the incident, leaving no way to verify whether one of them fired. Tagge demonstrates that Hatton was obsessively determined to kill the elephant (his last inland trip, and a chance to become the first white hunter to do so in North Borneo), while his servants repeatedly urged him to turn back as darkness fell.

The article then pivots to Conrad, arguing that Hatton's character—tall, immaculately dressed, intellectually gifted, and possessed of an unshakeable belief in his own mission—maps directly onto Lord Jim and other Conrad figures. Tagge draws on Herbert Ward's visit to the Hatton family in London, where Ward's description of Frank (his height, his white attire, his "commanding way," his quiet acceptance of hardship) closely parallels Conrad's opening portrait of Jim. The Company's handling of the death is examined critically: the diaries were returned only partially and much later than other effects, suggesting careful inspection, and the Company's public narrative of a heroic accident served its institutional interests. Tagge connects this to Conrad's own experience of the Company's obscuring of detail in the Congo, as dramatized in *Heart of Darkness* and *An Outpost of Progress*.

The broader argument extends to the commercial and political context of European Borneo in the 1880s. Hatton had discovered coal, copper, antimony, and petroleum deposits, and was close to gold in the Segama district, yet the Company assumed proprietary rights over all minerals without consulting local chiefs. The article places this within the wider pattern of Anglo-Dutch rivalry over the Borneo boundary, the Company's expansionist annexations, and the recurring deaths of European employees (the Witti murder, the killing of Dr. Fraser in 1885) that the Company consistently attributed to accident or uncontrolled tribes. Hatton's "folly," Tagge concludes, is not merely the proximate error of pursuing an elephant at dusk but the central folly of imperialism itself: the assumption that European goals are supremely important and that native peoples exist to serve them.

### Key Findings

- Hatton arrived in North Borneo in October 1881 at age twenty, recruited by the British North Borneo Company as a mineral explorer and metallurgical chemist; he died on 1 March 1883, and the inquest was held on 4 March 1883 at Elopura (p. 112–113).
- At the time of the fatal shot, seven natives had accompanied Hatton after the elephant and five were within 600 feet of him; the Winchester, normally loaded with fourteen cartridges, contained seven, including one in the discharging chamber (p. 114).
- Hatton had sighted elephant dung on 17 February 1883 and heard an elephant on 18 February; his diary records that he intended to cut the wages of Durahim and Murrim for capsizing their boat on 12 February, a grievance that may have contributed to the tensions at the time of his death (pp. 114–115).
- The British Borneo Gold Mining Company was floated in London with a capital of £100,000; the Company imposed a penalty of £80 for landing "diggers without means" to guard against a mining rush from Australia and California (p. 121).
- The Anglo-Dutch boundary in North Borneo formally ran through Sebatic Island just north of 4 degrees latitude; in 1888 the territory was made a British Protectorate, the "State of North Borneo" (p. 121).
- A 367-carat diamond was found in Sarawak, and the famous Star of Sarawak was eventually sold by the Ranee Margaret to finance her sons' education; the similarity of local clay to Kimberley blue had suggested the possibility of a combined gold-and-diamond find (p. 121).

### Conclusion

Tagge's definitive takeaway is that Hatton's death, like the fates of Almayer, Jim, and Lingard, cannot be reduced to a single proximate error of judgment. The true "folly" is the imperial premise itself: that white men in the tropics take their own commercial, scientific, and political goals as supremely important and attempt to bend native peoples to those purposes, regardless of whether those peoples wish to be directed. Hatton, softened by his youth and charm, embodies this same presumption in miniature—his irritation at Dusun food, his contempt for headhunting as "head stealing," his assumption that his men should share his obsession with the elephant—and Conrad, drawing on the Hatton diaries and the broader Borneo experience, transformed that presumption into the central moral architecture of his Malayan fiction.

## Context

- The article draws on the Sabah State Archives (File M1182 for Hatton's diary; File M11682 for North Borneo Company correspondence; the *North Borneo Herald* of 1 September 1883) and on the published Hatton memoir and diaries of 1885–86 (pp. 123).
- Historiographically, the piece contributes to Conrad studies by identifying a specific, documented Borneo figure whose biography and posthumous publication intersect with Conrad's known reading and presence in the region, and to Borneo colonial history by foregrounding the Company's institutional management of employee deaths and the erasure of native agency in official narratives.
