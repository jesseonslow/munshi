# Imperial cosmopolitan Malaya: a study of Realist fiction in the Straits Chinese Magazine

Nathan Porath's 2011 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 84, Part 2) reinterprets the *Hikayat Patani*—the principal literary source for the early history of the Patani sultanate—through the lens of René Girard's mimetic theory and Stephen Harrison's concept of "fracturing resemblances." Set against the political world of pre-modern Southeast Asian kingdom hierarchies, Porath argues that the text constructs Patani's identity by affirming mimetic similarity with Ayutthaya even as it narrates rebellion against that kingdom, while simultaneously denying similarity with Johor at every narrative juncture.

## Summary

Porath treats the *Hikayat Patani* not merely as a historical chronicle but as a symbolically political-cultural text whose underlying teleology is to supply a charter for the viability of the Patani *kerajaan*. The manuscript, written in 1839 and first copied and translated by Teeuw and Wyatt, comprises six books composed at different periods from the late seventeenth to the mid-eighteenth century—the era of the kingdom's decline under Kelantanese dynasties. Porath draws on Bradley's recent revision of the text's composition history and its oral origins to argue that the first book, in particular, is structured as authoritative mimetic speech in which explanatory "facts" are placed in the mouths of royal protagonists themselves (pp. 46–47).

The article's central analytical move is to read the text's two dominant relationships—Patani's with Ayutthaya to the north and its with Johor to the south—as two distinct mimetic processes. With Ayutthaya, the text affirms similarity: the founding king bears Thai titles, the sultan's attack on the Ayutthaya palace is framed as an attempted usurpation within Indic succession norms rather than a secessionist act, and even the legend of Patani's victory over the Siamese army relies on the premise that the two peoples are so alike they cannot be told apart. With Johor, the text fractures resemblance: the Johor raja is deflated in the Patani court, his entourage is portrayed as untrustworthy, and the final embassy scene explicitly raises the question of Malay identity only to assert difference in customs and rules (pp. 47–54). Porath contends that this structural contrast reveals an unstated recognition that Patani derived its prestige and "elitist confidence" in relation to other Malay-speaking states precisely from its mimetic bond with Ayutthaya (p. 48).

The conversion narrative is read as a second social contract, following Jordaan and Josselin de Jong's interpretation of skin-disease legends as metaphors for disrupted ruler-realm relationships. The king's cracked skin symbolizes political disunity; his cure is conditional on conversion to Islam, making the kingdom's health dependent on its Islamic character. Yet the text notes that the converted king "did not alter a single one of his heathen habits," suggesting that the Indic political-cultural framework persisted alongside the new Islamic identity (p. 50).

### Key Findings

- The *Hikayat Patani* manuscript was composed in 1839 and comprises six books written at different periods between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries; the first book was originally oral in composition (pp. 46–47).
- Sultan Mudhaffar Syah's attack on Ayutthaya (c. 1560s) is confirmed by both the *Luang Prasoert* chronicle and van Vliet's account; the sultan is described as riding one of King Cakkraphat's white elephants in the palace plaza, symbolically appropriating Ayutthaya's cosmic power (pp. 55–56).
- The phrase "dari pada sebuah negeri baiklah dua" ("rather than one country, two is better"), spoken by Sultan Mudhaffar Syah before his visit to Ayutthaya, is interpreted by Porath as an indirect reference to the Burmese-Ayutthaya war (Bayinnaung's march in 1563; fall of Ayutthaya in 1568) rather than a statement of Patani's desire for independence (pp. 56–58).
- The second rebellion, instigated under Queen Raja Ungu (who rejected her Thai title and assumed the Islamic title Paduka Syah Alam), was resolved in 1636; King Prasat Thong mobilized an army of 60,000 men for the campaign against Patani (pp. 60–61).
- Bradley's revision dates the Kelantanese deposition of Raja Kuning to 1649 and the installation of a Kelantanese prince to 1651, with Ayutthayan support—earlier than Teeuw and Wyatt's estimate of c. 1688 (p. 52).
- The Javanese poem *Desawarnana* (completed 1365) mentions Langkasuka and Sai as separate political entities but does not name Patani, suggesting the kingdom may not yet have existed as a distinct political entity at that date (p. 51).

### Conclusion

Porath's definitive takeaway is that the *Hikayat Patani* should not be read through a modernist ethnicist lens that would interpret Patani's rebellions as proto-nationalist secessionist acts. Instead, the text reveals that Patani's kingdomhood was constituted within the Indic mandala system shared with Ayutthaya—a shared cosmos inherited from its Buddhist past—and that one precondition for the viability of the Patani *kerajaan* was the maintenance of hierarchical order with that northern kingdom. The text's refusal to moralize against Ayutthaya, even after decades of warfare, and its deliberate fracturing of resemblance with Johor, together demonstrate that Patani's political identity was defined less by ethnic affinity with other Malay states than by its position within a fluctuating Indic-Kingdom hierarchical order (pp. 62–63).

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Teeuw and Wyatt (1970) edition of the *Hikayat Patani*, the Thai-language version edited by Baker et al. (2005) from van Vliet's sources, the *Luang Prasoert* chronicle (Cushman 2006), and Bradley's (2009) recent historical contextualization.
- Historiographically, the article extends Bougas's (1994) framework of Patani as caught between two mandala systems by arguing that Patani never perceived itself as subordinate to Johor and that its relationship with Ayutthaya was one of affirmed mimetic similarity rather than simple tributary subordination.
