# The kings of 14th century Singapore

W. Linehan's 1947 article reconstructs the chronology of the five rulers of 14th-century Singapore by working backward from the Ming-dynasty date of 1413 for the death of the first Malacca king, and argues that while the Malay Annals' sequence of reigns is broadly reliable, the text deliberately suppresses the fact that the last Singapore ruler was a usurper of Javanese origin rather than a legitimate heir of the Singapore royal line.

## Summary

Linehan's central task is to assess the value of the Malay Annals' account of the 14th-century Singapore kings, a passage that earlier authorities Wilkinson and Winstedt had dismissed as unreliable. Using the Raffles MS No. 184 text (held in the Royal Asiatic Society, London) as his primary Malay source, Linehan extracts the stated reign lengths for five successive rulers and works backward from the externally fixed date of 1413, arriving at a chronology that places Singapore's founding at 1299 and its final abandonment at 1391. He then tests this chronology against Chinese, Javanese, Siamese, and Portuguese records, finding substantial corroboration for the first three rulers and a more complex picture for the last two.

The most significant argument concerns the fourth and fifth rulers. Linehan demonstrates that the fourth magnate, Paduka Sri Maharaja, was never acknowledged as king but held Singapore as a chief under Siamese protection following the great Javanese sack of approximately 1376. The fifth ruler, Sri Sultan Iskandar Shah (identified with Parameswara in Chinese and Portuguese records), was an exiled prince of Javanese-Palembang origin who murdered his host and usurped the chieftaincy. The Malay Annals, composed under the patronage of the Malacca kings who were direct descendants of this usurper, altered the record to present him as a legitimate heir of the Singapore royal line. Linehan further argues that the Annals' attribution of Singapore's final fall to a Javanese attack is a confusion; the practical unanimity of the Portuguese sources (d'Albuquerque, de Barros, de Couto, d'Eredia) indicates that Siamese or Siamese-controlled forces were responsible for the last ruler's expulsion in 1391.

### Key Findings

- Working backward from 1413 A.D. (Ming chronicle date for the death of the first Malacca king), the reconstructed chronology places Singapore's founding at 1299 and its destruction at 1391, with Malacca founded in 1393 (p. 120).
- The *Nagarakrtagama* (1365) lists Temasek among territories subject to Majapahit, independently confirming the Annals' account of a Javanese attack during the second ruler's reign (1347–1362) (p. 121).
- The Ming History records a king *Tan-ma-sa-na-ho* as one of three rulers of San Fo-ts'i (Sri Vijaya) in 1373, dying in 1376 and succeeded by *Maharaja Wuli*, who required Chinese imperial authorization to ascend the throne—corroborating the Annals' date of 1375 for the death of the third ruler (p. 121).
- The *Pararaton* records that in 1388 the Majapahit minister Gajah Mada swore to conquer ten countries including Temasek, placing the great Javanese sack at the end of the third ruler's reign (p. 120).
- All Portuguese sources except de Couto agree that the last magnate of Singapore was expelled by Siamese or Siamese-controlled forces (Patani or Pahang), not by the Javanese as the Malay Annals claim (pp. 122–124).
- The fourth ruler's Chinese name *Maharaja Wuli* is conjectured to be a rendering of *Raja Muda* (heir-apparent), and his Portuguese equivalents (*Tamagi*, *Xabandar*, *Sangesinga*) confirm his subordinate status as a chief rather than a king (pp. 121, 124, 127).

### Conclusion

Linehan concludes that the Malay Annals' chronology of the 14th-century Singapore kings is substantially correct in its sequence and approximate dates, but that the text was deliberately altered by the Malacca royal house to conceal the usurpation by their ancestor. The final abandonment of Singapore in 1391 was caused by Siamese hostility, not Javanese aggression, and the fourth ruler's subordination to Siam after the 1376 sack represents a critical but underappreciated phase in the island's history.

## Context

- Primary source: Raffles MS No. 184 (Malay Annals), Royal Asiatic Society, London, identified by Linehan as one of the earliest and most authentic versions of the Annals yet discovered (p. 117).
- Historiographical contribution: The article provides the first systematic cross-referencing of the Annals' Singapore chronology against Chinese (Ming History, Wang Ta-Yuan), Javanese (*Pararaton*, *Nagarakrtagama*), Siamese (*Kot Monthieraban*), and Portuguese (d'Albuquerque, de Barros, de Couto, d'Eredia) sources, establishing a methodological template for evaluating Malay dynastic narratives against external evidence.
