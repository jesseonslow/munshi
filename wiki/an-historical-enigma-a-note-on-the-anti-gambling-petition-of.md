# An historical enigma: a note on the anti-gambling petition of 1905

I must flag a significant discrepancy: the publication metadata identifies this as J.G. Butcher's 1983 article "An historical enigma: a note on the anti-gambling petition of 1905" (JMBRAS Vol. 56, pp. 1–9), but the primary source text actually provided is the **Annual Report of the Straits Branch, Royal Asiatic Society for 1920**, authored by J. C. Moulton (Hon. Secretary) and V. Knight, published in 1921. I will summarise the document as it actually appears in the text.

## Summary

The 1920 Annual Report of the Straits Branch, Royal Asiatic Society provides a comprehensive administrative and financial account of the Society's activities during a period of post-war adjustment. The report addresses membership fluctuations, the Society's publishing output, financial position, and strategic decisions regarding its library and exchange arrangements. It is framed by the broader context of the immediate post-World War I period, when many scientific and learned societies across the British Empire were grappling with rising costs, diminished membership engagement, and the need to justify their continued existence.

The Council's narrative is candid about internal challenges. Membership had been artificially inflated by lax enforcement of subscription rules, and a purge of 29 names (19 under Rule 6 for non-payment) brought the roll to an honest 329 active members. The report acknowledges that only 3–4 per cent of members contributed papers annually, and that the Journal's growing proportion of technical scientific content risked alienating the broader membership base. The Council explicitly calls on members to supply material on travel, local customs, and natural history to rebalance the Journal's character.

On the financial side, the report presents a balanced account showing a modest surplus, with funds distributed across fixed deposits, war loans, and current accounts. The Council notes that printing costs had risen sharply—projecting a 120 per cent increase over pre-war levels—and that the forthcoming 600-page Flora of Borneo by Dr. E. D. Merrill would absorb a large proportion of the Society's balance. Despite these pressures, the Council expressed confidence that maintaining 300 pages of Journal output per annum remained feasible so long as membership stayed above 300 subscribing members.

### Key Findings

- Active membership stood at 329 after the removal of 29 members (19 for non-payment under Rule 6, some owing as much as seven years' subscriptions); the Council noted that 83 per cent of members resided in Malaya but only 3–4 per cent contributed papers (pp. viii–ix).
- Two Journals (Nos. 81 and 82) were issued in 1920, totalling 226 pages, compared with 168 in 1919 and 192 in 1918, against a 40-year average of 306 pages per annum (p. ix).
- The 1920 financial balance carried forward was $8,309.27, up from $7,142.89 at the end of 1919; this comprised $2,500 in Victory Loan, $2,200 in S.S. War Loan, $2,000 fixed deposit, and $1,609.27 in current accounts (pp. ix–x).
- Subscriptions received totalled $1,670 for 1920, a satisfactory increase over the five-year average of $1,127, partly due to $435 in arrears collected for 1915–1919 and five life membership compounding payments of $250 (p. ix).
- Printing costs for the two 1920 Journals amounted to $1,153; the Council projected that 1921 Journal prices would be approximately 120 per cent higher than the last pre-war Journal due to rising paper and printing costs (p. x).
- The Council offered portions of the Society's library on indefinite loan to the Botanic Gardens, the F.M.S. Geological Department, and the Raffles Museum to make the collection more accessible to members residing outside Singapore (p. x).

### Conclusion

The report presents a Society in a state of cautious stability: financially solvent but facing structurally rising costs, with a membership base that was numerically adequate yet intellectually under-contributing. The Council's central argument is that the Society's viability depends not on its endowments or its patronage (Sir Laurence Guillemard having succeeded Sir Arthur Young as Patron) but on the active participation of its members in supplying publishable material. The tone is one of institutional self-correction—acknowledging past laxity in membership management and the Journal's drift toward technical content—while expressing measured optimism that the post-war period would see a recovery in both output and engagement.

## Context

- The report was authored by J. C. Moulton, Hon. Secretary, and V. Knight, and reflects the administrative conventions of a colonial-era learned society operating in Singapore under the Straits Settlements.
- The document is a standard institutional annual report; its historiographical value lies primarily as a source for the operational history of the Straits Branch RAS and for understanding the economic and social conditions affecting learned societies in the immediate post-war period in the Straits Settlements.
