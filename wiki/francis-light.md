# Francis Light

Francis Light (c. 1745–1794) was a British merchant and East India Company officer who established the settlement of Penang in 1786, founding what would become one of the most commercially significant ports in the Straits of Malacca. His career spanned the northern Malay Peninsula and the western coast of the Thai-Malay world, encompassing trade in tin, opium, rice, and piece goods between Kedah, Thalang (modern Phuket), Junk Ceylon (modern Penang), and the broader maritime networks of the Bay of Bengal and South China Sea. Light's enterprise was motivated by personal commercial interest as much as by imperial strategy, and his legacy is contested: to some he was a man of probity and singular purpose who built a colony of enduring value; to others, a private trader who exploited the East India Company's institutional weight to protect his own mercantile operations [[1, p. 1]](#ref-1), [[2, p. 135]](#ref-2).

### Early Career and the Pursuit of a Settlement

Light was born at Dallington (or Dallingho) in Suffolk, and came to the East at an early age in the Marine Service of the East India Company [[1, p. 1]](#ref-1). The first record of him dates to 1771, when he entered into correspondence with Warren Hastings, Governor-General of India, regarding the desirability of a repairing harbour in the Straits, recommending Penang as a "convenient magazine for the Eastern trade" [[1, p. 1]](#ref-1). In 1779, the Danish botanist Dr. Koenig met Light trading at Junk Ceylon in May and at Malacca in November, describing him in his journal as an enlightened and sympathetic companion [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1).

The initial scheme, matured in 1780–81, was to settle Junk Ceylon through private subscription with the consent of the Governor-General in Council. The wars with France and the Dutch in 1781–83 delayed its execution, and shortly after Light decided on the superior merits of Penang harbour [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1). When the friendly ruler of Junk Ceylon died in December 1785, it was finally resolved by the Governor-General to make the experiment at Penang alone, which the young Raja of Kedah had offered to cede for $6,000 a year [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1).

However, R. Bonney's 1965 re-examination of the correspondence in the British Museum and Fort William Secret State Records presents a markedly different picture of Light's motivations. According to Bonney, every proposal Light put forward—Kuala Kedah, Junk Ceylon, Mergui, and finally Penang—was in an area where he already held significant trading connections and stood to profit from a Company presence that would deter Dutch competition [[2, p. 135]](#ref-2). His letters to his Madras firm and to Warren Hastings in 1771–72 contained deliberate misrepresentations of trading volumes, harbour depths, and the Sultan's motives, designed to drag reluctant employers into a commitment they would otherwise have refused [[2, p. 136]](#ref-2). In his 1771 letters, Light valued Kedah's trade at 150,000 Spanish Dollars and claimed opium sold at 800 Spanish Dollars per chest; Monckton's subsequent report to the Madras Council noted the Sultan "never traded in his life for upwards of Ten Thousand Dollars except in Elephants" and that opium had never sold for more than 300 [[2, pp. 136–137]](#ref-2). Light described the Kedah river entrance as "capable of receiving the largest Ships at half Flood" with no bar; Monckton reported "not being above One Fathom Water in some parts and not 14 as Mr. Light remarks" [[2, p. 138]](#ref-2).

Russell Jones's 1981 publication of two previously uncatalogued Malay letters from Sultan Muhammad Jiwa Muazzam Shah of Kedah to Light adds further nuance to this early period. After the failure of the proposed Kedah–East India Company alliance negotiated through Edward Monckton in 1772, Light relocated to Ujung Salang, an island under Thai suzerainty, where he established himself as a merchant with the backing of the Siamese authorities [[3, p. 24]](#ref-3). The letters reveal that the Sultan maintained an affectionate, almost paternal correspondence with Light, addressing him as "our son," while simultaneously expressing sharp displeasure with Monckton [[3, p. 25]](#ref-3). The commercial content of the longer letter concerns a cargo of *embalau* (lac, *Laccifer lacca*), *kesumba* (safflower, *Carthamus tinctorius*), and *timah* (tin) sent from Ujung Salang to Kedah, for which the Sultan could find no buyers at the quoted prices [[3, pp. 25]](#ref-3). Jones also demonstrates that the Malay title *Dewa Raja* applied to Light was simply a translation of the Thai honourific *P'ya Raja Capitan* conferred upon him by the Siamese court, not a separate Kedah honour [[3, p. 27]](#ref-3).

### The Founding of Penang, 1786

In June 1786, Light left Calcutta with Sir James Macpherson's authority to act. He was given 100 Native "new-raised Marines" and 30 Native Lascars, as well as 15 European Artillerymen and 5 Officers [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1). He first proceeded to Kedah, where he completed his negotiations and provisioned his party. Sailing thence with three vessels on the evening of 14 July 1786, he anchored off Pulau Tikus the following day [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1). On 17 July he disembarked Lieutenant Gray with the Marines upon Point Penagger—a low sandy point covered with wood—and employed them in clearing the ground, which is the present Esplanade [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). Formal possession of the island was taken on 11 August 1786, when the flag was hoisted and a Royal salute fired [[1, p. 4]](#ref-1). The following day being the birthday of the Prince of Wales, Light named the island in his honour, though this name has been unable to compete with the shorter one of native origin [[1, p. 4]](#ref-1).

The place was practically unoccupied; the only Malay inhabitants encountered were 52 who came over, apparently from near Tanjong Tokong, to help in felling the forest [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). With these exceptions, the whole place was one great jungle. Wells were dug which yielded water fit to drink but stained red by the roots of the *penaga* tree. Huts were run up for the marines and lascars, and a stockade was erected as the precursor of Fort Cornwallis [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1).

Bonney's account, however, casts a darker light on the legality of this occupation. Sultan Abdullah Marhum Shah, facing the prospect of Burmese invasion of Siam and the attendant demands for tributary service, was willing to lease Penang only on the condition that the Company guarantee military protection and pay fixed compensation [[2, p. 143]](#ref-2). Light, appointed the Sultan's *wakil* (representative) to Calcutta, presented these conditions to the Bengal Government as minor restrictions, downplayed the protection clause, reduced the compensation figure from 30,000 Spanish Dollars per annum to 10,000, and critically omitted any reference to the impending Burmo-Siamese war that was the true motivation behind the Sultan's offer [[2, pp. 143]](#ref-2). When the Bengal Government accepted the island on 2 March 1786 and appointed Light Superintendent, he returned to Kedah and, sensing the Sultan's reluctance to permit occupation without firm guarantees, took formal possession on 11 August 1786 without the Sultan's consent, thereby committing the Company to a settlement it had no legal right to hold [[2, pp. 149–150]](#ref-2).

### Governance, Commerce, and the Kedah Crisis

The task of governing the growing settlement fell entirely on Light's shoulders, for he received but little encouragement from the Indian Government, who long regarded the establishment at Penang with doubts and even with jealousy [[1, p. 5]](#ref-1). The sole tribunal up to the beginning of the nineteenth century was an informal kind of Court Martial composed of Officers and respectable inhabitants; all minor offences and petty disputes were adjudicated by the "Captains" or headmen of the various nationalities inhabiting the island [[1, p. 5]](#ref-1). Light continually urged his Government to provide proper Courts endowed with full authority, and in a despatch of 25 January 1794 he argued that "a regular form for administering justice is necessary, both for the peace and welfare of the society and for the honour of the nation who have granted them protection" [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1).

The settlement prospered notwithstanding the lack of institutional support. The number of settlers was stated at "about 10,000" by the end of 1789, and at over 20,000 in 1795, of whom the Chinese were not much over 3,000 [[1, p. 4]](#ref-1). In 1789, Light went to Calcutta and was closely questioned by the Government as to the capabilities of Penang. He concluded a despatch with an optimistic summary: a harbour with good anchorage; an island well watered, of excellent soil, capable of sustaining 50,000 people; a port favourable to commerce with imports amounting to upwards of $600,000 per annum; a place of refuge for merchant ships; and an emporium centrally situated where the merchants of all nations may conveniently meet [[1, p. 6]](#ref-1).

In 1790, Light began to find that the duties of Superintendent were incompatible with his position as a merchant, and proposed to the authorities in Calcutta that he should be precluded from engaging in trade, receiving "such increase of salary as will support the office with decency and enable me to make a small provision for approaching old age" [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). Skinner notes that few of his acts reveal an honourable and upright character more clearly than this, given that his combined position gave him abundant opportunity of enriching himself [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). By 1795, however, Governor Kyd reported that the Light–Scott trading partnership had become "so great a bar to all free enterprise that no commercial house or merchant of any credit ever attempted to form an establishment" in Penang [4, p. 149, n. 44].

The 1791 Kedah crisis tested Light's military judgment. The Raja of Kedah, grown jealous of the prosperous settlement, collected a force and instigated a fleet of twenty Lanoon boats to enter Prai River, joined by the Kedah Bandahara. A land force also came down to the banks and threw up entrenchments. Light's force numbered 400 men, all well armed and disciplined. He took the initiative and attacked by land and sea the force at Kuala Prai, which had swelled to over 8,000 Malays. After a few hours' fighting the enemy were dispersed [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). Bonney characterises this as a surprise night attack launched after Light interpreted a final diplomatic letter from Kedah as a declaration of war, after which Sultan Abdullah was compelled to recognise British tenure [[2, p. 152]](#ref-2).

Light's 1788 letter to Lord Cornwallis, published by Wurtzburg in 1938, provides the most detailed contemporary account of the commercial landscape he sought to exploit. He described how seventy Bugis prows (15–25 tons, crewed by 40–50 men each, valued at 20,000 Spanish Dollars apiece) formerly visited the Straits of Malacca, returning with cargoes of opium and piece goods worth 1.5 million Dollars [[4, p. 120]](#ref-4). Chinese merchants imported annually to the value of 250,000 Dollars and returned with double that value in tin, pepper, rattans, and other articles [[4, p. 120]](#ref-4). Javanese shipping of approximately 4,000 tons carried rice, sugar, arrack, tobacco, salt, sago, brass artillery, and cloth, with cargoes valued at about 250,000 Dollars [[4, p. 120]](#ref-4). Light argued that the Dutch monopoly policy had systematically destroyed the Bugis and Malay maritime networks that once sustained the commerce of the entire Bay of Bengal region, and proposed a bilateral treaty to restore free navigation [[4, pp. 116–120]](#ref-4).

### Personal Life and the Thalang Connection

E.H.S. Simmonds's 1965 study, drawing on fifty-nine Siamese-language letters from Thalang held in the Marsden Collection at SOAS, reveals a far more complex personal life than the standard Penang-founding narrative suggests. Light's relationship with Thalang was one of patronage, trade credit, and romantic attachment that ultimately constrained his ability to return to the island after 1785 [[5, p. 213]](#ref-5). The article examines six women who wrote to Light: Martina Rozells, his long-term partner; Lady Chan, widow of the governor Phya Phimon; her daughter Mae Prang; Chao Rat, with whom Light appears to have had a romantic relationship; and two others, Bun Kaeo and Chao Bun Chan [[5, p. 214]](#ref-5).

Light's house on Thalang was plundered by the Chao Phya following a dispute over 1,700 bags of rice sold to islanders payable in tin; Light's agent died in July 1780 with 700 *pecul* of tin still outstanding [[5, p. 215]](#ref-5). Lady Chan's four letters to Light span December 1785 to December 1787; in her July 1787 letter she requests fifty Sutan muskets, sandalwood oil, rose-water, and goods worth 30–40 *bahar*, promising "all the Thalang tin shall be at your disposal" in return [[5, p. 220]](#ref-5). Chao Rat's letter of 20 April 1787 describes her as "like a nestling or a tiny fawn bereft of its mother" during Light's absence [[5, pp. 222–223]](#ref-5).

Simmonds also documents the practice of bond service—wives and children pledged against debts to European traders—which Light himself participated in. A bond contract of 21 June 1779 records Nai Ru'ang pledging his wife Amdaeng Bun to the ship's captain Limao for six *phaen phukan* of silver, with a clause that if she ran away or died the captain would have a claim on the husband himself [[5, p. 224]](#ref-5). Light's will of 20 October 1794 frees at least nine bonded persons, all bearing Thai names with the slave prefixes *Ai* (male) and *I* (female) [[5, p. 225]](#ref-5).

Simmonds, following A. Francis Steuart, argues that the "Nonya" who mediated the earliest Kedah negotiations for Penang was almost certainly Martina Rozells herself—a woman of Portuguese-Malay origin with family connections to both Kedah and Thalang—rather than a princess, and that the romanticised "dowry" story popularised by Elisha Trapaud in 1788 was a later embellishment [[5, p. 214]](#ref-5). Light certainly allied himself in 1772 with Martina Rozells, who was neither a Malay nor a Princess, but was apparently a Portuguese Christian of the Roman Catholic Mission at Kedah or Junk Ceylon. She lived with him to his death and inherited his house "Suffolk" and other property. She bore him five children, one of whom—Colonel William Light, born 1784, died 1839—followed in his father's steps as the "Founder of Adelaide" and Surveyor-General of South Australia [[1, pp. 12–13]](#ref-1).

Light died at Penang on 21 October 1794, probably of a fever similar to the severe one recorded in his journal in February 1787 [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1). His grave at St. George's Church bears an inscription by the contemporary Penang resident Robert Scott, who described him as one "who watched over their interests and cares as a father" [[1, p. 17]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The historiography of Francis Light has shifted considerably across the Society's published literature. A.M. Skinner's 1895 memoir, written for the centenary of Light's death, established the foundational narrative: Light as a man of probity, practical judgment, and singular purpose whose pioneer enterprise established a colony of enduring commercial and strategic value [[1, p. 1]](#ref-1). Skinner drew primarily on Light's diary, his despatches to Cornwallis and Macpherson, the 1805 Court of Directors' despatch, and Colonel Low's 1848 chronicle, and his historiographical contribution lay in his early use of the 1787 Cornwallis despatch to settle the Kedah sovereignty question in Light's favour [[1, pp. 9–12]](#ref-1).

C.E. Wurtzburg's 1938 publication of Light's 1788 letter to Cornwallis provided the first substantial primary-source document of Light's commercial thinking, presented without editorial commentary to preserve the original spelling and orthography [[4, p. 115]](#ref-4). Wurtzburg's 1948 note, though fragmentary in the surviving record, appears to have extended his interest in Light's personal connections, touching on Portuguese figures in the region [[6, p. 116]](#ref-6).

The most significant revision came with R. Bonney's 1965 article, which drew on previously underutilised correspondence in the British Museum and Fort William Secret State Records to argue that Light functioned as a private trader who sought to leverage the Company's institutional weight to protect and expand his own mercantile operations [[2, p. 135]](#ref-2). Bonney's piece corrected the long-standing attribution of blame for the 1772 Monckton mission failure and challenged the post-1821 "paper war" consensus that had cast Light as a practical man hamstrung by Company cowardice and Sultan duplicity [[2, p. 135]](#ref-2).

E.H.S. Simmonds's 1965 study of the Thalang letters extended the investigation into Light's personal and domestic sphere, revealing a web of patronage, trade credit, and romantic attachment that the standard Penang-founding narrative had obscured [[5, p. 213]](#ref-5). Russell Jones's 1981 publication of two Malay letters from the Sultan of Kedah complemented the English-language sources by providing direct epistolary evidence of the Sultan's perspective on Light and Monckton [[3, p. 24]](#ref-3).

More recently, H.S. Barlow's 2000 note on a George III silver punch bowl bearing the initials "IS" and a Malay inscription with Penang-specific dialect features added a material-culture dimension to the record, suggesting the bowl was a gift from James Scott to Light commemorating their friendship, which began aboard HMS *Argonaut* in 1761 [[7, pp. 117–120]](#ref-7). A further note by Barlow and A.T. Gallop in 2024 indicates that this line of inquiry remains active [[8]](#ref-8).
