# Malacca under Jan van Riebeeck

W. Ph. Coolhaas, in a 1965 article for the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, examines the brief and troubled tenure of Jan van Riebeeck as Commandeur of Malacca (1662–1665), arguing that this period—overshadowed by Riebeeck's more famous Cape of Good Hope administration—reveals the economic and diplomatic challenges of maintaining a strategically vital but financially unprofitable VOC outpost in the Straits of Malacca.

## Summary

The article situates Riebeeck's Malacca appointment within the broader arc of his career. A trained surgeon and ambitious administrator, Riebeeck had just departed the Cape, where his establishment had been a financial failure in the eyes of the Heren XVII. He was appointed Commandeur rather than Governor because Malacca was a loss-making operation that did not warrant the higher salary attached to the governorship. His three-year tenure was marked by personal tragedy—the death of his wife Maria Quevellerius in 1664, leaving him with four young daughters—and by professional frustration, as his repeated petitions for promotion to Councillor of India went unanswered throughout his career (pp. 173–174).

The core of the article is an economic and diplomatic portrait of Malacca under Dutch rule in the 1660s. Coolhaas draws on the *Dagh-Register van Batavia*, the *Generale Missiven*, and the *Corpus Diplomaticum Neerlandico-Indicum* to reconstruct the town's commercial life: its dependence on the Japan trade (copper, silver, lacquer), the tin trade with Perak and Ligor, the pepper trade via Indragiri, and the vexing problem of Muslim merchant competition from Bengal and Coromandel. The article also covers the diplomatic entanglements with Atjeh, Perak, Kedah, Johore, and Siam, showing how the VOC's blockading strategy and tin monopoly attempts repeatedly failed or produced only partial success. The Perak tin monopoly, for instance, collapsed into a system of unenforced deductions and smuggling, while the Kedah blockade proved ineffective against small vessels navigating the many estuaries (pp. 175–179).

A significant portion of the article is devoted to the financial question of whether Malacca could ever be made self-sufficient. Riebeeck's proposal to reduce the fortress to a pentagon, saving garrison costs, was ultimately rejected by the Heren XVII, partly because the financial position was improving through increased textile sales and tolls. The article thus presents Malacca as a case study in the tension between strategic necessity and commercial viability within the VOC's Asian network (pp. 180–181).

### Key Findings

- Malacca's population in 1667 numbered approximately 7,560; at Riebeeck's departure there were only 21 Dutch burghers, 200 soldiers (40 above private rank), and 167 other Company servants (p. 180).
- The fortress reduction plan, estimated at 200,000 guilders, would have allowed a garrison reduction of 150 men and a yearly saving of 30,000 guilders; the Heren XVII rejected the plan (p. 181).
- Financial results show a trajectory from deficit to near-balance: 1662 expenditure of 228,806 guilders against receipts of 112,192; by 1665, receipts (234,050) marginally exceeded expenditure (230,423), with tolls contributing 62,502 guilders (p. 181).
- The Perak tin trade yielded approximately 738 bahar transported to Malacca in 1663, but by 1665 no increase was expected as two-thirds of Perak's population had disappeared (p. 177).
- Japan-bound trade through Malacca involved 4 ships annually carrying cargoes valued between 1,193,654 and 1,430,675 guilders, while Malacca departures ranged from 6 to 10 ships with cargoes of 1,171,967 to 1,738,819 guilders (p. 176).
- When Riebeeck departed in October 1665, he handed over Company assets valued at 1,159,807 guilders to his successor, Balthasar Bort (p. 182).

### Conclusion

Coolhaas's definitive takeaway is that Malacca, despite its strategic importance as a chokepoint in the Straits, remained fundamentally an economic liability for the VOC throughout Riebeeck's tenure. The town's commercial revival was real but modest, driven primarily by the Japan trade and tolls rather than local production, and the fortress's military costs perpetually outstripped its revenue. Riebeeck's personal disappointment—never achieving the governor's rank he believed he deserved—mirrors the broader institutional reality that Malacca was a place the Company maintained out of strategic necessity rather than commercial logic.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: *Dagh-Register van Batavia* (ed. van der Chijs), *Generale Missiven van Gouverneurs-Generaal en Raden aan Heren XVII* (ed. Coolhaas, vol. III, 1655–1674, unpublished at the time of writing), Pieter van Dam's *Beschrijvinge van de Oostindische Compagnie* (ed. Stapel), and the *Corpus Diplomaticum Neerlandico-Indicum* (ed. Heeres, vol. II, 1650–1675).
- Historiographical contribution: The article fills a gap in the administrative history of Dutch Malacca by providing a detailed economic and diplomatic account of a period (1662–1665) that had received little prior attention, and it serves as a companion piece to Coolhaas's own edition of the *Generale Missiven* for the same period.
