# The Malay Peninsula as known to the Chinese of the third century A.D

A. M. S., writing from Penang in 1895 to mark the centenary of Francis Light's death, produced this commemorative memoir reconstructing the career of the East India Company officer who founded the Penang settlement in 1786. The work draws on Light's own Diary, official despatches preserved in Logan's *Journal*, and contemporary correspondence to present a sympathetic portrait of a pioneer administrator who, with minimal support from Calcutta, established a thriving mixed-race colony in the face of disease, piracy, and political uncertainty. The author's overarching thesis is that Light's judgment—particularly his belief in the independence of Kedah and his policy of perpetual land grants—was sound and vindicated by subsequent events, and that later critics (notably Colonel Low) imported anachronistic political frameworks into their assessments.

## Summary

The memoir traces Light's trajectory from his 1771 correspondence with Warren Hastings proposing a repairing harbour in the Straits, through the aborted Junk Ceylon scheme of 1780–81, to the successful Penang expedition of July 1786. The author emphasises the practical difficulties of the early years: the jungle clearing, the absence of any judicial system until 1805, the financial dependence on a small salary of Rs. 1,000 per month, and the constant tension between Light's role as Superintendent and his continued commercial interests. Light's 1789 despatch to Calcutta, in which he enumerates five advantages of the settlement (harbour capacity, soil fertility, import value exceeding $600,000 annually, refit capabilities, and central emporium position), is presented as the document that should have secured institutional commitment but instead met with continued scepticism from the Indian Government.

A substantial portion of the memoir is devoted to the Kedah–Siam suzerainty controversy. The author argues that in 1786 no European concept of "siam suzerainty" was operative, that the triennial *bunga mas* was merely a token of relative prestige (offered by Siam to China in the same manner), and that Colonel Low's 1848 criticism of Light was based on ignorance of the 1787 despatch to Lord Cornwallis, which made clear that the negotiations concerned the merits of the harbour rather than questions of imperial sovereignty. The author further notes that the Dutch, not Siam, were the more immediate geopolitical threat in the 1780s, and that Light's selection of Penang was explicitly motivated by its value "as a barrier to the Dutch encroachment."

The memoir closes with the land-grant controversy. Light's policy of perpetual grants to encourage Chinese settlement was reversed by Lord Teignmouth's regulation of August 1794 (limiting European grants to 300 orlongs and imposing five-year leases), but Light and his successor Mannington effectively blocked its implementation, and the Governor-General in Council rescinded it in August 1796. The author contends this was a wise local judgment, later confirmed by Lord Auckland's 1837 minute, and that the responsibility for the original policy lay with Macpherson and Cornwallis, who had explicitly delegated discretion to Light.

### Key Findings

- Light arrived off Pulau Tikus on 15 July 1786 with 100 Native Marines, 30 Native Lascars, 15 European Artillerymen, and 5 Officers; formal possession was taken on 11 August 1786 (pp. 2–3).
- The settlement's population reached approximately 10,000 by the end of 1789 and over 20,000 by 1795, of whom the Chinese numbered not much over 3,000 (p. 4).
- In the 1791 Kedah crisis, Light's force of 400 men defeated an enemy that had swelled to over 8,000 Malays at Kuala Pry; Penang has not been attacked by any enemy since (p. 7).
- Light's 1789 despatch recorded annual imports exceeding $600,000; the first customs duty (2% *ad valorem* on tin, pepper, and betel-nut, imposed 1801) yielded $13,076 in that year (pp. 6, 14).
- The 1794 land regulation capped European grants at 300 orlongs and imposed five-year leases; it was rescinded by the Governor-General in Council on 22 August 1796 (pp. 14–15).
- Light received a salary of Rs. 1,000 per month and proposed in 1790 that he be precluded from trade in exchange for an adequate salary increase (p. 7).

### Conclusion

The author's definitive takeaway is that Light was "a man of sound sense, probity and judgment—active, practical, and moderate" whose eight years of solitary governance produced a settlement whose success was confirmed by the abandonment of Cornwallis's rival Andaman settlement in 1796 and by the Court of Directors' 1805 despatch establishing the Presidency Government. Light's work, the memoir argues, was that of a worthy forerunner to Raffles, and his death in 1794 deprived the settlement of the very leadership it most needed during its most vulnerable formative period.

## Context

- The author "A. M. S." is anonymous but writes from Penang with direct access to Logan's *Journal* (Vols. III–V), Light's Diary, and the official land-revenue papers published in 1884; the memoir is explicitly a centenary commemoration (dated 21 October 1894 at the close, though published in 1895).
- The text is openly apologetic in tone, particularly in its rebuttal of Colonel Low's 1848 criticism; the author acknowledges that both sides of the Kedah controversy (Low and Secretary Anderson) imported later political questions into the assessment of 1786 events, but sides firmly with Light's contemporary understanding of the situation.
