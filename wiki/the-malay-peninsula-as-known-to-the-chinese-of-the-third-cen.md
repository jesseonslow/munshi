# The Malay Peninsula as known to the Chinese of the third century A.D

Paul Wheatley's 1955 article reconstructs the earliest extant Chinese geographical and ethnographic knowledge of the Malay Peninsula, drawing on fragments of lost third-century A.D. texts compiled by the Wu dynasty envoys K'ang T'ai and Chu-Ying. The central thesis is that the Peninsula in the early centuries of the common era comprised a cluster of small river-valley-based political entities, several of which can be identified with confidence at specific modern locations, and that at least one of these—Tun-sun on the Kra isthmus—was already experiencing the cultural diffusion Wheatley terms "Indianization."

## Summary

Wheatley's source base consists of quotations preserved in later Chinese compendia—principally the *Shui ching chu* (late fifth century), the *T'ai p'ing yu lan* (977–983), the *T'ung tien* (eighth century), and the *T'ai p'ing huan yu chi* (c. 980)—which preserve fragments of K'ang T'ai's *Fu-nan t'u su chuan* and Chu-Ying's *Fu-nan i wu chih*, both products of a Wu dynasty embassy to Fu-nan dispatched around A.D. 245 (pp. 1–4). A fifth-century work by an Indian author named Chu-Chih, the *Fu-nan chi*, supplements these with first-hand observations. Wheatley systematically examines the place-names that appear in these texts—Tien-sun/Tun-sun, Ch'ü-tu-k'un/Tu-k'un, Chiu-chih/Chu-li, and Pi-sung—and attempts to fix their locations using internal geographical evidence, cross-referencing with Ptolemy's *Geography* and the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* (pp. 5–14).

The most significant identifications are Tun-sun, placed on the Kra isthmus on the basis of Bodhibhadra's itinerary in the *Lo yang chia lan chi*, which records it as thirty days' sail from Fu-nan and eleven days north of Chu-li (pp. 17–20); and Chu-li, located at the Kuantan River estuary on the east coast, where the description of a north-east-facing estuary and a prominent headland in shallow water matches the topography precisely (pp. 15–16). Tu-k'un, Pan-tou, and Pi-sung are assigned to the Peninsula more generally, on the far side of the Gulf of Siam from Fu-nan (pp. 10–14, 20).

Wheatley then draws out the political, economic, and ethnological implications. The Peninsula's "kingdoms" (*kuo*) were in reality small tribal groupings bounded by watersheds, each centred on a river valley. Tun-sun, partitioned among five kings and in communication with both the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea, represents a more advanced political formation whose prosperity derived from the trans-peninsular trade route (pp. 20–21). The *Fu-nan chi* provides what Wheatley identifies as the earliest literary reference to Indianization in South-East Asia: a community of five hundred Indian merchant families, two *fo-t'u* (Buddhist establishments or images), and over a thousand brahman who married local women and settled permanently in Tun-sun (p. 7).

### Key Findings

- The lost embassy report of K'ang T'ai and Chu-Ying (c. 245 A.D.) is the earliest written source describing the Malay Peninsula, preserved only in fragmentary quotations across five later Chinese compendia (pp. 1–4).
- Tun-sun is identified with the Kra isthmus region, supported by Bodhibhadra's itinerary placing it eleven days' sail north of Chu-li and thirty days from Fu-nan, and by its described function as an entrepôt where "East and West meet together" with over 10,000 daily traders (pp. 5–6, 17–20).
- Chu-li is identified with the Kuantan River estuary on the east coast, matching the *Nan chou i wu chih* description of a north-east-facing estuary and a large headland in shallow water, and corresponding to Ptolemy's "Kole" (pp. 12, 15–16).
- Tun-sun contained a settled community of Indian brahman and merchants who intermarried with local women, constituting the earliest extant contemporary description of Indianization in South-East Asia (p. 7).
- The pale-complexioned inhabitants of Pan-tou, Tu-k'un, and Chu-li are tentatively equated with the Besatae of the *Periplus* and the Saesadai of Ptolemy, the short-statured traders who brought malabathron (*huo-hsiang*) to the Indian Ocean trade network (pp. 22–23).
- The trans-peninsular route across the isthmus was in use before the fourth century A.D., as demonstrated by the existence and prosperity of Tun-sun as a land-based entrepôt (p. 21).

### Conclusion

Wheatley concludes that the third-century Malay Peninsula was not a single polity but a mosaic of small river-valley-based tribal entities, several of which had already been incorporated into the Fu-nan sphere of influence. The existence of Tun-sun on the isthmus, with its five kings, its Indian brahman community, and its role as a trans-peninsular entrepôt, demonstrates that both a higher form of political organization and the process of Indianization were already underway in the early centuries of the common era, even if the Chinese sources remain too fragmentary to trace how far these developments had penetrated beyond the isthmus (pp. 20–22).

## Context

- The article was published in the JMBRAS at a time when Sir Roland Braddell's foundational studies of ancient Malayan history (1936, 1939) had established the framework but left many place-name identifications unresolved; Wheatley's work directly engages with and extends Braddell's conclusions, particularly regarding the location of Tun-sun.
- The source texts examined here—K'ang T'ai's and Chu-Ying's embassy reports—remain the primary Chinese evidence for early first-millennium A.D. South-East Asian geography and continue to underpin subsequent scholarship on the region's pre-Angkorian political history.
