# Poisons and poisonous plants

## Poisons and Poisonous Plants

The use of plant-derived poisons in the Malay Peninsula and Borneo constitutes a subject of considerable medical, economic, and pharmacological interest, documented in the Society's literature from the late nineteenth century through the early twentieth. Two principal threads emerge from the archival record: the occupational hazards posed by toxic plant sap in the timber trade, and the systematic investigation of *tuba* (*Derris elliptica*) as a fish-poison whose physiological mechanism remained unexplained until controlled laboratory experiments were undertaken in the Straits Settlements. Together, these studies illustrate the transition from ethnographic description to experimental toxicology in the region's scientific literature.

## Occupational Toxicity: Rengas Sap

The Rengas tree, whose sap presents a serious hazard to woodcutters, was the subject of a clinical note by W. C. Brown, M.D., published in 1891. Brown documented the toxic properties of the sap as encountered in the woodcutting trade in Penang, where the tree was worked for cabinet-making purposes. The sap acts as a violent irritant on contact with the skin, producing pustular eruptions, and constitutes a dangerous internal poison when ingested, causing vomiting and purging [[1, p. 83]](#ref-1).

Of particular economic significance was Brown's observation that the wood retains its irritant properties even after years of seasoning, a fact that seriously impairs its value for cabinet-making [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1). This finding carried direct commercial implications for the timber trade in the Straits Settlements, where Rengas wood had been sought for fine furniture work. The persistence of the irritant in seasoned timber meant that the economic utility of the species was fundamentally compromised by its own toxic chemistry.

Brown recorded the clinical course of neglected cases, noting that in emaciated subjects the condition tended to develop into chronic ulceration between the fingers and toes [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1). Treatment, as he described it, involved wrapping affected areas in bandages impregnated with a dry alkaline powder such as bicarbonate of soda, while vaseline or lard served as a preventive barrier for woodcutters engaged in handling the wood [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1).

Brown also documented Malay beliefs surrounding the Rengas tree, which included claims that exhalations from a felled stem caused facial swelling, fever, and blindness, and that elaborate exorcisms against *djins* and *langsyar* (spiritual afflictions) rendered woodcutters immune to the sap [[1, p. 85]](#ref-1). On investigation, Brown found that the so-called "adepts" at Ayer Etam in Penang, who were reputed to be immune, were in fact simply protecting themselves with coco-nut oil [[1, p. 85]](#ref-1). This observation is notable for its methodological approach: rather than dismissing the local belief system wholesale, Brown subjected the claim to direct empirical verification and identified the material basis for the apparent immunity.

## Experimental Pharmacology: Tuba (*Derris elliptica*)

The fish-poison known in Malay as *tuba*, derived from the root of *Derris elliptica*, had been the subject of ethnographic and chemical investigation for decades before J. Argyll Campbell published his experimental pharmacological study in 1916. Previous investigators—Greshoff, Wray, Ridley, and Gimlette—had isolated resinous compounds from the root, naming them "derrid" and "tubaine," and had recorded clinical observations of its effects, but no one had performed controlled animal experiments to determine the site and mechanism of toxicity [[2, p. 129]](#ref-2). Campbell's central contribution was to establish, through a series of experiments on living animals and isolated tissues, that tuba poison kills by paralysing the respiratory centre in the medulla oblongata, producing death by asphyxia [[2]](#ref-2).

Campbell prepared the poison in the traditional Malay manner—pounding the root, soaking it in water or Ringer's saline, filtering, and boiling—and tested it on fish, tadpoles, mosquito larvae, toads, and two species of macaque monkey (*Macacus cynomolgus* and *M. nemestpinus*), both in living animals and on isolated tissues [[2]](#ref-2). The study was conducted with institutional support from the Raffles Museum and the Botanical Gardens in Singapore, reflecting the Straits Settlements' role as a centre for tropical pharmacological research in the early twentieth century [[2]](#ref-2).

The core experimental findings established several key physiological facts. The poison dilates blood vessels and weakens both voluntary and involuntary muscle, but has no direct action on heart muscle or on the blood cells themselves [[2, p. 125]](#ref-2). An isolated heart continues to beat strongly in Ringer's solution containing poison at a concentration of 1 in 8, and no haemolysis occurs; the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood is unaltered [[2, p. 125]](#ref-2). Crucially, Campbell demonstrated that the respiratory effect persists after vagotomy, proving that the action is central (medullary) rather than peripheral (vagal) [[2]](#ref-2). The poison also abolishes the constrictive action of adrenalin on blood vessels, explaining the marked fall in blood pressure observed in anaesthetised monkeys [[2, p. 125]](#ref-2).

Campbell further established a clear inverse relationship between phylogenetic complexity and susceptibility to the poison. The minimal lethal dose for monkeys, administered subcutaneously or by stomach tube, was an extract from 2 grams (30 grains) of root, producing death in approximately 40 minutes [[2, pp. 124–125]](#ref-2). For fish, solutions as weak as 1 in 100,000 were fatal to *Ophiocephalus gachua* (approximately 50 grams); a 1 in 4,500 solution killed in 11 minutes, 1 in 12,000 in 28 minutes, and 1 in 50,000 in 83 minutes [[2, pp. 122–123]](#ref-2). Mosquito larvae, by contrast, required far stronger solutions: 1 in 10,000 took at least two days to kill *Stegomyia* larvae, whereas 1 in 10 killed in one hour [[2, p. 123]](#ref-2). This gradient—most lethal to higher vertebrates, least lethal to lower forms—was consistent with a mechanism targeting the medullary respiratory centre, which is more developed in phylogenetically advanced organisms [[2]](#ref-2).

The extract was found to be colloidal in nature: only one-seventieth of the poison passed through parchment in five days, indicating that it did not form a true solution [[2, p. 122]](#ref-2). The extract was not antiseptic and was faintly acid [[2, p. 122]](#ref-2).

## Forensic and Practical Implications

Campbell's study addressed practical questions of direct relevance to public health and legal medicine in the Straits Settlements. He noted that fish killed by tuba could be eaten safely by humans, because the lethal concentration in the water was far below what would be toxic to a human consumer [[2]](#ref-2). This finding had immediate implications for the food trade in areas where tuba fishing was practised.

On the forensic side, Campbell discussed the implications for suspected poisoning cases in Singapore. Post-mortem examination revealed only venous congestion and an acrid odour in the lungs, with no specific chemical test available at the time to confirm the presence of tuba in a deceased person's system [[2]](#ref-2). He proposed a simple biological assay—testing stomach contents on small fish—as a practical forensic method that could be employed where chemical analysis was unavailable [[2]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on poisons and poisonous plants in the Malay Peninsula and Borneo reflects a clear trajectory from clinical observation and ethnographic recording to experimental pharmacology. Brown's 1891 note on Rengas poisoning represents the earlier mode: a physician's clinical account, attentive to both the medical and the economic dimensions of the problem, and incorporating local knowledge with a critical eye. The article is brief, practical, and grounded in direct observation, with the empirical verification of the "adepts" at Ayer Etam serving as a model of the methodological approach that would come to characterise the later work.

Campbell's 1916 study marks a decisive shift in both scale and method. Where Brown's note was a single-page clinical observation, Campbell's paper is a systematic experimental investigation employing controlled animal models, isolated tissue preparations, and quantitative dose-response relationships. The work bridges ethnographic description and experimental physiology, and it remains a primary source for the history of toxicology in Southeast Asia [[2]](#ref-2). The institutional context is significant: the study was conducted with the support of the Raffles Museum and the Botanical Gardens in Singapore, indicating that the Straits Settlements had developed the infrastructure for tropical pharmacological research by the first decade of the twentieth century [[2]](#ref-2).

The two studies together illustrate the broadening scope of the subject as it was understood by the scientific community. Brown's work is concerned with occupational health and the economic value of timber; Campbell's with the fundamental physiology of a well-known indigenous poison and its forensic applications. The gap between them—twenty-five years—corresponds to the wider development of experimental pharmacology in the colonial tropics, and the Society's journal served as the principal venue for publishing such work in the region.
