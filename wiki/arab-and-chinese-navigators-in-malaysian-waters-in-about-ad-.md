# Arab and Chinese navigators in Malaysian waters in about A.D. 1500

J.V. Mills's 1974 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the maritime activities of Arab and Chinese navigators in the waters surrounding the Malay Peninsula and the Straits of Malacca around the year 1500. Drawing on a wide range of primary sources—including Chinese official histories, Arab travel accounts, Portuguese chronicles, and Malay literary texts—Mills reconstructs the commercial and cultural networks that connected the Indian Ocean world to Southeast Asia during the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, arguing that the period represented a transitional phase in which established Arab trading dominance was being challenged by the arrival of Chinese treasure fleets and, shortly thereafter, the Portuguese.

## Summary

Mills's central concern is to establish who was sailing in Malaysian waters circa 1500, what they were trading, and how their presence shaped the political and commercial landscape of the region. He demonstrates that Arab merchants, particularly those from the Hadramaut, Oman, and the Persian Gulf, had maintained a continuous presence in the Strait of Malacca and the coasts of the Malay Peninsula for centuries, operating through a network of entrepôts that linked the spice islands to the markets of the Middle East and East Africa. These traders were not merely commercial agents; they were also vectors of Islamic culture, and their communities in places like Malacca, Patani, and Terengganu had become deeply embedded in local society by the fifteenth century.

The Chinese dimension of the argument is equally significant. Mills traces the trajectory of the Ming treasure voyages under Zheng He and his successors, showing that while the great expeditions had officially ceased by the 1430s, Chinese commercial shipping continued to operate in the region on a smaller scale. By 1500, Chinese junks were regular visitors to the ports of the Malay Peninsula, trading silk, porcelain, and copper for spices, tin, and camphor. Mills argues that the coexistence of Arab and Chinese maritime communities created a complex commercial ecology in which both groups competed for access to the spice trade while also cooperating in the broader Indian Ocean trading system.

The article also addresses the political implications of this maritime activity. The rise of the Malacca Sultanate under the Parameswara dynasty was intimately connected to its position as a entrepôt for both Arab and Chinese trade. Mills shows how the sultans cultivated relationships with both trading communities, granting them privileges and protection in exchange for customs revenue and political allegiance. The arrival of the Portuguese under Afonso de Albuquerque in 1511, which Mills treats as the closing note of his study, disrupted this equilibrium by introducing a European naval power that could project force across the entire Indian Ocean.

### Key Findings

- Arab trading communities in the Strait of Malacca region by 1500 included merchants from Hadramaut, Oman, the Persian Gulf, and the Swahili Coast, with established residential quarters in Malacca, Patani, and Terengganu (pp. 12–18).
- Chinese commercial shipping in the region circa 1500 operated primarily from bases in Hainan and Fujian, with junks of 200–500 tons regularly calling at Malacca, Johor, and the ports of the northern Malay Peninsula (pp. 22–28).
- The Malacca Sultanate derived an estimated 40–60% of its revenue from customs duties on Arab and Chinese trade, making the sultanate's political stability directly dependent on the continued flow of maritime commerce (pp. 30–34).
- Mills identifies at least seven major entrepôt sites in Malaysian waters active around 1500: Malacca, Patani, Terengganu, Johor, Pahang, Kedah, and the island of Pulau Pinang, each serving distinct commodity flows (pp. 36–42).
- The Portuguese arrival at Malacca in 1511 marked the end of the Arab-Chinese commercial equilibrium, as Portuguese naval superiority allowed them to impose a monopoly on the spice trade that neither Arab nor Chinese merchants could challenge (pp. 58–62).
- Mills documents the use of a shared maritime vocabulary and set of navigational practices among Arab, Chinese, and Malay sailors, suggesting a degree of cross-cultural technical exchange that facilitated the functioning of the Indian Ocean trading system (pp. 44–50).

### Conclusion

Mills's definitive historical takeaway is that the maritime landscape of Malaysian waters around 1500 was characterized by a sophisticated, multi-ethnic commercial system in which Arab and Chinese navigators operated as complementary rather than purely competitive forces. The disruption of this system by Portuguese naval power in 1511 was not merely a military event but a structural transformation of the Indian Ocean trading order, one that would have consequences for the region's political and economic development for centuries to come.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on Chinese official sources (the *Ming Shi* and the *Yingya Shenglan*), Arab travel literature (al-Mas'udi, al-Idrisi, and later accounts), Portuguese chronicles (Tomé Pires's *Suma Oriental* and the *Diário* of the first voyage), and Malay literary texts including the *Hikayat Merong Mahawangsa* and the *Sejarah Melayu*.
- Mills's contribution to the historiography lies in his systematic integration of these previously studied-in-isolation source traditions into a unified narrative of maritime activity in a specific geographic and temporal frame, moving beyond the earlier tendency to treat Arab and Chinese presence as separate phenomena.
