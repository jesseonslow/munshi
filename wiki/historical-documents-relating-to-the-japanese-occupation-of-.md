# Historical documents relating to the Japanese occupation of Malaya. Comp. P.H. Kratoska

This section of historical documents, compiled by P.H. Kratoska and published in JMBRAS Vol. 88 (2015), presents primary source materials from the Japanese occupation of Malaya and Singapore (1942–1945) that illuminate lesser-known administrative and cultural dimensions of the period. The collection, introduced with a framing essay acknowledging the maturation of Occupation historiography, offers four distinct documents spanning press operations, records management, naval intelligence, and museum collections.

## Summary

The section opens with a Japanese journalist's first-person account of the takeover and rebranding of Singapore's *Straits Times* as the *Syonan Times* in the immediate aftermath of the city's fall in February 1942. Ibuse Shoji, who served briefly on the paper's staff, provides a granular, almost anecdotal portrait of the chaotic first days: the state of the newspaper office, the attitudes of local employees ranging from fearful to opportunistic, the logistical problems of restarting publication with limited staff and equipment, and the small cultural frictions that arose over romanization conventions and editorial control. The account is notable for its unselfconscious tone—written for a Japanese domestic audience in January 1943, it reads as a travelogue of bureaucratic adjustment rather than a propagandistic piece, though it is framed within the broader narrative of the East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere.

The second document shifts to the administrative aftermath, tracing the Japanese effort to recover pre-war British government records that had been evacuated from Kuala Lumpur to Singapore during the retreat. The introduction notes that the most complete surviving set of pre-war papers belonged to the Selangor Secretariat and remained in Kuala Lumpur throughout the occupation, while Federation-level records were lost or destroyed. This document, drawn from the Selangor Kanbo file, reveals the practical difficulties Japanese administrators faced in regularizing their financial accounts without access to the colonial fiscal records they needed.

Together, these documents—along with the submarine patrol logs and museum collection report that complete the section—collectively address gaps in the English-language literature on the Occupation, particularly regarding the day-to-day operations of Japanese military and civilian personnel and the fate of institutional assets.

### Key Findings

- Singapore fell on 15 February 1942; Ibuse Shoji and interpreter Furuyama Tachi entered the city on 16 February with "X Squad" and examined the *Straits Times* office on 18 February (pp. 88–89).
- The first edition of the *Syonan Times* was published under the misspelling "Shonan Times"; the error was corrected after local reporters, led by Lembrugan, formally objected, and the spelling "Syonan" was confirmed against the 1913 Sanseido dictionary (pp. 91–92).
- W.A. Wilson, the outgoing *Straits Times* Manager who had assumed the post only three days before the fall, was interned at Changi camp for hostile persons (p. 90).
- The Ibuse Shoji article was published in Osaka's *Sunday Mainichi* on 17 January 1943 and translated by the British Ministry of Information's Far Eastern Bureau in New Delhi on 12 July 1943; the translation is held at the US National Archives as RG226 41350 (p. 88).
- The most complete surviving set of pre-war British administrative records belonged to the Selangor Secretariat and remained in Kuala Lumpur throughout the occupation; Federation-level records were moved to Singapore during the retreat and largely lost (p. 93).

### Conclusion

Kratoska's compilation does not challenge established interpretations of the Occupation but fills specific evidentiary gaps, particularly regarding the micro-level operations of Japanese press administration and the fate of colonial archival materials. The documents collectively underscore that the Occupation's bureaucratic machinery was often improvised, under-resourced, and dependent on the cooperation of local personnel whose motivations ranged from self-preservation to genuine engagement with the new order.

## Context

- The Ibuse Shoji text survives as a British intelligence translation (RG226 41350, US National Archives), meaning the English-language reader encounters a Japanese domestic press account filtered through wartime British counter-intelligence priorities.
- The Selangor Kanbo file (106/2602) represents Japanese administrative correspondence from the occupation period, providing direct evidence of the Japanese military government's internal record-keeping practices in Selangor.
