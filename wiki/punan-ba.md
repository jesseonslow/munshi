# Punan Ba

Rodney Needham's 1955 article in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* addresses the persistent taxonomic confusion surrounding the Punan Ba, a settled longhouse-dwelling people of the Rejang River basin in Sarawak who had been repeatedly misidentified as either nomadic Punan or Penan throughout the colonial ethnographic literature. Drawing on a brief 1952 field visit to two longhouses in the Bintulu district and a systematic review of prior sources, Needham argues that the Punan Ba are a distinct segment of the Kajang grouping, historically, linguistically, and culturally separate from both the nomadic Punan and the Penan with whom they had been conflated for over a century.

## Summary

The article's central problem is one of ethnographic misidentification. From Burns's 1849 description of a Kayan-like Punan people on the Rejang through the confused accounts of Windt, Low, Hiller, Baring-Gould and Bampfylde, and the 1947 census report, the literature had failed to distinguish the settled longhouse Punan Ba from the nomadic Punan (who in many cases were in fact Penan). Needham traces this confusion through each major source, showing how the shared name "Punan" obscured fundamentally different peoples: one a sophisticated, house-dwelling group with mortuary posts, hereditary class divisions, and rice cultivation; the other a forest nomad with no fixed settlement. The breakthrough came with Leach's 1948 administrative report, which for the first time explicitly separated the Punan Ba from the "Nomadic Punan" and located their longhouses, and with Urquhart's 1949 letter to the *Sarawak Gazette*, which recorded the critical fact that the "Jungle Punans" spoke a completely different language from the longhouse Punan of the Batang Rejang.

Needham's own contribution is modest by design. He was in the field primarily to delimit the Penan, and his Punan Ba material consists of notes gathered in conversation (conducted in Malay, since he did not speak the Punan Ba language) with T. R. Berasap of Pandan, supplemented by information from Dr. J. D. Freeman, R. F. Mole, and I. A. N. Urquhart. He is explicit about the limitations of his account, presenting it as the "random notes of an apprentice ethnographer" that a more closely situated observer could correct and expand. What he does provide is a framework: the Punan Ba's own account of their origin from the Batang Kayan through the Kajang to the Ba River; their three-tier class system (aristocrats, commoners, slaves) and its expression in marriage payments, burial forms, and social sanctions; their kinship terminology; and their spiritual cosmology with its three types of spirit and the supreme creator Telangan.

The article thus functions less as a full ethnography than as a corrective taxonomy and a provocation for further study. Needham's closing observation—that the Punan Ba are "very easily accessible" and would make "a valuable introduction to the understanding of the Kajang complex of peoples, of whom we know so very little"—frames the paper as a call to action for the small community of Sarawak ethnographers of the period.

### Key Findings

- The Punan Ba numbered approximately 600 individuals divided among seven longhouses on the Rejang, Kakus, and Ba rivers; the three houses on the Rejang and Ba (Rh Lutun, Rh Ubeng, Rh Lanyieng) alone totalled 397 according to Belaga Fort records, matching Urquhart's 1949 figure (pp. 31, 29).
- Marriage payments varied by class: $2,000–$3,000 for aristocrats, $900 for commoners, and $100 for slaves, making the class system materially visible in the economy of exchange (p. 33).
- Aristocratic burials involved a *kelireng* (mortuary post) approximately twenty feet high, with a porcelain jar set in the carved top and a vertical opening for grave goods; in former times a slave was crushed or bound beneath the post at its erection, though at Pandan a dying aristocrat ordered a pig substituted (p. 34).
- The Punan Ba recognise three spirit categories—*beyo* (equivalent to Kenyah *bali*), *otu* (comparable to Kayan *toh*), and *etun* (the personal soul or ghost)—and place the supreme creator Telangan in Tuli, the land above the sky, where the dead live a second life in longhouses, plant rice, and make canoes (p. 35).
- The name "Punan" derives from Sungei Punan, a tributary of the Ba River, and carries no implication of relationship to the nomadic Punan; the Punan Ba simultaneously identify as *orang Kajang*, placing them within the Kajang grouping alongside the Sekapan, Kejaman, and Lahanan (pp. 31–32).
- The 1947 Sarawak census, compiled before Leach and Urquhart had published their corrections, inextricably confused the Punan Ba with settled Penan under the catch-all category "Penan and Settled Punan" within "Other and indeterminate indigenous" (p. 29).

### Conclusion

Needham's definitive takeaway is that the Punan Ba are a settled, linguistically distinct, and culturally sophisticated segment of the Kajang complex who have been systematically misclassified for a century due to the ambiguity of the name "Punan." Their longhouse architecture, hereditary class structure, mortuary practices, and spiritual system align them firmly with the Kenyah-Kayan-Kajang world rather than with forest nomadism, and their proper identification opens a tractable entry point into the broader Kajang peoples, about whom ethnographic knowledge remained extremely thin in 1955.

## Context

- The article draws on a small corpus of colonial-era travel accounts, administrative reports (Leach 1948, Urquhart 1949/1951), the 1947 census report, and Needham's own 1952 field notes; no dedicated archival collection is cited beyond these published and semi-published sources.
- Historiographically, the paper is a corrective taxonomy within the emerging post-war Sarawak ethnographic tradition, building directly on Leach's and Urquhart's administrative-ethnographic work and anticipating the more systematic Kajang studies that would follow in the 1960s and 1970s.
