# Origin of the Malay keris

G.C. Woolley published this concise rebuttal in 1938, responding directly to G.C. Griffith Williams' December 1937 paper in the same journal, which had argued that the Malay keris evolved from a detachable spear point. Woolley's overarching thesis is that the keris was not a converted spear head but a purpose-built thrusting weapon whose distinctive features—particularly the *ganja* crossguard and the talismanic character of the oldest Majapahit specimens—point to an independent origin rooted in the practical and spiritual needs of Malay warriors.

## Summary

Woolley's argument proceeds on two fronts: typological and functional. On the typological side, he contends that the earliest known keris forms—the Keris Pichit and the Keris Majapahit—are precisely those least compatible with a spear-head derivation. The Pichit's thin, weak blade was unsuited to piercing as a spear point, and the Majapahit type's hilt, cast as a single piece of metal with the blade in the form of a deity figure, strongly suggests talismanic manufacture rather than utilitarian design (p. 36). Woolley further challenges Williams' etymological evidence, noting that Wilkinson's Dictionary (1901) provides no Malay word *karis* and treats *keris* as the specific name for a specific weapon, undermining the claim that the term simply meant "dagger" (p. 36).

On the functional side, Woolley argues that the *ganja*—the crossguard unique to the keris—constitutes the strongest objection to the spear-head theory, since no fighting spear requires or possesses such a feature, and a protruding crossbar would impede withdrawal from a wound or shield (p. 37). He reinterprets the *ganja* as a hand guard analogous to that of a fencing foil, protecting the forefinger and knuckles in a closed thrusting grip, and notes that the keris hilt's kink mirrors the geometry of a foil hilt rather than a dagger or spear (p. 38). Finally, Woolley offers an environmental explanation for the keris's existence: in the confined spaces of narrow jungle tracks and crowded vessel decks where Malays fought, a short thrusting weapon was superior to a sword, and the keris's proven efficacy in such encounters generated the superstitious and magical associations that accumulated around it over time (pp. 38–39).

### Key Findings

- The Keris Pichit, with a blade allegedly welded by finger pressure alone, and the Keris Majapahit, whose hilt is a deity figure cast in one piece with the blade, are identified as the oldest surviving Majapahit types and the least consistent with a spear-head origin (p. 36).
- Wilkinson's Dictionary (1901 edition) contains no entry for a Malay word *karis* and gives no derivation for *keris* other than as the proper name of a specific weapon, contradicting Crawford's etymology cited by Williams (p. 36).
- A Brunei Malay informant explained that the keris hilt was removed at home to keep the *paksi* (tang) and blade free from rust, and reattached with rag, hair, damar, or hard gutta before use—indicating the loose fit was for maintenance, not for re-conversion to a spear (p. 37).
- The *ganja* is typically a separate piece of metal fixed tightly to the blade, making its removal and safekeeping during a spear-expedition impractical; Woolley concludes it was a later addition designed for the keris's own requirements (p. 37).
- The *dagu* (short end of the ganja) and *aring* (long end) function as a flat hand guard: the *dagu* protects the forefinger tip in the closed grip, the *aring* protects the knuckles, and the smooth-edged variant allows an opponent's blade to slide off while the fretted variant can catch and break it (p. 38).
- The Keris panjang is reinterpreted not as a "monstrosity" but as a purpose-built weapon for open-country duels, tournaments, and executions, with fighting technique comparable to Elizabethan "sword and dagger" combat (p. 39).

### Conclusion

Woolley concludes that even if one were to accept a spear-point ancestry for the keris, the *ganja* must have been a later addition serving the weapon's own distinctive thrusting function, and that the keris's true origin lies in the tactical demands of confined Malay combat environments, where a short, thrusting, easily carried weapon filled a niche that neither spear nor sword could occupy. The magical and talismanic qualities attributed to the keris are thus understood as secondary accretions that grew around a weapon whose practical superiority in close-quarters fighting earned it the highest regard among Malay warriors.

## Context

- This article is a point-and-counterpoint contribution to a 1937–38 debate within the MBRAS Journal on keris origins; its historiographical value lies in introducing a functional-morphological analysis (comparing the keris to the fencing foil) and an environmental explanation that shifted the discussion away from purely typological speculation.
- No specific archival collections are cited; the argument rests on surviving weapon specimens, comparative typology, a single oral informant (a Brunei Malay), and published lexicographic sources (Wilkinson 1901; Crawford via Williams).
