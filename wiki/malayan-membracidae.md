# Malayan Membracidae

Lee Boon Thong (2006) examines the origins and first two decades of Petaling Jaya, Malaysia's first new town, tracing its transformation from a post-war squatter resettlement scheme into a thriving middle-class satellite city. The article situates this development within the demographic explosion of Kuala Lumpur after World War II and the political imperatives of the Malayan Emergency, arguing that the original resettlement objective was quickly eclipsed by market-driven middle-class demand.

## Summary

The article opens by establishing the demographic crisis in Kuala Lumpur, whose population surged from 80,000 in 1920 to an estimated 220,000 within five years of the war's end, with a record annual growth rate of 12.5% between 1951 and 1955. Housing construction could not keep pace — between 1936 and 1941 only about 20% of housing needs were met, and the Japanese Occupation saw uncontrolled temporary housing fill even designated areas. By 1953, 21,851 families lived in 14,089 squatter shacks. The political context of the Emergency compounded the problem: the Newboult Committee (1948) and subsequent Colonial Office papers identified squatter communities as potential sources of social unrest with possible communist ties, making resettlement a security as well as a welfare imperative.

The Federal Housing Trust was established in 1951, and the Federal Town Planning Department designed a new town modelled on British New Towns such as Stevenage, at a site 10 km south-west of Kuala Lumpur comprising 1,437 ha of former rubber, mining, and estate land. The first phase — clearing rubber trees and laying unpaved roads — began in February 1953, yielding 1,300 wooden houses for relocated squatters. However, the author stresses that the "new town" label was ambiguous from the outset: government correspondence described the project as "largely a Kuala Lumpur slum clearance scheme," the housing was temporary timber construction on 60-year leases, and the industrial zone failed to attract tenants in its early years due to water shortages and competition from closer sites.

The transformation from squatter settlement to middle-class suburb was driven by several converging factors. The completion of the Federal Highway through the town in 1957 — though it bisected the planned recreational area — made Petaling Jaya far more accessible to Kuala Lumpur commuters. The entry of private developers, particularly the Malaya Borneo Building Society, introduced brick bungalows at prices ranging from $4,800 to $25,000, catering to a new middle-class clientele. The Pioneer Industries Act and the First Malaya Five-Year Plan filled the industrial zone, while the establishment of the University of Malaya campus nearby in 1959 added further demand. By the late 1950s, the modern "New Town" centre (PJ State) with its shopping complex, banks, and air-conditioned cinema had entirely eclipsed the "Old Town" of the original squatter sections, and the town's character had fundamentally shifted.

### Key Findings

- Kuala Lumpur's population grew from 80,000 (1920) to an estimated 220,000 within five years post-war; the average annual growth rate between 1951 and 1955 reached a record 12.5% (p. 2).
- By 1953, 21,851 families occupied 14,089 squatter shacks; approximately 5,400 squatter households (about 22,000 persons) lived within 8 km of the Kuala Lumpur town centre, with another 40,000 in an outer ring (p. 4).
- The initial land acquisition totalled 1,437 ha: 486 ha of former Petaling Estate (rubber), 554 ha of Parkland and Chooi Lin rubber estates, and 397 ha of ex-mining land (p. 7).
- The Federal Government allocated an emergency fund of $3.1 million; within 18 months of the February 1953 start, 28.8 km of road had been built and 800 houses completed with 200 more under construction (p. 8).
- By the end of 1957, Petaling Jaya contained approximately 3,193 houses, 100 shops, and 28 operating factories; by mid-1959, 40 more industries had been added, and by 1960 no industrial lots remained (pp. 12–13).
- The Malaya Borneo Building Society constructed 150 houses in 1956 and 800 in 1957, priced from $4,800 to $8,000 (standard) and $11,000 to $25,000 (premium), with loans up to 90% of valuation repayable over 15–18 years (p. 14).

### Conclusion

Lee Boon Thong's definitive historical takeaway is that Petaling Jaya's original purpose — squatter resettlement — was rapidly superseded by market forces and middle-class demand. Good planning based on residential neighbourhood units, strategic accessibility via the Federal Highway, and the entry of private capital transformed a rudimentary timber settlement into a modern suburban city. The town's growth from 1,300 wooden houses in 1953 to 97,208 homes over 97.2 sq km by the 2000s (nearly seven times its original size) validates its claim as Malaysia's first new town, though the author notes it never fully achieved the economic self-containment of a true British-style new town, remaining largely a dormitory satellite of Kuala Lumpur where the majority of residents worked outside the town.

## Context

- Primary archival sources include District Office Kuala Lumpur monthly reports (File No. 505, 1951–61), Selangor State Secretariat correspondence, and Colonial Office papers (CO 717/201, CO 717/178, CO 852/1621, CO 1030/279) from the British National Archives and National Archives, Malaysia (pp. 20–21).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malaysian urban planning by applying Golany's typology of new towns to classify Petaling Jaya as a satellite/dormitory town, and by providing a detailed institutional account of how British planning concepts were adapted (and ultimately subverted by private capital) in a post-colonial Southeast Asian context (pp. 1–2, 17).
