# The 1839–1841 settlement of Kedah: the Siamese compromise

Kobkua Suwannathat-Pian's 1986 article examines the 1839–1841 political settlement of Kedah, arguing that the reinstatement of the Siamese tributary system in that state was a deliberate, far-sighted compromise initiated by King Rama III rather than a concession extracted by British pressure. Drawing extensively on Thai official correspondence and court memoirs, the study repositions the episode as a masterclass in Siamese diplomatic pragmatism, demonstrating how Bangkok abandoned a failed policy of direct provincial rule after seventeen years of persistent Malay resistance.

## Summary

The article addresses a central puzzle in Siamese-Malay relations: why, after conquering Kedah in 1821 and imposing direct Siamese administration through the viceroyalty of Nakorn, Bangkok chose to reverse course and restore Malay rule. Suwannathat-Pian identifies three converging pressures that made the continuation of direct rule untenable. First, the Kedah Malays had mounted three serious uprisings (1828–29, 1831–32, 1838–39), each exposing the structural weakness of a small standing garrison backed by an unreliable feudal levy. Second, the punitive policies of Chao Phraya Nakorn—mass deportations of Malay elites to Siam as war spoils—had hollowed out the state's population and leadership, driving tens of thousands into British Province Wellesley and Penang. Third, the death of Chao Phraya Nakorn (Noi) in 1839 removed the single forceful figure who had propped up Siamese authority in the south, leaving behind a disunited set of his sons and a corrupt, incompetent chain of provincial governors.

Rama III's solution, articulated in detailed royal instructions (*tongtra*) to his generals Chao Phraya Yomraj and Phraya Sipipat, was to reinstate the tributary system with a carefully chosen Malay governor. The King rejected his generals' counter-proposals—either appointing a Malay from the Pattani principalities or dissolving Kedah into *mukim* under direct Siamese control—on the grounds that Kedah's strategic position on the western seaboard demanded a recognized ruler who could command local loyalty and deny the British a pretext for intervention. The King personally named Tengku Anum, a loyalist recommended by the late Nakorn, and stipulated that the ex-Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin would be pardoned and restored if he humbly petitioned the Siamese Court. The author emphasizes that this willingness to restore the very man whose party had rebelled was a calculated risk taken by Rama III himself, not a British imposition, and that the division of Kedah into four sub-entities (Kedah, Setul-Lingu, Kubang Pasu, Perlis) served as a contingency safeguard rather than a permanent diminishment.

The primary sources underpinning the argument are Thai official documents: *tongtra* (royal instructions), *sarn-tra* (administrative orders), and the *Memoirs of Luang Udom-sombat*, supplemented by British records (Burney Papers, CO 273/1) and population statistics from Province Wellesley. The article's evidential strength lies in its use of the King's own words—his analogies, his strategic reasoning, his explicit rejection of his generals' proposals—to reconstruct the decision-making process at the highest level of the Siamese court.

### Key Findings

- The population of Province Wellesley (British Penang's mainland dependency) surged from 5,399 in 1820 to 17,805 in 1827 and 41,702 in 1833, reflecting mass Malay flight from Siamese rule in Kedah; Burney estimated at least 10,000 Malays left Kedah immediately after 1821 (p. 36, fn. 7).
- Three major Malay uprisings challenged Siamese direct rule: 1828–29 (low-key), 1831–32 (Tengku Den's rebellion, which forced the Siamese Governor to flee and required a full army from Nakorn to suppress), and 1838–39 (Tengku Mohamed Saad's offensive campaign reaching as far as Songkhla) (pp. 37–38).
- The 1826 Burney Treaty's Article 13 contained the British undertaking not to attack, disturb, or permit the former Governor of Kedah or his followers to injure Kedah or any territory subject to Siam; in exchange, Siam granted free trade in Kedah, Kelantan, and Trengganu (Articles 12–13) and accepted Perak as a buffer (Article 14) (pp. 35–36).
- By the final settlement of 1841, Kedah was divided into four entities of equal tributary status: Kedah (Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin), Setul-Lingu (Tengku Dewa, under Songkhla), Kubang Pasu (Tengku Anum, 24 *mukim* detached from Kedah, under Nakorn), and Perlis (Syed Hussein, promoted from subdivision to full tributary, under Nakorn) (pp. 42–43).
- Under King Mongkut (Rama IV, 1851–1868), Kubang Pasu was returned to Kedah proper after Tengku Anum's death, and Rama III had already accepted the nomination of Tengku Mohammad Akib—a participant in the 1838 uprising—as ruler of Setul, indicating the contingency of territorial division was abandoned once loyalty was confirmed (p. 44).
- Rama III's own summary, recorded in a *tongtra* of J.S. 1204 (1842), stated that the ruin of Kedah resulted from Tengku Den and Tengku Ismail's failure to "humbly nor honestly petition for the return of their land" and instead instigating uprisings; upon the ex-Sultan's humble submission, the King restored him and even dispatched *mariams* (cannons) from Nakorn and Trang to arm the restored state (p. 45).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian's definitive takeaway is that the 1839–41 settlement represents one of the clearest demonstrations of Rama III's statesmanship—a king "least appreciated by scholars" until recently. The settlement succeeded beyond even the King's expectations: after 1839, no substantial disturbances occurred in Kedah or the other Siamese Malay tributaries, and Siamese-Kedah relations became so solid that Bangkok could rely on Kedah for the security of the western seaboard. The episode confirms that the tributary system, properly applied with flexibility and respect for local political culture, was a more durable instrument of imperial control than direct provincial administration, and that its survival throughout the nineteenth century owed much to this single act of royal self-correction.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on Thai official correspondence held in the Vajirana National Library (tongtra and sarn-tra documents from J.S. 1201–1204), the *Memoirs of Luang Udom-sombat* (B.E. 2515/1972 edition), and British records including the Burney Papers and CO 273/1.
- Historiographically, the article challenges the prevailing British-centric narrative that attributed the 1841 restoration of Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin to British diplomatic pressure, reasserting agency to the Siamese court and contributing to a broader reassessment of Rama III's reign as a period of sophisticated, if under-recognized, diplomatic innovation.
