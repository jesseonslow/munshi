# The megaliths in East Malaysia II: stone urns from the Kelabit Highlands, Sarawak

Lim Teck Ghee's 1974 article examines the Stevenson Restriction Scheme (1922–28) and demonstrates how the Malayan colonial administration systematically discriminated against peasant rubber smallholders in favour of the British plantation sector. Drawing on extensive colonial correspondence and agricultural survey data, Ghee argues that the scheme was engineered by plantation capital, implemented through mechanisms that penalised smallholders, and ultimately weakened Malaya's competitive position in world rubber by accelerating Dutch East Indies expansion.

## Summary

The article traces the political economy of rubber restriction from the 1920 trade depression through the scheme's termination in 1928. Ghee establishes that the restriction lobby was dominated by the Rubber Growers' Association and that the Stevenson Committee's pivotal volte face—from requiring Dutch cooperation to recommending unilateral British restriction—was driven by plantation interests rather than any genuine concern for the peasant sector. The Malayan administration, which should have articulated the smallholder case, instead capitulated to planter pressure and allowed figures like Swettenham to misrepresent peasant positions in London, claiming smallholders were unorganised "natives" who "do not understand the situation" (p. 109).

The core of Ghee's argument concerns the mechanics of discrimination embedded in the scheme's implementation. The Duncan Scale, which set smallholder standard production at 320 pounds per mature acre, was grossly below actual yields of 733 to 1,200 pounds recorded in government surveys across the Federated Malay States. When a committee investigated the resulting peasant unrest and violence in Perak and Johore, it recommended raising the allowance to 533 pounds, but the High Commissioner scaled this down to 426 pounds, which was accepted by London. The inspection system compounded the injustice: European planters were deployed as field assessors for smallholdings, creating an obvious conflict of interest, and the Smallholder Inspection Committees established to hear appeals were dominated by the same planter and official interests that had made the original assessments (pp. 110–113).

Ghee also addresses the broader strategic consequences of the scheme. While restriction restored rubber prices and brought prosperity to Malaya in the mid-1920s, it simultaneously encouraged the Netherlands East Indies to expand production and planting, narrowing the gap between the two producers. Edward Gent, the Stevenson Committee's own secretary, warned in 1925 that the scheme was no longer serving its stated purpose of restoring plantation predominance but merely maintaining grower profits (p. 118). By 1928, the Dutch were within a few thousand acres of displacing Malaya as the world's largest rubber producer.

### Key Findings

- The Duncan Scale set smallholder standard production at 320 lbs per mature acre, while government surveys recorded actual yields ranging from 733 to 1,200 lbs per acre across the FMS (p. 111).
- Smallholders' share of the total standard production quota was 27.10%, 26.0%, and 26.47% in the first three restriction years, despite comprising 31.33% of total rubber area in bearing; plantations received 66.25–67.38% of the quota against 63.57% of the area (pp. 114–115).
- When restriction was lifted in 1929, FMS rubber exports jumped from 174,490 tons (1928) to 261,352 tons, an increase of nearly 87,000 tons almost entirely from the peasant sector, which achieved an average output of 480 lbs per mature acre against the ~200 lbs allotted during restriction (p. 115).
- Using a conservative estimate of 180 lbs per mature acre under-assessment and an average export value of 49 cents per pound across approximately 350,000 mature smallholding acres, Ghee calculates the financial loss to peasants at $173 million over the scheme's duration (p. 116).
- Plantation standard production allowances were raised from 400 to 500 lbs on 1 August 1925, and the maximum limit was removed entirely in May 1926, while smallholders remained capped at 426 lbs (p. 116).
- Between 1922 and 1928, Dutch East Indies rubber production rose from 102,000 to 229,000 tons and rubber area nearly doubled from 1,480,000 to 2,699,000 acres, directly benefiting from Malaya's unilateral restriction (p. 119, n. 45).

### Conclusion

Ghee's definitive takeaway is that the Stevenson Restriction Scheme, despite its superficial success in restoring rubber prices and Malayan prosperity, constituted a "legalised fraud" against the peasant sector and represented the most significant strategic setback to the Malayan rubber industry since its inception. The scheme was conceived, lobbied for, and administered in the interests of British plantation capital; the Malayan administration's failure to defend smallholder interests was not a matter of powerlessness but of complicity; and the long-term consequence was the acceleration of Dutch competitive expansion that threatened Malaya's pre-eminence in world rubber production.

## Context

- Primary archival sources include Colonial Office files (CO 717 series), Selangor Secretariat Files, Federal Council Proceedings, and Department of Agriculture survey reports, indicating a study grounded in both imperial and local administrative records.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malayan economic history by shifting focus from the plantation sector (previously treated by Drabble, 1967) to the peasant smallholding experience, and by demonstrating how colonial policy mechanisms produced structural inequality within the rubber industry.
