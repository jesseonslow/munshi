# The circumstances surrounding the untimely death of Jan S. Timmerman-Thijssen, governor of Malacca 1818-1823

Diane Kraal's 2010 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* investigates the unexplained death of Jan S. Timmerman-Thijssen, the Dutch Governor of Malacca who died at age 40 in January 1823 without any official inquiry being held. Drawing on Dutch archival material translated into English for the first time, Kraal identifies four individuals—Tunku Hussein of Singapore, Arong Bilawa of the Riau Bugis, the trader Hendrik Kraal, and Timmerman-Thijssen's own wife Gesina Couperus—whose financial or political grievances against the Governor raise questions that remain unresolved, while simultaneously presenting evidence of the Governor's chronic ill-health that keeps the natural-causes hypothesis viable.

## Summary

The article addresses a historiographical gap: no extended English-language study had previously examined Timmerman-Thijssen's financial and political dealings as a colonial official, and no record of an official inquiry into his death has ever been found. Kraal's central argument is that the convergence of multiple disaffected parties around the time of the Governor's death, combined with the absence of any formal investigation, creates a set of circumstances that "give rise to more questions than answers" (p. 10). The piece is deliberately structured to balance suspicion against plausibility, presenting both the suspicious elements and the medical evidence that could account for a natural death.

Kraag's narrative is built primarily on Timmerman-Thijssen's personal correspondence (held at the Centraal Bureau voor Genealogie, The Hague), which reveals a pattern of financial mismanagement stretching back to his years in Java, where he borrowed interest-free from the Orphan Fund and whose trading partnership ended in bankruptcy. This pattern repeated in Malacca, where he again stripped the Orphan Fund and accumulated debts to local burghers. The primary sources also document his recurring health problems—shingles, stomach ailments, cholera morbus—providing the counterweight to the suspicion narrative. The most dramatic piece of evidence is an 1825 affidavit by Eduard van Angelbeek, which places Hendrik Kraal in the Governor's presence during his final days and describes a last-minute attempt to secure a tin shipment for Kraal's benefit.

The article also notes a puzzling cluster of deaths among the Koek, Kraal, and Van Angelbeek families between 1825 and 1828—individuals all connected to Timmerman-Thijssen by debt and by marriage—which Kraal flags as an anomaly requiring further research but does not attempt to explain.

### Key Findings

- Timmerman-Thijssen died on 15 January 1823 at age 40; no official inquiry into his death was ever conducted, and the *East India Register and Directory* did not gazette his death until 1825 (pp. 10, 22).
- In his final act of office around 13 January 1823, Timmerman-Thijssen directed the Secretary to draft a letter requesting 400 piculs of tin be secretly forwarded to Hendrik Kraal; the request could not be fulfilled because the Malay tin trader had just died, and the Governor died one or two days later (pp. 21–22).
- Timmerman-Thijssen had borrowed 250,000 guilders from the Malacca Orphan Fund and by March 1822 had "confiscated what remained of the fund, leaving only a small residual to meet emergency commitments" (p. 19).
- In December 1822, Timmerman-Thijssen and Adriaan Koek led a Dutch contingent that forcibly seized the Johore royal regalia from Princess Hamidah, enabling the investiture of Abdul Rahman as Sultan and reducing Tunku Hussein to ruler of Singapore alone (p. 17).
- In February 1820, approximately 500 Bugis fled to Singapore under chieftain Arong Bilawa following clashes with Dutch trade controls; the British Resident in Singapore refused to hand Arong Bilawa over to the Dutch (p. 19).
- Between May 1825 and January 1828, six individuals connected to Timmerman-Thijssen by debt and marriage died in succession: Adriaan Koek (aged 66), Eduard van Angelbeek (aged 24), Abraham Kraal (aged 28), Hendrik Kraal (aged 68), Willem Kraal (aged 30), and George Koek (aged 32) (pp. 24–25).

### Conclusion

Kraag's definitive takeaway is that the circumstances of Timmerman-Thijssen's death remain genuinely indeterminate. The four identified individuals each had plausible motive—Hussein for the loss of his sovereignty claim, Arong Bilawa for trade suppression, Hendrik Kraal for unresolved debts, and Gesina Couperus for reasons obscured by her refusal of public condolences and the absence of any official death proclamation—but no firmer evidence can be drawn to implicate any of them. Equally, the Governor's documented history of chronic illness means that death by natural causes cannot be discounted. The article ultimately functions as a case for further archival research rather than a resolution.

## Context

- Primary archival collections include the Timmerman-Thijssen family letters (Centraal Bureau voor Genealogie, The Hague), the Ministerie van Kolonien records (National Archives, The Hague), and the India Office Records at the British Library (IOR R/9/12/39, IOR G/34/165, IOR R/9/20/11).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in being the first extended English-language study of Timmerman-Thijssen's administrative and financial conduct, and in translating Dutch primary sources that had previously been inaccessible to English-speaking scholars.
