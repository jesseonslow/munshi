# The Kong-Moon System in Larut: Chinese Social Relationships in Nineteenth-Century Perak

## Abstract

The Larut Wars for mining resources were among the most severe conflicts in Malaysian Chinese history. They also provide a window into how dialects, consciousness of ancestral land, and secret societies shaped Chinese society in the Malay Peninsula during the mid-nineteenth century. The tin production chain embedded both the society and industry in the *kong-moon* system, a socioeconomic unit that integrated political power, economic interests, and social concerns. Within the *kong-moon* system, the Chinese miners, tin ore dealers, smelters, traders, and coolies, became interlocking components. Together, they formed a considerable interest group, and since all of them reaped the benefits of mining, they engaged in a level of inter-cooperation that superseded any existing divisions between various ethnic groups or across different regions. The *kong-moon* system thus serves as a counterweight to arguments about irreconcilable relationships in Chinese society.

## Summary

This article re-examines the famous Larut Wars of 1861, 1865, and 1872 by proposing the *kong-moon* system—a Hakka term for a mining concession area governed by a Chinese headman—as the fundamental analytical unit for understanding Chinese social organization in mid-nineteenth-century Perak. Rather than treating the conflict as an irreconcilable Hakka-versus-Cantonese ethnic clash, as has been standard since Wynne's 1941 study, Pek argues that the two warring factions were in fact two distinct *kong-moon* production systems (Klian Pauh under the Hai San and Klian Bahru under the Ghee Hin), each an integrated socioeconomic entity that linked political authority, economic interest, and community life within the broader Malay feudal structure (pp. 25–27).

The core of the argument traces every role in the tin production chain—from the *kong-moon-choo* who held the concession and paid royalties to the Malay chief, through miners, investors (*kiow-choo*), ore dealers, smelters, shipowners, provision suppliers, revenue farmers, and coolies—and shows how these roles were distributed across dialect groups in a pattern of inter-ethnic cooperation. In Klian Pauh, for instance, Hakka and Cantonese miners worked the pits while Hokkien merchants from Penang supplied capital, provisions, and smelting capacity; in Klian Bahru, Fuichew Hakka occupied the upstream positions and Cantonese of the Ho Hup Seah handled downstream trade. The article then turns to cemetery evidence, using tombstone inscriptions and donor lists to demonstrate that while the *kong-moon* boundaries created clear social divisions between the two concessions, cultural differences (e.g., between Hokkien and Hakka) still produced sub-divisions within a single concession (pp. 39–41).

The narrative is grounded in colonial correspondence (CO 273), Birch's journals, Swettenham's journals, the *Singapore and Straits Directory*, the *Perak Government Gazette*, Pasqual's 1890s mining accounts, and Doyle's 1879 study of Larut mining, supplemented by epigraphic material from Larut cemeteries (pp. 48–50).

### Key Findings

- Captain Speedy's 1874 report recorded 26,000 Chinese in Larut, four-fifths of them miners (p. 32).
- *Kong-moon-choo* Chung Keng Kwee paid a monthly royalty of 8,000 dollars for the first six months of his Klian Pauh concession, later raised to 10,000 dollars (p. 34).
- By the mid-1860s there were thirty mining *kongsis* at Klian Pauh (twenty Chengsheng, three Fuichew, three joint) and approximately twenty mines at Klian Bahru (pp. 36, 40).
- A 1862 dispatch records Chinese tobacco at 15 cents in Penang versus 50 cents in Larut; by 1875 coolies paid 200–300 per cent above cost for necessities (pp. 34–35).
- During the 1861 war, Khoo Oon Keong advanced $60,000 to Chung Keng Kwee for military supplies, illustrating cross-ethnic capital cooperation within the *kong-moon* (p. 39).
- In 1888, Chung Keng Kwee purchased 23 per cent of Larut's total tin output, Khoo Oon Keong 8 per cent, Tye Lee 5 per cent, and Loh Ah Pang 3 per cent (pp. 37, 40–41).

### Conclusion

The *kong-moon* system demonstrates that the tin production chain, not dialect or ancestral-land identity, was the primary social organizing principle in Larut: it generated a single interest group in which every participant—miner, investor, dealer, smelter, trader, coolie—reaped benefits from cross-ethnic and cross-regional cooperation, and the Larut Wars were best understood as a contest between two such systems rather than between ethnic factions (pp. 46–47).

## Context

- Primary archival collections: CO 273 (Straits Settlements original correspondence, UK National Archives), Birch's journals, Swettenham's journals, *Singapore and Straits Directory* (1877–1910), *Perak Government Gazette* (1888–1890), and Larut cemetery epigraphic material.
- Historiographically, the article challenges the essentialist dialect-group framework that has dominated Larut scholarship since Wynne (1941) and offers an economic-integration model applicable to comparable mining districts such as Selangor and Sungai Ujong (pp. 47–48).
