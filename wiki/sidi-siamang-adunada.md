# Sidi, Siamang, Adunada

K. T. Joseph's 2008 survey traces the agricultural history of Peninsular Malaysia from the 1780s to the early twenty-first century, arguing that Indonesia—first as the Netherlands East Indies, then as an independent nation—has been the indispensable source of planting materials, scientific research, and labour for virtually every major export crop in the Malayan plantation economy. Published in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, the article reframes a narrative that has traditionally centred on British colonial agency by foregrounding the Indonesian contribution as foundational rather than peripheral.

## Summary

Joseph structures the article around a sequence of crops—spices, pepper, sugar, coffee, rubber, and oil palm—demonstrating that in each case the planting material originated in the Indonesian archipelago. The early Penang pepper enterprise drew its vines from Aceh; nutmeg and clove seedlings came from the Moluccas after the British occupation of 1796; rubber bud-grafting was invented at Bogor and Medan; and the four mother oil palms from the Bogor Botanical Gardens supplied all early Malayan plantings. The author draws on a wide range of secondary literature—colonial administrative reports, agricultural bulletins, and modern plantation histories—to construct this parallel history, occasionally citing primary sources such as the 1891 correspondence between the Acting Collector of Kuala Langat and the Selangor Resident, or the 1920 statement by Dr Rutgers of AVROS.

A second thread concerns labour migration. Joseph documents the official encouragement of Javanese settlement in Selangor under Sultan Samad (r. 1857–1898), where immigrants were allocated two acres each, granted three years' rent-free tenure, and provided with seed, fertiliser, and farm animals. These Javanese settlers developed the *dusun* (village) agricultural culture that underpinned coffee and later rubber smallholding. The article then bridges to the present, where approximately 200,000 legal Indonesian workers sustain the oil palm industry in Peninsular Malaysia alone, a dependency Joseph characterises as a *sine qua non* whose absence would cause total industry collapse.

The article also highlights a structural contrast between the two territories' approaches to market management. The Dutch East Indies refused to join the Stevenson restriction scheme of the 1920s, leaving smallholder rubber production to market forces, while Malaya's self-imposed restrictions stagnated its own industry. This divergence, Joseph argues, allowed the Indies to produce half the world's rubber supply by 1927 and to develop a more dynamic smallholder economy.

### Key Findings

- By 1920, bud grafting developed at Bogor and Medan had doubled average rubber yields from 500 lbs/acre to 1,000 lbs/acre; by the early 1930s, clones AVROS 49 and Tjir 1 raised productivity to 4,400 lbs/acre (pp. 11–12).
- All early oil palm plantings in Peninsular Malaysia descended from four mother palms originally from West Africa, planted in the Bogor Botanical Gardens in 1848; by 1941, 31,600 hectares were under oil palm in Malaya, all from this stock (pp. 13–14).
- In 1960, Malaya had only 135,000 acres of oil palm, while Sumatra was producing more than half the world's palm oil supply; by 1999, Peninsular Malaysia had reached 2 million hectares (p. 14).
- Javanese settlers in Selangor were allocated 2 acres each with three years' rent-free tenure, free seed, fertiliser, and farm animals, a scheme initiated under British Resident J. G. Davidson during Sultan Samad's reign (p. 9).
- The Netherlands East Indies produced half the world's rubber supply by 1927 after refusing to join the Stevenson restriction scheme, while Malayan production stagnated under self-imposed quotas (p. 12).
- In 2006, oil palm plantings in Malaysia covered 4.1 million hectares (2.2 million in Peninsular Malaysia), supported by approximately 200,000 legal Indonesian workers; at an average price of RM2,000/ton, January 2007 output of 14 million tons represented a value of RM28 billion (p. 15).

### Conclusion

Joseph's definitive takeaway is that the agricultural development of Peninsular Malaysia cannot be understood without acknowledging Indonesia's continuous and foundational role—from the provision of all initial planting materials, through pioneering research at Bogor, Medan, and Pasuruan, to the present-day labour force that sustains the palm oil industry. He calls for institutionalised Malaysia–Indonesia cooperation in downstream processing, buffer stock management, and joint historical research, arguing that the archives in The Hague and Jakarta remain an untapped resource for understanding this shared agricultural past.

## Context

- The article draws heavily on secondary literature (colonial administrative reports, agricultural bulletins, and plantation histories) rather than primary archival material; Joseph explicitly identifies the Dutch and Indonesian archives as a neglected field requiring future investigation (p. 17).
- Historiographically, the piece contributes to a growing body of work that challenges the Anglo-centric framing of Malayan plantation history by re-centring the Indonesian archipelago as the origin point for both biological materials and agronomic innovation.
