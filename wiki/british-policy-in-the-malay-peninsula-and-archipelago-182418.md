# British policy in the Malay Peninsula and Archipelago, 1824–1871

Nicholas Tarling's 1957 study, originally his Cambridge PhD thesis (1956), provides the first comprehensive analysis of British policy in the Malay Peninsula and Archipelago between 1824 and 1871, drawing extensively on previously unused Foreign Office, Colonial Office, East India Company, and India Office records (p. 5). The work argues that British policy in the region was shaped by a persistent tension between broad strategic imperatives—protecting India, securing the China trade route, and excluding rival powers—and the narrower commercial interests of the Straits Settlements, whose entrepot trade depended on the independence of native states (pp. 16–18).

## Summary

Tarling demonstrates that the Treaty of 1824, which divided the Malay world between British and Dutch spheres of influence, was a strategic settlement that satisfied neither of the two principal British commercial constituencies. The Straits merchants, who had hoped for political influence over the native states to protect their entrepot trade, found the treaty's territorial dispositions inadequate; the Java merchants, who had expected liberal commercial access, discovered that the treaty's second article permitted the Dutch to maintain protective duties on British textiles (pp. 16, 84–86). The subsequent history of the period is, in Tarling's framing, "the study of the reaction between local commercial considerations and broad strategic policies dictated by wider imperial interests" (p. 18).

On the Peninsula, the most significant finding is the divergence between the cautious policy of the Supreme Government at Calcutta—which sought to avoid Siamese jealousy—and the more assertive practice of the local Governors at Penang and Singapore. From Fullerton through Cavenagh, the Governors used the suppression of piracy as a vehicle for establishing political influence over the native states, preserving their integrity against Siamese encroachment and interstate conflict (pp. 26–27, 51). Cavenagh's unauthorized bombardment of Trengganu in 1862, which violated the Burney treaty and provoked disapproval from both the India Office and the House of Commons, marked the breaking point of this system (pp. 70–72). The transfer of the Straits Settlements to the Colonial Office in 1867 and the arrival of Governor Ord, who accepted Siamese claims over the northern states, effectively reversed the Governors' life work (pp. 79–80).

In the Archipelago, the narrative centres on the long dispute over the interpretation of the treaty's commercial articles and the Dutch expansion in Sumatra. The Foreign Office, initially guided by the India Board's "vessels" interpretation of Article 2, was progressively drawn by the Board of Trade and manufacturing interests toward an "origins" interpretation that proved untenable (pp. 89–104). The failure of the Java negotiations in the 1830s and 1840s led the Foreign Office to challenge Dutch territorial claims in the native states, a policy that found its most dramatic expression in the support for James Brooke's enterprise in Borneo and the founding of Labuan in 1846 (pp. 144–147, 191–195). By the 1860s, however, the prospect of a general settlement with the Netherlands—driven by the threat of French expansion in Indo-China and the liberalization of Dutch commercial policy—rendered this challenge obsolete. The Sumatra Treaty of 1871, which recognized Dutch sovereignty over the east coast in exchange for the abolition of differential duties, represented the Foreign Office's definitive acceptance of Dutch hegemony in the Archipelago, much to the dismay of the Straits merchants (pp. 182–183).

### Key Findings

- The Treaty of 1824's commercial provisions were negotiated by the India Board with a focus on the outer islands, leaving the Java trade inadequately protected; the Dutch imposed duties of 25 per cent on British textiles (free for Dutch goods) within months of signature, and the British Government was unable to enforce a remedy for over two decades (pp. 89–104).
- The Straits Governors, operating with considerable autonomy from Calcutta, established the framework for British political development in Malaya by preserving the integrity of native states through naval operations against piracy and the cultivation of relationships with individual rulers, particularly Temenggong Ibrahim of Johore (pp. 51–53, 83).
- Cavenagh's bombardment of Trengganu in November 1862 was an unauthorized act of war that violated the Burney treaty of 1826; the India Office disapproved his haste "without deciding the doubtful question whether Trengganu was an independent state," and the episode weakened the position of the Temenggong's supporters in Singapore (pp. 70–72).
- The Foreign Office's challenge to Dutch territorial claims in the Archipelago, initiated in 1838 and expanded through the Borneo venture, was fundamentally a by-product of the failed Java commercial negotiations rather than a coherent strategic policy; it was abandoned as soon as a general settlement with the Netherlands appeared achievable (pp. 144, 151–152).
- James Brooke's career as a British agent was enabled by the temporary hostility of the Foreign Office to the Dutch in the 1840s; his dismissal from the Governorship of Labuan in 1852 and the subsequent Commission of Inquiry reflected both Radical parliamentary pressure and the Government's decision to abandon a forward Borneo policy in favour of rapprochement with the Netherlands (pp. 200–205).
- The Sumatra Treaty of 1871 was secured through a complex negotiation in which the Foreign Office traded the recognition of Dutch sovereignty over the east coast (including the abandonment of the "Aceh clause" of 1824) for the abolition of differential duties by 1873; the treaty was initially rejected by the Dutch Second Chamber but passed in December 1871 (pp. 176–182).

### Conclusion

Tarling's definitive takeaway is that the territorial and political configuration of the Malay world by 1871 was the product not of any single grand design but of the continuous interaction between strategic necessity, commercial interest, and the independent agency of local officials. The Governors of the Straits Settlements, working with scanty naval forces and often in defiance of their superiors' instructions, established the tradition of British association with integral Malay states that would define colonial policy for the next century; their work was partially obscured by the dramatic intervention of 1873–74 in Perak and Selangor, which was prompted not by the needs of Straits commerce but by the Colonial Office's fear of foreign intervention in a region where Dutch and Siamese influence had been successfully excluded (p. 83).

## Context

- The work draws on the Foreign Office and Colonial Office documents at the Public Record Office, and the East India Company and India Office records at the Commonwealth Relations Office, none of which had previously been used for a similar purpose (p. 5).
- Tarling acknowledges that the private papers of Straits officials, particularly the Governors, remain concealed, creating a gap in the personal dimension of the narrative that is less serious for the Calcutta and Simla Secretaries whose interest in the Malay world was comparatively limited (p. 5).
