# Joseph Balestier

Joseph Balestier (c. 1785–after 1852) served as the first American consul in Singapore from 1833 to 1852, a tenure of nineteen years during which he shaped the early American commercial and diplomatic presence in the Straits Settlements. Arriving from a respectable New England family at approximately 49 years of age, Balestier combined his consular duties with mercantile and agricultural pursuits, acting as agent for the majority of American vessels calling at the port and operating a sugar plantation on the island's outskirts [[1, pp. 108–109]](#ref-1). His career was defined by the legal ambiguities of the 1815 US–British Convention of Commerce, which had excluded Singapore from the list of ports open to American trade, and by the personal and financial constraints of a consular post that paid approximately $100 in annual fees while demanding an expenditure of $3,000 to $5,000 per year to maintain the dignity of the office [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1).

## Consular Appointment and Legal Constraints

Balestier's commission was originally made out for Rhio, a dependency of the Dutch East Indies, with the ambiguous phrasing "and such other places as are nearer thereto than to the Residence of any other Consul or Vice Consul of the United States" [[1, p. 109]](#ref-1). Although he resided in Singapore, where the commercial activity was concentrated, a revised letter from the Secretary of State in February 1834 restricted his authority to ports "within the same allegiance," effectively excluding Singapore, which was not under Dutch sovereignty [[1, p. 110]](#ref-1). The Court of Directors in India had, however, permitted Americans to trade at Singapore in a Public General Letter of 4 May 1831, on the grounds that the island was united with Malacca and Prince of Wales Island as the Straits Settlements, and Article III of the 1815 Convention had granted Americans the right to trade with Prince of Wales Island [[1, pp. 110–111]](#ref-1). It was not until 12 April 1837 that Balestier learned of his formal appointment as Consul for Singapore, dated 6 July 1836, and in June of that year the Honourable East India Company gave its assent for him to act in that capacity [[1, p. 111]](#ref-1).

The practical effect of the legal exclusion was that American traders resorted to the island of Batam, 14 miles from Singapore, transshipping cargoes in local boats. Despite "positive verbal assurance" from the Governor of the Straits Settlements that American ships would not be molested, American traders remained reluctant to trade openly until June 1836, when American trade at Singapore was finally declared legal [[1, pp. 111–112]](#ref-1).

## Commercial and Agricultural Activities

In his personal capacity, Balestier was a merchant and planter. In 1837, of the 16 American vessels that entered Singapore roads, he acted as agent for 15, handling their incoming and outgoing cargoes, provisioning, and passage arrangements [[1, p. 110]](#ref-1). His godown stocked French wines, Manila cheroots, Trueman and Hanbury's Beer, American bread, Holland gin, sail cloth, champagne, and Eau de Cologne [[1, p. 111]](#ref-1). He also engaged in shipping, advertising the Spanish barque *Trinidad* for Manila in October 1837 and the Dutch brig *Eagle* for sale in May 1840, though both ventures appear to have failed [[1, p. 111]](#ref-1).

Balestier's most ambitious enterprise was a 40-acre sugar plantation two miles outside the town, equipped with a steam engine, distilling apparatus, and a 14-foot canal that carried cane to the mills and the finished product to ships in the roads. The mill was capable of producing 5,000 to 6,000 lbs of sugar per day, and the plantation yielded an average of 20 to 25 *pikuls* (a unit of weight) of raw sugar per acre [[1, pp. 111–112]](#ref-1). Governor Butterworth noted in his 1844 annual land report that Balestier "has laid out large sums of money in clearing ground for the cultivation of the land and in erecting a steam engine for the manufacture of sugar" [[1, p. 112]](#ref-1). The enterprise ultimately failed, Balestier attributing the loss to the higher duty charged in Great Britain on Singapore-grown sugar compared to that from other British colonies, and to inadequate capital for large-scale operation [[1, p. 112]](#ref-1).

## Diplomatic Missions and Retirement

In August 1849, Balestier was appointed special agent at a salary of $4,500 per year to negotiate treaties in South Eastern Asia [[1, p. 114]](#ref-1). He succeeded in concluding a convention of peace, friendship, and trade with the Sultan of Brunei, granting US citizens property rights and most-favoured-nation privileges; apart from Britain, the United States was the only other European power to sign a treaty with Brunei [[1, p. 114]](#ref-1). His mission to Siam, however, ended in failure. Arriving on 24 March 1850, Balestier stayed only a month, and a letter by Phraya Siphiphat recorded that he "became angry, cut matters off, not having us speak, saying it was a waste of time" and was "insulting and threatening in the hall of assembly" [[1, p. 115]](#ref-1). The American–Siamese treaty was not obtained until 1856 [[1, p. 115]](#ref-1).

Balestier's health deteriorated following the deaths of his son Joseph Warren Revere Balestier in March 1844 and his wife Marie Revere Balestier in August 1847 [[1, p. 112]](#ref-1). He left Singapore for America in May 1848 and, upon his return in May 1851, found himself "in a most infirm state of health" [[1, p. 115]](#ref-1). On 21 January 1852, he wrote to the Secretary of State to resign as US Consul at Singapore [[1, p. 116]](#ref-1). Governor Butterworth's farewell letter of 1848 recorded "the united testimony of the whole of the Government Officers" to Balestier's "urbanity of manners and courtesy of demeanour" and his standing as "one of the oldest and most respected Residents in the place" [[1, pp. 115–116]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

Sharom Ahmat's 1966 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* remains the principal biographical study of Balestier's consular career. Drawing primarily on the T-128 series—26 volumes of despatches from US consuls in Singapore to the State Department, available in microfilm at the University of Singapore Library—supplemented by the *Singapore Free Press*, *Logan's Journal*, and Governor Butterworth's correspondence, the article recovers the consular record as a source for understanding commercial and cultural exchange in the Straits Settlements during the 1830s and 1850s [1, p. 109, n. 6]. The study situates Balestier within the broader context of American maritime expansion following the War of Independence and the closure of West Indian trade routes, and it highlights the tension between the legal framework of the 1815 Convention and the commercial realities of a free port that had been founded after the treaty's signature. No subsequent comprehensive study of Balestier appears in the Society's literature, though his Brunei treaty is referenced in passing in later works on American activity in North Borneo [1, p. 114, n. 46].
