# William Girdlestone Shellabear

William Girdlestone Shellabear (1862–1948) was a British Royal Engineers officer who became a Methodist missionary, Malay scholar, Bible translator, and Islamicist, serving in colonial Malaya from 1887 to 1916 before relocating to the United States, where he taught Arabic and Islamic studies until his death [[1, pp. 37–38]](#ref-1). His singular conviction—that the Malay language and culture were fully adequate to modern life and Christian existence—drove a career that profoundly shaped Malayan publishing, education, and letters [[1, p. 37]](#ref-1).

## Early Career and Conversion to Missionary Work

Born on 7 August 1862 at Holkam Hall in Norfolk, where his father managed the Earl of Leicester's estate, Shellabear experienced a childhood marked by the rigid class hierarchies of rural England [[1, p. 39]](#ref-1). He attended school in Folkestone, Kent, where open-air revival meetings first exposed him to evangelical Christianity, and subsequently enrolled at Haileybury College in 1876 [[1, p. 40]](#ref-1). In 1879 he sat for examinations to enter the Royal Academy at Woolwich, and on 22 February 1882 was commissioned a lieutenant in the Royal Engineers [[1, p. 41]](#ref-1).

Assigned to Fort Blockhouse in Gosport, Shellabear was immersed in an environment of intense evangelical activity. On 13 December 1885, after reading a religious tract based on Romans 6:23, he knelt and prayed, "accepting God's gift and promising to be his follower" [[1, p. 41]](#ref-1). He subsequently courted Fanny Kealy, daughter of a local physician, and in the spring of 1886 resolved to abandon his military career for missionary work [[1, p. 43]](#ref-1).

Shellabear arrived in Singapore on 7 January 1887 aboard the troopship *Himalaya*, where he was tasked with commanding a company of Malay soldiers assisting in harbour defences [[1, p. 43]](#ref-1). Through daily contact with his subordinates and the Methodist missionaries, particularly Rev. William Oldham, he developed proficiency in spoken Malay and began translating tracts and hymns [[1, pp. 43–44]](#ref-1). He married Fanny Kealy on 30 July 1890 and returned to Singapore in September of that year [[1, p. 45]](#ref-1).

## Missionary Work, Publishing, and Malay Scholarship

In 1890 Shellabear founded the Mission Press, serving as its chief printer, author, editor, and manager for a decade [[1, p. 37]](#ref-1). By February 1894 the Press had produced tens of thousands of Bibles, almanacs, school textbooks, and tracts, and was well on its way to financial self-sufficiency [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1). A major breakthrough came in 1891 when the British and Foreign Bible Society contracted with the Press to produce the *Gospel of Matthew* in Romanised Malay, establishing a long-standing partnership [[1, p. 47]](#ref-1).

Shellabear's first major scholarly achievement was his edition of the *Sejarah Melayu*, published in 1896, which sold 300 copies in its first six weeks and secured his election to the Council of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society [[1, p. 52]](#ref-1). He followed this with the *Practical Malay Grammar* (1899) and the *Malay-English Vocabulary* (1902), both of which went through multiple editions over a forty-year period [[1, pp. 37]](#ref-1). In 1908 he published the first complete romanised edition of the *Hikayat Hang Tuah*, compiled from manuscripts borrowed from R.N. Bland, Raja Abdul Jalil of Perak, and other sources, with editorial assistance from Sulaiman bin Muhammad Nur [[1, pp. 55–56]](#ref-1).

His most ambitious project was the translation of the Bible into Malay. Seconded to the British and Foreign Bible Society in 1900, Shellabear produced the first Malay translation based on the Revised Version Greek text rather than the *Textus Receptus*, and the first to adopt a vernacular rather than classical literary style [[1, p. 54]](#ref-1). The complete Bible was published in August 1912 [[1, p. 65]](#ref-1).

## Plantation Years and Final Period in Singapore

In 1909 Shellabear was appointed to oversee the Mission Plantation at Sitiawan in the Dindings, where he also managed his own 160-acre Kampong Acheh Estate near Lumut [[1, pp. 58–59]](#ref-1). He cycled an estimated 30 miles daily between estates, handled an average of 75 letters per week as District Superintendent for the Federated Malay States, and continued proofing the Malay Bible [[1, p. 59]](#ref-1). By late 1910, recurrent malaria and physical strain were taking their toll, compounded by news of his wife Emma's mental breakdown [[1, p. 60]](#ref-1).

Returning to Singapore in 1912, Shellabear resumed editing the *Malaysia Message* and turned his scholarly attention to Islam, presenting papers to the Straits Philosophical Society on "The Influence of Islam on the Malay Race" (1913) and "Islam as seen through its Literature" (1915) [[1, pp. 62–68]](#ref-1). He was elected President of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society in March 1914 [[1, p. 65]](#ref-1). His wife Emma opened a school for Malay girls in October 1914, which by 1915 had 34 students and received favourable inspection from the Straits Settlements Director of Education [[1, p. 66]](#ref-1).

## Legacy and Documentation

Shellabear's health collapsed in 1917 following a dispute with W.T. Cherry over mission policy, and he was forced to leave Malaya permanently [[1, p. 69]](#ref-1). In the United States he taught at Drew University and later at Hartford Seminary, where he held the Duncan Black MacDonald chair of Arabic and Islam from 1925 [[1, pp. 38]](#ref-1). He served as an editor of *Muslim World* from 1923 until his death in 1948, and continued to write and translate Malay literature, including two book-length *syair* attempting to render the Bible story in classical Malay poetic form [[1, p. 70]](#ref-1).

Robert Hunt's 1993 biographical study, drawing extensively on Shellabear's unpublished autobiography and personal correspondence held in the Hartford Seminary Foundation Library Archives, the Methodist Church of Singapore Archives, and the Seminari Theoloji Malaysia Archives, remains the definitive account of his life [[1, pp. 37]](#ref-1). Hunt argues that Shellabear's legacy lies not only in his literary accomplishments but in his rare demonstration of "affection undimmed by paternalism, and loyalty unmarred by the expectation of personal gain" within colonial society [[1, p. 70]](#ref-1).
