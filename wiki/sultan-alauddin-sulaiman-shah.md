# Sultan Alauddin Sulaiman Shah

## The Formative Years

Sultan Alauddin Sulaiman Shah (1865–1898) was the ninth Sultan of Selangor, ascending to the throne in 1898 upon the death of his grandfather, Sultan Abdul Samad. Born at *kampong* (village) Tengah, Kuala Selangor, on 11 September 1865, he was the eldest son of Raja Muda Musa and his first wife, Che Rahimah [[1, pp. 2–3]](#ref-1). His childhood was shaped by the Selangor Civil War (1867–73), which displaced his family to Singapore before their return to Kuala Langat, where he received his early education at one of the first Malay vernacular boys' schools in the state [[1, pp. 2–3]](#ref-1). The district officer's wife, Emily Innes, recalled him as "a charming little boy... a bright, gentle-mannered boy of about eleven, who could both read and write Malay fluently, a most unusual accomplishment with Malays of rank" [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). His father, Musa, was a figure of considerable eccentricity—described by Swettenham as "essentially weak" and by Douglas as "boorish"—who broke with aristocratic convention by sending his son to school and apprenticing him in sugar cultivation at Batu Kawan estate in Province Wellesley [[1, pp. 3–4]](#ref-1).

## The Plantation Fiasco and Appointment as Raja Muda

Musa's sudden death from dropsy on 7 July 1884 left the nineteen-year-old Sulaiman to inherit a 1,100-acre plantation enterprise that was already in difficulty. The sugar venture collapsed within months, with only a modest contract to supply 12 *pikuls* (units of weight) of sugar monthly to a Kuala Lumpur headman, while the coconut grove fell into neglect [[1, pp. 6–7]](#ref-1). Gullick concludes that "Sulaiman was not gifted as a man of business" [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1).

The succession to the office of Raja Muda was complicated by the ambitions of Raja Mahmud, son of Tunku Panglima Raja Berkat, a charismatic but volatile figure who had led forces in the civil war. British officials, unwilling to see Mahmud in the role, arranged for his transfer to Pahang as adviser to British Agent Hugh Clifford, clearing the way for Sulaiman's appointment in 1887 [[1, pp. 7–8]](#ref-1). The installation was further delayed by the death of Mahmud's father, which gave the latter a consolation place on the state council [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1).

As Raja Muda, Sulaiman occupied a position straddling two worlds: he represented his grandfather at Jugra while participating in the emerging colonial bureaucracy in Kuala Lumpur [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1). His 1890 request for an English tutor led directly to the foundation of the Malay Raja School in Kuala Lumpur, of which he was among the first pupils [[1, pp. 8–9]](#ref-1). He built a new mosque near the *astana* (palace) at Kuala Langat, personally designing the carved wooden chair, pillar mouldings, and stained glass windows [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1).

## Religious and Social Reform

Sulaiman inherited from his father the oversight of Islamic affairs and proved well suited to the task. He secured the appointment of the first state *kadi* (Islamic judge), Haji Jafar, and introduced registers and standard certificates for marriage and divorce records, addressing what officials described as "a class of native affairs which owing to neglect were fast attaining a state of chaos" [[1, pp. 10–11]](#ref-1). In 1894 he presented a pamphlet on matrimonial law to the colonial government; Gullick characterises it as "thoughtful rather than radical," and its minimalist views eventually found expression in the FMS Mohamedan Laws Enactment of 1904 [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1).

He was also a pioneer of Malay girls' education in Selangor, establishing a school at Kuala Langat at his own expense and providing transport in curtained bullock carts to overcome parental objections to sending girls out of the home [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1). His 1896 Klang girls' school offered a curriculum of Koranic reading, literacy, and traditional women's handicrafts [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1).

Sulaiman's marriage in 1891 to Tunku Maharum, daughter of the former regent Tunku Kudin and the Sultan's own daughter Raja Arfah, was a union of high rank but troubled by the bride's difficult family background [[1, pp. 12–13]](#ref-1). The couple had five children, including a son, Tunku Musa'eddin [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1).

## Legacy and Documentation

Sulaiman's reign was brief, ending with his death in 1898, but his formative years as Raja Muda established him as a reformer of quiet but genuine commitment. Contemporaries described him as "an authority on the Koran and Mohammedan law" who "lives an abstemious life" and was "soon at work... attending to state business" [[1, p. 14]](#ref-1). His woodcarving talent, inherited from a Bugis tradition of hands-on royal labour, found expression in the decorative work of mosques and palaces throughout his career [[1, pp. 3–4]](#ref-1).

The principal modern treatment of his life remains J. M. Gullick's 2008 article in the *JMBRAS*, which draws extensively on the unpublished Kuala Langat Journal (1882–1885), the Douglas diary, Selangor Government Gazette reports, and the *Selangor Journal* (1892–97), supplemented by Emily Innes' *The Chersonese with the Gilding Off* (1885) for contemporary observations of court life [[1, pp. 15–18]](#ref-1).
