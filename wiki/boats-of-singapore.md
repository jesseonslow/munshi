# Boats of Singapore

Virginia Matheson and Paul Tichell composed this obituary for Professor Cyril Skinner (1924–1986), Chairman of the Department of Indonesian and Malay at Monash University, who died suddenly in Penang in December 1986. The piece is a eulogy that simultaneously functions as a concise intellectual portrait, arguing that Skinner's greatest contribution to Malay studies was revealing the Malay world as "a robust, active and sometimes innovative participant in the international world" rather than a closed or isolated one (p. 102).

## Summary

The obituary traces Skinner's career from his 1955 appointment at the University of Singapore through his 1962 move to Monash University, where he built the Department of Indonesian and Malay into a centre for the study of traditional Malay literature. His doctoral thesis at SOAS, London, analysed a 17th-century *syair* composed by a Malay court scribe at Makassar, and this established the method he would follow throughout his career: setting a text in its specific time and place, describing its provenance and editorial history, presenting it in Malay with a facing English translation, and annotating it with an extraordinary range of contemporary sources (p. 99).

The authors identify two defining features of Skinner's scholarship. First, his focus on "transitional" writers and areas — individuals like Ahmad Rijaluddin and Munshi Abdullah who stood between the traditional Malay world and European influence, and geographically ambiguous zones like northern Malaya and southern Thailand where Malay, Thai, Burmese, and European cultures intersected (p. 100). Second, his pioneering approach to translation, rendering traditional Malay into idiomatic, sometimes colloquial English that made the material "lively, credible and a pleasure to read" (p. 100). His command of both Malay and Thai — a rare combination — allowed him to exploit Thai archival sources from the National Library in Bangkok that remained largely untapped by other scholars (p. 101).

The obituary also captures Skinner's distinctive authorial voice: his unashamed use of parentheses, his cross-cultural allusions (from Marlowe to Kipling to the Roman admiral Claudius Pulcher), and his wit, all of which made his scholarship accessible and enjoyable. The authors note that his works were never rushed but left to "settle, mature, and be re-written," sometimes for a decade (p. 102).

### Key Findings

- Skinner's doctoral thesis analysed a 17th-century *syair* by a Malay court scribe at Makassar, establishing his signature method of contextualizing texts within their intellectual and historical milieu (p. 99).
- His major monographs include *Hikayat Perintah Negeri Benggala* (KITLV, The Hague, 1982, 198 pp.) and *The Battle for Junk Ceylon* (KITLV Bibliotheca Indonesia No. 25, Dordrecht, 1985), the latter containing detailed annotations on early 19th-century warfare techniques, strategy, and tactics drawn from the Malay text (p. 99).
- He was one of the very few scholars who mastered both Malay and Thai, and his research on Kedah, Kelantan, and Trengganu drew extensively on Thai sources from the National Library, Bangkok (p. 101).
- His 1978 article "Transitional Malay Literature" in *BKI* (134, 4, pp. 466–87) articulated his central thesis that writers like Ahmad Rijaluddin and Munshi Abdullah produced material revealing "a wider range of options open to his society than would have seemed possible to his predecessors" (p. 100).
- He learned Malayalam for several years while working on *Hikayat Perintah Negeri Benggala*, and listed "learning Thai" as his recreation in the Australian *Who's Who* (p. 100).

### Conclusion

Matheson and Tichell present Skinner as a scholar whose method — meticulous philology combined with an encyclopaedic command of contemporary sources and a gift for cross-cultural analogy — fundamentally changed how the Malay world was understood in Western scholarship. His legacy, they argue, is not merely a body of texts but a demonstration that Malay literary and historical sources, properly contextualized, reveal a dynamic and internationally engaged civilization.

## Context

- The obituary draws on Skinner's published monographs and articles as its primary evidence, supplemented by the authors' personal recollections as his colleagues and students.
- The piece contributes to the historiography of Malay studies by documenting the methodological shift Skinner represented: from purely philological text-editing toward a contextual, source-rich approach that integrated archival, linguistic, and comparative evidence.
