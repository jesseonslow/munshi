# Cargo boats of the east coast of Malaya

C.A. Gibson-Hill published this article in 1949 in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, presenting a fieldwork-based catalogue of the locally built cargo-carrying boats operating on the east coast of the Malay Peninsula south of the Siamese frontier. The article's central thesis is that the dominant vessels in this trade—the Pinas-Bedar group—represent a hybrid maritime tradition in which European hull forms are combined with Chinese sail plans, a synthesis that sharply distinguishes the east coast from the west coast, where carrying boat designs are entirely extra-liminal.

## Summary

The article addresses the absence of a systematic account of locally built boats in Malayan waters, noting that the principal published data prior to this study occurred in H. Warington Smyth's 1902 paper, which did not attempt a comprehensive survey and which has been superseded by subsequent changes. Gibson-Hill groups the cargo boats by sail plan into four categories in order of their importance in the local trade: the Pinas-Bedar group, adapted fishing boats, Chinese-owned motor launches, and Chinese sailing boats (the Singapore Trader or Tongkang and the Gulf of Siam Trader). The Pinas-Bedar group handles the greater part of the commerce, with hulls largely or completely on European lines and a sail plan partly Chinese and partly European in origin.

The article explores the environmental and navigational constraints that shaped these designs: shallow estuaries with shifting sand bars that limit draught to four or five feet in most cases, the north-east monsoon (*Musim tutup kuala*) that renders the coast a lee shore from October to March, and the Tenggara winds that raise a short, choppy sea in the extremely shallow waters. These factors explain why the boats are of relatively shallow draught, why they use dipping lugs that can be set and let down quickly, and why local knowledge of conditions is essential to safe navigation. Gibson-Hill also traces the etymological and design origins of the various types, identifying the Pinas as a corruption of the French *pinasse* and the Katar as derived from the English *Cutter*, and discusses the local tradition attributing the Pinas hull to a French frigate wrecked on the Trengganu coast some seventy to eighty years before the article was written.

The study draws on fieldwork conducted in July 1940, June 1947, and July–August 1948, supplemented by registered hull measurements from Kuala Trengganu and direct observation of boats under sail. The article includes profile drawings and photographic plates documenting the major designs, and it notes that local builders do not work to set designs, making a complete descriptive list with basic lines impossible at present.

### Key Findings

- The Pinas, the largest local craft, measures between 60 and 70 feet stem to stern with a breadth of 15–17 feet and a depth of 7–8 feet; registered examples show capacities of 1,047 to 1,230 piculs, with a maximum speed of 5–6 knots (pp. 106–107).
- The smaller Bedar (Luang Sudu), at 30–35 feet, achieves speeds of 8 knots in good sailing weather with a total sail area of approximately 500 square feet (p. 110).
- The Pinas is built at Kuala Trengganu over approximately 10 months by a gang of 20 men, and is employed mostly on runs between Kuala Trengganu and Bangkok, making about three trips per year during the south-west monsoon season (p. 106).
- The Gulf of Siam Trader, a Chinese-built boat of 55–65 feet, carries a cargo capacity of 800–1,000 piculs; one such boat reached the Kemaman coast from China in March 1948 with 109 persons on board (p. 123).
- During the Japanese occupation, the Japanese attempted to build large numbers of heavy hulls along Tongkang lines at Mersing, Pontian, Kuantan, Chukai, and Kuala Trengganu, but the great majority were destroyed by Allied bombing before they put to sea (p. 121).
- The Singapore Trader (Tongkang) built at Trengganu yards measures 110 feet stem to stern, 29 feet beam, 7 feet draught, with 73 tons register (p. 121).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive historical takeaway is that the east coast of Malaya represents a distinctive maritime zone in which local boat-building traditions have produced a hybrid fleet—European hulls married to Chinese rigging—that is fundamentally different from the west coast, where carrying boat designs are entirely of foreign origin. The article serves as a baseline catalogue of these designs at a moment of transition, noting that changes have already occurred since Warington Smyth's 1902 survey and that more will follow, and it explicitly positions itself as an introductory contribution toward a complete descriptive list that will require further fieldwork.

## Context

- Gibson-Hill was a colonial-era anthropologist and ethnographer; his fieldwork was conducted during official visits to Trengganu and Kelantan, and he acknowledges assistance from Customs officers, the Director of Education, and the State Medical Officer, reflecting the administrative networks through which such surveys were typically facilitated.
- Historiographically, the article extends and corrects Warington Smyth's 1902 survey, providing the first systematic post-war catalogue of east coast cargo boats and documenting the hybrid European-Chinese design tradition that characterises the region.
