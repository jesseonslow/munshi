# Kuala Lumpur in 1884?

J.M. Gullick published this short identification note in 1959, addressing the problem of dating and locating an undated photograph from Sir Roland Braddell's family album. His thesis is that internal topographical and architectural evidence permits a confident identification of the image as a view of Kuala Lumpur taken from the slopes of Bukit Nanas in or about 1883–84, making it a rare surviving visual record of the town before its rapid brick-and-tile expansion in the later 1880s.

## Summary

The photograph in question comes from an album of some thirty undated prints that Sir Roland Braddell presented to the MBRAS. Dr Gibson-Hill had already narrowed the Singapore views to the 1875–1885 window and placed several Malayan views more precisely (Birch and Davidson's 1875 Lukut/Selangor/Perak tour, Major McNair's 1875/6 Perak War series, a Speedy guard at Taiping between 1875 and 1877, and a Sungai Ujong wedding scene from late 1878 or January 1879). The print under discussion shares the same size, quality, and age as the rest of the collection, so Gullick proceeds on the assumption that it depicts a Malayan scene of approximately the same period.

Gullick's identification rests on a convergence of topographical features: a confluence of two comparatively small rivers, a central block of Chinese-style buildings, a mining pool, government or police buildings on higher ground behind the Padang, and—most critically—a substantial timber bridge. He argues that the conjuncture of a Chinese village with a competent engineered bridge is unique for the Malay States of the 1880s, when tin-mining magnates typically saved money and let their men cross rivers by boat. He then cross-references the photograph against a modern map, showing that the relative positions of the river junction, the bridge, and the government buildings correspond to the Klang/Gombak confluence, the Market Street bridge, and the Selangor Government offices on Bluff Road.

The dating is narrowed further by what is *absent* from the image. The Klang River widening works of 1884 are not visible, and the $54,000 bridge built above the junction in 1884 is also missing. The visible bridge is therefore identified as the first Market Street bridge, built of timber and 150 feet long in 1883, and later replaced by an iron bridge in 1890. The central block of Chinese houses corresponds to buildings known to have stood on the west bank of the Gombak from the early 1880s until their staged demolition up to 1894 for the Secretariat building.

### Key Findings

- The photograph is dated to 1883/84 on the basis of the presence of the timber Market Street bridge (built 1883, 150 feet long) and the absence of both the 1884 Klang River widening works and the $54,000 bridge constructed above the river junction that same year (p. 201).
- The Selangor Government occupied offices on the Bluff Road site (now Federal Police Headquarters) from 1882 to 1896, matching the buildings visible on higher ground behind the Padang in the photograph (p. 202).
- The central block of Chinese shops and houses corresponds to structures documented on the west bank of the Gombak from at least the early 1880s, demolished in stages up to 1894 for the Secretariat building (pp. 200–202).
- The course of the Klang River below the bridge runs westward toward the buildings beyond the boats, consistent with the known pre-1890 river alignment; the present north-south line is the result of works undertaken just before 1890 (p. 201).
- The visible stretch of the east bank of the Klang above Market Street (corresponding to the later Embankment and Johore Street) appears relatively empty, which Gullick attributes either to the flooding disaster of 1881 or to Swettenham's rebuilding programme having cleared the area (p. 202).
- The photograph is significant as one of the very few extant images of Kuala Lumpur before its rapid growth in brick and tile during the later 1880s (p. 199).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that the convergence of topographical, architectural, and negative evidence (what is absent) makes a compelling case for identifying the print as Kuala Lumpur viewed from Bukit Nanas in 1883 or 1884. He presents this as a most important addition to the small corpus of pre-expansion photographs of the city, filling a gap in the visual record of the town's earliest urban form.

## Context

- The primary source is a single undated photograph from Sir Roland Braddell's family album, held by the MBRAS, examined alongside Dr Gibson-Hill's internal-evidence dating of the other prints in the collection (p. 198).
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the small body of work reconstructing early Kuala Lumpur's physical geography and urban layout from visual sources, supplementing written administrative records with photographic evidence of the town's topography before the major river-modification and embankment works of the 1880s and 1890s.
