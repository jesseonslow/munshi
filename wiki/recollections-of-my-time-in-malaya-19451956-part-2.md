# Recollections of my time in Malaya (1945–1956) Part 2

J. M. Gullick, a British army officer and former colonial administrator, published this second part of his memoirs in 2014, recounting his service in Negri Sembilan from September 1945 through the Malayan Union period (1946–1948). The work offers a granular, first-hand account of the administrative challenges of restoring British governance after the Japanese occupation, the fraught negotiations with Malay rulers over the MacMichael treaties, and the social and economic dislocation of the post-war period.

## Summary

Gullick's narrative is organised around the two successive regimes under which he served: the British Military Administration (BMA, September 1945–March 1946) and the Malayan Union government (April 1946–January 1948). The BMA period is characterised by institutional vacuum—destroyed records, absent expatriate staff, a demoralised police force, and a BMA team that was, in Gullick's own frank assessment, ignorant of Malay language, custom, and the legal frameworks governing land tenure and local government. The central administrative dilemma was how to reassert authority over a population that had endured three years of Japanese rule, much of it marked by food scarcity, forced requisitioning, and the social disruption of a collapsed rationing system, while simultaneously screening local civil servants for collaboration and managing the expectations of Malay rulers who had been told their sovereignty was under review.

The Malayan Union period shifts the focus to constitutional and political questions. Gullick, serving as Secretary to the Resident Commissioner, provides an intimate portrait of the Residency's working culture under three successive incumbents (Calder, Gordon-Hall, and Pengilley) and the delicate negotiations over the 1947 written constitution for Negri Sembilan, particularly the contentious rules governing the election of a Yam Tuan. He documents the financial precarity of the Malay aristocracy, whose fixed political allowances had not kept pace with post-war inflation, and the practical difficulties of implementing central government directives—such as the "Grow More Food" campaign—without local knowledge. Throughout, Gullick's account is informed by his daily contact with district officers, Malay notables, and the local civil service, and he is candid about the BMA's institutional shortcomings and the extent to which continuity depended on the competence and loyalty of Malay and other local staff who had maintained government functions through the occupation.

### Key Findings

- The MPAJA removed and destroyed all records, including essential land registers, from the Jelebu District Office; separately, a Kempeitai military fatigue party under a sergeant burned most of the State Secretariat files in Seremban, representing two major losses of irreplaceable administrative records (pp. 53–54).
- The Undang of Jelebu was elected within approximately one month of the previous holder's death in August 1945, an unusually rapid process compared to the normal two-year period, necessitated by the need for a signatory to the MacMichael treaty in November 1945 (p. 56).
- The BMA ended on 31 March 1946; the Malayan Union period ran from 1 April 1946 to 31 January 1948, during which three men held the post of Resident Commissioner Negri Sembilan: Calder, Gordon-Hall, and Pengilley (pp. 69, 71).
- The 1947 constitutional compromise on Yam Tuan succession stipulated that the Undang would first seek a candidate among the "issue" of the late ruler and only if no suitable son or grandson existed would they consider other descendants of Yam Tuan Antah (p. 79).
- The "Grow More Food" campaign, directed from Kuala Lumpur, required all owners of agricultural land to set aside approximately 1 per cent of their holdings for food crop cultivation; the programme was ultimately abandoned as unworkable after the State Agricultural Officer demonstrated that production costs exceeded market prices (p. 81).
- The Dato' Klana Ma'amor, Undang of Sungei Ujong since 1895, had appointed every *penghulu* in the Seremban district as a son or son-in-law, and the Dato' Bandar was also his son-in-law, illustrating the extent of nepotism in local administration (p. 59).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive historical takeaway is that the BMA's principal failure was not military or political but epistemic: the administration lacked the institutional knowledge of Malay custom, language, and legal precedent that the pre-war Malayan Civil Service had accumulated over decades, and this deficit was only partially compensated by the willing cooperation of local civil servants. He argues implicitly that the Malayan Union's constitutional overreach—stripping the rulers of temporal authority without adequate consultation or transitional arrangements—was a direct consequence of this ignorance, compounded by the Colonial Office's impatience, and that the rapid reversal to the Federation of Malaya in 1948 was the inevitable correction.

## Context

- Gullick served as a BMA officer in Seremban (1945–46) and then as Secretary to the Resident Commissioner, Negri Sembilan (1946–48); his account is that of a mid-level administrator with direct responsibility for district-level governance, land records, and liaison with Malay rulers, rather than a policy-maker in Kuala Lumpur.
- The memoir was originally written in 1970 and revised for publication; Gullick acknowledges confusions in his recollection (e.g., conflating two different Malay DOs in Jelebu) and explicitly corrects the widely repeated claim that Dato' Klana Ma'amor was "brought protesting from his deathbed" to meet MacMichael, asserting instead that the old man was simply expressing polite unwillingness (pp. 54–55, 59–60).
