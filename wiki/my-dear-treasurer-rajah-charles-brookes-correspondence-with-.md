# ‘My dear treasurer’: Rajah Charles Brooke’s correspondence with F.H. Dallas, 1902-1917

Ismail Ali and Jane Wong Kon Ling (2008) present a cultural heritage study of the *boo'gok-boo'gok*, a small winged fishing boat used by the Sea Bajau (*Pala'u*) community along Sabah's east coast. The article argues that this vessel, which has existed since the eighteenth-century Sulu Sultanate period, has been systematically overlooked in scholarship that has focused almost exclusively on the more famous *lepa-lepa* dwelling boat, and that its design lineage, construction techniques, and economic functions warrant formal recognition as part of Sabah's intangible maritime heritage.

## Summary

The Sea Bajau are a sea-nomad community that migrated from the southern Philippines to Sabah's east coast during the Sulu Sultanate period, roughly the eighteenth century. Their maritime economy was historically integrated into the broader Sulu trading network, where *datus* who owned large winged vessels such as the *barangayan* and *kora-kora* relied on Sea Bajau expertise to extract sea cucumber, pearls, salted fish, and turtle shells for Chinese and European merchants (pp. 19–20). Ali and Wong Kon Ling trace the *boo'gok-boo'gok* as a direct descendant of these larger Sulu-era craft, arguing that the winged design was inherited and subsequently scaled down by the community itself.

During the British North Borneo Chartered Company period, the loss of *datus* sponsorship prompted the Sea Bajau to modify the winged boat into a smaller, more practical form called the *dapang*. The British administered these vessels through the Boats and Fishery Ordinance No. 10 of 1914 and later the *Undang-Undang Perahu dan Perikanan Penggal 16* (1959), which governed vessel registration and fishing licences (p. 21). After Sabah's independence in 1963, the community continued the tradition, but the introduction of outboard engines transformed the *dapang* into a fundamentally different craft. The traditional rowing-pole version was consequently renamed *boo'gok-boo'gok* (or *katig*, "wing," in Suluk) to distinguish it from its motorised successor (p. 21).

The authors document the boat's construction by specialist elderly craftsmen (*tukang*), its dimensions (15–20 feet long, 2–3 feet wide), its plywood-and-hardwood wing structure, and its maintenance cycle of repainting every three to five months (pp. 21–23). Field observations across Mabul, Omadal, Gusungan, Bum-Bum, and Banggi Islands confirm that nearly all Sea Bajau families own one, using it for hook-and-line fishing around coral reefs and for sea cucumber diving, the dried product of which sells in Semporna at RM50–80 per kilogram (pp. 24–25). The article closes by noting that the Sabah Maritime Department has prohibited the boat's use due to associations with illegal cyanide and dynamite fishing, placing the tradition under direct institutional threat.

### Key Findings

- The *boo'gok-boo'gok* measures 15–20 feet in length and 2–3 feet in width, constructed from 0.5-inch plywood walls with hardwood wings and bamboo or plastic rod stabilisers (p. 21).
- The vessel's design lineage is traced from Sulu Sultanate-era *barangayan* and *kora-kora* → BNBCC-era *dapang* → post-independence *boo'gok-boo'gok* (p. 21, p. 26).
- The Boats and Fishery Ordinance No. 10 of 1914 and *Undang-Undang Perahu dan Perikanan Penggal 16* (1959) were the principal colonial and post-colonial regulatory instruments governing Sea Bajau vessels (p. 21).
- Dried sea cucumber, harvested via diving from the *boo'gok-boo'gok*, commands a market price of RM50.00–RM80.00 per kilogram in Semporna depending on type and quality (p. 25).
- The Sabah Maritime Department has prohibited the making and use of the *boo'gok-boo'gok* in connection with illegal cyanide and fish bombing along Sabah's coast (p. 25).
- Fishermen typically depart at 5 a.m. and return at noon or in the evening, catching only coral-based species using hook and line (p. 24).

### Conclusion

Ali and Wong Kon Ling conclude that the *boo'gok-boo'gok* is a living cultural artefact with an unbroken design lineage stretching back to the eighteenth-century Sulu Sultanate, yet it remains absent from the scholarly and policy discourse that has centred on the *lepa-lepa*. They urge the Sabah Ministry of Tourism, Culture and Heritage to formally recognise and preserve the boat as a heritage asset, proposing its exhibition for ecotourism and intergenerational knowledge transfer before the tradition is lost entirely to modernisation and regulatory prohibition.

## Context

- The study draws on field observations across multiple island communities in Sabah's east coast (Mabul, Omadal, Gusungan, Bum-Bum, Banggi) and references colonial-era regulatory documents (Boats and Fishery Ordinance No. 10 of 1914; *Undang-Undang Perahu dan Perikanan Penggal 16*, 1959) alongside the fisheries report of J. A. Tubb (1952).
- Historiographically, the article fills a gap identified in the limited body of Sea Bajau scholarship (Warren 1981, 2002; Sather 1977; Sopher 1965; Yap Beng Liang 1993; Sendera 2007), none of which had previously documented the *boo'gok-boo'gok* as a distinct vessel type.
