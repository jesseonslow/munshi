# The Johore Military Forces: the oldest army of Malay regulars in the Peninsula

Tunku Shahriman bin Tunku Sulaiman Tunku Tan Sri Dato' published this article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 2004, tracing the full arc of the Johore Military Forces (JMF) from their founding in 1885 under Sultan Abu Bakar to their final disbandment in 1946–1947. The article argues that the JMF was not merely a small ceremonial unit but a genuine instrument of state sovereignty, military cooperation, and Malay elite formation that reflected the broader tension between Johore's hard-won independence and the expanding British imperial presence in the Straits Settlements.

## Summary

The article frames the JMF's creation as a deliberate act of political assertion by Sultan Abu Bakar, who, having secured the designation "Independent State of Johore" in the 1885 Anglo-Johore Treaty, sought a military force of his own to complement the treaty's joint-defence obligations with the Straits Settlements. Initially a modest body of 60 Malay infantrymen and 20 Afghan guards, the Force evolved through a period of decline in the 1890s—driven by Abu Bakar's debts and the departure of its European commandants—before Sultan Ibrahim's personal intervention from 1901 onward restored its discipline, equipment, and morale. The author draws on a combination of official despatches, the *Straits Times*, the *Johore Annual Report*, and a Jawi-language history by Major Dato' Haji Mohd Said to reconstruct this trajectory, emphasizing how the JMF functioned simultaneously as a military unit, a patronage network for the Johore elite, and a pipeline into the civil service.

The article's most detailed treatment is reserved for the 1915 Sepoy Mutiny in Singapore, where a detachment of 190 JMF officers and men under Captain Cullimore, armed with almost no ammunition, helped suppress the uprising by the 5th Light Infantry. The author uses this episode to demonstrate the Force's practical military value and the personal commitment of Sultan Ibrahim, who accompanied his men to the barracks and later personally persuaded four armed mutineers to surrender in Kulai. The narrative then moves through the interwar period of modernization—marked by the 1915 Enactment, improved pay, British military advisers, and participation in large-scale manoeuvres—before culminating in the JMF's role in the 1941–42 defence of Johore against the Japanese advance, where the Force fought alongside Australian Imperial Force units before being ordered to surrender its weapons at the 13th mile on the Kota Tinggi–Johore Bahru Road.

### Key Findings

- The JMF was established in late 1885 with 60 Malay infantrymen and 20 Afghan Astana Guards; by 1895 total strength reached 270, but fell to 188 by 1897 due to the state's debt crisis (pp. 95–97).
- During the 1915 Sepoy Mutiny, JMF troops captured 180 mutineers and seized 81 rifles, 75 bayonets, and 3,000 cartridges over ten days; approximately 40 people were killed and 36 sepoys were publicly executed (pp. 98–99).
- The Johore Military Forces Enactment of December 1915 legally bound soldiers to serve not only in Johore but also in the Straits Settlements and any part of the Malay Peninsula, and empowered the Sultan to order continued service in emergencies (p. 100).
- By the end of 1938, the Force's actual strength was 950 men against an authorized 971, stationed at Johore Bahru, Muar, Batu Pahat, and Mersing; by 1937 it was considered one of the best-equipped infantry units in the Far East, with Vickers machine guns on mono-wheel vehicles (p. 101).
- In the 1941–42 campaign, the JMF comprised two Battalions of four Companies each under Major General Sultan Ibrahim; after the fall of Muar on 15 January 1942 and the collapse at Batu Pahat, troops were ordered to surrender their weapons to Australian forces at the 13th mile, Kota Tinggi–Johore Bahru Road (pp. 102–103).
- Following the Malayan Union of 1946, all JMF members except a retained company of 100 were discharged with effect from 31 July 1946; the Sultan was appointed an honorary Major General in the British Army in 1947 (pp. 104–105).

### Conclusion

Tunku Shahriman's definitive takeaway is that the JMF embodied a distinctive form of Malay statehood under colonial conditions: a small but genuinely professional force that served as a symbol of Johore's sovereignty, a practical instrument of regional defence, and a social institution through which the Malay elite gained military training, status, and access to government service. Its disbandment in 1946–47 marked not just the loss of a military unit but the final dissolution of the particular model of independent Malay statecraft that the Johore Sultans had cultivated for over six decades.

## Context

- The author, a member of the Johore royal family, draws on a mix of official colonial despatches, the *Straits Times*, the *Johore Annual Report*, the *Johore Government Gazette*, and a Jawi-language manuscript history by Major Dato' Haji Mohd Said (1952), alongside a private letter from Lieutenant General Gordon Bennett (1958).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malay state military institutions by treating the JMF as a continuous entity across the colonial and wartime periods, rather than as a footnote to British military history in Malaya.
