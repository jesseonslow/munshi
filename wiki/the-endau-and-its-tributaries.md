# The Endau and its tributaries

H.S. Barlow's 1985 report documents the Malayan Nature Society/Star Endau-Rompin Expedition, a year-long field programme launched in 1984 to survey and promote conservation of the last extensive tract of lowland primary forest in southern Peninsular Malaysia. The article situates the expedition within a century of exploration history beginning with D.F.A. Hervey's 1881 account, and frames it against the political and ecological pressures that threatened the area's designation as a national park.

## Summary

Barlow provides a concise historiographical overview of the Endau-Rompin area's exploration and conservation history, tracing it from Hervey's 1881 journey up the Endau, Sembrong, and Madek rivers through H.W. Lake's 1894 account of reaching the Endau's source, to the establishment of wildlife reserves by Sultan Ibrahim of Johore in the 1930s and the Lesong Forest Reserve in Pahang in 1906. Development pressure in the 1950s through 1970s eroded much of these protected areas, and attempts in the late 1970s to grant logging concessions over parts of the Pahang side provoked significant public opposition, including a half-page advertisement in the *New Straits Times* of 7 May 1977. The Federal National Parks Act (Act No. 226) passed in 1980 after eleven years of discussion, but implementation remained stalled because no state government had exercised the initiative to request federal reservation of land.

Against this backdrop, the MNS and *The Star* launched the expedition in 1984 with the patronage of Tunku Abdul Rahman. Barlow describes the logistics of reaching Base Camp at Kampong Peta on the Endau River — a Jakun Orang Asli settlement of about 30 households — and details the camp's construction, which notably used fronds of *Johannesteysmannia altifrons* for thatching and walling. The expedition's scientific work focused on the Endau and Jasin River corridors, including the ascent of Bukit Janing and Bukit Janing Barat, and a four-day traverse by Tunku Mohd. Nazim Yaacob covering approximately 36 km from Bekok to Kampong Jasin.

Preliminary findings included observations of vegetational zonation along the Janing Barat Ridge — lowland forest to about 140 m, *Livistona* palm forest to 440 m, and swamp forest beyond — and the recording of the endemic slender climbing bamboo *Racemobambos setifera*, possibly in its last undisturbed habitat. Faunal records included tiger and panther sightings, rhinoceros tracks, and notable bird records including a Black-and-White Bulbul and a Lesser Fish Eagle in the Bukit Janing swamps. Entomological light trapping by the Forest Research Institute yielded several first peninsular records for moth species previously known only from Sumatra and Borneo.

### Key Findings

- The Endau-Rompin area was the subject of the earliest published exploration by D.F.A. Hervey in JSBRAS No. 8 (December 1881), covering the Endau, Sembrong (traced to its source), and Madek rivers (p. 135).
- The Endau-Kluang Wildlife Reserve was established by Sultan Ibrahim of Johore in 1933 and the Segamat Wildlife Sanctuary in 1937; the Lesong Forest Reserve on the Pahang side dates to 1906 (p. 135).
- The Federal National Parks Act (Act No. 226) was enacted in 1980 after 11 years of discussion, but no state government had yet requested federal reservation of land for a national park (p. 136).
- Base Camp, capable of holding 70 people, was reached via a 10-mile dirt road through palm oil estates followed by 20 miles of logging tracks and a 6-mile boat trip upstream from Kampong Peta (p. 136).
- A 9-storey tree tower (103 ft above ground) was constructed by the Malaysian Armed Forces in the crown of a giant forest keruing to facilitate microclimate studies at different forest levels (p. 137).
- The expedition was extended by a further 6 months to the end of May 1986 to allow a more thorough survey and inventory of the area (p. 142).

### Conclusion

Barlow's account serves as both a field report and a conservation advocacy document, demonstrating that the Endau-Rompin area retains significant ecological value — including endemic species, large mammal populations, and intact lowland primary forest — while underscoring the political inertia that continued to prevent formal national park designation despite public awareness and legislative frameworks already in place.

## Context

- The article draws on the expedition's own field observations and references the conservation literature of the period, particularly Aiken & Leigh (1984) on the Endau-Rompin controversy.
- It contributes to the historiography of Malayan exploration by explicitly linking the 1984–85 expedition to the 1881 Hervey account and the 1894 Lake and Kelsall papers, establishing a continuous thread of scientific engagement with the area spanning over a century.
