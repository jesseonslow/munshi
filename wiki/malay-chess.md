# Malay chess

T. B. Elcum, a British colonial administrator in the Straits Settlements, published this ethnographic study of Malay chess in 1907 in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*. Writing from direct observation of village games in the Malay Peninsula, Elcum documents the rules, apparatus, and social context of the game, arguing that its distinctive features represent a mixture of survivals from early European chess and innovations peculiar to the Malay tradition.

## Summary

Elcum's article is primarily a descriptive and comparative study of the rules of Malay chess (known locally as *makro* or *makruk*), set against the backdrop of European chess as it stood in 1907. He opens with a vivid sketch of the social setting: a village game in which the entire population gathers, offers unsolicited advice, and frequently interferes with the pieces themselves. The physical apparatus is typically crude—uncoloured boards, rough hand-carved pieces often all of one colour, and pawns sometimes replaced by stones or bits of leaf—though Elcum reproduces a plate of a more elaborate set lent by a member of the Johore Royal Family, in which the king, queen, and pawns are distinguished by size alone rather than shape.

The substantive core of the article is a rule-by-rule comparison with European chess. Elcum identifies several key divergences: the starting arrangement places the *menteri* (queen) on the right of the king, directly opposite the opposing king rather than the opposing queen; the king possesses a one-time "leap" (a knight's move or a two-square lateral move) that functions as a substitute for castling; and the promotion rules are markedly more restrictive, requiring pawns on files other than the rook's file to play back diagonally a specified number of moves before they may promote. He also notes a seven-move time limit for mating a lone king, the prohibition on discovered check as a mating mechanism, and a strong cultural prejudice against exchanging queens. Elcum frames these rules as making the game harder to win by a material advantage, thereby favouring the weaker player and producing a different strategic character from European chess.

On the question of origin, Elcum is deliberately agnostic. He considers the possibilities of transmission via the Arabs or directly from India, but finds that neither the piece names nor the peculiar rules resolve the question. The etymological table he appends, drawing on Wilkinson's dictionary, shows a mixture of Sanskrit (*seri* for draw), Persian (*mat*, apparently cognate with *sah*), and pure Malay terms (*buah*, *kuda*, *makan*, *suap*), which is consistent with either route of transmission. His final observation is that the most interesting historical question is whether the distinctive rules are survivals of an early form of chess as originally taught to the Malays, or independent Malay inventions.

### Key Findings

- The starting arrangement places the *menteri* on the right of the king, directly opposite the opposing king, unlike the European convention of king opposite king and queen opposite queen (pp. 88–89).
- The king may, once and only if he has not been checked or moved, leap like a knight or move two squares laterally (over intervening pieces), a power Elcum identifies as a survival of the mediaeval European "king's leap" that preceded modern castling (p. 89).
- Promotion rules are file-dependent: a rook's pawn reaching the eighth rank may promote immediately (to any piece already off the board), while a pawn on the knight's file must play back diagonally one square, on the bishop's file two squares, and on the king's or queen's file three squares before promotion is permitted (p. 90).
- A lone king must be mated within seven moves or the game is drawn; discovered check cannot deliver mate; and there is a strong cultural prejudice against exchanging queens unless the game can be immediately won or saved (pp. 90–91).
- The etymological table records *seri* (draw) as Sanskrit, *mat* as apparently Persian (cognate with *sah*), and *buah*, *kuda*, *makan*, *suap* as pure Malay; the origin of *tir* (rook) is noted as doubtful (p. 92).
- The game is played across the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, and Borneo, with the proportion of men who can play described as "probably greater than with most races" (p. 88).

### Conclusion

Elcum's definitive takeaway is that Malay chess is "probably equal in essentials" to European chess but is strategically distinct because its peculiar rules—particularly the restrictive promotion and the seven-move mate limit—make it materially harder for the stronger force to convert an advantage into a win. He finds the game a "pleasant change" precisely because no openings have been analysed, forcing the player to rely entirely on individual judgement from the first move.

## Context

- Elcum was a British colonial official in the Straits Settlements; the article reflects the standard ethnographic practice of the colonial administrative class in cataloguing "native" customs for a metropolitan learned society, with the Malay village game rendered as a curiosity to be described and classified.
- The study draws on direct personal observation and a single loaned set of pieces from the Johore Royal Family; no archival or textual Malay sources are cited, and the etymological analysis relies entirely on Wilkinson's dictionary.
