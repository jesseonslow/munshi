# Jakun

The Jakun are a group of indigenous peoples historically documented across the Malay Peninsula, with attested communities in Malacca, Johore, and Pahang. They have been variously classified alongside other non-Muhammadan aboriginal groups in colonial administrative records, and their languages, customs, and physical characteristics have been the subject of ethnographic and linguistic investigation from the late nineteenth century through the mid-twentieth century. The records indicate a population in process of assimilation, with dialects heavily impregnated with Malay, declining use of indigenous vocabulary, and increasing cultural blending with Malay and Chinese communities [[1, pp. 177–178]](#ref-1), [[2, pp. 29–30]](#ref-2).

### Scope and Historical Definition

The earliest administrative classification of the Jakun appears in a memorandum by C.O. Blagden, dated 1892, describing the non-Muhammadan aborigines of the Jasin district, Malacca. Blagden identifies three principal tribes in the district — Mentera, Jakun, and Besisi — and notes that the Mentera claimed superiority and shared descent with the Muhammadan Biduanda clan [[1, p. 177]](#ref-1). Linguistically, Blagden observes that the Mentera and Jakun dialects are so heavily impregnated with Malay as to be "little better than Malay patois," while the Besisi language is entirely different and of Sakai stock [[1, pp. 177–178]](#ref-1). This distinction places the Jakun in a category of partial linguistic assimilation, differentiated from both the fully Malay-speaking Mentera and the linguistically distinct Besisi.

In Johore, A.D. Machado's 1902 note documents the Jakuns of Batu Pahat, who lived at the headwaters of the Sembrong, Bekok, and Simpang Kiri rivers. Machado records that this group self-designated as *Orang Ulu* — a term meaning "people of the uplands" — rather than "Jakun," which they considered an opprobrium [[2, pp. 29–30]](#ref-2). This self-designation is significant: it indicates that the exonym "Jakun" carried a pejorative connotation in the communities' own perception, and that the group identified more broadly with their upland habitat than with a specific tribal label.

In Pahang, P.D.R. Williams-Hunt's 1951 note documents Jakun speakers in the Maran area, recorded in June 1950, and contrasts their numerals with a list previously obtained by I.N.H. Evans from the Ulu Tekam group [[3, p. 175]](#ref-3). The existence of distinct sub-groups — Ulu Tekam, Maran, Batu Pahat, Jasin — suggests that "Jakun" functioned as a broad classificatory term encompassing several communities that shared certain linguistic and cultural features while maintaining local particularities.

### Physical Typology and Social Customs

Blagden provides a physical typology distinguishing the Jasin aborigines from the normal Malay type, noting prognathism, wavy or curly hair, and a generally lower stature, with the Jakun presenting a coarser type than the Mentera [[1, p. 178]](#ref-1). This observation, while characteristic of the anthropological conventions of its era, reflects the classificatory framework within which colonial administrators understood the relationships between indigenous groups.

On social customs, Blagden describes the Mentera as living in small Malay-style houses with plantations, practising monogamy with occasional bigamy, and observing no religious ceremony beyond belief in malignant spirits and ghosts of the dead, which prompted them to shift settlements after a funeral [[1, p. 179]](#ref-1). He notes the influence of the French Mission in raising their standard of comfort, and records a 1891 census figure of 77 aborigines in the district, which he considers an understatement, estimating the true number at nearly double [[1, p. 179]](#ref-1). While Blagden's memorandum addresses the Jasin aborigines collectively, the Jakun are positioned within this same social and religious framework.

Machado's observations of the Batu Pahat Jakuns reveal a community in active process of cultural blending. He notes their employment by Chinese pepper and gambier cultivators, their trading of jungle produce with Malays, and their increasing adoption of Chinese cultural practices — using chopsticks, accepting Chinese marriage alliances, and feigning horror at traditional delicacies while reportedly consuming them privately [[2, pp. 29–30]](#ref-2). This pattern of selective assimilation, in which outward conformity coexisted with private retention of older practices, is a recurring theme in the documentation of these communities.

A particularly distinctive custom recorded by Machado is a peculiar longitudinal circumcision rite, explained by a legend involving a great *Batin* — a traditional leader or chief — and a Muhammadan *Pawang* — a spiritual practitioner or shaman [[2, pp. 30–31]](#ref-2). Machado draws a comparison with the northern Sakais, who rejected circumcision as a barrier to Islam, suggesting that the Jakun rite represented a negotiated cultural position between indigenous and Islamic traditions [[2, pp. 30–31]](#ref-2).

### Linguistic Documentation

The linguistic records for the Jakun span three decades and three different localities, providing a picture of a language in progressive decline. Machado's 1902 vocabulary from Batu Pahat comprises approximately fifty words still in use, interspersed with Malay, covering kinship terms, body parts, animals, plants, and basic verbs [[2, pp. 31–33]](#ref-2). He observes that the rising generation shows little inclination to use even these remnants, and that many entries overlap with those previously collected by Lieut. Kelsall from the Endau Jakuns [[2, pp. 31–33]](#ref-2). The overlap between the Batu Pahat and Endau vocabularies suggests a shared linguistic substrate, while the heavy Malay interspersion indicates advanced stages of language shift.

Williams-Hunt's 1951 note from Maran, Pahang, provides a more structured linguistic comparison. He presents a side-by-side comparison of number words (one through ten, plus "half" and "a unit of ten") between the Ulu Tekam group and his Maran informants, Harun and his son Long Chit. The two lists show considerable correspondence but also notable divergences, particularly at higher numbers — for instance, *Genting* versus *Kitwid* for eight, and *Mogenor* versus *Kumai* for ten [[3, p. 175]](#ref-3). These divergences suggest either dialectal variation between the two groups or, alternatively, differential rates of language loss, with higher numbers being more vulnerable to replacement by Malay equivalents.

Williams-Hunt further records that the Maran Jakun distinguished between separate and composite units of ten — for example, *Kantim Riale* for nine dollars versus *Moie-ganal Riale* for one ten-dollar note — and that formerly items were counted individually up to ten and then rounded to the nearest unit of ten, whereas Malay numbers were now employed above ten [[3, p. 176]](#ref-3). A set of numerical classifiers for humans, animals, houses, small objects, trees, weapons, and pieces is also listed with illustrative phrases [[3, p. 176]](#ref-3). The presence of a classifier system, a feature absent from Malay, represents a significant structural remnant of the indigenous language, preserved in a domain — counting and commerce — where precision was practically necessary.

## Research and Documentation

The documentation of the Jakun in the Society's literature spans approximately sixty years, from Blagden's 1892 memorandum (published in 1917) through Williams-Hunt's 1951 note, and reveals a shift in both methodological focus and interpretive framework. The earliest record, Blagden's memorandum, is an administrative document concerned with classification, physical typology, and census enumeration. Its primary concern is to place the Jakun within a hierarchical taxonomy of aboriginal groups and to estimate their numbers for colonial governance purposes. The publication of this document in 1917, at the suggestion of Resident L. E. Pipe Wolferstan, from an official file in the Malacca records, indicates that it was valued as a historical administrative record rather than as a contemporary ethnographic study [[1, p. 177]](#ref-1).

Machado's 1902 note represents a shift toward linguistic and cultural documentation. While still brief, it prioritises the recording of a vocabulary and the description of distinctive customs — particularly the circumcision rite — over physical typology or census data. The explicit observation that the rising generation shows little inclination to use the indigenous vocabulary signals an awareness of language loss that is absent from Blagden's earlier account [[2, pp. 31–33]](#ref-2). The comparison with Kelsall's earlier collection from the Endau Jakuns also indicates that by 1902, a body of comparative linguistic data was already accumulating, and that scholars were beginning to recognise patterns of shared vocabulary across different localities.

Williams-Hunt's 1951 note marks a further methodological advance. The side-by-side comparison of numerals between two sub-groups, the recording of a classifier system, and the documentation of the transition from indigenous to Malay counting above ten all reflect a more sophisticated linguistic approach, concerned with structural features and diachronic change rather than mere lexical collection. The note is also explicitly comparative, situating the Maran data within a broader framework of Jakun linguistic variation [[3, pp. 175–176]](#ref-3).

Across these three records, a consistent narrative emerges: the Jakun were a group in advanced stages of cultural and linguistic assimilation, maintaining distinctive features — a classifier system, a unique circumcision rite, a self-designation as *Orang Ulu* — while progressively adopting Malay and Chinese linguistic and cultural practices. The records do not, however, provide a comprehensive account of Jakun territory, population, or political organisation, and the available documentation is limited to specific localities and specific aspects of their culture. The absence of any record addressing the broader distribution of the Jakun across the Malay Peninsula, or their relationship to the broader Austroasiatic or other language families, represents a significant gap in the archival record.
