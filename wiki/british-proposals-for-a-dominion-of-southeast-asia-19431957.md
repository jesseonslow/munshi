# British proposals for a dominion of Southeast Asia, 1943–1957

D.S. Ranjit Singh (1998) examines British wartime and post-war planning to create a political union of their Southeast Asian territories—Malaya, Singapore, North Borneo, Sarawak, and Brunei—conceived as a self-governing "dominion" within the Commonwealth. Drawing extensively on Colonial Office correspondence and the dispatches of Commissioner-General Malcolm MacDonald, the article traces how the scheme was repeatedly proposed, reconfigured, and ultimately abandoned between 1943 and 1957, undone by local opposition, inter-territorial rivalries, and the accelerating political dynamics of Malayan decolonization.

## Summary

The article addresses a long-neglected chapter in imperial history: the British attempt to forge a single political entity from their scattered Southeast Asian possessions. Singh argues that the dominion concept was not a single fixed plan but a shifting aspiration that adapted to political circumstances. The scheme was first articulated in 1943 by the Colonial Office's Eastern Department under Edward Gent, who proposed a "Malayan Union" encompassing all the territories. After the war, the plan was shelved, revived, narrowed to two separate blocs (Malaya-Singapore and the Borneo territories), briefly expanded again to a wider federation, and finally abandoned when Malaya's path to independence outpaced the integration timetable. The central analytical argument is that the British consistently misjudged local political dynamics—first by excluding Singapore from the 1946 Malayan Union to avoid Malay opposition, then by failing to anticipate the speed at which UMNO and the Alliance would seize control of Malayan politics and reject merger outright.

The narrative is informed by a rich body of primary sources, particularly the Colonial Office file CO 1022/61, which contains the internal deliberations of the Secretary of State, his advisers (Higham, Paskin, MacGillivray, MacKintosh), and the Commissioner-General's secret dispatches. Singh uses these to reveal the contradictions within Whitehall: the Secretary of State's oscillation between enthusiasm and complacency, the split between officials who favoured a strong federation and those content with loose consultative machinery, and the failure of the "climate of opinion" strategy that relied on the Commonwealth Parliamentary Association to generate grassroots support for union. The Borneo territories receive particular attention, with Singh showing how the Anti-Cession Movement in Sarawak and the governors' resistance to any "forcing of the pace" effectively froze integration in that bloc.

A recurring theme is the structural tension between the two principles underpinning British policy: the commitment to decolonization and the desire for larger political units. Singh demonstrates that these were mutually reinforcing in theory but mutually destructive in practice, because the very constitutional advances that prepared territories for self-government also made them less willing to surrender sovereignty to a union. The article thus contributes a corrective to the tendency in the literature to treat the Malayan Union controversy in isolation, showing instead that it was one episode in a broader, region-wide experiment in imperial reorganization.

### Key Findings

- The term "British Dominion of Southeast Asia" first appeared in official Colonial Office correspondence in 1951 and was publicly announced by Commissioner-General Malcolm MacDonald in 1952, though the underlying concept dates to 1943 planning by the Eastern Department (pp. 2–3).
- Singapore's demographic composition—761,000 Chinese out of a total population of 980,000, with only 119,000 Malays—was the single most cited obstacle to Malaya-Singapore merger, as Malay leaders feared being swamped by a Chinese majority (p. 7).
- The Sarawak-North Borneo-Brunei Conference, the first formal inter-territorial body for the Borneo bloc, met at Kuching on 21 April 1953, attended by Governor Anthony Abell, Governor Ralph Hone, and Sultan Omar Ali Saifuddin, chaired by MacDonald (p. 12).
- The Joint Co-ordinating Committee for Malaya-Singapore integration held five meetings in 1953 alone (8 July, 31 July, 4 September, 9 October, 4 December), yet by year's end discussion had not progressed beyond exploring areas of administrative cooperation; constitutional arrangements remained untouched (p. 13).
- The Alliance (UMNO, MCA, MIC) won 51 of 52 elected seats in the 1955 Federal Council elections, after which the Colonial Office acknowledged that "the course of Malayan Politics was taking fundamental decision-making out of their hands" (p. 14).
- The Colonial Office's 1953 Memorandum on Political Objectives specified a conservative twenty-year horizon for self-government in Malaya, a timetable that was rendered obsolete when the Tunku accelerated the independence process to 1957 (p. 13).

### Conclusion

Singh's definitive takeaway is that the dominion scheme failed not because of any single decisive event but because of a cumulative series of miscalculations: the British underestimated Malay communal politics, overestimated the utility of informal consultative bodies, and allowed the divergent economic trajectories of Malaya and Singapore to harden into political antagonism. By 1957, when Malaya achieved independence ahead of schedule, the political window for any form of union had closed. The dominion idea survived only as a rhetorical aspiration, to be partially realized in a different form with the creation of Malaysia in 1963—but even then without the full territorial scope the British had originally envisioned.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on Colonial Office records held at The National Archives, Kew (CO 1022/61, CO 1022/381, CO 1022/246, CO 1030/287), supplemented by the published works of Stockwell, Albert Lau, Nordin Sopiee, and Allan Healy.
- Historiographically, the article extends the well-studied Malayan Union controversy into the broader regional framework, demonstrating that the Borneo territories' integration attempts and the Malaya-Singapore question were interdependent strands of a single imperial policy rather than separate episodes.
