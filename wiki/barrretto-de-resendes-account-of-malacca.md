# Barrretto de Resende’s account of Malacca

J.C. Moulton, Curator of the Sarawak Museum, published this fourth instalment of his systematic list of Bornean butterflies in 1914, covering the family Papilionidae (swallowtails and whites). The work consolidates the state of knowledge on Bornean Pierinae and Papilioninae as of the early 1910s, arguing for a more conservative taxonomic approach that favours lumping variable forms into fewer species while acknowledging subspecific differentiation where geographical isolation is clear.

## Summary

Moulton's paper addresses the taxonomic confusion that had accumulated in Bornean butterfly nomenclature over nearly fifty years of collecting and description. He traces the history of Pierine knowledge from Snellen van Vollenhoven's 1865 list of 13 species through Wallace, Druce, Distant, Pryer, and Bartlett, showing how species counts fluctuated wildly (from 13 to 61) before settling at 35 species plus 5 subspecies in the present work. For the Papilioninae, he recognizes 43 species, a reduction from Bartlett's 1896 figure of 61, achieved through the application of subspecific nomenclature and a broader view of intraspecific variation. The paper introduces dichotomous keys for both sub-families, a departure from the three preceding parts of the series, intended to aid field identification.

The taxonomic arguments are grounded in direct examination of the Sarawak Museum collection, supplemented by references to the British Museum, the Raffles Museum (Singapore), and the Tring collection. Moulton makes several significant corrections to the literature: he demonstrates that *Delias glauce* Butler, long recorded from Borneo, is in fact a Sumatran form (confirmed by N.D. Riley of the British Museum, who verified the type's locality register); he unites *Catopsilia crocale* and *catilla* as one variable species on the basis of intergrading Sarawak specimens and recent breeding evidence; and he argues that *Prioneris vollenhovii* should be treated as a subspecies of *clemante* rather than a full species, proposing a notational system to indicate relative degrees of subspecific divergence.

A notable biogeographical discussion revisits Wallace's 1865 observation that Sumatra shares a closer faunal relationship with Borneo than with Java. Moulton confirms this with Papilionid data: of 43 Bornean species, 35 also occur in Sumatra and 33 in the Malay Peninsula, but only 25 in Java. He concludes that the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, and Borneo form a coherent biogeographical unit with a specialized Malayan fauna, while Java, though geographically adjacent to Sumatra, has been isolated long enough to develop a more generalized Indian fauna alongside its specialized Javan element.

### Key Findings

- The Bornean Pierinae fauna comprises 35 species and 5 subspecies (40 named forms total), a figure Moulton considers "tolerably complete" given that very little of Borneo remained unexplored by 1914 (p. 2).
- Of 43 Bornean Papilioninae species, only 4 are endemic to the island; 35 also occur in Sumatra, 33 in the Malay Peninsula, but only 25 in Java, confirming Wallace's 1865 hypothesis of Sumatra–Borneo faunal affinity over Sumatra–Java affinity (pp. 33–34).
- *Delias glauce* Butler, described from "Borneo" in 1865, is re-assigned to Sumatra on the basis of the British Museum type register, which records the locality as "Sumatra" despite the original label (p. 9, n. 1).
- *Catopsilia crocale* and *catilla* are united as a single variable species, supported by intergrading Sarawak specimens and by Bell's 1913 finding that the two forms "grade so completely into each other in both sexes that it is impossible to separate them absolutely" (pp. 25–26).
- Two new names are proposed for the Papilioninae: *Papilio polytes theseus* form *ignea* (a female form with a large yellow cell-spot bordered by red discal spots, single specimen from Kuching) and *P. antiphates itamputi* ab. *aperta* (two specimens from Kinabalu and Malinau with the outer black fore-wing bands not joined anally) (pp. 33, 51–52).
- *Papilio memnon memnon* (the typical form) is one of the very rare instances where Java and Borneo share the same subspecies while Sumatra has a distinct race (*anceus*), reversing the usual pattern of Sumatra–Borneo similarity (p. 49).

### Conclusion

Moulton's definitive historical takeaway is that the butterfly fauna of Borneo, particularly the conspicuous Papilionidae, had reached a state of near-complete enumeration by 1914, and that the principal remaining task was taxonomic clarification rather than discovery. His broader argument is that the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, and Borneo constitute a single biogeographical province of recent origin, sharply distinguished from Java, a conclusion he presents as now firmly established by entomological evidence beyond Wallace's original mammalian and avian data.

## Context

- The primary evidential basis is the Sarawak Museum collection in Kuching, supplemented by the British Museum (Natural History), the Raffles Museum (Singapore), and the Tring collection; Moulton's direct field observations on Kinabalu (1913) and in Sarawak (1897–1914) provide locality and seasonal data.
- The paper represents the culmination of a four-part series begun by the late G.H. Shelford in 1904, and it stands as the most comprehensive single treatment of Bornean Papilionidae published in the early twentieth century.
