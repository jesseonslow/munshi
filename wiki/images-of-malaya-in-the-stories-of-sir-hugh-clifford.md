# Images of Malaya in the stories of Sir Hugh Clifford

P.C. Wicks (1979) examines the literary output of Sir Hugh Clifford, a British colonial administrator who served in the Malay States from 1883 to 1903, arguing that Clifford's novels and short stories constitute a valuable historical source for understanding the period when traditional Malay society encountered both British colonialism and accelerating Chinese immigration. Wicks contends that Clifford's fiction reveals an "intriguingly ambiguous gap between public life and private thought" (p. 54), exposing doubts about imperialism that the official record does not capture.

## Summary

Wicks draws on three widely available collections of Clifford's short stories—*In a Corner of Asia* (1926), *Bush-Whacking* (1929), and Roff's 1966 Oxford University Press edition—to analyse the features of the Malayan world that Clifford chose to portray. The article is organised thematically rather than chronologically, moving through Clifford's physical descriptions of the landscape, his characterisations of Malay social structure and custom, his reflections on the purposes and outcomes of British rule, his treatment of Malay-Chinese friction, and his recurring theme of heroism and self-sacrifice among ordinary people.

The central historical problem Wicks addresses is the tension between Clifford's public role as a "central figure in the Forward Movement" extending British control and his private literary expression of scepticism toward that very process (p. 53). Clifford's stories, Wicks argues, provide a "rather different and much more complex picture of the Residents and the world they ruled" than what Colonial Office files alone would yield (p. 55). The article situates Clifford's ambivalence within the broader historiographical debate of the late 1970s about the appropriate balance between indigenous and imperial perspectives in Malaysian history, positioning Clifford's writings as a source that resists easy categorisation in either camp.

Wicks also traces the formative influences on Clifford's literary sensibility—his Catholic aristocratic upbringing, his mother's thwarted literary ambitions, and his thirteen years of near-total isolation in the Pahang interior—to explain why his fiction carries both a romantic nostalgia for a vanishing world and a genuine empathetic engagement with non-European cultures. The article demonstrates that Clifford's generalisations about Malay character, while not fundamentally challenging popular stereotypes, were presented as outgrowths of a "defensible and legitimate cultural context" rather than as the offhand pronouncements of a superior race (p. 64).

### Key Findings

- Clifford served as British Agent to Pahang (1887–1888), Superintendent of Ulu Pahang (1890–1896), and second British Resident of Pahang (1896–1900, 1901–1903), making him one of twelve Englishmen who successively held Resident positions in the Protected Malay States between 1874 and 1896 (p. 56).
- In *The East Coast* (1897), Clifford wrote of "the boot of the ubiquitous white man" which "crushes down the forests, beats out roads, strides across the rivers, kicks down native institutions, and generally tramples on the growths of nature and the works of primitive man" (p. 57).
- Clifford's story *At the Court of Pelesu* is based on the actual murder of Go Hui, a Chinese British subject, by the Sultan's bodyguard in Pahang in February 1888, an event that provided the pretext for British intervention in the state (p. 66).
- In *The East Coast*, Clifford observed that in Perak and Selangor "the alien population far out-numbers the people of the land in most of the principal districts," making it possible for a European to spend weeks without meaningful contact with the indigenous population (p. 67).
- Clifford's literary career began in June 1895 when Arnot Reid, editor of the Singapore *Straits Times*, approached him during a short visit to write a series of articles on the east coast Malay States; these were republished as *East Coast Etchings* (1896, Singapore) and *In Court and Kampong* (1897, London) (p. 61).
- Clifford's 1927 fly-leaf note to *Studies in Brown Humanity* states that "every one of them is true in every detail," confirming the autobiographical basis of his fiction (p. 72).

### Conclusion

Wicks concludes that despite the "heavy cloak of romanticism and nostalgia" overhanging Clifford's writing, his literary efforts possess genuine historical significance because of his "intimate, hard-won knowledge, his sensitivity to cultural nuances, and his genuine attempt to perceive events, customs, and people from a viewpoint other than that of a triumphant colonial administrator" (p. 72). The most noteworthy discovery in Clifford's work, Wicks argues, was his recognition that the moral qualities he admired—virtue, innocence, charm, courtesy, faith, and nobility—were not exclusively European but were possessed by the common peoples of Malaya, even as the onward thrust of British imperialism was destroying the conditions under which those qualities could survive.

## Context

- Wicks draws primarily on Clifford's published fiction (three story collections) and secondary literature on Clifford's career (Stockwell 1976, Allen's "Two Imperialists"), rather than on archival collections; the article's contribution is methodological, demonstrating how literary texts by colonial officials can supplement and complicate the administrative record.
- The article engages directly with the late-1970s Malaysian historiographical debate over the "imperial theme" versus indigenous focus, citing Ernest Chew's observation of a "revisionist tendency" and J. de V. Allen's work on the Residents, to argue for the continued relevance of British-source material in constructing a comprehensive Malaysian history (pp. 55–56).
