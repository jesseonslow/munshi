# Some Illanun and Bajau marriage customs in the Kota Belud district, North Borneo

D. Headly, a colonial administrator in North Borneo, published this brief ethnographic note in 1951 describing the marriage customs of the Illanun and Bajau peoples of the Kota Belud district. The article documents a stratified dowry system, reciprocal payment obligations, and wedding ritual practices among two closely intermarried Muslim communities on the Bornean plain.

## Summary

Headly's note is a compact field description of marriage among the Illanun and Bajau, two Muslim groups who have intermarried freely in the Kota Belud district for generations. The Illanuns arrived from Mindanao approximately 150 years prior, while the Bajaus' origins are less certain but probably also Philippine. Both groups are now primarily peasant farmers whose main wealth lies in buffaloes, though some Illanuns also fish. Headly situates them within a broader cultural milieu of weaving, wood carving, horsemanship, pantun poetry, and a history that shifted from piracy to cattle-lifting.

The core of the article is a detailed account of the social classification system that governs marriage payments. Illanun society divides into two main classes—Dato (two subclasses) and Pitu (three subclasses)—each with a fixed dowry (*berian*) originally denominated in the weight of brass cannon but now converted to cash. The system encodes lineage prestige: the Dato are said to descend from the original Mindanao leaders, while mixed Illanun-Bajau descent lowers a person's class and corresponding dowry value. Headly notes that in practice the rigid classification is frequently modified by the social standing of the lower-class party, and that many Bajaus can trace some Illanun blood in their pedigrees.

Beyond the dowry system, Headly describes several associated customs: *Pelit*, a reciprocal payment obligation between relatives of marrying parties; *Peramaian-Peramaian*, a small collection from the groom's guests distributed to the bride's household helpers; and *Benga* (or *Getasrirai*), a sum paid to the Master of Ceremonies and elders who officiate the ceremony. He also records that marriages are parent-arranged but the children may object, that elopement is handled through a respected intermediary who negotiates between the fathers, and that the *berian* balance functions as a deterrent against hasty divorce. The article closes with a vivid description of the *Berunsai*, a communal line-dance performed in the open-plan houses.

### Key Findings

- The Illanun social hierarchy comprises two Dato grades (15 pikul / $300 and 10 pikul / $200) and three Pitu grades (8 pikul / $160, 7 pikul / $140, and 6 pikul / $132), with dowry values originally expressed in weight of brass cannon (pp. 159–160).
- Children of mixed-class marriages are theoretically reduced one step in the social ladder; a child of a 15-pikul parent and a lower-class parent is valued at only 10 pikuls (p. 160).
- The *Pelit* payment, a reciprocal obligation between relatives of marrying parties, varies by class and is payable whether the child is male or female (p. 160).
- The groom's side must supply oxen (*sapi*) for the wedding breakfast: 7 head for all Pitu 7 grades and 10 head for all Dato 10 grades, with a sapi valued at $4 for this purpose against a current market price of $70 per head (p. 160).
- The *Benga* (or *Getasrirai*) is a sum of $5–$10 handed by the groom's father to the Master of Ceremonies, who then divides it among himself, the lecturer, and supporting elders (p. 161).
- The *Peramaian-Peramaian* collection from the groom's guests rarely exceeds $10 in total (p. 160).

### Conclusion

Headly presents the Illanun-Bajau marriage system as a functional blend of rigid hereditary classification and pragmatic flexibility. The dowry structure encodes lineage prestige in a quantifiable form, but in practice social standing, intermarriage, and negotiation routinely modify the theoretical values. The *berian* balance, payable upon faultless divorce, serves as a built-in check on marital impatience, while the *Pelit* and *Benga* customs distribute economic and social obligations across the wider kinship network.

## Context

- D. Headly was a colonial administrator in the North Borneo Protectorate; the article was received in July 1950 and reflects the ethnographic note tradition of the British colonial service, in which district officers recorded local customs for the Society's journal.
- The study draws entirely on Headly's direct observation and local informants in Kota Belud; no archival or documentary sources are cited. Its historiographical value lies in recording a classificatory dowry system and associated payment customs at a moment when the brass-cannon standard had long been replaced by cash but the old terminology persisted.
