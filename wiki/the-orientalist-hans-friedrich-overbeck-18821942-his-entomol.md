# The orientalist Hans Friedrich Overbeck 1882–1942: his entomological work, prisoner-ofwar experiences and known photographic images

Robert W. Taylor's 2014 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* reconstructs the dual scholarly identity of Hans Friedrich Overbeck (1882–1942), a German mercantile trader and Malayologist based in Singapore and the Dutch East Indies who was simultaneously an active amateur entomologist and myrmecological collector. Drawing on Australian wartime archives, Dresden Museum correspondence, and recently discovered photographs, Taylor bridges two academic communities—Malayologists and entomologists—that had been largely unaware of each other's knowledge of the same individual, and in doing so recovers a significant body of entomological work produced under the unusual conditions of World War I internment in Australia.

## Summary

The article addresses a historiographical gap: while Overbeck was well known to Malayologists through his four books and over sixty papers on Malay language, poetry, and ethnography (published largely in the JMBRAS itself), his entomological contributions were essentially unknown to that community, and vice versa. Taylor, writing as an entomologist with access to Australian archival collections, demonstrates that Overbeck's scientific output was not merely interrupted by his two periods of wartime imprisonment but was in part *produced* during them. At Holsworthy and Trial Bay Gaol in New South Wales, Overbeck collected Australian ants and other insects for the Dresden myrmecologist Hugo Viehmeyer, resulting in the description of forty-five new ant taxa and a new beetle genus from his internment-period collections.

The narrative is informed by several primary source streams: the Dresden Museum's surviving correspondence with Overbeck (1925–1939), which reveals his collecting methods, sites, and scientific temperament; Australian government records of enemy alien internment, including his 1917 alien registration form and prisoner identification photograph; and the Behn, Meyer company history, which provides corporate context for his career. Taylor also reconstructs the circumstances of Overbeck's death aboard the *Van Imhoff* in January 1942, situating it within the broader pattern of Dutch internment of German residents in the East Indies and the Japanese advance through Sumatra.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to the identification and reproduction of seven archival photographs—Overbeck's 1915 prisoner identification image, a c. 1922 office photograph from Semarang, and five WWI camp images by fellow prisoner Paul Dubotzki—thereby ending a long-standing assumption that no photographs of Overbeck had survived the conflagrations of the 1940s.

### Key Findings

- Overbeck's Australian internment collections yielded 45 new ant species or subspecies across 20 genera and 6 subfamilies; Trial Bay was type-locality for 24 of these taxa and Liverpool (Holsworthy) for 19 (pp. 40–41).
- Among ants alone, a genus (*Overbeckia*) and 13 species or subspecies have been named in his honour; a new beetle genus was also described from his Liverpool collection (pp. 38, 41).
- Overbeck was interned at Tanglin Barracks, Singapore from 1914, transported with 271 other prisoners to Australia in March/April 1915, held at Holsworthy until August 1915, transferred to Trial Bay Gaol, and repatriated on the SS *Kursk* on 29 May 1919 (pp. 39–40).
- He died on 18 January 1942 when the *Van Imhoff*, carrying 364 prisoners from Allas Vallei camp, was bombed south of Nias; 55 men survived in a single lifeboat (pp. 44–45).
- Overbeck published four books and 67 papers or book reviews on Malayological subjects, documented 1,500 Javanese children's songs and 175 theatrical pieces, and compiled a two-volume German translation of the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* while a prisoner in Australia (pp. 43–44).
- His first post-war insect specimens were received by the Museum für Tierkunde, Dresden in 1919; the earliest surviving letter in the correspondence is dated 13 August 1925, with a gap from 1927 to 1932 (p. 42).

### Conclusion

Taylor's definitive takeaway is that Overbeck was a resolutely dedicated scholar and scientist whose life was shaped by the collision of his German nationality with the geopolitical upheavals of two world wars, and whose entomological legacy—particularly the Australian taxa described from his internment collections—represents a body of work that would have been lost to both Malayological and entomological historiography without the convergence of archival research and the recent discovery of his photographic images.

## Context

- Primary archival collections explored include the National Archives of Australia (Canberra), the Australian War Memorial, the National Library of Australia, the Migration Heritage Centre (Sydney), the Staatsarchiv Bremen, and the Museum für Tierkunde, Dresden (now Humboldt Museum, Berlin).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its explicit bridging of two disconnected scholarly literatures and in the recovery of Overbeck's entomological output as a product of wartime internment, a context previously unexamined in either the Malayological or the myrmecological record.
