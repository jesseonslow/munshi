# Notes on Satingphra

Alastair Lamb, writing from the Department of History at the University of Malaya, published this short note in 1964 to document and analyse a collection of bronzes, ceramics, and stone objects recovered from the site of Satingphra, a former port town on the South China Sea coast of South Thailand near modern Songkhla. The article's central thesis is that Satingphra functioned as a major entrepôt from approximately 1200 to the late 15th century A.D., and that it likely formed the eastern terminus of trans-Peninsula trade routes whose western counterpart was Pengkalan Bujang in Kedah, Malaya.

## Summary

Lamb's note grew out of a series of reconnaissance visits to South Thailand undertaken by the University of Malaya between 1960 and 1964, during which he and colleagues surveyed the archaeological potential of the provinces south of the Isthmus of Kra. The Satingphra site, where the outline of ancient town walls can still be traced, had never been scientifically excavated; all finds had come to light through chance discovery by local inhabitants over the preceding decade and were held in the museum at Wat Matchemawat, Songkhla. Lamb's task was therefore to publish a representative selection of these objects—eight bronzes, a wide range of ceramics, a grinding slab, a five-chambered stone container, and six Neolithic stone implements—and to situate them within the broader archaeological picture of the northern Malay Peninsula.

The article's analytical core is a detailed ceramic comparison between Satingphra and Pengkalan Bujang, the latter having been extensively excavated by Lamb himself in 1961. The two assemblages are closely parallel in their major categories of Chinese celadons, white wares, stonewares, and earthenwares, but diverge in specific ways that allow Lamb to refine the chronology: Satingphra yields wares slightly later than the latest Pengkalan Bujang material (notably Sawankalok stoneware and a few Annamese blue-and-white fragments), while lacking the glass beads and Middle Eastern glazed wares characteristic of the west coast site. This pattern supports the interpretation that both sites were active during the same broad period but that Satingphra's occupation extended somewhat longer, dying out as a major trading centre by around 1500 A.D. The absence of any T'ang-dated ceramics at Satingphra is noted as a fact of considerable chronological significance, distinguishing it from earlier west coast sites such as Takuapa.

Beyond ceramics, Lamb draws attention to the cosmopolitan character of the bronze assemblage, which shows stylistic influences from South India (Cola), Bengal (Pala), and possibly Java, consistent with a port engaged in wide-ranging Southeast Asian commerce. The five-chambered stone container is identified as the nearest geographical parallel to the nine-chambered reliquary or foundation deposit containers known from Kedah, raising the possibility that ancient Kedah and Satingphra once formed part of the same political unit.

### Key Findings

- The Satingphra ceramic assemblage dates the site's major occupation to approximately 1200–1500 A.D., with the scarcity of Chinese blue-and-white wares arguing against significant trade continuing into the 16th century (pp. 77–78).
- A single 10 × 14 ft trench at Pengkalan Bujang, 6 ft deep, yielded approximately 4,500 glass beads, whereas the entire Satingphra collection at Wat Matchemawat contains only one string of about 20 glass and stone beads, underscoring the east–west asymmetry in the trade (p. 77).
- The Satingphra bronze collection includes the base and feet of a standing figure that must have been at least 3 feet high, alongside smaller figures (mostly under 12 inches) representing Mahayana Buddhist and Hindu deities (p. 75).
- A five-chambered sandstone container (base 8 × 8 inches, 5 inches high, with a cruciform arrangement of square chambers) was identified as the closest geographical parallel to the nine-chambered reliquary containers from Kedah, though it differs in being solid rather than hollow and in having square rather than circular chambers (pp. 84–86).
- Four stone grinding slabs with rollers were found at Satingphra, closely comparable to a specimen from the Kerubong hoard near Malacca (dated to the 15th century); the sandstone used occurs in neither locality, suggesting the objects were traded widely, possibly manufactured in India (pp. 83–84).
- Six Neolithic stone implements of quadrangular section, made from black stone or yellowish-brown chert, were found in the Satingphra region, possibly from caves in the limestone islands of Thale Luang (p. 87).

### Conclusion

Lamb concludes that Satingphra and Pengkalan Bujang were complementary entrepôts—eastern and western termini of a common system of trans-Peninsula trade routes—and that a synthesis of evidence from both sites would substantially advance understanding of the economic and political organisation of the northern Malay Peninsula in the 13th to 15th centuries. He further suggests that local legends concerning early Satingphra bear interesting parallels to the pre-Islamic section of the *Hikayat Marong Mahawangsa* (Kedah Annals), and that Satingphra may prove to be a most important source for understanding the archaeology of ancient Kedah.

## Context

- The primary material examined is the unexcavated collection held at Wat Matchemawat, Songkhla, supplemented by sherds Lamb collected personally from the Satingphra site; no scientific excavation had been carried out at the site by 1964.
- The article represents an early attempt to integrate South Thai archaeological evidence into the study of pre-Islamic Malayan history, a field then dominated by west coast sites; it laid groundwork for the comparative framework that would later underpin studies of the trans-Peninsula trade system.
