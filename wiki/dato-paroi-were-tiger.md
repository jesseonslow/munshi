# Dato’ Paroi, were-tiger

Zainal Abidin bin Ahmad, a prominent Malay scholar of the 1920s, published this ethnographic account in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1925, documenting the widespread Negri Sembilan belief in Dato' Paroi as a were-tiger who commands a civilised army of tigers on Gunong Angsi. The article assembles multiple narrative episodes—mythical, anecdotal, and ritual—to present a coherent picture of how this figure functions in local popular religion, culminating in a description of the *Keramat To' Paroi* and its syncretic ceremony. Ahmad's overarching thesis is that the Dato' Paroi complex represents a layered folk belief in which pre-Islamic animist survivals have been partially overlaid with Muslim ritual elements, and that the figure has become a cultural by-word of extraordinary magnitude throughout the "Nine States."

## Summary

The article opens by establishing the cosmological geography of the belief: Dato' Paroi resides atop Gunong Angsi, where his tiger subjects live in settlements, farm, study, read the Koran, perform Muslim devotions, and practise *bersilat*. Ahmad then presents a series of narrative episodes that collectively build the character. The most detailed is the story of a boy who, on a Hari Raya eve, is taken by his deceased grandfather (now a tiger) to Gunong Angsi, where he witnesses the full spectrum of the tiger brotherhood's activities for two days before being returned in "the twinkling of an eye." The meat-buying incident provides what Ahmad calls the empirical anchor for the belief in a boy-attendant hidden in the tiger's left foot: villagers sell meat to a strange lad who carries it into the forest to roast for a tiger, and the small human footprint found within the tiger's tracks is thereafter identified as that boy's.

The war narrative with Dato' Gunong Ledang introduces a rival tiger-clan centred on Mt. Ophir, whose members were formerly enchained for their ferocity. The battle at what is now Bukit Putus—where the hills were "cut in two"—serves as an aetiological myth explaining the landscape. Ahmad explicitly flags this as an *ex parte* version glorifying the Paroi side, and notes that the expression "Dato' Paroi" has become a general interjection for anything of stupendous size or awe-inspiring quality in Negri Sembilan.

The final section shifts from myth to observable ritual practice at the *Keramat To' Paroi*, a modest sacred spot by a small rill on the Seremban–Kuala Pilah road. Here Ahmad describes the actual ceremony in ethnographic detail: Chinese light joss-sticks and red candles; Malays sacrifice goats, bathe the supplicant's child in the blood, feast on saffroned rice, boiled eggs, and curried goat, burn incense, scatter toasted rice, read Arabic prayers, fire crackers, and distribute alms. Ahmad's interpretive verdict is that except for the Arabic prayers and alms-giving, the ceremony is "heathen"—a survival of primitive animist and superficially Hindu practice from the "Days of Ignorance."

### Key Findings

- The village of Paroi, from which the title originates, lies approximately 6 miles from Seremban on the road to Kuala Pilah; the Dato' himself is believed to reside on the summit of Gunong Angsi, which stands in a triangle formed by Kuala Pilah, Seremban, and Tampin (p. 74).
- The *Keramat To' Paroi* is situated by a small rill a few yards to the left of the Seremban–Kuala Pilah road; the physical remains consist of nothing more than a few chips of stone soiled by red wax from Chinese candles or wrapped in strips of red and white cloth (p. 77).
- Alms distributed at the Keramat ceremony range from one to fifty cents (Straits currency) per person present (p. 78).
- The Gunong Ledang tiger-clan was formerly entirely enchained (*harimau berantai*) on account of their wildness; the aetiological battle that gave Bukit Putus its name ("cut hill") took place on the outskirts of Gunong Angsi proper (pp. 76–77).
- The Keramat is venerated by Malays, Chinese, and Tamils alike; Malays travel from Terachi, Jelebu, Johol, and Jempul to pay vows, sacrificing one or more goats (p. 77).
- Ahmad identifies the meat-buying episode as an *ex post facto* invention constructed to uphold a pre-existing popular belief, and the war narrative as an *ex parte* account (pp. 75, 77).

### Conclusion

Ahmad's definitive historical takeaway is that the Dato' Paroi complex is a palimpsest: its core is a pre-Islamic animist belief in a powerful spirit-being associated with a specific landscape feature, onto which Muslim ritual elements (Arabic prayers, alms-giving, the Koran-reading tigers) have been grafted in a manner he considers "strange" and "incongruous." The figure endures not as a theological entity within orthodox Islam but as a folk-cultural symbol of overwhelming power, still believed to roam the forest reserves of Negri Sembilan "immortal and unchanged" at the time of writing.

## Context

- Zainal Abidin bin Ahmad (1891–1959) was a Malay scholar, writer, and later a senior civil servant; in 1925 he was establishing himself as a leading Malay-language intellectual. His authorship positions this article as an insider's documentation of his own community's folk beliefs, written for a mixed scholarly readership of the MBRAS.
- The interpretive framing—labelling the Keramat ceremony "heathen" and treating Muslim elements as later accretions that have "crept in"—reflects the colonial-era ethnographic convention of positioning Islam as the normative civilisational layer and pre-Islamic practice as a primitive survival. This framing, while analytically useful for its time, imposes a hierarchical teleology on the syncretic ritual that modern scholarship would treat more neutrally as cultural layering.
