# Nora Chatri in Kedah: a preliminary report (with appendix

Ghulam Sarwar Yousof's 1982 preliminary report documents the Nora Chatri (locally Manora) folk dance-theatre tradition in Kedah, a genre sustained by approximately twenty groups of Thai-descended communities in the state's northern districts. The article functions as a structural ethnography of a living performance form, tracing its ritual architecture from opening invocations through trance possession, dance, and dramatic narrative to closing rites, while situating the tradition within the broader Malay-Thai cultural corridor along the Malaysia-Thailand border.

## Summary

Yousof's central concern is to map the complete performance structure of Nora Chatri as practised in Kedah, distinguishing between ritual and non-ritual occasions while demonstrating that the sacred dimension permeates even ostensibly secular performances. The article proceeds through the constituent elements of a typical three-night performance: the Wai Kru (salutation to teachers) with its invocations to Mesi Mala, Hindu divinities, and ancestral spirits; trance sessions in which performers and observers alike may be possessed by named beings; the Manora dance depicting mythical bird-maidens (kinnaris); the lakons (dramatic stories) performed in a mixture of southern Thai dialect and Kedah Malay; and the Sip Song Bot, a recitation of twelve melodies summarising the traditional repertoire. Yousof notes that the genre retains "a considerable aura of the sacred" even in its entertainment functions, and that non-ritual performances are comparatively rare (p. 58).

The article's analytical framework draws on prior scholarship by Ginsberg and Nichols on the etymology and Thai antecedents of the genre, and on Archaimbault's work on the Sam Sam (Thai immigrant) communities of Kedah and Perlis. Yousof identifies an animistic or shamanistic substrate beneath the Buddhist Sudhana-Manohara narrative, arguing that the Mesi Mala origin myth and the trance-possession practices reveal a pre-Buddhist ritual base upon which the Jataka tale has been grafted (p. 51). The relationship between the Kedah variety and its southern Thai cousin is noted but left for further investigation, as is the precise distinction between Nora Chatri on either side of the border.

The appendix reproduces the Mesi Mala origin myth in full, a narrative in which a princess banished by her father for her obsessive drumming lands on Pulau Kacang, receives divine aid, and is eventually summoned back to cure a plague through performance. The myth encodes the genre's essential features—the coconut-shell drum as proto-instrument, the crown (chada) as a gift from the Buddha via Indian visitors, the bird-tail costume element derived from a city gate design, and the belief that Mesi Mala's spirit still possesses female dancers in trance.

### Key Findings

- Approximately twenty Nora Chatri groups operate in the Naka, Jitra, Kodiac, Baling, and Pedu districts of Kedah, some attached to Thai Buddhist temples (wat) (p. 51).
- The traditional repertoire comprised twelve lakons; the Sip Song Bot recites summaries of these twelve stories—Nang Manora, Phra Rot, Thip Ke Son, Lakhana Wong, Kho But, Thasa Bun, Dara Wong, Si Suan, Nang Mora, Sinurat, Nang Boon Tha, and Chau Krai (Nai Krai)—with one song performed per night and all twelve in abridged form on the final night (p. 57).
- The orchestra comprises a double-reed oboe (serunai), a pair of single-headed stick drums (gedug), a pair of hour-glass drums (gedumbak), hand cymbals (kesi/ching), and bamboo or wooden clappers; the serunai carries the melody and the ensemble falls silent during lakons except to punctuate dramatic moments (p. 53).
- Trance possession follows a strict sex-coincidence rule: Mesi Mala can only emplace in a female body, while Phran Bun and other male spirits enter only males; the moment of possession is marked by progressive shivering, after which the trancer is covered with a white sheet and questioned by the guru (p. 55).
- The tiered crown (chada) is worn exclusively by initiated performers and is believed to possess magical protective powers; its conferral during the initiation rite (which includes ceremonial cutting of the forelock) marks the dancer's eligibility to establish a Nora Chatri school (pp. 54–56).
- In the Mesi Mala myth, the group was banished on a raft, survived a storm to reach Pulau Kacang, and was sustained for fifteen years by a visiting angel (dewa) who restored and enlarged their lost instruments; two Indian bead-sellers (Phran Bun and Khun Sat Tha) later brought back a crown given by the Buddha, which remains in use in performances to this day (pp. 59–60).

### Conclusion

Yousof presents Nora Chatri as a syncretic performance form whose layered structure—animistic trance practice, Buddhist narrative, Hindu divine invocations, and Thai dramatic convention—testifies to prolonged cultural contact across the Malay-Thai border. The genre's continued vitality among Thai-descended communities in Kedah, its retention of sacred functions, and the persistence of the Mesi Mala myth as a living explanatory framework all indicate a tradition that resists easy categorisation as either purely entertainment or purely ritual.

## Context

- The article draws on field observation in Kedah and builds on prior published work by Ginsberg (1972), Nichols (1924), Archaimbault (1957), and Sheppard (1973); no specific archival collection is cited, the evidence being primarily ethnographic and oral.
- As a preliminary report, it explicitly flags unresolved questions—the precise Kedah-southern Thai stylistic distinctions, the full lakon repertoire, and the choreographic analysis of the Manora dance—as areas for future research.
