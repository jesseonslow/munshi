# A history of classical Malay literature. Rev. ed

W. W. Skeat, a British colonial administrator and scholar in the Straits Settlements, published this detailed eyewitness account of Malay magical ceremonies in 1898, drawing on direct observation of agricultural, healing, and maritime rituals to demonstrate that these practices constitute a coherent system of sympathetic magic rather than "mere childish folly." The article's overarching thesis, articulated through reference to Frazer's *Golden Bough*, is that Malay padi-ceremonies, spirit-possession healing, and water-spirit invocations are all intelligible expressions of an old-world religious logic in which effects are produced by imitating them.

## Summary

Skeat's account is organized around three major ceremonial complexes he either witnessed or had described to him by pawangs (medicine men and women) in the Malacca and Negri Sembilan region. The first and most extensively documented is the padi (rice) ceremony, in which the first seven ears of a selected sheaf are treated as a "rice-child": swathed in cloth, placed in a basket-cradle with an egg, a jungle-nut, a shell, and an iron nail, carried home under an umbrella, and laid upon a sleeping mat with pillows. A three-day taboo follows, during which no food or money may leave the house, no hair may be cut, and the hearth fire must not go out. Skeat interprets each element through the lens of sympathetic magic—the sugar-cane stem as a May-pole of fertility, the iron nail as a charm against evil spirits, the pawang's eye-rolling as an imitation of rice-whitening, and the throat-click as an imitation of slow, easy eating.

The second complex is a healing ceremony for a sick child, in which a male pawang is possessed by a tiger spirit after reciting a 61-line invocation song (*Lagu Pemanggil*) that summons a spirit aboard a golden ship. The possessed pawang licks the patient's body from head to foot, lets blood from his own arm over the patient, and engages in an imaginary combat with the disease-spirit using a kris and an areca-blossom spike. Skeat provides a glossary of *Bhasa Hantu* (spirit language), a coded vocabulary the pawang uses while in possession, which he notes shows cognate relationships with New Zealand Māori terms. The third complex covers kelong (fishing-station) ceremonies, including invocations to water-spirits, the daubing of fishing-stake uprights with *tepong tawar* (magic flour), and a set of taboos imposed on kelong workers (sexual continence for seven days, prohibition on bathing without a cloth, and ban on using sarongs or umbrellas on the platform).

Throughout, Skeat grounds his narrative in extensive verbatim Malay text—charms, prayers, and songs—often providing his own translation and philological notes. He is careful to distinguish between what he personally witnessed and what was merely described to him, and he flags uncertainties in his readings of obscure phrases.

### Key Findings

- The padi ceremony's "cradle" contained exactly seven items: a strip of white cloth, a piece of partly coloured thread (*benang pancharona*), an egg, a hard jungle-nut (*buah kras*), a shell called *krang*, a long iron nail, and five *hasta* of red cloth for slinging (p. 3).
- The *Lagu Pemanggil* (calling song) comprises 61 lines and invokes a spirit aboard a ship called *Lanchang Kuning* (the Yellow Ship), whose mast is named *Raja Mendelu*, rudder *Lebah Bergantong* (the Hanging Bees' Nest), and oars *Jari Lipan* (the Centipede's Feet), with fourteen oarsmen (p. 16–17).
- The *Bhasa Hantu* (spirit language) glossary records 18 terms; notably, the word for "child" in spirit language is *demit*, which Skeat compares to the New Zealand Māori *tamaita* (p. 23–24).
- The kelong offering tray contained 28 small woven rice-receptacles (14 *katupat* and 14 *lepat*), each set comprising seven sorts of cooked and seven sorts of uncooked food, plus four banana-leaf buckets of sugar-cane juice and a central receptacle of fowl's blood (p. 31).
- The three-day padi taboo prohibited: (1) money, rice, salt, oil, or tamarinds leaving the house; (2) any noise; (3) hair-cutting; (4) reapers' shadows falling on the rice; (5) the lamp or hearth fire going out; and (6) reapers failing to repeat a protective charm at the start of each reaping session (p. 9–10).
- The 1891 census showed that in Rembau (Negri Sembilan), not one per cent of native customary land holdings were registered as the property of men, reflecting the matrilineal inheritance customs traced by Skeat to intermarriage between the Waris tribe and the Sakai (p. 42).

### Conclusion

Skeat's definitive takeaway is that Malay magical ceremonies are not irrational superstition but a systematic application of the principle of sympathetic magic—"that any effect can be produced by imitating it"—and that they should be studied with the same rigour as any other branch of comparative religion. He explicitly warns against the tendency to dismiss such customs as "entirely devoid of any real significance" and argues that the ethnographer's task is to supply "rigid and accurate knowledge" in place of conjecture.

## Context

- Skeat was a senior British colonial official (later Secretary to the Governor of the Straits Settlements and a judge) writing from a position of administrative authority over the communities he described; his framing as "eye-witness" and his use of Frazer's evolutionary framework position the Malay pawang as a subject of scientific observation rather than a peer interlocutor.
- The article draws on no archival collections per se but on direct field observation in the Malacca and Negri Sembilan region, supplemented by references to earlier publications in the Society's Journal (notably Blagden's account of planting-out ceremonies in No. 29). Its historiographical contribution lies in providing one of the earliest detailed, verbatim records of Malay spirit-possession healing and the *Bhasa Hantu* vocabulary, which remained a reference point for subsequent scholars of Malay religion.
