# English country traders and their relations with Malay rulers in the late eighteenth century

W.G. Miller's 2011 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the activities of English country traders—private merchant mariners operating outside the East India Company's formal service—in the Malay Archipelago between 1750 and 1820. Miller argues that these largely forgotten figures accumulated as much practical knowledge of Malay languages, protocols, politics, and geography as Company officials did, and that this knowledge was a critical enabler of British authority in Southeast Asia at the turn of the nineteenth century.

## Summary

The article addresses a significant historiographical gap: the overwhelming focus on East India Company officials in narratives of British re-entry into Southeast Asian affairs, at the expense of the private merchant mariners who actually formed the dominant European presence in many Malay ports. Miller identifies four concurrent forms of contact between country traders and Malay principalities—provisioning, commerce, political engagement, and socializing—and uses a range of individual case studies to demonstrate how each generated distinct but complementary bodies of knowledge. The political setting he describes is one of "loosening centres of control" among major Malay polities, intensified by Bugis migration and Iranun raiding, which created both hazards and opportunities for independent traders operating in the gaps of Dutch enforcement.

The commercial analysis reveals a circular trade pattern linking India, the Malay Archipelago, and Canton, in which country traders sold Indian opium and cotton goods in Malay ports and used the proceeds or exotic products to purchase Chinese goods. Miller draws on the correspondence of John Adolphus Pope (as edited by Anne Bulley), the voyage accounts of Thomas Forrest, and Dutch port records from Batavia to illustrate the barter systems, the mediating role of the *saudagar raja*, and the accumulation of what he terms "corporate knowledge" among the trading community. The political section is the article's richest contribution, showing how country traders served as diplomatic intermediaries, intelligence gatherers, and even active participants in local power struggles—most dramatically in the Moluccas, where traders like Risdon, Court, and Stewart maintained a close alliance with Sultan Nuku of Tidore that directly facilitated British military operations.

The socializing dimension challenges the prevailing stereotype of the Malay as treacherous. Through extended stays—Forrest's eight months at Mindanao, Lander's four and a half years in the Moluccas, Pope's repeated visits to Aceh and Kedah—several country traders developed genuine affection for Malay society, learned the language, and in some cases adopted local dress and customs. Miller uses these examples to argue that the country traders' cultural immersion preceded by nearly a century the better-known European settlers of the nineteenth century who formed the basis of Joseph Conrad's fictional characters.

### Key Findings

- Of the 355 European inhabitants recorded in Bombay in 1811, 251 were classified as "free mariners," the EIC's official term for country traders, indicating their numerical dominance in the European population of Indian ports (p. 25).
- By 1780, country traders were bringing three-fourths of all British goods to China, underscoring their centrality to the triangular trade that underpinned EIC profitability (pp. 24–25).
- The hazards of trading were real: Captain Coston and officers of the *Friendship* were murdered while anchored in the Kedah River in 1782, and in 1795 Captain Sadler and several crew members of the *Transfer* disappeared at Mempawah along with all goods taken ashore (p. 31).
- Thomas Forrest remained at Mindanao for eight months (May 1775 to January 1776), producing a detailed description of the country, a vocabulary of the local language, and a genealogy of the ruling family—evidence of deep cultural engagement beyond mere commerce (p. 37).
- A joint letter written by country traders Risdon, Court, Stewart and Sultan Nuku called on the Dutch-supported Sultan of Tidore, Kamaloedin, and his chiefs to desert the Dutch and acknowledge British suzerainty, demonstrating the traders' direct political agency (p. 36).
- Captain Court, after 27 months cruising in the Moluccas, provided Admiral Rainier's fleet with both meteorological and political intelligence before the 1796 British occupation, warning that "a red English ensign is the only English colours they are acquainted with" in the Halmahera Sea (p. 35).

### Conclusion

Miller's definitive takeaway is that the English country traders constituted an informal but highly effective network of knowledge accumulation and political intermediation that made British expansion into Southeast Asia materially possible. The body of practical intelligence they generated—linguistic competence, personal trust with rulers, familiarity with trading protocols and products, nautical charts, and political awareness—was transferred to Company officials, naval commanders, and eventually the colonial administration. In this sense, the country traders were "inadvertent imperialists" whose commercial and social activities laid the groundwork for formal British authority without any deliberate imperial design on their part.

## Context

- Primary archival collections explored include the British Library India Office Library and Records (Madras Military Consultations and Madras Public Consultations), Dutch Records from Malacca (Protocol Books), and the published navigational works of Laurie and Whittle and Horsburgh.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in shifting analytical focus from EIC institutional actors to the private merchant mariner as an agent of British knowledge production and political influence, building on but extending the work of Bassett, Bulley, Lewis, and Warren.
