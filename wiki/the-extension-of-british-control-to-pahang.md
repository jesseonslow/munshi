# The extension of British control to Pahang

Eunice Thio's 1957 article traces the protracted British effort to bring Pahang under colonial control from 1874 to 1888, arguing that the extension of the residential system was driven less by humanitarian concern over misgovernment than by the commercial interests of concession-holding companies and the strategic imperative of pre-empting German and French imperial expansion in the region.

## Summary

Thio reconstructs the sequence of diplomatic and political manoeuvres that culminated in Pahang's subjection to the British residential system, drawing heavily on Colonial Office Records (Series 273) to challenge the conventional account that the murder of the Chinese shopkeeper Goh Hui in 1888 was the proximate cause of British intervention. The article's central argument is that the 1887 Treaty, which established only a British Agent with consular powers, was inadequate to protect the substantial European capital already invested in Pahang's mineral concessions, and that it was the Pahang Corporation Ltd and allied concession-holders who exerted decisive pressure on both the Colonial Office and the Straits Government to escalate from an advisory to a controlling role.

The narrative is structured around the interplay of three sets of actors: the British officials (Weld, Swettenham, Clifford, Cecil Smith) who pursued a forward policy; Bendahara Wan Ahmad, whose absolute and arbitrary rule made him resistant to any curtailment of his powers; and the Sultan of Johore, Abu Bakar, whose financial interests in Pahang concessions and personal debts (approximately £140,000 in London) made him a fickle ally who alternately obstructed and facilitated British objectives. Thio shows how the Sultan's agents—his solicitors Rodyk and Davidson, his private secretary William Hole, and the Dato Mentri—operated as a covert network to delay the treaty until terms acceptable to the concession-holding community could be secured.

Primary sources are deployed to expose the gap between official justifications and underlying motives. Swettenham's 1885 travel journal documents the economic stagnation and oppressive taxation in Pahang; Clifford's 1887 journal records the widespread discontent among displaced Malay chiefs and Chinese miners; and most critically, the Colonial Secretary John Dickson's 1891 Legislative Council speech provides a candid contemporary admission that the 1888 treaty was concluded "not for the avenging of the life of a British subject, but really for the preservation of British capital." Thio uses these sources to demonstrate that the standard historiography of Swettenham and Winstedt, which presents the Goh Hui murder as the trigger for intervention, obscures the commercial logic that actually governed British decision-making.

### Key Findings

- In November 1883, the Bendahara leased approximately 2,000 square miles (about one-fifth of the state) for mining and planting to Lim Ah Sam and partners; by 1884, George Scaife had obtained two further concessions of roughly 900 square miles each for terms of fifty and seventy-five years (pp. 51–52).
- The 1887 Treaty established only a British Agent with consular powers, not a Resident whose advice "must be asked and acted upon," reflecting the Bendahara's successful resistance to the full residential system (pp. 65–67).
- Cecil Smith issued an ultimatum on 29 June 1888 giving the Sultan ten days to accept a British Resident; the Sultan's letter of 24 August 1888 acknowledged responsibility for Goh Hui's murder and requested a British officer "on a similar system to that existing in the Malay States under English protection" (pp. 71–72).
- The Pahang Corporation Ltd, formed in 1887 with Sir Edmond Pontifex (Cape Copper Company) as chairman and N. Story Maskelyne, M.P. on the board, held the largest concession and lobbied directly at the Colonial Office for recognition of its grant (pp. 68–69).
- A police force of fifty Sikhs and one hundred and fifty Malays was organized by July 1889, at which point Magistrates and Collectors began to supersede the Malay chiefs; intermittent disturbances continued from the Orang Kaya of Semantan's revolt in December 1890 until 1895 (p. 74).
- Sultan Abu Bakar of Johore's debts in London amounted to approximately £140,000, and his legal advisers were attempting to use Johore's opium revenues as security for a loan in the London money market, a factor that influenced his shifting stance toward the British (p. 64).

### Conclusion

Thio's definitive historical takeaway is that the introduction of the residential system in Pahang in 1888 was fundamentally a commercial operation disguised as a humanitarian intervention. The Goh Hui murder provided a convenient pretext, but the real engine of change was the pressure exerted by the Pahang Corporation and other concession-holding interests, who were "absolutely at the mercy" of the Sultan's arbitrary power over their grants and who could mobilize influence at the Colonial Office to compel the escalation from an advisory to a controlling British presence.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on Colonial Office Records, Series 273 (Volumes 104–157), supplemented by Swettenham's published journal, Clifford's unpublished journal entries, and the proceedings of the Straits Settlements Legislative Council (1891).
- Historiographically, the article challenges the established narrative of Swettenham and Winstedt by foregrounding the role of commercial capital in shaping imperial policy, and by demonstrating that the Colonial Office's apparent support for the Pahang Corporation was more ambiguous than the standard account suggests.
