# Frank Swettenham and Yap Ah Loy: the increase of British political influence in Kuala Lumpur

Ernest Chew's 1984 article examines the political relationship between British Resident Frank Swettenham and Capitan China Yap Ah Loy in Kuala Lumpur during the critical period of 1871–1885, when British "influence" in Selangor expanded from coastal gunboat diplomacy to effective control of the interior. Chew argues that this relationship, though it began on terms of personal cordiality, contained a political conflict that Swettenham himself never acknowledged in his published writings, and that the Residential system's displacement of indigenous and immigrant authorities undermines Swettenham's later claim to have originated the principle of "Indirect Rule."

## Summary

Chew structures the article around the successive phases of British involvement in Selangor, using the Swettenham–Yap relationship as a lens through which to examine the broader process of colonial encroachment. The narrative begins with Swettenham's first encounter with Yap in 1871–72, when the young cadet was struck by the Capitan's "redoubtable" authority over a town still ravaged by the Selangor civil war. The initial period of British intervention (1874–75) saw Swettenham and Davidson operating as useful auxiliaries to the established Malay and Chinese authorities, with Swettenham lavishing praise on Yap's achievements in his official reports. The tone shifted decisively with the arrival of Bloomfield Douglas as Resident in 1876, whose administrative incompetence and intolerance of rival authority eroded both Malay and Chinese autonomy. Douglas displaced Tunku Kudin as effective head of government, clashed with Yap over the latter's right to collect local revenues, and presided over a period of economic stagnation punctuated by fires and floods that he did little to address.

When Swettenham himself became Resident in September 1882, the relationship with Yap took on a new character. Chew identifies three interlocking sources of tension: Swettenham's training and experience were oriented toward Malay affairs rather than Chinese; his practical supremacy in the state (accountable only to the Straits Governor and Colonial Secretary) emboldened him to overrule Yap on municipal and fiscal matters; and his explicit philosophy of governing the Chinese through "determination of character" rather than amicability made conflict inevitable. The disputes over the market and gambling booth, the auctioning of revenue farms, and the railway's mode of management all illustrate the assertion of British paramountcy over a town that Yap had built. Chew draws on State Council minutes, correspondence between the Resident and the Governor, and Swettenham's own reports to demonstrate that the Council was a rubber-stamp body whose proceedings had been predetermined elsewhere.

The article's final section turns to the question of whether the Residential system can genuinely be called "Indirect Rule." Chew concludes that while indigenous and immigrant headmen were given a share in local government, the "direction and control of public affairs" rested firmly with the British Resident and his staff. The Malay *penghulus* were transformed from hereditary leaders into government servants supervised by European district officers, and the Chinese Capitan's magisterial functions were progressively subordinated to British authority.

### Key Findings

- The Selangor State Council met only four times between October 1882 and October 1883, and four times each in 1884 and 1885; agenda items were frequently settled in advance of their formal introduction, as when Swettenham's district administration paper was submitted to Governor Weld in June 1883 but presented to the Council only in September (pp. 85–86).
- The Kuala Lumpur revenue farm tender for 1883–84 showed an increase of 330 per cent over the previous holding, and Yap remained a partner in the highest tender received, ensuring that the Selangor Chinese retained control of the farms despite the auction (p. 80).
- At the time of his death in April 1885, Yap Ah Loy owned more than 150 houses, extensive mines and plantations, and employed more than 5,000 men; his death "not only paralysed the working of his own numerous miners, but also, for some weeks, affected the whole mining industry of Selangor" (p. 83).
- The transfer of the Resident's headquarters from Klang to Kuala Lumpur in 1880 marked a decisive encroachment on Yap's domain, fixing the British presence permanently at his doorstep and replacing the "genial" Sultan Abdul-Samad and Tunku Kudin with Douglas and his son-in-law Dominic Daly (p. 76).
- Swettenham's railway survey began in April 1883 and the first sod was cut by July 1883; the State Council's preference for private enterprise backed by a government loan was overruled in favour of a state-run railway financed by a Straits Government loan (p. 80).
- Swettenham's 1896 lecture to the Royal Colonial Institute declared that "it is almost hopeless to expect to make friends with a Chinaman" and that they "only realise two positions — the giving and the receiving of orders," a philosophy that Chew demonstrates was directly consonant with his actions toward Yap during 1882–84 (p. 82).

### Conclusion

Chew's definitive takeaway is that the Residential system in Selangor was not "Indirect Rule" in any meaningful sense. The British Resident functioned as a de facto regent exercising direct personal rule, with the State Council reduced to a forum for ratifying decisions already made. Swettenham's own claim to have originated the principle of "Indirect Rule" is therefore exposed as a retrospective myth; the reality was a steady increase in British administrative control that displaced both Malay and Chinese authorities, a process that Lugard himself had more accurately described as "the very antithesis" of the Nigerian system of indirect rule.

## Context

- Primary archival sources include the Colonial Office Records (CO 273 series, Public Record Office, London), Parliamentary Papers, Selangor State Council Minutes, Selangor Secretariat Files and the Swettenham Collection (all at the Malaysian National Archives), and Swettenham's edited *Malayan Journals 1874–1876*.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its challenge to P.J. Gullick's account, which Chew argues "tends to underrate the elements of conflict in the Swettenham-Yap relationship," and in its confirmation of Dr. Sadka's thesis on the declining influence of the State Councils in Perak and Selangor.
