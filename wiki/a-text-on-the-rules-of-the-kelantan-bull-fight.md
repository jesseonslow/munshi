# A text on the rules of the Kelantan bull-fight

John Waterstradt's 1902 account, published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, documents two arduous attempts to ascend Gunong Tahan—the mountain then believed to be the highest in the Malay Peninsula—via the Kelantan river system, driven by a desire to compare its fauna with that of Mount Kinabalu in Borneo. The narrative reveals that the mountain, long thought to approach 10,000 feet, was in fact only 5,500 feet high, and that the route from the Pahang side (from which earlier expeditions had failed) was far more precipitous than the Kelantan approach.

## Summary

Waterstradt's account is fundamentally a naturalist's field narrative, interweaving zoological and botanical collecting with detailed observations of the physical geography, indigenous peoples, and colonial administrative structures of Kelantan in the early 1900s. His first attempt, via the Aring river (a tributary of the Lebeh), took him to a peak he initially believed to be part of the Tahan range, only to discover it was an entirely separate mountain. His second and successful attempt, via the Galas river and a long traverse along the Bukit Gajah ridge, finally brought him to the true summit after a grueling crossing of the Relai river valley.

The narrative is rich in ethnographic and administrative detail that contextualizes the natural history. Waterstradt describes the Siamese political control over Kelantan, the Rajah's installation by Siamese authority, the Chinese gold-mining community at Pulai, the Sakai head-hunters who served as his guides, and the floating bamboo-raft villages of the riverine Malays. His observations on the mountain's geology (predominantly white quartz), its three parallel ridgelines, the discoloured water of high-altitude streams, and the distribution of rattan jungle above 4,000 feet provide a baseline physical description of the Tahan massif that remained authoritative for decades.

The account also serves as a record of the practical difficulties of pre-modern fieldwork in the Peninsula: the constant attrition of coolies, the impossibility of maintaining provisions in remote highland terrain, the seasonal flooding that rendered rivers impassable, and the near-total absence of infrastructure. Waterstradt's trip ultimately consumed seven months rather than the three he had planned, and he was forced to abandon his orchid collection on the mountain.

### Key Findings

- Gunong Tahan's summit was measured at approximately 5,500 feet, far below the 10,000 feet previously assumed; the mountain consists of three parallel east-west ranges connected at their highest points (pp. 13, 20).
- The mountain's rivers were identified as follows: on the Kelantan side, the Relai and two branches of the Aring; on the Pahang side, the Kechau, the Tahan, and possibly a branch of the Tembeling (p. 20).
- The Rajah of Kelantan, a man of about thirty-five, had been installed by the Siamese in preference to the rightful heir; a Siamese Commissioner and small garrison maintained oversight, and Waterstradt predicted Kelantan would become "to all purposes, except in name, a Siamese province" (p. 2).
- At Pulai on the Galas river, Chinese gold miners were earning approximately 75 cents per day; the village contained a couple of hundred inhabitants, mostly Chinese who had transitioned from mining to paddy cultivation as alluvial returns declined (p. 9).
- Waterstradt remained eighteen days near the summit, collecting birds and orchids; the best collecting ground was between 5,000 and 7,000 feet, and nighttime temperatures dropped to 50°F (pp. 21–22).
- The entire expedition, from departure in Singapore to return, took seven months rather than the three originally planned (p. 27).

### Conclusion

Waterstradt's definitive historical takeaway is that Gunong Tahan, despite its reputation as the Peninsula's highest peak, is a modest mountain of 5,500 feet whose true difficulty lies not in altitude but in the precipitous terrain of its Pahang-facing slopes and the extreme logistical challenges of provisioning in a remote, roadless interior. His account effectively corrects the geographical record and establishes the Kelantan-side route as the viable approach for future scientific expeditions.

## Context

- This is a contemporary colonial-era field narrative by a European naturalist operating under Siamese suzerainty in Kelantan; his observations on Siamese administrative control, the Rajah's position, and the Chinese mining community reflect the political realities of the period rather than a detached scholarly analysis.
- The account draws on no archival collections but constitutes a primary field record; its historiographical value lies in providing the first detailed physical and biological description of the Tahan massif from the Kelantan side, predating later botanical and geological surveys of the area.
