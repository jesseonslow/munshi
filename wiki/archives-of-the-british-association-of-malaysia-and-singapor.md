# Archives of the British Association of Malaysia and Singapore relating  to the Second World War

## Abstract

Through the initiative of Hugh Patterson Bryson, an archive of the British Association of Malaysia and Singapore was assembled in the 1960s, bringing together personal papers, memoirs, and photographs submitted by the Association’s members. This research note outlines the Second World War material, in particular first-hand accounts of the Japanese invasion and subsequent internment of civilians, along with surviving administrative records of two internment camps in Singapore. Reference is made to a digitisation and conservation project carried out by Cambridge University Library to open up this collection to a global audience of researchers.

## Summary

This research note by Sally Kent, curator for the Royal Commonwealth Society at Cambridge University Libraries, provides a guide to the Second World War material within the archive of the British Association of Malaysia and Singapore, one of the most heavily used collections in the Royal Commonwealth Society holdings at Cambridge. The archive, comprising personal papers, memoirs, photographs, and administrative records contributed by the Association's members, documents British settler life in Malaya and Singapore from the late nineteenth century through the Malayan Emergency. Kent's note focuses specifically on the wartime subset: first-hand accounts of the Japanese invasion and the fall of Singapore, and the subsequent internment of civilians in the camps at Changi Gaol and Sime Road.

The note traces the archive's provenance from the Association's origins as a lobbying body in 1868 through its re-establishment in 1920, highlighting the pivotal role of Hugh Patterson Bryson, who in the early 1960s initiated the systematic gathering of original documents and solicited written recollections from members. When the Association disbanded in 1973, its archive was deposited with the Royal Commonwealth Society and later transferred to Cambridge University Library in 1993. Kent categorises the wartime material into two broad types—personal memoirs and camp administrative records—and illustrates the range of sources with examples including the illustrated memoir of William Foster Norton Churchill, the quartermaster's ration records, camp commandant John Weekley's daily-life records, nominal rolls, and the camp newspapers.

A significant portion of the note is devoted to a Wellcome Trust-funded conservation and digitisation project (2015–2017) that produced the online collection 'Voices of civilian internment: WWII Singapore,' making the material accessible to a global research audience. Kent also addresses the archive's limitations: it presents a predominantly male and British perspective, and researchers seeking fuller understanding of female internees or those of mixed heritage must turn to supplementary sources. The note closes by situating the archive within ongoing debates about the commemoration of the Second World War in Southeast Asia and the questions of who is remembered and how.

### Key Findings

- The archive comprises approximately 24 boxes of material, including personal papers, memoirs, photographs, and administrative records, documenting British settler life from the late nineteenth century to the Malayan Emergency (p. 132).
- Hugh Patterson Bryson (1898–1977), secretary of the Association from 1952 to 1967, initiated the collection of original documents and the solicitation of written recollections in the early 1960s; his provenance notes and donor correspondence are preserved within the collection (p. 132).
- The quartermaster's records, assembled by Norman Jarrett (former food controller for Malaya), document purchases, deliveries, and Red Cross supplies in three recycled school exercise books, revealing the progressive deterioration of provisions and frequent reductions in daily rations (p. 133).
- Two surviving nominal rolls record the personal data of each internee—name, age, nationality, prewar occupation and location, marital status, and camp address—and were updated in ink and pencil, including with dates of death; they are divided into sections for men, women, and children (p. 134).
- The camp newspapers preserved include the *Karikal Chronicle* (first issue 19 February 1942), the *Changi Chronicle* (edited by Harry Miller and Gus Wade), the *Changi Times* (Sunday edition), and the *Changi Guardian*; the Japanese authorities suppressed these publications following the 'Double Tenth incident' of October 1943, with a sombre final edition of the *Changi Chronicle* printed on 12 October 1943 (pp. 134–135).
- A Wellcome Trust Research Resources Award funded a conservation and digitisation project between 2015 and 2017, resulting in the online collection 'Voices of civilian internment: WWII Singapore' published on Cambridge University Digital Library in mid-2017 (p. 136).

### Conclusion

Kent's definitive takeaway is that the archive, while of immense value for family historians and for research in medicine, psychology, memory, and comparative internment studies, remains a partial record that reflects the male, British settler perspective. In the context of the eightieth anniversary of Victory over Japan Day, she argues that questions about who is remembered and how in Southeast Asian wartime commemoration remain unresolved, and that the custodianship of this archive—and the recognition of Bryson's foresight in creating it—remains as important as ever.

## Context

- Primary archival collection: GBR/0115/RCS/RCMS 103 (Archive of the British Association of Malaysia and Singapore) and GBR/0115/RCS/BAM (Photograph collection), both held at Cambridge University Library within the Royal Commonwealth Society holdings; supplementary material at the Imperial War Museum (Changi camp register, Mrs F. Bloom's papers).
- Historiographical contribution: The note serves as a finding aid and provenance guide for a collection that has underpinned several recent reassessments of civilian internment in Japanese-occupied Southeast Asia, including works by Akashi and Yoshimura (2008), Hack and Blackburn (2011), and Yap (2013).
