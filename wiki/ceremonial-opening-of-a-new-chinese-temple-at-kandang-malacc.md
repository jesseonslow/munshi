# Ceremonial opening of a new Chinese temple at Kandang, Malacca, in December, 1938

S.M. Middlebrook, a Malayan Civil Service officer, published this first-hand observational account in 1939 describing the two-week Cho Cheo (praying) ceremony marking the dedication of a new temple to Dato Choo Ong Yah at Kandang, Malacca. The article documents one of the oldest continuous Chinese religious traditions in the Straits Settlements, arguing that the Five Brothers worship system and its associated Wangkang cleansing rite represent a distinctive Malayan adaptation of Hokkien folk religion that has no parallel elsewhere in the Malay Peninsula.

## Summary

Middlebrook's account is structured around the sequential ceremonies of the temple dedication, but it is framed by a broader historical narrative of how Chinese folk religion took root in Malacca. The god at Kandang was brought from the Chiang Chew district of Fukkien by a crew of fishermen more than two centuries before 1938, and the temple being replaced had stood for ninety-seven years. The author traces the genealogy of all Five Brothers, noting their individual origins, the stories attached to their iconography, and the hierarchy of seniority that governs their interactions. The eldest, Choo Ong Yah, is the most respected; the youngest was not carved until 1933, and his quick temper is said to necessitate his continued residence with his more temperate elder brother at Bandar Hilir.

The core of the article is a detailed description of the ceremonial sequence: the selection of auspicious dates through a necromancer and the casting of bamboo tallies, the invitation of the four younger brothers in procession, the dispatch of heavenly bodyguards to the five cardinal directions, the planting of two signal bamboos, and the culminating eye-marking of the centre beam on the seventh day. Middlebrook explains the symbolic logic behind each element—the sugar cane as a Hokkien emblem of resistance to Manchu conquest, the Taoist guiding instruments (scissors, ruler, mirror, sword), the pomegranate blossom in the holy water bowl—drawing on both Chinese cosmological principles and local Malayan practice. The article also situates the Kandang ceremony within the broader cycle of the Wangkang, a grand cleansing rite performed roughly every fifteen years to expel evil spirits from Malacca, which Middlebrook identifies as possibly the oldest Chinese religious ceremony in Malaya.

The narrative is informed entirely by the author's direct observation of the ceremonies and conversations with temple officials and the medium. No archival documents are cited; the evidential basis is ethnographic and oral, supplemented by the physical details of the temple's layout, decorations, and ritual objects that Middlebrook describes in considerable specificity.

### Key Findings

- The temple being dedicated replaced a structure built approximately 97 years earlier (c. 1841); the god himself had been in Malacca for over 200 years, brought by fishermen from Chiang Chew, Fukkien (p. 98).
- The Wangkang (grand cleansing ceremony), in which a model junk carrying captured evil spirits is floated out to sea, is performed roughly every fifteen years and was first staged at Kandang about 100 years prior; it is unique to Malacca in the Malay Peninsula and is known only from two districts in Fukkien in China (p. 100).
- The fifth and youngest of the Five Brothers was carved in 1933 by a special craftsman in Singapore at the expense of Towkay Ee Kong Guan of Malacca; his 'humanising' ceremony involved a priest painting his features with a vermilion pen after reciting prayers (p. 100).
- The two signal bamboos planted at the temple entrance were 50–60 feet tall, with thirteen cash (one per month plus the intercalary month) buried at their bases; lanterns hung eight feet from the ground were kept burning all night to guide heavenly spirits down the bamboo into the temple (p. 103).
- The eye-marking ceremony on the seventh day, in which a pen dipped in the blood of a red cock's comb was used to paint the eyes of two phoenixes and touch the Pat Kwa (eight diagrams) on the centre beam, lasted approximately six hours despite the actual painting taking only a few minutes (pp. 105–106).
- The centre beam bore a gold-lettered inscription calling for the blessing of heavenly spirits on all persons in Malacca regardless of race and creed, partly because some of the temple's construction funds had been subscribed by non-Chinese, including Europeans (p. 106).

### Conclusion

Middlebrook's definitive takeaway is that the Kandang temple and its associated ceremonies represent a living link to the earliest Chinese religious settlements in Malaya, and that the Five Brothers system—particularly the Wangkang rite—constitutes a uniquely Malayan development of Hokkien folk worship with no counterpart elsewhere in the country. The article closes with an explicitly multiracial note, observing that the temple's blessing is invoked for all peoples of Malacca and that the ceremony's success is a guarantee of peace for the colony as a whole.

## Context

- The author was an officer of the Malayan Civil Service (M.C.S.), and the article reflects the colonial administrative interest in documenting indigenous and immigrant religious practices as part of the broader ethnographic project of the Straits Settlements.
- The piece was published in the Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, the principal scholarly outlet for colonial-era studies of the region, and its tone—respectful, detailed, and free of overt editorialising—typifies the Society's convention of presenting native customs as objects of learned observation rather than critique.
