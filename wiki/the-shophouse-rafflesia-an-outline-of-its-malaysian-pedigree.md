# The “Shophouse Rafflesia”: an outline of its Malaysian pedigree and its subsequent diffusion in Asia

## Abstract

It has been assumed that the pre-war shophouse in the Malaysian region, consisting of a narrow frontage and a plan of considerable depth, had its origin in China. However, this view does not take into account the distinctive regular facade and ‘five-footways’ It was in fact Sir Stamford Raffles who was the ‘architect’ of this prototype. Hence, it is hereby termed the ‘Shophouse Rafflesia’.

This article furthermore suggests that the Shophouse Rafflesia contributed positively to the urban development in Asia. The lack of intra-regional research makes the task of validating this view difficult; however, there are obvious indications that this was due to municipal enactments which followed closely town planning and built-environment principles as first promulgated by Raffles in Singapore.

## Summary

This article challenges the prevailing assumption that the narrow-frontage, deep-plan shophouse of the Malaysian region originated in China. Lim argues that the distinctive features that define the shophouse as a coherent urban type—the regular facade, the continuous public verandah (five-footway), fire walls, and adherence to street alignment—were not inherited from Chinese building traditions but were promulgated as municipal policy by Sir Stamford Raffles in Singapore in 1822. He terms this prototype the "Shophouse Rafflesia" and traces its antecedents to the Dutch building codes of Batavia, where Raffles resided between 1811 and 1815, rather than to the *Jie Wu* street houses of southern China, which lacked both regular facades and public verandahs.

The article then follows the typology through three developmental periods in the Straits Settlements and British Malaya, documenting how the basic Rafflesian grid was progressively enriched through eclecticism, reinforced concrete technology, and Art Deco styling. Primary evidence is drawn from nineteenth-century paintings by J. T. Thomson and Edward Cree, photographs by John Thomson, engravings by Rach of Batavia, and the text of successive conservancy regulations and municipal by-laws from Selangor, Perak, Penang, Malacca, and Singapore.

The final section maps the diffusion of the Shophouse Rafflesia across Asia, distinguishing between transmission through colonial administrative edicts (Burma, Hong Kong, the Chinese treaty ports, Taiwan under Japanese rule) and through personal patronage (King Chulalongkorn's 1871 visit to Singapore, Chinese trading houses in Phuket and Medan). Lim notes that the type did not take root in French Indo-China or the interior of mainland China, where no equivalent by-law mandated regular fronts or arcades.

### Key Findings

- Raffles' Town Building Committee ordinance of 4 November 1822 stipulated that all brick or tile houses must have "a uniform type of front each having a verandah of a certain depth, open to all sides as a continuous and open passage on each side of the street" (p. 49).
- The standard by-law clause, replicated across Selangor (1890), Perak (1893), Penang (1905), Singapore (1908), and Malacca (1910), required a verandah-way of at least seven feet from the road boundary, with a clear footway of at least five feet (p. 51).
- The "Verandah Riots" of 1888 in Singapore erupted after Section 129 of the Municipal Ordinance (IX) of 1887 invoked prohibition orders against obstruction of verandahs, exposing a legal contradiction between public right of passage and private property rights established in the Summary Criminal Jurisdiction Ordinance of 1872 (p. 50).
- In Kuala Lumpur, 53 prosecutions for obstruction of verandahs were recorded on 8 April 1909 and 28 more on 12 March 1909; fines in Singapore ranged from Straits $2.00 in 1880 to $5.44 in 1925 (p. 50, n. 22).
- King Chulalongkorn's introduction of shophouses to Bangkok following his March 1871 visit to Singapore began with 458 units at Bumrung-Mueng Road, though these lacked five-footways, air wells, and back lanes (p. 62).
- The *chi loh* (five-footway) was introduced to Taiwan by British Sanitary Engineer Henry Barton during the Japanese occupation (1895–1945); the red brick examples at San Hsia province of Taipei are among the most distinctive in the island (p. 65).

### Conclusion

Lim's definitive takeaway is that the Shophouse Rafflesia was a product of Enlightenment urban planning—Raffles' vision of a model tropical city with registered property titles, fire-proof construction, and public health provisions—and that its diffusion across Asia was driven primarily by municipal enactments rather than by organic cultural transmission from China. However, he acknowledges a fundamental tension: the type's incorporation of verandahs by edict clashed with the "Asian expansive attitude towards the use of urban space for work and leisure," producing persistent enforcement problems that the by-laws never fully resolved (p. 66).

## Context

- The article draws on the Thomson paintings (National Museum, Singapore), the Rach engravings of Batavia (1770), John Thomson's photographs (1862), and the published texts of conservancy regulations and municipal by-laws across the Straits Settlements and Federated Malay States.
- Historiographically, the study corrects the then-dominant narrative in architectural history that attributed the shophouse form to Chinese origins, repositioning it as a colonial administrative product with Dutch-Batavian antecedents.
