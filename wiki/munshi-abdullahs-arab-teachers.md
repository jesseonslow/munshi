# Munshi Abdullah’s Arab teachers

Associate Professor Yusof A. Talib's 1990 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the three Arab religious teachers that Munshi Abdullah names in his autobiography, the *Hikayat Abdullah*, as having instructed him in Malacca. By cross-referencing Abdullah's account with classical Hadrami biographical works and Dutch colonial reports, Talib demonstrates that at least one of the three figures was almost certainly a fabrication by Abdullah, while the third — a celebrated Sufi leader in Java — presents a striking contrast between his veneration among Muslim communities and his portrayal as a financial fraud by European observers.

## Summary

The article addresses a broader historiographical problem: the scarcity of documentary evidence for Islamic educational centres in the Malay Archipelago, which makes it difficult to trace the networks of religious teachers who sustained Muslim communities in the region. Abdullah's autobiography is one of the few indigenous sources that names specific teachers, but its reliability is questionable. Talib evaluates each of the three teachers Abdullah claims to have studied under, using Hadrami hagiographical literature and colonial administrative reports as independent verification.

The first figure, Habib 'Abdullah al-Haddad, is immediately exposed as an anachronism. The celebrated Hadrami saint and composer of the *Ratib al-Haddad* died in Tarim in 1719, some seventy-six years before Abdullah's birth, and is known to have travelled only within the Arabian Peninsula. Talib concludes that Abdullah inserted this venerable name to lend distinction to the circumstances surrounding his own birth. The second figure, Mu'allim Muhyi'l-Din, a non-Hadrami Yemenite from Aceh who taught Quranic recitation (*tajwid*), leaves no trace in Hadrami chronicles, though Aceh was a significant centre of the Arab diaspora in the archipelago.

The third and most complex figure is Saiyyid Shaikh bin Ahmad Ba Fakih. Hadrami sources, particularly the *Tarikh al-Shu'ara al-Hadramiyin* of al-Sakkaf, portray him as a great Sufi leader who settled in Surabaya and attracted numerous followers. The Dutch orientalist L.W.C. Van den Berg, however, provides a far more unflattering account: Shaikh was a failed merchant who secured a position as tutor to the Sultan of Sumenep, lost that position upon the Sultan's death, fell into debt, was imprisoned, and repeated the same pattern in Batavia and Semarang. Van den Berg, who met him in Semarang in 1870–71, recorded the stark contrast between the veneration he received from local Muslims and the contempt of his European creditors. Talib notes a chronological impossibility — Abdullah was born before Shaikh's known activities in the archipelago — and suggests Abdullah may have met him during a period of economic hardship following his dismissal from Sumenep, again embellishing his own educational pedigree.

### Key Findings

- Habib 'Abdullah al-Haddad died in Tarim, Hadramawt, in 1132/1719, approximately 76 years before Abdullah's birth; no records exist of his ever visiting the Malay World (p. 29).
- Mu'allim Muhyi'l-Din, a Yemenite from Aceh, taught *tajwid* in Malacca for a fee of five dollars per student per year, with forty to fifty students enrolled; he is absent from all extant Hadrami chronicles (pp. 27–29).
- Saiyyid Shaikh bin Ahmad Ba Fakih was born in al-Shihr circa 1212/1797, studied in Mecca, Medina, and Cairo (Al-Azhar), and settled in Surabaya circa 1250/1834, where he died in 1279/1862 (pp. 31–32).
- The Sultan of Sumenep, Paku Nata Ningrat (r. 1812–1854), was a fluent Arabic speaker who actively patronised Hadrami scholars and made his sultanate a major Islamic cultural centre; his death dealt a fatal blow to Sumenep's scholarly pre-eminence (pp. 33–34).
- Van den Berg recorded that Shaikh was imprisoned for debt in both Sumenep and Semarang, and that his European creditors considered him "a man of bad faith, if not a rascal and a swindler," while local Muslims venerated him as a saint (p. 32).
- A Malay translation of a work on the *Ratib al-Haddad* by the saint's grandson, Saiyyid Ahmad bin Hassan, was produced in Penyengat (Riau) in 1224/1809, attesting to the litany's early circulation in the Malay World (p. 33).

### Conclusion

Talib's definitive takeaway is that Abdullah's autobiographical account of his teachers cannot be taken at face value: at least one figure (al-Haddad) is a clear fabrication, and the most prominent (Shaikh Ba Fakih) is likely embellished or misdated. Nevertheless, the article affirms A. H. Johns's assessment that even these fragmentary vignettes reveal a real and significant phenomenon — the presence of peripatetic Arab religious teachers in Malay port towns who formed the connective tissue of Islamic intellectual life in the region, and whose status was enhanced by Arab and Saiyyid lineage.

## Context

- The study draws on Hadrami biographical and hagiographical manuscripts (al-Sakkaf, Djamal al-Lail, al-Attas), the Dutch colonial report by L.W.C. Van den Berg (*Le Hadhramout et les Colonies Arabes dans l'Archipel Indien*, 1886), and Harold Ingrams' British colonial report on the Hadramawt (1936).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Munshi Abdullah's reliability as a source and to the broader question of how Islamic educational networks operated in the Malay Archipelago during the nineteenth century.
