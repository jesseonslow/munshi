# Singapore

### Scope and Historical Definition

Singapore has been a node in maritime trade networks for at least 1,700 years, with the earliest identifiable reference in Chinese sources dating to approximately A.D. 231 [[1, pp. 6–8]](#ref-1). The island's strategic position at the southern entrance to the Strait of Malacca has made it a recurring focal point for commercial, political, and military actors across successive centuries. The name "Temasek" (recorded in Chinese sources as "Tan-Ma-Hsi" in Wang Ta-Yuan's 1330 *Tao Yi Tchih Lueh*) is the earliest attested designation for the settlement on the island, while the 3rd-century text of K'ang T'ai refers to a place called "P'u Luo Chung" at the extremity of the peninsula, which Hsü Yün-Ts'iao equates with the Malay *Pulau Ujong* ("Island at the End") [[1, pp. 1–2]](#ref-1). The Dragon's Teeth Gate (*Lung-Ya-Men*), identified with Keppel Harbour, appears in Chau Ju-Kua's 1225 *Chu Fan Tchih* and in Chang Hsih's 1617 *Tung Hsi Yang Khao*, attesting to the strait's continuous recognition as a navigational waypoint from the Sung Dynasty onward [[1, pp. 2–4]](#ref-1).

The historiography of pre-colonial Singapore has been shaped by a persistent paradox: the settlement is mentioned in multiple fourteenth-century court records—Chinese, Javanese, and Vietnamese—yet is conspicuously absent from fifteenth-century navigational texts [[2, p. 113]](#ref-2). Derek Heng Thiam Soon resolved this by demonstrating that the shift to the Ming tributary system after 1400 fundamentally altered the trade landscape, with a single designated entrepôt at Malacca absorbing the function of smaller ports and rendering them invisible in subsequent records [[2, p. 113]](#ref-2). Borschberg and Khoo (2018) have since argued that no single analytical framework—whether Polanyi's "port of trade" model, Bronson's upstream–downstream riverine economy, the concept of strategic location, or the *longue durée*—can adequately resolve the history of pre-1819 Singapore, and that the plurality of evidence demands a correspondingly plural historiography [[3, pp. 19–20]](#ref-3).

### Commerce and Economic Development

The commercial significance of Temasik during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries has been systematically underestimated by scholars who relied on fifteenth-century records shaped by the Ming tributary trade system rather than on contemporaneous sources and archaeological evidence [[2, p. 113]](#ref-2). Drawing on the *Tao-i Chih Luo*, the *Ling-wai Dai-da*, the *Chu-fan-zhi*, and the *Nagarakatagama*, Heng establishes that the main settlement, Pan-Tzu (Fort Canning Hill), was known internationally for hornbill casques of exceptional quality and a distinctive type of lakawood designated for construction timber—commodities unavailable at other ports in the Malay Peninsula [[2, pp. 115]](#ref-2). Archaeological excavations at Fort Canning Hill and the north bank of the Singapore River, combined with survey finds from the Riau Islands of Midai and Bintan, reveal that Chinese green celadon wares, white wares, coarse stoneware, glass vessels, and glass beads shared similar decorative styles, forms, and chemical compositions across both locations, indicating a direct commercial link [[2, pp. 119–120]](#ref-2). By analogy with early Malacca, whose population numbered approximately 2,000 before its formal recognition as China's chief entrepôt in 1409, Heng suggests that Pan-Tzu's population and settlement size were comparable or larger [[2, p. 122]](#ref-2).

The post-1819 transformation of Singapore into a major entrepôt is documented in exhaustive detail by Wong Lin Ken (1960), whose monograph provides the first comprehensive quantitative study of the island's commercial development from its founding to the opening of the Suez Canal [[4, pp. 194–201]](#ref-4). Wong demonstrates that Singapore's total trade grew from $11,417,036 in 1823–24 to $58,944,141 in 1868–69, an increase of 416 per cent, with the most rapid growth occurring in the first four decades [[4, pp. 162]](#ref-4). The entrepôt mechanism operated through a credit system under which European merchant houses sold goods on credit to Chinese middlemen, who then distributed them to Bugis, Malay, and Siamese traders [[4, pp. 160–163]](#ref-4). The Bugis merchants, who had superseded the Malays and Arabs as the principal carriers of trade in the Archipelago, brought the products of the eastern islands to Singapore and took back British and Indian manufactures, opium, and other goods [[4, pp. 71–77]](#ref-4). The gradual displacement of native prows and Chinese junks by square-rigged vessels is documented through shipping statistics: in 1829–30, 62.5 per cent of Singapore's total trade was carried by prows and junks, but by 1863 this proportion had fallen to 11.1 per cent [[4, p. 161]](#ref-4).

The relationship between Singapore and the Dutch colonial system was a central thread of commercial development. Wong shows that while Dutch tariffs in Java materially harmed specific branches of trade—most notably the export of British cotton piece goods, which fell from $29,219 in 1828–29 to $2,805 in 1835–36 before recovering after the tariff revision of 1837—the overall trade of Singapore with the Archipelago grew from $4,066,528 in 1823–24 to $11,882,584 in 1868–69 [[4, pp. 47]](#ref-4). Similarly, the rise of Hong Kong and the opening of the China Treaty Ports in 1842 did not diminish Singapore's China trade; the average annual value of trade with China in the decade 1860–69 was $2,384,061 higher than in the preceding decade [[4, p. 132]](#ref-4).

### Politics, Governance, and Social Life

The founding of Singapore in January 1819 remains a subject of contested attribution. Sir Stamford Raffles, writing from Singapore on 10 June 1819 to Colonel Addenbrooke, framed the establishment as a necessary British counter to Dutch monopoly across the Archipelago, describing the settlement four months after its founding as having a population exceeding five thousand, a harbour "filled with Shipping from all quarters," and troops healthy and provisions abundant [[5, p. 178]](#ref-5). Raffles conceived Singapore not as a territorial acquisition but as "a great commercial Emporium, and a *fulcrum* whence we may extend our influence politically," with the aspiration that "what Malta is in the West, that may Singapore become in the East" [[5, p. 178]](#ref-5). William Farquhar, the first British Resident (1819–1823), published a pointed rebuttal in 1830 asserting that he was the original architect of the Singapore project, having first recommended a new settlement in the Straits of Malacca to the Supreme Government in 1816, and that Raffles' role, while real, was secondary and brief [[6, pp. 141–142]](#ref-6). Farquhar claims that it was he who proposed stopping at Singapore after the Carimons proved unsatisfactory, who travelled to Rhio to negotiate with the Viceroy, and who jointly signed the treaty with the local chief before Raffles sailed for Penang the very next day [[6, p. 142]](#ref-6).

The island's early electoral history reveals a transition from elite-dominated to mass politics. Yeo Kim Wah (1972) demonstrates that the 1948 legislative election was a narrow, elite-driven exercise: only 22,395 voters registered out of a potential electorate exceeding 200,000, and the registered roll was 45 per cent Indian, 25 per cent Chinese, and 14 per cent Malay—a composition that bore little resemblance to the island's actual demographics [[7, pp. 57–62]](#ref-7). The 1955 election represented a qualitative rupture. Automatic registration, introduced in 1954 following the Rendel Report, swelled the electorate from approximately 76,000 to 300,299, ending Indian predominance and vastly enlarging the numbers of women and rural voters [[7, p. 64]](#ref-7). The result was a leftwing landslide: the Labour Front, People's Action Party, and Alliance bloc won 16 of 25 seats (66 per cent) against 6 for the Progressive Party and Democratic Party group (24 per cent) [[7, pp. 77–80]](#ref-7).

The social geography of colonial Singapore is preserved in the layered toponymy documented by H. T. Haughton. His 1891 street-name reference reveals a city whose physical landscape was understood and navigated through multiple linguistic and cultural lenses simultaneously, with streets identified by the trades concentrated there (iron-smiths, cabinetmakers, potters, dhobies, milk shops), by the institutions that anchored them (the Supreme Court, the old jail, the ice factory, the gasometer), and by the *kongsi* and secret society meeting-houses that occupied them [[8, pp. 49–65]](#ref-8). The recurring distinction between "Sio Po" (Small Singapore, i.e., Kampong Glam) and "Twa Po" (Big Singapore, across the river) reveals how the Chinese community itself partitioned the island's urban space into meaningful zones [[8, p. 52]](#ref-8). His earlier 1889 etymological survey, written at a moment when Singapore's original Malay and Orang Laut populations were rapidly diminishing under the pressure of Chinese and Indian immigration, sought to record the correct Malay forms of place names before they were lost entirely to cartographic corruption [[9, p. 75]](#ref-9).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Singapore spans more than a century and reveals shifting priorities from colonial administrative documentation to modern archaeological and historiographical synthesis. The earliest contributions are practical and philological: Haughton's toponymic surveys of 1889 and 1891 served the administrative needs of the Straits Settlements government, while Raffles' 1819 letter, published in 1878, provided the foundational primary document for the settlement's origins [[5, p. 175]](#ref-5), [[9, p. 75]](#ref-9), [[8, p. 49]](#ref-8). Leechman's 1937 tidal analysis represented the applied scientific tradition, offering mariners a practical method for predicting stream strength and direction in the Main Strait [[10, pp. 153–159]](#ref-10).

The post-war period saw a turn toward systematic economic and political analysis. Wong Lin Ken's 1960 monograph, an enlarged version of his 1955 M.A. dissertation at the University of Malaya, provided the first consolidated trade returns for Singapore in the pre-Suez era, compiled from annual statements that had never previously been aggregated or converted into a single currency [[4, pp. 4–5]](#ref-4). Yeo Kim Wah's 1972 study of the 1948 and 1955 elections was among the first systematic investigations of Singapore's early electoral history, providing a socio-political explanation rooted in class, language, and the dynamics of the Chinese community for the transition from elite-dominated to mass politics [[7, pp. 57–80]](#ref-7).

The 1970s and 1990s witnessed a decisive challenge to the "150-year history" narrative that had dominated public discourse since Singapore's 1969 sesquicentennial celebrations. Hsü Yün-Ts'iao's 1972 article, published to coincide with that anniversary, directly contested the assumption that Singapore's history began with Raffles, arguing instead that the island had been a node in maritime trade networks for at least 1,700 years [[1, p. 1]](#ref-1). Heng Thiam Soon's 1999 article extended this challenge by integrating textual and material evidence to argue against the prevailing view, associated with O. W. Wolters, that Temasik was a minor or inconsequential settlement, demonstrating instead that regional access points to international trade traffic were as significant a driver of a port's flourishing as its role in the broader international context [[2, p. 113]](#ref-2).

The most recent contributions have been methodological in character. Borschberg and Khoo's 2018 survey of Singapore as a port city from c. 1290 to 1819 functions as a methodological roadmap, demonstrating that no single framework or source type can resolve the history of pre-1819 Singapore and that the frameworks should serve as interrogative tools rather than prescriptive templates [[3, pp. 19–20]](#ref-3). Their 2022 companion study of European compendia entries traces the evolution of knowledge about Singapore through four broad phases of accumulation, transformation, and de-contextualization between approximately 1511 and 1819, revealing that the alphabetical ordering popularized by Moreri (1674) caused severe fragmentation of Singapore's identity in European reference works, with no publication from the 1650–1750 period identifying Singapore as an island [[11, p. 18]](#ref-11). Kratoska's 2016 editor's note on the water supply, drawing on Colonial Office records, illustrates the persistent tension between imperial fiscal restraint and the public health imperatives of a rapidly growing tropical port city, from the Government of India's 1861 refusal to sanction waterworks to the 1902 drought that reduced the reservoir to barely above the minimum for pure water extraction [[12, pp. 144–147]](#ref-12).
