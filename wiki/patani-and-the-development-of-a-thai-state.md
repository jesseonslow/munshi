# Patani and the development of a Thai state

Margaret L. Koch's 1977 article examines how Siam's nineteenth-century administrative centralization in the Patani states of the Malay peninsula was driven by British colonial demands for effective Siamese control, and how the resulting injustices toward Malay-Muslim populations paradoxically gave Britain the grounds to undermine Siam's claims over the remaining northern Malay states. The article traces the crisis from the introduction of Siamese commissioners in 1895 through the deposition of Raja Abdul Kadir in 1902, arguing that Siam's success in establishing firm authority over Patani directly caused its loss of influence over Kelantan and Trengganu.

## Summary

Koch situates the Patani crisis within the broader transformation of Southeast Asian polities from traditional centripetal kingdoms into modern bureaucratic nation-states. The central tension she identifies is between two incompatible British colonial objectives: the primary demand that Siam exercise effective administrative control over the northern Malay states to protect British commercial and strategic interests, and the secondary but powerful conviction that British civilization entailed liberty and justice for subject peoples. As Siam met the first demand by extending bureaucratic authority into Patani, it inevitably destroyed the autonomy of historically independent Malay kingdoms and imposed cultural and religious injustices on a Muslim population. British officials who had long wished to exclude Siam from the peninsula could then cite these side effects to argue that Siam should be kept out entirely.

The article reconstructs the sequence of administrative encroachments — the forced integration of opium farms, the seizure of poll tax collection, the imposition of Siamese law on Islamic matters, and the systematic stripping of revenue from the rajas — and shows how these measures pushed the rajas from appeals to Bangkok, to appeals to Britain, to the planning of an armed uprising in 1901. Koch draws heavily on the petitions the rajas sent to Governor Swettenham and on the correspondence between British colonial and foreign offices to demonstrate how the British response shifted from indifference to active (if ultimately limited) sympathy. The turning point was the rajas' threat to seek the assistance of another European power, which transformed the Patani question from a local grievance into a matter of imperial strategic concern.

The article's most significant analytical contribution lies in its treatment of the February 1902 meeting between the King of Siam and Swettenham in Singapore, and the simultaneous Siamese decision to depose Raja Abdul Kadir. Koch argues that Siam's strategy of establishing effective, undeniable control in Patani succeeded in removing that territory from the treaty negotiations with Britain, but the brutality of the deposition and the documented abuses of Siamese officials hardened British resolve to prevent any direct Siamese control over Kelantan and Trengganu. The result was a paradox: Siam won Patani but lost the rest.

### Key Findings

- The opium farms of all seven Patani states were forcibly integrated into a single farm in the late 1890s, with an initial contract rent of $30,200 per year; the rajas received their previous amounts in the first year but none of the promised additional balance in subsequent years (pp. 69–70).
- In mid-1901, the Siamese high commissioner ordered over six hundred men from Nongchik to patrol Patani to enforce collection of the poll tax for the commissioner, directly contradicting the raja's proclamation that the tax should be paid to him for the traditional *bunga mas* tribute (p. 69).
- The rajas of Patani, Reman, and Sai submitted formal petitions to Governor Swettenham between August and October 1901, enumerating grievances centered on revenue encroachment and interference with Islamic practice, and explicitly threatening violence if no redress was obtained (pp. 72–73).
- On 21 February 1902, Phya Sri Sahadheb presented Raja Abdul Kadir with a document demanding compliance with administrative reforms and gave him five minutes to sign or be deposed; thirty Siamese soldiers surrounded the raja and carried him to a gunboat (pp. 86–87).
- The British Colonial Office's internal minute on the arrest summarized its response as "This is outrageous!" and the first draft of a diplomatic note proposed to "insist upon the immediate release of the Raja of Patani as a signal of the Siamese government's wish to remain on friendly terms with England" (p. 87).
- British Minister Tower at Bangkok deliberately suppressed the protest over the raja's deposition, arguing it would "injure our cause" to take drastic measures over "a non-coterminous state" while negotiations concerning Kelantan and Trengganu were still pending (p. 87).

### Conclusion

Koch's definitive takeaway is that Siam's attempt to satisfy one British demand — effective administrative control — inevitably violated another — the provision of justice to subject peoples — and that this contradiction produced a self-defeating outcome. By establishing firm, undeniable authority over Patani, Siam removed that territory from the sphere of British treaty-making, but the methods used to achieve this result destroyed any remaining British tolerance for direct Siamese control elsewhere in the northern peninsula. The article thus frames the modern Thai state's southern minority problem as a direct consequence of the administrative centralization imposed under British pressure in the early twentieth century.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on British colonial correspondence held in the Public Record Office (CO 273 series), including letters from the rajas to Swettenham, dispatches between the Colonial and Foreign Offices, and Swettenham's own reports, supplemented by R.D. Davies's *Siam in the Malay Peninsula* (1902) and Walter Vella's *The Impact of the West on the Government in Thailand* (1955).
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the emerging 1970s scholarship on Siam's southern periphery by foregrounding the agency of the Malay rajas and the internal contradictions of British imperial policy, rather than treating Patani as a passive object of Siamese or British action.
