# Art

## Scope and Historical Definition

The term "art" as applied to the material cultures of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo encompasses a wide range of creative production, from the woven and plaited objects of everyday life to monumental stone sculpture, painted canvases, and prehistoric ceramic forms. The Society's literature documents these traditions across several distinct media—pictorial, sculptural, and artefactual—each carrying its own technical vocabulary, regional distribution, and symbolic significance. The records span from the late nineteenth century to the early 1980s, reflecting a progression from brief descriptive notes to sustained analytical studies, and from the documentation of living crafts to the scientific examination of archaeological remains.

The earliest contribution in the corpus is a single-page note by R. O. Winstedt on foliated patterns in Malay carving and silverwork, published in 1917 [[1]](#ref-1). Though the substantive text is not preserved in the available record, its existence confirms that the decorative arts of the Malay Peninsula attracted scholarly attention from the earliest decades of the journal. From that modest beginning, the literature expanded to cover basketry, wood carving, stone sculpture, painting, and prehistoric pottery, establishing a body of work that treats "art" not as a unified aesthetic category but as a set of culturally embedded practices whose meaning must be recovered through ethnographic, stylistic, and scientific methods.

## Pictorial and Woven Traditions

The largest single body of documentation concerns woven and plaited artefacts, which the contributors consistently treat as the most developed of the indigenous arts and crafts of their respective regions. G. C. Woolley, writing in 1929 and again in 1932, documented the basketry, mat-making, and hat-weaving traditions of the Murut peoples across the Tenom, Keningau, and Pensiangan districts of British North Borneo [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3). Woolley argued that Murut weaving represented the most developed of their indigenous arts, and that its patterns carried encoded narratives and social meanings. He recorded several etiological narratives attached to specific patterns—*Negimpong* ("Quarrel"), *Namboyunan* ("mad"), *Nagulalan* ("The Raider's"), *Nantuapan* ("The Meeting"), and *Pinangulun* ("man")—which encode stories of betrothal refusal, madness, head-hunting, and social interaction [[2, pp. 291–293]](#ref-2). Only two patterns in the corpus represent human beings: *Nantuapan*, showing four figures around a central circle, and *Pinangulun*, a single male figure that when inverted becomes a fruit bat [[2, p. 293]](#ref-2).

Woolley's 1932 follow-up article documented actual functional objects—sitting mats, carrying baskets, tobacco pouches, and hats—photographed in situ, with emphasis on regional variation in form, material, and pattern nomenclature across the Pensiangan, Keningau, Tenom, Upper Padas, and Bokan districts [[3]](#ref-3). He noted that the *Tabir* sitting mat was no longer used by Keningau or Tenom plain Muruts, indicating a documented decline in a once-widespread practice [[3, p. 23]](#ref-3). Pattern names showed significant dialectal variation: the "hook" motif was called *Pinansit* in Pensiangan, *Nongkoitan* or *Linokuitan* in Kwijau, and *Pinangko* in another variant [[3, pp. 24–25]](#ref-3).

J. C. Swayne's 1933 article on Rejang baskets from Sarawak extended this documentation to the Seduan and Kenauit peoples of the Rejang River, tracing the full production cycle from raw material preparation through weaving to finishing [[4]](#ref-4). Swayne explicitly challenged Dr. Otis Mason's suggestion that time had obliterated all symbolic content in Malayan basketry, pointing to Woolley's earlier work as evidence that meaning persisted [[4, p. 183]](#ref-4). He provided a complete catalogue of pattern names for one illustrated basket, drawing on referents as varied as fish, bird ticks, bamboo shoots, rotan fishing hooks, pillar tombs, and belt toggles [[4, p. 183]](#ref-4).

C. A. Gibson-Hill's 1951 catalogue of Malay hats and dish covers addressed a different problem: documentation under conditions of rapid cultural change [[5]](#ref-5). He framed the article explicitly as a record of what remained, acknowledging that the account "would have been far more interesting" had it been compiled seventy to eighty years earlier, before the replacement of traditional rotan fibres with cheap crochet cotton and before the isolation of riverine communities was broken by trade networks [[5, p. 128]](#ref-5). The historical problem he identified was twofold: material substitution had altered the quality and character of the work, and a small number of production centres—principally Trengganu on the east coast and the Kuala Sungei Bahru area in Malacca on the west—had come to dominate the market, their products spreading at low prices and displacing local variants [[5, pp. 128]](#ref-5). Gibson-Hill documented approximately nine hat types and ten dish-cover types still in current use across the peninsula, noting the consistent gendered division of labour: hats were normally sewn by men, dish-covers plaited by women [[5, p. 131]](#ref-5).

E. Banks's 1941 catalogue of Sea Dayak (Iban) wood carving patterns drew on a collection of fragile paper drawings prepared decades earlier by the late Rev. E. W. Howell of Simanggang and held in the Sarawak Museum [[6]](#ref-6). Banks's central thesis was that Sea Dayak male carvings were overwhelmingly naturalistic and geometric in character, with animal representations confined to a narrow set of motifs—a pattern that corroborated Dr. Haddon's earlier distinction between Iban male and female decorative traditions [[6, p. 220]](#ref-6). The few animal motifs that did appear—a fowl's gizzard, centipede legs, and several caterpillar variants—were treated as exceptions that confirmed the rule, while the Hornbill and Dragon were reserved for religious festival contexts [[6, p. 220]](#ref-6).

## Sculpture and Monumental Forms

The sculptural record, though smaller in volume, extends the chronological range of the literature far further back than the woven traditions. Tom Harrisson's 1973 article reported the discovery of five stone sculptures from the middle Kapuas river region of Kalimantan Barat, found in the Capuchin Mission museum at Tilburg, Holland [[7]](#ref-7). Prior to this discovery, the record of Bornean stone sculpture in the round was confined to four half-life-size figures in soft white schist found near Sarikei in south-west Sarawak, attributed to the recently extinct Seru people, and a single life-size figure seen at Long Po on the upper Kayan in 1945 [[7, pp. 179–180]](#ref-7). The five new pieces—three larger sandstone figures and two small carved pebbles—therefore nearly doubled the known corpus and pointed to a once widespread, now largely lost tradition of stone working across the island [[7, p. 179]](#ref-7). Harrisson argued that the sandstone figures likely served an apotropaic function analogous to the well-known perishable wood carvings of interior Borneo, and that they may share a remote common origin with the Seru figures, both deriving from a stock of Bornean animists who migrated along the classic Kapuas-to-Sarawak corridor during the early Iron Age [[7, p. 181]](#ref-7). The dating problem remained unresolved; Harrisson leaned toward an ancient origin, noting that the pecking technique visible on one figure was characteristic of prehistoric megaliths dated to c. 1,000 A.D. in the Sarawak River delta, while acknowledging that Hindu stone figures known from the same Kapuas sector—where Indian influence was strongest between the ninth and late fifteenth centuries—may have provided a marginal stimulus [[7, pp. 181–182]](#ref-7).

Harrisson's 1971 monograph on prehistoric double-spouted vessels excavated from the Niah Caves in north-west Sarawak addressed a different category of sculptural form: pottery whose shape carried symbolic meaning [[8]](#ref-8). Drawing on fifty vessels recovered across nine caves over a decade of excavation, he established a typology and traced a developmental sequence spanning roughly two millennia, from c. 2000 B.C. to c. 1000 A.D. [[8, pp. 44–45]](#ref-8). The vessels were consistently associated with secondary burials and human skeletal remains, and Harrisson proposed that the double spout may have symbolized the passage of the spirit from this world to another, with the bridge or the space between spouts representing a threshold [[8, pp. 39–40]](#ref-8). He suggested the form may derive from the hornbill casque—the most persistent head symbol in Borneo—citing ethnographic parallels including a Kayan bead mat depicting human heads with hornbill beaks emerging from the ears [[8, pp. 71–72]](#ref-8).

Sarah Gill's 1967 article on Dayak masks presented a pilot stylistic survey of masks held in Western museum collections, arguing that the extraordinary formal assurance of central Kalimantan and Sarawak mask traditions derived from a deeply rooted, ancient Southeast Asian protective demonic imagery that predated and was distinct from Indianizing influence [[9]](#ref-9). Of 43 documented masks from central Kalimantan and Sarawak examined for the study, 30 possessed demonic features, making the demonic image the dominant iconographical theme in these style areas [[9, p. 87]](#ref-9). Gill traced parallels to the *kala* heads of Javanese architecture, the *singa* faces of Batak Sumatra, the *Rangda* of Balinese drama, and ultimately to a wooden figure from a fourth- to third-century B.C. funerary chamber at Ch'ang-sha in south China that reproduced the full vocabulary of the Southeast Asian demonic image [[9, pp. 87–92]](#ref-9).

M. C. Subhadradis Diskul's 1980 article reported on the excavation and restoration of a brick chedi at Wat Keo, Chaiya, Suratthani, arguing that the monument represented a rare syncretic fusion of Cham, Indonesian, and Dvaravati architectural traditions dating no earlier than the mid-ninth century A.D. [[10]](#ref-10). The most significant sculptural find was a 69 cm red sandstone seated Buddha in the attitude of subduing Mara, whose double vajra on the pedestal and half-Indian, half-Chinese lions suggested an original Mahayana Vajrayana dedication later converted to Theravada; Professor Jean Boisselier dated this statue to the tenth century A.D. [[10, pp. 3–4]](#ref-10). Hindu objects—a Sivalinga and two seated Ganesa statuettes—were also recovered from the eastern chamber, indicating a period of Buddhist-Hindu syncretism at the site [[10, p. 4]](#ref-10).

## Pictorial Art and Material Analysis

The literature extends to painted works and to the scientific analysis of precious metal artefacts. Peter B. R. Carey's 1982 article examined Raden Saleh's 1857 oil painting of Prince Dipanagara's capture at Magelang on 28 March 1830, cross-referencing the artwork against three primary eyewitness accounts to assess its historical accuracy [[11]](#ref-11). Carey argued that while the painting displayed remarkable attention to detail in its depiction of Dutch officers and the Magelang Residency House, it fundamentally misrepresented Dipanagara's emotional state at the moment of his arrest, portraying him as defiantly heroic rather than resignedly accepting of his fate [[11, p. 2]](#ref-11). The painting was produced twenty-seven years after the event and was largely a studio work, as Saleh's applications to visit the princely states for preparatory sketches were denied in both 1852 and 1857 [[11, pp. 1–2]](#ref-11).

Ann Manning, E. Edwards McKinnon, and the late F. E. Treloar published in 1980 the first scientific analysis of gold fragments recovered from the Kota Cina archaeological site near Medan, northeastern Sumatra, a cosmopolitan entrepôt active from the twelfth to the mid-fourteenth century [[12]](#ref-12). The article argued that gold was both manufactured and traded at Kota Cina, that the metal was derived from alluvial sources in the Bukit Barisan hinterland, and that Chinese craftsmen participated in the local gold trade during the Sung-Yuan period [[12, p. 107]](#ref-12). A particularly significant find was a piece of gold foil stamped with two Chinese characters, one of which meant "gold," implying the foil was prepared or at least assayed by Chinese craftsmen [[12, p. 107]](#ref-12). The authors drew a parallel with the Santubong site in Borneo, where Chinese and Indian influences mingled with indigenous activity, and suggested a similar pattern of cultural convergence at Kota Cina [[12, p. 107]](#ref-12).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on art reveals a clear evolution in methodological approach and institutional purpose across the period covered. The earliest contributions—Winstedt's 1917 note and the colonial-era field reports of Woolley, Swayne, Banks, and Gibson-Hill—were produced by British administrative officers documenting indigenous material culture as a form of administrative record [[2]](#ref-2), [[5]](#ref-5), [[4]](#ref-4), [[3]](#ref-3), [[6]](#ref-6), [[1]](#ref-1). These works share a descriptive character: they catalogue forms, record pattern names, and note regional distributions, but they rarely attempt sustained analytical argument. Gibson-Hill's 1951 article is notable for explicitly framing its purpose as documentation under conditions of rapid cultural change, acknowledging that the diversity of local patterns was being eroded by material substitution and commercial centralisation [[5, p. 128]](#ref-5).

A shift toward analytical and comparative methods is evident in the later contributions. Gill's 1967 study of Dayak masks introduced a stylistic framework drawn from the then-emerging field of comparative primitive-art history, proposing Borneo as a critical "back door" through which Asian influence may have passed into Oceania, and challenging the assumption that all demonic imagery in Southeast Asian art derives from Indian sources [[9, pp. 87–92]](#ref-9). Harrisson's 1971 and 1973 articles applied archaeological method—typology, radiocarbon dating, and stratigraphic context—to prehistoric and early historic sculptural forms, correcting earlier surveys that had significantly underestimated the extent of the record [[7]](#ref-7), [[8]](#ref-8). Carey's 1982 article brought the methods of art history into dialogue with diplomatic and literary sources, using the painting as a document to be tested against eyewitness testimony [[11]](#ref-11). Manning, McKinnon, and Treloar's 1980 study introduced atomic absorption spectroscopy and specific gravity analysis to the study of Southeast Asian archaeological gold, representing one of the earliest applications of these techniques to the region [[12, pp. 111–112]](#ref-12).

The corpus also reveals persistent debates. The question of Indian versus indigenous origin for demonic imagery in Southeast Asian art was taken up by Gill and remains a live issue in the field [[9, pp. 87–92]](#ref-9). The problem of dating Bornean stone sculpture, where the absence of secure stratigraphic contexts and the perishability of organic materials leave wide chronological gaps, was acknowledged by Harrisson as unresolved [[7, pp. 181–182]](#ref-7). The extent to which symbolic meaning in woven patterns had survived into the modern period was contested between Mason's scepticism and the affirmative evidence presented by Woolley and Swayne [[2]](#ref-2), [[4]](#ref-4). And the question of Chinese participation in the Southeast Asian gold trade, raised by the Kota Cina findings, opened what the authors described as important questions about the scale of overseas Chinese activity during the twelfth to fourteenth centuries [[12, p. 107]](#ref-12). Together, these debates illustrate how the Society's literature on art has functioned not merely as a record of objects but as a forum for the testing of competing interpretive frameworks.
