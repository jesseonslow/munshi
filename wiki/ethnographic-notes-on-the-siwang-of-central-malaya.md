# Ethnographic notes on the Siwang of central Malaya

Rodney Needham, a social anthropologist, published these ethnographic notes in 1956, presenting the first systematic account of the social organisation of the Siwang, a semi-nomadic foraging and shifting-cultivation group in the mountainous rain forest of western Pahang. Building on the linguistic and material-culture records of C. S. Ogilvie (1940–1949), Needham's two-day interview with two Siwang informants at Kuala Tahan in May 1955 yielded the first substantive data on kinship, political authority, marriage, law, and religion among this small population, which by then numbered approximately 140 individuals and was in the process of being forcibly relocated from its ancestral territory during the Malayan Emergency.

## Summary

Needham's central aim was to fill a conspicuous gap in the Siwang literature: Ogilvie's three articles, while valuable for their word lists and descriptions of food, dress, and material objects, had provided "practically no information on kinship, political authority, or other features of the social organisation" (p. 50). Needham was explicit that his data, gathered in a few hours of questioning, could not match the reliability of intensive fieldwork, but he argued that a trained anthropologist conducting a rapid survey could still ascertain "certain structural essentials" (p. 48). The article therefore reads as a structural sketch rather than a full cultural monograph, deliberately omitting birth rites, food preparation, and material detail in favour of the principles governing social life.

The narrative moves from the Siwang's position among their neighbours (Bateg Negritos, Jah Chong, Bi Nyeb) through their economy of shifting cultivation supplemented by intensive forest foraging, to the core of the article: a three-tiered political hierarchy (to menteri, to pelima, to jinang) with patrilineal hereditary succession, an ambilineal kinship system with a strong patrilineal bias in degree-reckoning, and a marriage system that is endogamous, permits union down to second-cousin (with the condition of no divorce), and in practice produces marriages with aunts, cousins, and nieces. The legal section records a fine-based justice system in which all penalties are expressed in spears with a peculiar arithmetic convention (a fine of three is pronounced "63"), and the religious section documents a single God (Ta'ala), a class of protective ancestral spirits, and the shamanic office of putau with its distinctive tree-platform burial rite.

Throughout, Needham cross-references his informants' statements against Ogilvie's published accounts, noting where the two agree and where they diverge—most notably on the tribal name itself (Siwang versus Che Wong), the prefix for neighbouring tribe names (Bi versus Beri), and the conditions for tree-platform burial. This critical collation gives the article its evidential texture and makes it function both as a primary ethnographic record and as a corrective to the earlier literature.

### Key Findings

- **Population**: The 1947 census recorded 13 families in 6 houses (35 males, 25 females); Ogilvie estimated a total of about 80 after correcting for missed households. By 1955, Begng reported 62 in the Teris groups and 78 in the Dong area, a total of 140 (pp. 52, 55). The 1918 influenza epidemic is said to have killed two-thirds of the population, and infant mortality was such that "perhaps not more than half" of births survived to maturity (p. 52).
- **Political offices**: Three named hereditary positions exist—*to menteri* (supreme authority), *to pelima* (conducts marriages, divorces, settles disputes; the effective administrator), and *to jinang* (assistant to the to pelima). All are succeeded patrilineally by males only. At the time of writing, the to menteri position was vacant following Maroi's death in 1944; his son Begng was entitled but not yet recognised as commanding authority (pp. 57–59).
- **Marriage and incest**: Of 18 recorded Siwang–Siwang marriages, 1 was with an aunt, 13 with a cousin, and 4 with a niece. Only 3 of 19 total marriages (including one with a Bi Ja man) were matrilocal. Marriage with a second cousin is permitted only on the strict condition that no divorce will follow (pp. 62–64).
- **Legal fines**: Theft is fined 3 spears (pronounced "63"); seduction, 3 spears and 1 plate; adultery (single night), 3 spears and 1 plate; adultery (continued liaison), 6 spears and 1 plate; rape, 4 spears and 1 plate; homicide after extreme provocation, 100 spears and 1 plate. A murderer who cannot plead provocation may be killed, with the sentence carried out by a close kinsman of the victim empowered by the to menteri (pp. 65–66).
- **Bridewealth**: The traditional payment is 3 spears, 5 rings, plates, and a cooking pot, though in practice items and quantities vary (e.g., Wen paid no spears when he married Main) (p. 64).
- **Crops and land tenure**: Crops include maize, cassava, sweet potatoes, plantains, papayas, red peppers, sugar cane, and tobacco. Forest clearings are cultivated for 2 years (Ogilvie) or 3–4 years (Begng), then abandoned. Land is cleared cooperatively by the whole settlement; there are no individual land rights, though crops belong to the planter. Outsiders (Bi Ja, Bi Nyeb) may plant only with special permission; Malays have no right at all (p. 56).

### Conclusion

Needham closes with an urgent note: the Siwang, "being so few and so meek," have been uprooted from their mountain settlements and thrust into contact with other peoples, and their culture "cannot survive long without great changes" (p. 68). The article is thus framed as a salvage document—Ogilvie provided the basic corpus of linguistic and material data, and Needham has added what little he could on social structure under circumstances he acknowledges are "inappropriate to reliable knowledge" (p. 68). His definitive historical takeaway is that the Siwang represent a small, isolated society whose structural principles (ambilineal kinship with patrilineal emphasis, hereditary but non-centralised political authority, fine-based justice, and a syncretic religion blending indigenous spirit categories with Islamic-derived theism) are in immediate danger of dissolution under the pressures of the Emergency and forced resettlement.

## Context

- The primary data derive from two days of questioning (May 1955) of two Siwang informants, Begng (c. 40) and Patong (28), both employed by the Game Department at Kuala Tahan, supplemented by critical collation with Ogilvie's three published articles (1940, 1948, 1949) and Collings's 1949 appendix. No archival collections were consulted; the study is entirely informant-based.
- Historiographically, the article's contribution is to shift the Siwang from a linguistic and material-culture curiosity (as Ogilvie had treated them) to a case study in the comparative anthropology of small-scale social organisation, providing illustration for the study of societies "composed of small, isolated, and relatively self-sufficient local groups" (p. 48).
