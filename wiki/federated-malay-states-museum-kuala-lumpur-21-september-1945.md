# Federated Malay States Museum, Kuala Lumpur, 21 September 1945, Office of Strategic Services, India Burma Theater. Facsimile reprint

Leonard Wray, Jun., a Perak civil servant and later Curator of the Perak Museum, composed this account in 1894 as part of a government publication on Perak's mining industries, drawing on first-hand reports and Captain Speedy's narrative to explain how factional violence among Chinese tin miners in Larut precipitated British intervention in 1874. Reprinted in the *JMBRAS* in 2021, the document serves as a rare contemporary administrative record of the crisis that led to the Pangkor Treaty.

## Summary

The account traces the development of Larut's tin mining industry from its discovery in 1848 by the Malay trader Che Long Jaffar, through successive waves of Chinese immigration and the establishment of competing *kongsee* (mining associations), to the catastrophic factional warfare of 1872–73. Wray frames the crisis as a failure of local governance: the Mantri Ngah Ibrahim, son of the original discoverer, lost the confidence of both factions by alternating his support between them, while his police force proved incapable of maintaining order. The fighting between the Sih Quan (Five Districts) and Goh Quan (Four Districts) factions reduced the population to a mere 4,000 fighting men and caused an estimated $1,000,000 decline in Penang's tin imports over two years.

The document's most vivid element is the full text of a petition submitted by Chinese miners to the Governor of the Straits Settlements in May 1873, which details specific massacres, the Mantri's alleged orchestration of attacks, and the flight of thousands of miners who were pursued and killed along the overland route to Penang. This petition, alongside Speedy's narrative, provides the evidentiary basis for the British decision to intervene. Wray's own commentary then describes the post-intervention recovery: the disarmament of miners, the drawing of a boundary line between the two factions' mining territories, the founding of the towns of Thaipeng and Kamunting, and the rapid population recovery from 4,000 to 33,000 within eleven months of peace.

The overarching argument, implicit in Wray's framing, is that the local Malay administration was structurally incapable of managing the complex multi-ethnic mining economy, and that British intervention—however abrupt—was the necessary condition for restoring prosperity.

### Key Findings

- The 1872–73 faction fighting reduced Larut's population to 4,000 (fighting men only); by the end of 1874, eleven months after the Pangkor agreement, the population had recovered to 33,000, of whom 26,000 were Chinese (p. 200).
- Penang's tin import values decreased by nearly $1,000,000 during 1872 and 1873 due to the cessation of mining in Larut (p. 198).
- Captain Speedy's agreement with the Mantri of Larut provided him a salary of $500 per month plus a tenth of all district revenues for ten years, in exchange for restoring order; he recruited 100 men in India and returned to Larut in late September 1873 (p. 198).
- The Chinese petition of 3 May 1873 alleges that approximately 2,000 See Yip *kongsee* members were killed in their houses and mines, and a further 3,000 were killed or died of hunger and fatigue while fleeing to Penang, with their women sold as slaves (p. 199).
- The Pangkor agreement was signed on 20 January 1874; Commissioners Dunlop, Swettenham, and Pickering arrived in Larut on 23 January and departed on 21 February after disarming the Chinese and destroying stockades (p. 199).
- The town of Thaipeng (Goh Quan division) had 5,000 inhabitants by year's end, one-tenth of whom were shopkeepers; Kamunting (Sih Quan division) had 4,000 inhabitants and 300 shops (p. 200).

### Conclusion

Wray presents the British intervention as an unambiguous success: the rapid demographic and economic recovery of Larut within a single year is offered as proof that colonial administration, however minimal, was superior to the failed local governance that had produced mass violence and economic collapse. The account functions as both a justification of the Pangkor Treaty and a demonstration of the administrative competence of the Straits Settlements government.

## Context

- Wray joined the Perak civil service in 1881, seven years after the events he describes, and his 1894 publication was produced under the auspices of the Perak Government Printing Office, reflecting an official colonial narrative of intervention as benevolent pacification.
- The document draws on Captain Speedy's first-hand account and the Chinese petition of May 1873, both of which survive in the Straits Settlements archives; the reprint in JMBRAS makes these accessible in a single consolidated text.
