# Floods

Floods constitute one of the most recurrent and destructive natural hazards in the Malay Peninsula, with documented episodes of catastrophic inundation affecting riverine communities from the early colonial period through the interwar years. The two principal archival records preserved in the Society's journal literature—one a first-hand administrative dispatch from the Jelebu district in 1896, the other a consolidated government report covering the Straits Settlements and Federated Malay States in 1926–1927—together reveal a pattern of extreme riverine flooding that reshaped landscapes, displaced populations, and tested the logistical capacities of colonial administration. The affected areas span the western and eastern coasts, from the Triang Valley in Negri Sembilan to the great plains of Kelantan and Trengganu, and the economic consequences ranged from the loss of individual rice paddies to the siltation of thousands of acres of cultivated land and the destruction of rubber estates [[1, p. 155]](#ref-1), [[2, pp. 296–309]](#ref-2).

### Scope and Historical Definition

The earliest detailed account in the archival record is Arthur Keyser's dispatch of 30 December 1896, written in his capacity as Collector and Magistrate of Jelebu and addressed to the British Resident of Negri Sembilan [[1, p. 155]](#ref-1). Keyser's letter documents a flood in the lower Triang Valley that began with a report on 14 December from a Malay constable and a companion from Jerang, who had swum for hours through inundated terrain to reach Kuala Klawang and warn that the whole country was under water [[1, p. 155]](#ref-1). The constable reported that water had risen to a height of four feet inside the Police Station building at Jerang, a structure situated on a high site, which made the account seem almost incredible to Keyser upon first hearing [[1, p. 155]](#ref-1). The flood's physical scale was confirmed on Keyser's return journey by land, where water had passed through trees at heights of fifteen feet, and the constable's original report was thus vindicated [[1, p. 158]](#ref-1).

The 1926–1927 flood, compiled by R. O. Winstedt from official district reports, represented a far greater event in terms of geographic extent and human toll. Winstedt attributes the disaster to an extraordinary concentration of rainfall over the eastern half of the peninsula, with the Kemaman–Kuantan area receiving over 70 inches in the latter part of December, while the western coast received as little as 8 inches [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2). This meteorological asymmetry meant that the East Coast states—Pahang, Kelantan, and Trengganu—suffered the most severe inundation, with river rises of 50 to 80 feet above normal levels [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2). Specific gaugings recorded by Winstedt include the Jelai River rising 60 feet at Kuala Lipis, the Pahang River 53 feet at Temerloh, the Tembeling at least 80 feet at Kuala Tahan, and the Kelantan River 72 feet at Kuala Krai [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2). In Perak, the Perak River at Kuala Kangsar reached 143.8 inches above mean sea level on 30 December 1926, exceeding the 1897 flood by 8 feet [[2, pp. 296–297]](#ref-2).

Winstedt opens his account with a brief observation about the folklore surrounding the floods—Malay tales of a crocodile king and a medicine-man's revenge—before dismissing them in favour of the meteorological and hydrological record, a framing that signals the article's purpose as a factual, evidence-based account that supersedes popular narrative while acknowledging the cultural context in which the disaster was experienced [[2, p. 295]](#ref-2).

### Physical Destruction and Landscape Transformation

The physical devastation recorded in both accounts demonstrates the power of flood waters to reshape the physical geography of riverine environments. Keyser's 1896 account provides a striking example of this transformative capacity at Pasir Panggil, where an island that had existed "so long as anyone in Jelebu can remember" was entirely destroyed; all the usual landmarks of the river had gone, huge tree trunks unmoved by previous floods were no longer there, while *tanjongs* (small points of land jutting into the river) had been broken and new islands created [[1, p. 156]](#ref-1). At Jerang itself, all houses recently built for *tuba* fishing (a practice involving the use of rotenone extracted from *Derris elliptica* roots to stun fish) had disappeared, and on the site of the house occupied by the Resident-General floated an immense tree trunk [[1, p. 157]](#ref-1). Keyser's own house was reduced to a ruin, and the Police Station leaked like a sieve with no dry spot anywhere [[1, p. 157]](#ref-1).

The 1926–1927 flood produced destruction on a vastly larger scale. In the Kuala Kangsar district of Perak alone, approximately 800 *kampong* (village) houses were destroyed and 10,000 acres of *padi* (rice) lost [[2, pp. 296–297]](#ref-2). In Kelantan, at least 800 square miles of the main plain were deeply flooded, 1,700 houses were washed away, and the damage in Kuala Krai town alone was estimated at $300,000 [[2, pp. 306–307]](#ref-2). The loss of livestock was substantial: 2,900 cattle and buffaloes were recorded as lost in Kelantan [[2, pp. 306–307]](#ref-2). The long-term agricultural consequences were equally severe, with silted rice fields that would not be plantable for one or two seasons, and destroyed rubber estates adding to the economic disruption [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2). A post-flood fire in Kota Baharu on 13 January 1927 destroyed a further 250 houses and caused damage exceeding $500,000, compounding the flood losses [[2, p. 308]](#ref-2).

### Human Impact and Relief Operations

The human dimension of both floods is documented with considerable detail. Keyser's account records no loss of life at Jerang, Juntai, or downstream in Pahang, though individual experiences were harrowing [[1, p. 157]](#ref-1). One woman described how she and her children sat on the roof for a night and a day in pouring rain with nothing to eat, while the house supports cracked and they momentarily expected to fall into the water and be drowned [[1, p. 157]](#ref-1). Others took refuge on little hillocks in the *padi* fields and were rescued by the police in their boats after shouting their whereabouts in the darkness [[1, p. 157]](#ref-1). A man who had gone to look after his *padi* was caught by the flood and, unable to swim, only escaped after a terrible struggle for his life [[1, p. 157]](#ref-1). An elderly resident recalled a flood of even greater magnitude approximately 14 years earlier, that is, circa 1882, indicating that such events, while catastrophic, were not without precedent in local memory [[1, p. 158]](#ref-1).

The 1926–1927 flood claimed at least 50 lives by drowning in Kelantan alone [[2, pp. 306–307]](#ref-2). The relief response was organised at the level of the colonial state, with Winstedt recording the evacuation of hospitals, the rationing of rice to thousands of refugees, and the deployment of military platoons to prevent looting, which in the event did not materialise [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2). A recurring theme in Winstedt's compilation is the tension between providing free relief and maintaining self-reliance: in Kuantan, philanthropic Chinese were distributing free rice to able-bodied Malays who consequently refused to work on road repairs, and this had to be stopped [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2). The Perak Relief Fund closed at $100,000 on approximately 20 January 1927, while the State Engineer's estimate of road and bridge damage in Perak was $225,000, of which $100,000 was in the Perak River Valley [[2, p. 298]](#ref-2). Winstedt notes the likelihood of a "large programme of relief work" being required later in the year when reconstruction employment dried up, indicating an awareness of the secondary economic effects of the disaster [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2).

The logistics of relief in the 1896 flood were constrained by the limited capacity of the available watercraft. Keyser notes that the boats in use were only small dug-outs capable of carrying a very limited quantity of stores, and that the chiefs and old men he consulted assured him it was impossible for any boat to pass through the rapids in safety [[1, p. 156]](#ref-1). The two boatmen on whom Keyser relied volunteered to go despite the danger, and Keyser records his anxiety at despatching them on such a hazardous journey [[1, p. 156]](#ref-1). The absence of boats at Jerang was a recurring complaint among the villagers, and Keyser notes that this would be remedied in future [[1, p. 157]](#ref-1).

### Administrative and Logistical Dimensions

Both accounts illuminate the challenges of colonial riverine administration in the face of sudden, large-scale inundation. Keyser's letter is a formal dispatch within the hierarchical reporting structure of the Straits Settlements-era colonial administration in the Malay states, addressed to the British Resident and reflecting the chain of command through which information and instructions flowed [[1, p. 155]](#ref-1). The Acting Financial Assistant gave immediate orders for rice to be bought and conveyed to Rawit, the place of embarkation on the river, and Keyser personally supervised the division of rice into bags and its loading into boats [[1, pp. 155–156]](#ref-1). The distribution of relief goods—rice, salt, and tobacco—was recorded systematically, with the name of each recipient duly noted and all recipients promising to repay after harvest [[1, p. 158]](#ref-1).

Winstedt's compilation, drawn from the official reports of civil servants, police, engineers, and other government officers, presents a more comprehensive picture of the administrative response across multiple states [[2, pp. 295–309]](#ref-2). The article reveals the colonial administration's dual concern with immediate humanitarian relief and the preservation of order and economic productivity [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2). The careful management of food supplies was designed to avoid both starvation and the demoralisation of able-bodied workers, a tension that Winstedt documents with characteristic administrative precision [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2). The rainfall data he provides—Trengganu recording 42.64 inches in December and 42.07 inches in November, a two-month total of nearly 85 inches—demonstrates the systematic meteorological observation that underpinned the colonial government's understanding of the disaster's causes [[2, pp. 308–309]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's journal literature on flooding is characterised by a shift from individual administrative dispatches to consolidated multi-state reports, reflecting both the increasing scale of documented events and the maturation of colonial administrative reporting systems. Keyser's 1896 account, reprinted in 2016 under the "Documents from Malaysian History" series, represents the recovery of a primary administrative source for scholarly use; no additional archival research or historiographical argument is offered beyond the original text and three explanatory footnotes [[1, pp. 155]](#ref-1). Its value lies in capturing the lived experience of affected communities through the eyes of the district officer responsible for their welfare, and in documenting the physical transformation of the river landscape with a specificity that no later compilation matches [[1, p. 156]](#ref-1).

Winstedt's 1927 article, by contrast, is a consolidated administrative report drawn from district-level accounts submitted by the officers who managed the crisis on the ground [[2, pp. 295–309]](#ref-2). The source material consists entirely of internal government communications—district officer reports, police accounts, engineering assessments, and medical officer observations—making it a primary administrative record of the disaster rather than an independent scholarly analysis [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2). Winstedt's framing, which dismisses folklore in favour of the meteorological and hydrological record, signals a methodological commitment to evidence-based documentation that distinguishes the article from the anecdotal quality of Keyser's dispatch [[2, p. 295]](#ref-2). Together, the two records establish a baseline for understanding the frequency, scale, and administrative response to major flooding in the Malay Peninsula during the colonial period, while also revealing the limits of the archival record: both are written from the perspective of the colonial state as rescuer and economic manager, and the voices of affected communities are mediated through the observations and reports of their administrators [[1, p. 155]](#ref-1), [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2).
