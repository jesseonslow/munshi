# The Scitamineae of the Malay Peninsula

H.N. Ridley, then Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, published this comprehensive monograph in 1899 to provide a systematic treatment of the Scitamineae (the ginger order) of the Malay Peninsula, a group he argued had been severely neglected by botanists due to the difficulty of preserving its fugacious flowers in herbarium specimens. The work covers all five families within the order—Zingiberaceae, Marantaceae, Cannaceae, Lowiaceae, and Musaceae—drawing on field observations in the jungle and living plants cultivated at the Botanic Gardens rather than relying on the inadequate dried specimens that had frustrated earlier workers like Miquel and Blume.

## Summary

Ridley's central methodological argument is that the Scitamineae cannot be adequately studied from pressed specimens alone, because the flowers are thin, fugacious, and water-filled, and the spikes rot in the press unless the flowers are dried separately. He therefore compiled descriptions from plants observed in the wild or grown in cultivation, giving the work a distinctive emphasis on living morphology, colour, scent, and ecological habit that sets it apart from the purely typographical treatments that preceded it. The monograph is organized taxonomically, beginning with a general introduction to the order's floral structure and its adaptive significance for insect pollination, followed by a synopsis of the Zingiberaceae and then detailed genus-by-genus treatments.

The work is substantially a revision of the genus *Globba*, for which Ridley describes several new species and reorganizes the classification around anther-spur morphology, explicitly rejecting Baker's reliance on bulbil production as a classificatory character. He also erects two new genera—*Camptandra* (for plants previously placed in *Kæmpferia* but distinguished by a unique dorsifixed versatile anther with a specialized bee-pollination mechanism) and *Conamomum* (for stout plants with woody elevated rhizomes and curved anther arms). Throughout, Ridley corrects misidentifications in the *Flora of British India*, reassigns species between genera (notably separating *Gastrochilus* from *Kæmpferia* and *Hornstedtia* from *Amomum*), and records Malay and Sakai vernacular names alongside medicinal and culinary uses.

The treatment of *Alpinia* is particularly extensive, with Ridley proposing a three-group subdivision (Hellenia, Catimbium, Cenolophon) that he considers more natural than Baker's four-section scheme, and describing several large, showy species new to science from the hills of Pahang, Selangor, and Perak. The Marantaceae, Cannaceae, Musaceae, and Lowiaceae receive briefer but still substantive treatments, with Ridley noting that the wild plantain *Musa malaccensis* is probably the parent of several local cultivated bananas.

### Key Findings

- Ridley describes or re-describes at least 15 new species across the order, including *Globba montana*, *G. calophylla*, *G. malaccensis*, *G. integra*, *G. Keithii*, *G. albiflora*, *G. elegans*, *G. violacea*, *G. perakensis*, *Hedychium microchilum*, *H. macrorrhizum*, *H. denticulatum*, *H. collinum*, *Camptandra lalifolia*, *Conamomum citrinum*, *C. utriculosum*, *Zingiber chrysostachys*, *Z. citrinum*, *Z. puberula*, *Amomum flavum*, *A. lappaceum*, *A. ochreum*, *A. perakense*, *A. testaceum*, *A. cylindraceum*, *A. hastilabium*, *A. micranthum*, *Hornstedtia grandis*, *H. conica*, *H. affinis*, *H. pusilla*, *H. pauciflora*, *H. velutina*, *H. metriocheilus*, *H. macrochilus*, *H. fulgens*, *H. venusta*, *Plagiostachys strobilifera*, *P. lateralis*, *Elettariopsis latiflora*, *E. pubescens*, *E. multiflora*, *Geostachys decurvata*, *G. secunda*, *G. rupestris*, *G. penangensis*, *G. elegans*, *Alpinia melanocarpa*, *A. rosella*, *A. secundiflora*, *A. assimilis*, *A. glabra*, *A. latilabris*, *A. nobilis*, *A. comosa*, *A. vitellina*, *A. cannaefolia*, *Phrynium cylindricum*, *P. malaccense*, *P. hirtum*, *P. basiflorum*, and *Donax grandis* (pp. 89–184).
- Two new genera are proposed: *Camptandra* (pp. 103–105) and *Conamomum* (pp. 121–123), the latter for plants with woody elevated rhizomes, free staminodes, and curved anther arms resembling those of *Amomum*.
- Ridley identifies *Musa malaccensis* as the probable parent of several local cultivated Pisangs, noting its small, seed-filled but edible fruit (pp. 87, 182–183).
- The leaves of *Hornstedtia* species, when beaten up and treated with caustic potash, produce a paper stuff of sufficient quality for practical use if demand warranted (p. 87).
- *Amomum uliginosum* is recorded as being deliberately planted by the Jakuns for its edible fruit, and its round red fruit is covered with soft processes resembling those of a rambutan (p. 137).
- The genus *Globba* is reorganized around anther-spur morphology (Aplantanthera, Ceratanthera, Marantella sections), with Ridley explicitly dismissing bulbil production as a classificatory character of "absolutely worthless" value since any species in a sufficiently wet habitat will produce them (p. 89).

### Conclusion

Ridley's definitive contribution is the demonstration that the Scitamineae of the Malay Peninsula constitute a flora of considerable diversity and endemism that had been grossly under-described by earlier workers, and that accurate taxonomy of the group demands observation of living plants in their ecological context. By combining field collection, garden cultivation, and detailed morphological description—including pollination mechanisms, fruit dispersal, and economic uses—he established a baseline for all subsequent work on the order in the region.

## Context

- The primary sources are Ridley's own field collections from across the Peninsula (1880s–1898), specimens from the collections of Dr. King (Perak), Curtis, Wray, Kelsall, Feilding, Derry, and others, plus living plants in the Singapore Botanic Gardens.
- The monograph represents a significant historiographical correction to the *Flora of British India* (Baker) and Miquel's *Flora India Batavica*, both of which Ridley found riddled with misidentifications and incomplete descriptions for this group.
