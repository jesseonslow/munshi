# British North Borneo

E.P. Gueritz, a resident of British North Borneo for nearly three years, published this descriptive survey of the territory under the British North Borneo Company in 1884, drawing on personal observation and the official reports of company officers Pryer, Von Donop, Frank Hatton, and Witt. The article presents the colony's geography, natural resources, climate, population, and administrative prospects as a case for its strategic and commercial importance to the British Empire. It was read on Gueritz's behalf at the Montreal Meeting of the British Association for the Advancement of Science in August 1884, at the suggestion of J.S. O'Halloran, Secretary of the Royal Colonial Institute (p. 333).

## Summary

Gueritz's paper is a comprehensive natural and economic survey of the British North Borneo Company's territory, covering approximately 20,000 square miles of coastline and interior between the 4th and 7th parallels of North latitude. The dominant argument is one of latent potential: the territory possesses harbours of strategic value on major shipping routes between China, Australia, and the West; soils suited to nearly every tropical cash crop; abundant jungle products including timber, camphor, gutta-percha, and edible birds' nests; and mineral deposits of coal, gold, copper, and silver ore that remain largely unexploited. Gueritz frames these assets against the backdrop of a thinly populated, sparsely administered colony where the native population is content with subsistence agriculture and the labour supply is the principal constraint on development (pp. 323–333).

The article also addresses the administrative and moral dimensions of colonial governance. Gueritz highlights the Company's recent anti-slavery legislation—prohibiting importation of slaves, ending debt-bondage seizure, and freeing children born to slave parents after November 1883—as evidence of civilising progress. He notes that the Dusuns of the interior, though peaceful and agricultural, are "naturally indolent" for wage labour, and that the Chinese, numbering around 3,000 at Sandakan alone, are the essential pioneer element who will extend the Government's reach into the interior (pp. 332–333). The concluding section positions the colony as an experiment of "more than local importance," watched by rival powers with interests in the surrounding seas, and argues that governing through and with the natives is the only viable administrative model (p. 333).

An editorial supplement appended to the article reproduces remarks by A. Dent, made before the Royal Colonial Institute in May 1885, which provide a fiscal and commercial update: fiscal revenue rose 60 per cent from 1883 to 1884, land sales fell 39 per cent, and total revenue increased 23 per cent. Dent confirms the existence of alluvial gold in 30 to 40 locations along the Segama and Kinabatangan rivers, reports timber exports of $10,000 for 1884, and notes that 430 coolies were working tobacco plantations by February 1885 (pp. 334–335).

### Key Findings

- The territory comprises approximately 20,000 square miles with a 500-mile coastline; Mount Kina Balu rises to 13,698 feet, the highest peak in the range (p. 323).
- The Gomanton birds' nest caves on the Kinabatangan river yield an annual value of $25,000; the bats' guano found within is valued at £8 to £15 per ton (p. 329).
- Coal is worked from five seams at the Moara mines (mouth of the Brunei River) of thicknesses 26, 24, 6, 5, and 4 feet, leased by the Sultan of Brunei to Messrs. Cowie Brothers of Labuan (p. 329).
- Anti-slavery regulations promulgated by the Company in 1883 prohibited slave importation, ended creditor seizure of debtors, and declared free all children born to slave parents after November 1883 (p. 332).
- Fiscal revenue for 1884 increased 60 per cent over 1883; timber exports for 1884 amounted to $10,000; a force of 180 police maintained order across the territory (pp. 334–335).
- The population was roughly estimated at 150,000, with approximately 3,000 Chinese settled at Sandakan alone; rainfall at Kudat in 1883 was 120.56 inches (pp. 331–332).

### Conclusion

Gueritz's definitive takeaway is that British North Borneo, though still in its infancy as a colony, occupies a position of strategic and commercial importance that justifies continued British presence. The combination of defensible harbours on key maritime routes, fertile land suited to tropical cultivation, and abundant natural products means the territory will inevitably attract settlement and development; the only question is whether that development will occur under British administration or fall to a rival power. The Company's governance model—governing through native intermediaries and relying on Chinese labour—is presented as both practical and morally progressive, particularly in its abolition of slavery.

## Context

- The article was prepared for the Royal Colonial Institute and the British Association for the Advancement of Science, reflecting the late-Victorian institutional interest in colonial administration and imperial strategy in Southeast Asia.
- The editorial supplement by A. Dent (May 1885) clarifies that the Company's legal authority derived entirely from cessions by the Sultans of Brunei and Sulu, with the British Crown's role limited to incorporation by Royal Charter—a distinction Dent emphasises to counter the perception that the Company held "enormous powers" granted directly by the Queen (p. 335).
