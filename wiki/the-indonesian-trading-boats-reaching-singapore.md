# The Indonesian trading boats reaching Singapore

C. A. Gibson-Hill published this detailed ethnographic and nautical study in 1950, based on direct observations of Indonesian sailing vessels in Singapore roads during 1947–1949. Working in the immediate post-war period when these traditional craft were experiencing fluctuating numbers due to freight economics and political disruption, Gibson-Hill provides a taxonomic account of the non-Chinese trading boats reaching Singapore, arguing that each type represents a distinct evolutionary lineage shaped by local building traditions, monsoon trading patterns, and selective adoption of European design elements.

## Summary

The article systematically describes six categories of Indonesian trading vessel: the Palari (Makassar trader), the Golekkan (Madura trader), the Leteh-Leteh, the Sekochi, the Lambok, and two Sumatran coasters. Gibson-Hill's central analytical concern is the relationship between hull form, rig, and construction technique, and how these features reveal the historical development of each boat type. He traces the Palari's origins to the Pajala hull of southern Celebes, modified with a raised deck and overhanging stern that he attributes partly to sixteenth-century Portuguese contact with Gowa. The Golekkan, he argues, evolved from the double-outrigger coasting boat of East Java, retaining the outrigger boom as a vestigial element even after the hull became beamy enough to dispense with lateral stability devices. The Leteh-Leteh presents what Gibson-Hill calls "an interesting admixture" of Madurese and Celebes practices, with its Pajala-like hull and twin steering paddles suggesting Celebes origins, while its single large triangular sail points to a Madurese or East Javanese derivation.

The study is grounded in first-hand measurement and observation. Gibson-Hill examined over sixty boats in Singapore roads, recording dimensions, sail areas, crew sizes, cargo capacities, and construction details. He supplements this with historical illustrations (Matthes's mid-nineteenth-century drawings of Bintak, a model in the Prinsenhof Museum at Delft) and the travel literature of Collins, Nooteboom, and Poujade to reconstruct earlier forms. A recurring theme is the tension between traditional Indonesian building methods—particularly the practice of shaping planks before inserting ribs, which produces the characteristic "stepped" plank joints that constitute the weakest part of the hull—and the practical demands of an increasingly competitive trading environment in which the slower, more unwieldy traditional designs are being displaced by the European-modelled Lambok.

Gibson-Hill also addresses the question of European influence on these boats with considerable nuance. While accepting that the Palari's high overhanging stern likely derives from Portuguese vessels, he rejects the more extravagant claims of earlier authorities (such as Poujade's invocation of "the barque of Sennacherib") and demonstrates that most distinctive features—the tripartite bowsprit, the ketch rig, the jib-headed topsails—are products of the last fifty to a hundred years, introduced with the transition from square sails to fore-and-aft rig.

### Key Findings

- The Palari, built primarily at Bira and Lemo Lemo in southern Celebes, measures 50–70 feet overall with a waterline length of 30–43 feet; a 53-foot example sets approximately 1,000 square feet of canvas and carries a crew of 7–8 men earning $10–20 per month plus food; masters reckon the 1,200-mile passage from Makassar to Singapore takes 8–9 days with the north-east monsoon (pp. 112–113).
- The Golekkan, built exclusively on Madura, is remarkably consistent in size at 50–55 feet overall with a waterline of 41–45 feet; a 52-foot waterline hull carries 500–550 piculs of cargo, and the 535-mile run from Batavia to Singapore is estimated at 5–6 days at a maximum speed of 8–8½ knots (pp. 122–123).
- The Leteh-Leteh, built on both Madura and in southern Celebes, ranges from 30 to nearly 60 feet overall; the larger Madurese examples (56 feet overall, 50 feet waterline) set approximately 900 square feet of canvas in a single sail, carry 550–600 piculs, and are worked by crews of 5–6 men (p. 128).
- The double-masted Lambok ketch from Bonerate reaches 58–80 feet overall with a waterline of 40–55 feet, a sail area of about 2,000 square feet, and a cargo capacity exceeding 700 piculs, yet requires only 6–7 men—demonstrating the efficiency advantage of the European-modelled design over the traditional Palari and Golekkan (p. 134).
- The Palari's theoretical hull life is fifty years, but major timber replacement begins at twenty years; the boats are afloat approximately nine months annually, laid up on beaches from the start of the south-west monsoon (end of year) until relaunching at the end of March (p. 111).
- The Portuguese are recorded as having reached Gowa (southwestern Celebes) as early as 1512, and the Dutch established a trading post at Makassar in 1607; Gibson-Hill dates the introduction of the ketch rig to the last forty to fifty years before 1950, replacing an earlier square-sail rig still visible in mid-nineteenth-century illustrations (pp. 118–120).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive takeaway is that the Indonesian trading boats reaching Singapore represent a living archive of maritime cultural exchange, in which European design elements have been absorbed selectively and adapted to local building traditions and monsoon trading rhythms. He documents a clear trajectory of change: the traditional Palari and Golekkan, with their shallow keels, paddle steering, and limited ability to sail to windward, are being progressively displaced by the Lambok, a clumsy but practical copy of European hull form that can be handled by a smaller crew and makes a better course. The article thus captures a transitional moment in which centuries-old vessel types are in the process of obsolescence, a process driven not by any single technological breakthrough but by the cumulative economic pressures of a modernizing freight market.

## Context

- The study is based on Gibson-Hill's direct examination of boats in Singapore roads between 1947 and 1949, supplemented by observations in North Borneo (July–August 1949); it forms the third in a series of papers on Malayan watercraft, following his 1949 article on the Pinas-Bedar group of east coast boats (JMBRAS 22, pt 3).
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the nautical ethnography of the Indonesian archipelago by providing the first detailed measurements and construction analysis of these vessel types as observed in a Singapore context, and by critically evaluating earlier European accounts (Poujade, Nooteboom, Hornell) against direct evidence.
