# Christianization in Sabah and the development of indigenous communities: a historical study

Mat Zin bin Mat Kib's 2004 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the role of Christian missionaries in the social, educational, and economic development of indigenous communities in Sabah from the mid-nineteenth century through the early post-independence period. The central thesis is that missionaries succeeded in converting interior indigenous groups—particularly the Rungus, Dusun-Kadazan, and Murut—by combining evangelical work with tangible social services (education, medicine, agricultural training, and land tenure reform), a strategy that proved far more effective than the earlier and less institutionalized spread of Islam among the same populations.

## Summary

The article addresses a historiographical gap: while passing references to missionary activity in Sabah appear in the works of Shirley Lees, Sabihah Osman, Mohd Nor bin Long, K. M. George, and John Rooney, no prior study had systematically examined the full spectrum of missionary contributions to indigenous community development. Mat Zin situates the indigenous peoples of pre-missionary Sabah as animist, illiterate, and afflicted by endemic disease, with 85 per cent living in the interior as late as the mid-1960s (p. 53). This condition of social and material deprivation, he argues, created what he terms "fertile ground" for both Islam and Christianity, but it was the Christian denominations that institutionalized a comprehensive development programme.

The core argument proceeds through several interconnected domains. Education is treated as the primary vector of change: mission schools produced the first generation of indigenous literates and, by extension, the political and professional elite of independent Sabah. The article traces how missionaries—Catholic, Anglican, Basel, SDA, SIB, and PCS—assimilated into local communities, learned indigenous languages, and used that cultural proximity to introduce not only scripture but also hygienic practices, agricultural innovation, and concepts of individual land ownership. The case of the Rungus of Kudat is developed in some detail: missionaries eroded animistic taboos around digging and farming in open land, introduced wooden pegs to demarcate boundaries (the origin of Rungus land tenure), and progressively dismantled the longhouse system by encouraging nuclear family housing and sanitation infrastructure (pp. 9–10).

Health and economic development are presented as reinforcing the educational mission. Medical officers who doubled as missionaries (such as Dr Campbell in Beaufort, 1916, and Dr Willis at Murok, 1967) and Catholic nursing orders provided the first modern medical contact for many interior communities. Agricultural projects—the Catholic Welfare Society's Bundu Tuhan scheme (1959) and the BCCM's Sapong scheme (1963)—introduced cash crops and terracing techniques, linking conversion to material improvement. Mat Zin draws on church records, the *British North Borneo Herald and Official Gazette*, North Borneo Central Archive files, and oral interviews to construct this narrative, though the evidential base is uneven and at times relies on a single source for a claim.

### Key Findings

- Literacy among indigenous Sabahans stood at 6.9 per cent in 1921, 9.0 per cent in 1931, and 11.7 per cent in 1951; the first government school in Labuan (Sekolah Kebangsaan Karupang) was not built until 1936, whereas Catholic and Anglican mission schools had been operating since the 1880s (pp. 53, 58).
- At least two former Heads of State (Tun Mustapha Harun, Tun Ahmad Koroh) and five former Chief Ministers (including Joseph Pairin Kitingan, Peter Lo Su Yin, Bernard Dompok, Donald Stephens, and Chong Kah Kiat) were products of Christian mission schools (pp. 58–59).
- The 1981 Sabah statistical bulletin recorded 84,042 Dusun-Kadazan Christians against 16,890 Muslims, and 14,734 Christian Muruts against only 1,510 Muslims, demonstrating the comparative success of Christian evangelization in the interior (p. 62).
- The PCS established three mission schools in 1965 (Lajong, Tinangol, Ludong) and a Home Science School at Tinangol in 1966, the latter limited to twenty students at a time, teaching sewing, cookery, farming, and hygiene (p. 60).
- The BCCM channelled mountain water to Murut villages in Pensiangan district, replacing silted water sources, and the Catholic church's Bundu Tuhan Agricultural Project (1959) introduced tobacco and cabbage cultivation at high altitude, later renamed the Kota Kinabalu Development Project (pp. 10–11).
- No fewer than 30 Christian denominations operated in Sabah, divided into major, medium, and minor categories; the major denominations (Catholic, Anglican, BCCM, SIB, SDA, PCS, True Jesus) were all established between 1850 and 1937 (p. 55).

### Conclusion

Mat Zin's definitive takeaway is that Christian missionaries in Sabah functioned as de facto agents of modernization whose social-service orientation—education, medicine, agriculture, land tenure—was inseparable from their evangelical purpose and was the decisive factor in the durable Christianization of interior indigenous groups. He explicitly contrasts this with the earlier arrival of Islam (1380s), which, despite its longer presence, failed to achieve comparable conversion rates because it lacked an equivalent institutional infrastructure of schools, clinics, and development projects directed at the interior.

## Context

- Primary archival sources include the North Borneo Central Archive (NBCA files 66 and 1307), the *British North Borneo Herald and Official Gazette*, the Sabah Archive "pink files," and church-internal publications (*BCCM Information*, *Catholic Sabah*, *P.C.S. Silver Jubilee Souvenir*); the author also conducted oral interviews (notably with Jimmy Ojilim Assam of the PCS, Kudat).
- The article extends the author's own monograph *Kristian di Sabah, 1881–1994* (UKM Press, 2003) into a conference-derived journal format and contributes to the small body of Malaysian historiography that treats missionary activity as a development variable rather than solely a religious phenomenon.
