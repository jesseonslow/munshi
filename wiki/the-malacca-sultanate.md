# The Malacca sultanate

R.J. Wilkinson, a colonial administrator based in Malacca, published this comprehensive reconstruction of the Malacca Sultanate's political history in 1935, drawing on the Malay Annals, the Bostan al-Salatin, Chinese Ming dynasty records, and Portuguese sources to trace the state from its origins as a trading mart in the early fifteenth century through the reign of Mahmud Shah on the eve of the Portuguese conquest. His overarching argument is that the Malay Annals, while invaluable, are fundamentally a family history of the bendahara lineage and must be read critically against external evidence to separate political reality from dynastic legend.

## Summary

Wilkinson's central methodological contribution is the systematic cross-referencing of Malay chronicle tradition with Chinese diplomatic records to establish a reliable chronology and to identify where the Annals' narrative serves the interests of its author—a bendahara descended from the Tamil merchant Tun Ali who engineered the coup that placed Mudzafar Shah on the throne. He demonstrates that the Annals' account of the founding of Malacca as a kingdom in 1406, when the Ming eunuch Yin Ching delivered a commission, seal, and yellow umbrella to the local chief (the *permaisura*), is corroborated by the Ming records, but that the elaborate court ritual attributed to the third ruler, Mohammed Shah, reflects a later aspiration to replicate the Sri Vijaya imperial tradition rather than a contemporary reality (pp. 27–29). The article then moves through the reigns of Mudzafar Shah, Mansur Shah, Alaedin Riayat Shah, and Mahmud Shah, using Chinese tribute records and dated tombstones to anchor events that the Annals leave undated.

The political narrative Wilkinson reconstructs centres on the tension between the royal house and the bendahara family, which effectively controlled the succession. Tun Perak, the great Malay bendahara who served three rulers, placed his own nephews on the throne at the expense of princes with stronger claims by primogeniture or royal birth. This pattern culminated in the rise and fall of Tun Mutahir (Bendahara Seri Maharaja), the Tamil-descended minister who monopolised the three highest offices in the state and whose execution by Sultan Mahmud—prompted by the laksamana Khoja Hasan on a false accusation of treason—marked the end of the bendahara's political dominance just before the Portuguese arrival (pp. 63–67). Wilkinson is careful throughout to distinguish what the Annals claim from what the evidence supports, noting that the famous Hang Tuah legend of the Java mission is almost certainly a conflation of events that occurred locally in Malacca (p. 41).

The article also provides a detailed reconstruction of the Malacca court system, including the sumptuary laws, the hierarchy of titles (*seri* and *sang*), the investiture ceremonies, and the economic basis of the state in tin production and trade duties. Wilkinson argues that the early prosperity of the trading port was ultimately undermined by the greed of the Malay chiefs, who introduced the *kerah* corvée, toll-stations, and feudal exploitation of the surrounding population (p. 27).

### Key Findings

- The kingdom of Malacca is dated to 1406, when the Ming eunuch Yin Ching delivered a commission, seal, silk robes, and yellow umbrella to the *permaisura*; Cheng Ho's fleet confirmed the city's status in 1409 (pp. 23–24).
- The *permaisura* visited China in 1411 with a suite of 540 persons and was received by the Emperor in person; he died in 1414 (pp. 24–25).
- Mudzafar Shah announced his accession to the Ming court in 1445; Mansur Shah did the same in 1459; Mansur Shah died in 1477 (pp. 37, 59).
- At its height under Mahmud Shah, the Malacca state encompassed Pahang, Siak, Kampar, Trengganu, Johor, Manjong, Bruas, Kelantan, Indragiri, and the Riau-Lingga Archipelago—co-extensive with modern British Malaya except for Kedah and Perlis (p. 58).
- The royal palace built by Mansur Shah had a frontage of 272 feet (calculated at 5′ 4″ per *depa*), pillars a *depa* in circumference, and was destroyed by lightning and fire; it was rebuilt in one month using labour distributed among named fiefs (pp. 44–45).
- The execution of Bendahara Seri Maharaja (Tun Mutahir) and his relatives—Tun Tahir, Tun Hasan, Tun Ali, and others—was carried out by the guardsmen Tun Sura Diraja and Tun Indera Segara; only the young Tun Hamza was spared to perpetuate the family (pp. 66–67).

### Conclusion

Wilkinson's definitive takeaway is that the Malacca Sultanate's greatness was a product of its trading position and the administrative genius of its bendahara ministers rather than the personal qualities of its rulers, most of whom were weak or colourless. The state reached its maximum territorial extent in the decade before 1511, but the internal corruption and factional violence that characterised Mahmud Shah's reign—culminating in the massacre of the bendahara's family—left it politically fragile at the moment the Portuguese arrived.

## Context

- Wilkinson held the rank of C.M.G. and was based in Malacca at the time of writing; his administrative vantage point gave him access to local traditions and the Perak regalia (including the "thunder-seal" of the first Malay king) that informed his discussion of court ritual (pp. 22, 28).
- The study draws primarily on the Malay Annals (*Sejarah Melayu*), the *Bostan al-Salatin*, Chinese Ming court records, and Portuguese accounts; its historiographical contribution lies in the critical chronology it constructs from dated tombstones and Chinese tribute records to correct the Annals' unreliable dating (p. 59).
