# The Moluccan spice monopoly, 1770–1824. Passages concerning Bencoolen written in collaboration with J. Bastin

W. W. Skeat's 1898 article "Some Records of Malay Magic by an Eye-Witness" presents first-hand ethnographic observations of Malay spiritual ceremonies conducted in the Rembau district of Negri Sembilan, collected during his fieldwork in the late 1890s. Skeat, then a young Straits Settlements civil servant, documents in meticulous detail the rituals of the *pawang* (shaman) surrounding rice cultivation, healing, and fishing, and offers an interpretive framework drawing on Frazer's *Golden Bough* to explain these practices as manifestations of sympathetic magic.

## Summary

Skeat's article is structured around three major ceremonial complexes: the padi (rice) harvest ritual, the healing ceremony involving spirit possession, and the fishing-stake (*kelong*) consecration. The padi ceremony occupies the largest portion of the text and is described as a complete sequence from the identification of the spirit-bearing sheaf through the cutting of the first seven ears, the "cradle" procession, the three-day taboo period, and the final pounding and deposit of the sacred grain in the rice bin. Skeat records the full Malay-language incantations, the specific botanical and material components (the seven-leaf brush, the *tepong tawar* powder, the *penuwei* reaping knives, the sugar-cane stem), and the omens observed during the rite.

The healing ceremony is described with particular vividness: a male *pawang* enters a state of spirit possession, speaks in a "spirit language" (*Bhasa Hantu*), and is seized by a tiger spirit that causes him to lick the patient's body from head to foot, let blood from his own arm over the patient, and engage in an imaginary combat with the disease-spirit. Skeat provides a partial vocabulary of this spirit language and the full text of the *Lagu Pemanggil* (summoning song), which he translates into English verse. The fishing-stake section documents the elaborate offerings suspended from *kelong* structures, the invocations to water spirits, and the taboos imposed on fishermen.

Throughout, Skeat's method is that of a careful observer recording what he witnessed or was shown, supplemented by charms and explanations provided by the *pawang* and local informants. He is transparent about the limits of his access—certain ceremonies (such as the sowing rite) could only be described to him rather than observed—and he explicitly invites future scholars to verify and extend his notes.

### Key Findings

- The padi ceremony involves a three-day taboo period during which no money, rice, salt, or oil may leave the house, hair may not be cut, the hearth fire must not go out, and the reapers must not allow their shadows to fall on the rice in their baskets (pp. 9–10).
- The "cradle" of the rice-spirit contained seven specific items: a strip of white cloth, a piece of partly coloured thread (*benang pancharona*), an egg, a hard jungle-nut (*buah kras*), a shell called *krang*, a long iron nail, and five *hasta* of red cloth (p. 3).
- The *Lagu Pemanggil* (summoning song) comprises 61 lines and describes a golden-plated ship called *Lanchang Kuning* with a mast named "Raja Mendelu," a rudder named "The Hanging Bees' Nest," and twice seven oarsmen (pp. 16–19).
- The spirit language (*Bhasa Hantu*) vocabulary table records 16 Malay-to-spirit-language correspondences, including *anak* (child) = *demit*, *mati* (dead) = *merat*, *nyawa* (life) = *kēlēbu*, and *ayam* (fowl) = *mendong* (pp. 23–24).
- The *kelong* (fishing-stake) offering tray contained 28 small ornamental rice receptacles (14 *katupat* and 14 *lepat*), five portions of cooked and five of uncooked fowl, seven kinds of coloured rice, five banana-leaf buckets of sugar-cane juice, five waxen tapers, five betel chews, five cigarettes, and five stones (pp. 30–31).
- The 1891 census showed that in Rembau the native women outnumbered the native men, and not one per cent of native customary landholdings were registered as the property of men, reflecting the matrilineal land-tenure system inherited from Sakai intermarriage (p. 42).

### Conclusion

Skeat's definitive takeaway is that these ceremonies are not "mere childish folly" but are intelligible expressions of sympathetic magic operating on the principle that "any effect can be produced by imitating it." The rice-child is a fertility analogue (comparable to the English "corn-baby"), the sugar-cane is a May-pole, the iron nail wards evil, and the *pawang*'s eye-rolling and throat-clicking are homoeopathic gestures intended to whiten the rice and make it eat well. Skeat positions his work as a foundation for future rigorous study, urging that "rigid and accurate knowledge" rather than conjecture is what the field requires.

## Context

- Skeat was a junior officer in the Straits Settlements civil service at the time of writing; his observations were made in the Rembau district of Negri Sembilan, a Malay state under British protection, and the article was published in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society.
- The article draws on direct field observation supplemented by informants' descriptions; it represents one of the earliest systematic ethnographic records of Malay *pawang* practice and was explicitly intended as a working document for future scholars to verify and extend.
