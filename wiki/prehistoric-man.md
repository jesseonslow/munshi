# Prehistoric Man

The study of prehistoric man in the Malay Peninsula and Borneo rests on a small but significant body of osteological and archaeological evidence, comprising fragmentary human remains from cave and rock-shelter sites in Perak, Pahang, and Perlis, submerged deposits near Kuala Selinsing, and megalithic slab-graves spanning the peninsula, Sumatra, and Java. The material is characteristically incomplete—crushed crania, dispersed limb bones, and commingled assemblages—yet it has generated sustained scholarly debate over the racial affinities of the earliest known inhabitants of the region, the cultural attribution of their artefacts, and the chronological depth of human occupation. The principal questions have concerned whether the skeletal evidence points to Negrito, Protomalayan, or a broader Australoid stock, and whether the associated cultural material—iron implements, beads, pottery, and in one case a Pallava-inscribed seal—reflects indigenous development or external influence from South Asia or Mainland Southeast Asia.

## Osteological Evidence from Caves and Rock-Shelters

The most substantial osteological material from prehistoric contexts in the peninsula derives from four Perak cave sites—Gua To Long in Gunong Sennyum, Gua Kajang at Lenggong, Gua Kerbau in Gunong Pondok, and Bukit Chuping—alongside cranial fragments from a submerged locality near Kuala Selinsing. The Perak material was collected by Ivor Evans and others and is in a state of extreme fragmentation and commingling, with bones dispersed across cave floors and packed in plaster-of-Paris slabs, making definitive allocation of limb bones to crania nearly impossible [[1]](#ref-1).

W.L.H. Duckworth, a Cambridge-trained physician and physical anthropologist, published the first systematic osteological survey of this material in 1934. His central thesis was that the cave-dwellers—comprising both pygmy-statured and taller individuals—belonged to a dolichocephalic, slender-limbed racial type he identified as "Dravidian" or "Pre-dravidian," linking them to the Veddas of Ceylon, the Nicobarese, the Palaiyans of southern India, the Wadjak of Java, and even Australian aborigines [[1]](#ref-1). Duckworth excluded the Semang (Negrito) on the grounds of their brachycephalic head-form, and supported his Australoid affiliation through craniometric comparisons, dental morphology, and the distinctive slenderness of the long bones, which he placed in a graduated sequence alongside Nicobarese and Australian femora [[1]](#ref-1).

The Gunong Sennyum calvaria, with a cephalic index of 61, a maximum calvarial width of 117 mm, and a glabello-occipital length of 173 mm, represents an extremely dolichocephalic and small skull [[1, p. 153]](#ref-1). The Lenggong individual is estimated at approximately 5 ft. 2 in. stature, with a femur and humerus of remarkable slenderness closely resembling those of a Nicobarese islander in the Cambridge Collection [[1, pp. 154–155]](#ref-1). The Gunong Pondok No. 4 Box femora, with an oblique length of 467.5 mm, indicate a stature of nearly 5 ft. 7 in., placing this individual well above the Lenggong example and within the range of Australian aborigines [[1, p. 157]](#ref-1). The Bukit Chuping material is of particular interest because it appears to represent only two skeletons, allowing a more confident association between a small skull (175 × 128 mm, cephalic index 73.1) and a long femur (estimated 470 mm, implying a stature of approximately 1700 mm)—a combination Duckworth argues is genuine rather than an artefact of misallocation, citing Kloss's record of a Senoi Sakai of Sungkai at 1698 mm as corroborating evidence that such stature was attainable among peninsula jungle tribes [[1, pp. 158]](#ref-1).

Duckworth also noted that dental attrition in the Gunong Pondok specimens was so extreme in the anterior teeth that he attributed it to artificial filing, a practice known in south-east Asia, citing Koganei's 1922 study of artificial dental deformation among Jōmon peoples [[1, p. 160]](#ref-1).

The Selinsing collection, comprising cranial fragments from fifteen individuals recovered from shallow marine deposition, was treated more briefly by Duckworth. He noted the presence of barnacles and oyster shells on several specimens, the abrasive weathering of limb fragments, and the relatively "recent" appearance of two juvenile skulls. On the basis of these taphonomic observations and the similarity of the adult crania to recent comparative specimens, he cautioned against assigning great antiquity to the collection, suggesting its age may be limited to "a matter of decades" [[1]](#ref-1). Of the eight measurable Selinsing crania, only one has a cephalic index over 80 (brachycephalic); one registers exactly 80, and the remainder fall below that figure, contradicting Evans's earlier characterization of the collection as brachycephalic [[1, p. 167]](#ref-1).

## The Kuala Selinsing Canoe Burials

A separate and equally important body of evidence comes from the Kuala Selinsing excavations, where skeletal remains were recovered from canoe burials. Gordon Harrower, Professor of Anatomy at Singapore, published his osteological analysis of this material in 1933. He received three groups of skeletal remains (designated Associations 1, 2, and 3) from I. H. N. Evans, Ethnographer at the Perak Museum, for anatomical examination. The material was fragmentary in all three cases, with the skulls particularly badly crushed, requiring careful reconstruction from numerous pieces [[2]](#ref-2).

Harrower's central finding was that the buried individuals were of Negrito or Protomalayan origin rather than Malay or Indian—a conclusion that complicated the interpretation of the site's associated cultural material, which included objects of clear Indian origin (a seal bearing a Pallava inscription, cornelian and agate beads) alongside items of probable Malayan or Bornean affinity [[2]](#ref-2). The comparative methodology relied heavily on the recently published measurements of three Semang skulls by Schebesta and Lebzelter (1926), which at the time represented the only published Negrito craniometric data from the Malay Peninsula region [[2]](#ref-2).

The Association 2 skull had an estimated cranial capacity of 1,236.5 ccms (via Manouvrier's formula), a cephalic index of 76.98, and a nasal index of 66 (markedly platyrhine), placing it in the microcephalic, mesaticephalic, platyrhine group most closely comparable to Schebesta's Menri Semang skull (capacity 1,270 ccms, cephalic index 77.7) [[2, pp. 195–198]](#ref-2). The Association 1 skull measured 190 mm in maximum length with a cephalic index of 75.0, substantially larger than the Association 2 skull (176 mm) but sharing the same low-vaulted, square-faced, platyrhine morphology; Harrower classified it as a probable Semang–Dyak hybrid [[2, pp. 198–200]](#ref-2). Both Association 2 and Association 3 individuals were of pigmy stature, estimated at approximately 1.54 m, while Association 1 was estimated at 1.60 m; the presence of two pigmy individuals among three skeletons was taken as evidence of a dominant Negrito element rather than an adventitious occurrence [[2, pp. 204–205]](#ref-2).

The Association 2 tibiae showed pronounced platycnemism (indices 70.9 and 62.5), consistent with habitual squatting, and the radio-humeral index of 81.7 placed the individual in the dolichokerkik group—a primitive trait shared with Fuegians, Andamanese, and simians [[2, pp. 204–205]](#ref-2). The Association 2 skull also exhibited extreme dental wear with complete loss of enamel and exposure of ground-smooth dentine on all occlusal surfaces, a condition Harrower also observed in a modern Hylam Chinese skull but could not attribute to any identifiable dietary cause [[2, pp. 186–187]](#ref-2).

Evans's appended note contextualised the finding within his broader interpretation of the Kuala Selinsing site, proposing that Proto-Malayan sea-roving peoples may have conducted the bead trade across the Malay Archipelago under Indian influence, with Negritos present as dependants or through intermarriage—a hypothesis that reconciled the Indian cultural material with the Negrito skeletal remains [[2, pp. 209–210]](#ref-2).

## Megalithic Slab-Graves and the Iron Age

The megalithic slab-graves of the Malay world represent a distinct category of prehistoric evidence, and their interpretation has been the subject of sustained debate. R.O. Winstedt, writing in 1941 as a senior colonial administrator and amateur archaeologist, synthesised a decade of scattered reports on megalithic slab-graves across Perak, Sumatra, and Java to evaluate competing hypotheses about their builders. His overarching thesis was that the origin of these graves—whether Indian, Indo-Chinese, or otherwise—remained genuinely unresolved, and that the existing evidence, while suggestive, was insufficient to commit to any single cultural attribution [[3]](#ref-3).

The material evidence is consistent across sites: no skeletal remains, coarse pottery fragments, glass and stone beads, and iron implements—socketed tools in Malaya, adze-type implements in Java. The absence of gold in any grave is noted as a significant negative datum [[3]](#ref-3). Five Perak slab-graves at Sungkai and the Bernam yielded socketed iron tools, coarse pottery, and no skeletal remains; the Changkat Mantri grave uniquely contained a cross-hatched stone bark-pounder, a small bronze bowl, and three cornelian beads [[3, p. 93]](#ref-3). H.C. Beck dated the bead assemblages from the Slim and Sungai Kruit graves to between 1 and 400 A.D., based on their similarity to Kuala Selinsing and Johore beads [[3, p. 94]](#ref-3).

In South Sumatra, nine slab-graves were identified between Pagar 'Alam and Tanjong Sakti; one at Tegoerwangi contained a gold pin and over 100 beads, while a second held 1,372 tiny ochre, white, and red beads plus eight bronze fragments [[3, p. 94]](#ref-3). The Wanasari slab-grave in Java contained 35 superimposed bodies, thousands of glass beads (including three long cornelian beads of a type also found in Malaya), woven patterns preserved in oxidised iron, and iron adze-type implements—none of the socketed type found in Malaya [[3, p. 95]](#ref-3).

The interpretive debate centres on two main rival hypotheses. Braddell argued the Perak graves lay on a route to Pahang gold-mines and that the Bernam was Ptolemy's Khryscanas, implying Indian miners; Winstedt concedes the gold argument but notes the total absence of gold in the graves undermines it [[3]](#ref-3). Wilkinson proposed an Indo-Chinese (Mon) origin, linking the graves to the same cultural sphere that produced the bronze drums. Winstedt raises several objections: the absence of bronze in the Malayan graves (whereas bronze drums are well attested), the fact that only one megalithic burial vault is known from Indo-China itself, and the linguistic argument that the relevant Mon words entered local speech only in a later wave [[3]](#ref-3). Winstedt then turns to the Indian hypothesis, which Wilkinson had dismissed on the grounds that Indians practised cremation and did not export iron. Winstedt counters by citing Cumming's survey of South Indian Iron Age burial sites, which include pottery cists, sarcophagi, funeral urns, grinding stones, iron weapons, bronze articles, cornelian and crystal beads, and buffalo figures—a profile that overlaps considerably with the slab-grave assemblages. He notes that iron reached southern India by approximately 500 B.C., removing any *a priori* chronological objection. Yet he stops short of endorsing the Indian hypothesis, acknowledging that the Bernam site's later Hindu and Buddhist remains merely suggest earlier Indian contact without proving it [[3]](#ref-3).

The only authenticated Indo-Chinese bronze found in Malaya is a drum-head of the type Callenfels allocated to 400–300 B.C., recovered from the Tembeling River [1, p. 97, n.].

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on prehistoric man in the region reflects a progression from descriptive collection to comparative analysis, and from racial typology to cautious cultural attribution. The earliest substantive contributions were the osteological surveys of Harrower (1933) and Duckworth (1934), both of which relied on material collected by Ivor Evans and examined by European-trained specialists at Cambridge and Singapore. These studies employed the classificatory frameworks of their period—Duckworth's "Dravidian" and "Australoid" categories, Harrower's Negrito–Protomalayan distinction—and would not survive subsequent decades of population-genetic and post-colonial critique [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2).

Winstedt's 1932 article "The prehistory of Malaya" [[4]](#ref-4) and his 1941 synthesis on slab-graves [[3]](#ref-3) represent a shift toward integrative, comparative approaches that attempted to place the Malayan evidence within a broader South and Southeast Asian context. The 1941 article in particular is notable for its explicit methodological caution against premature cultural attribution—a stance that anticipated later, more rigorous approaches to the region's prehistory [[3]](#ref-3). The Cambridge University Expedition of 1899–1900, documented in the 1953 monograph by Gibson-Hill, Skeat, and Laidlaw, contributed the first recorded fossils from the Malay Peninsula and the first anthropometric measurements and photographs of Kelantan and Kedah Negritos, establishing a baseline for subsequent comparative work [[5]](#ref-5).

The overall record remains limited in scope: the skeletal material is fragmentary, the cultural assemblages are small, and no single site has yielded a stratified sequence sufficient to establish a firm chronological framework. The debates over racial attribution and cultural origin that dominated the 1930s and 1940s literature have not been definitively resolved within the Society's published record, and the evidence continues to invite reinterpretation as new analytical methods become available.
