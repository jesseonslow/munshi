# Malayan women during the Japanese occupation

## Abstract

Accounts of the Japanese occupation of Malaya and Singapore have portrayed women as war victims, as heroines and as participants in the resistance, but Japanese attitudes towards Malayan women have received little attention. Drawing on archival records, memoirs, newspapers and oral history interviews, this article shows that women, whether they resisted Japanese rule or acquiesced in it, enthusiastically embraced change during the period of war and occupation. Through officially sponsored community organizations, Japanese and local leaders implanted ambitions that led to broader participation of women in Malayan society and politics after the war.

## Summary

Mahani Musa's article examines the relationship between Japanese occupation authorities and Malayan women across ethnic lines, moving beyond the dominant narrative of women as passive victims of wartime brutality. Drawing on archival records from the Johor Religious Office, wartime newspapers (*Syonan Shimbun*, *Semangat Asia*, *Fajar Asia*), memoirs, and oral history interviews from the National Archives of Singapore and Malaysia, the article traces how Japanese propaganda and administrative programmes actively sought to reshape women's social roles, economic participation, and political consciousness during 1942–45. The central argument is that women—whether they resisted or acquiesced to Japanese rule—used the occupation as a catalyst for broader social transformation, and that the Japanese, through officially sponsored community organizations and labour mobilization, inadvertently implanted ambitions that led to greater female participation in Malayan society and politics after the war.

The article addresses a historiographical gap: while women's suffering (rape, torture, comfort women) is well documented in memoirs and oral histories, Japanese attitudes towards Malayan women and the mechanisms by which they were mobilized have received comparatively little scholarly attention. Musa shows that the Japanese treated different ethnic groups differently—Malay women were encouraged into associations and anti-colonial rhetoric, Chinese women were channeled into economic projects like the New Syonan agricultural settlements, and Indian women were mobilized for the Indian independence cause through the Rani of Jhansi Regiment. The article also highlights how pre-existing movements toward women's education and social freedom, which had been stalling before the war, were accelerated by the occupation's disruption of traditional gender roles.

A significant portion of the analysis is devoted to the anti-Japanese movement, where Chinese women played a substantial role in the Malayan Communist Party and the MPAJA, often with the tacit approval of parents who saw guerrilla affiliation as a form of personal protection. Musa argues that the wartime experience intensified a transformation already underway, producing women who emerged from the occupation "emancipated and newly confident."

### Key Findings

- The 1931 census recorded 1,787,323 women in British Malaya's total population of 4,385,346, making them a significant demographic target for Japanese propaganda and mobilization (p. 102).
- The New Syonan project, launched in 1943, aimed to relocate 300,000 Singapore residents (40% of the island's population) to rural agricultural settlements at Endau (Johor) and Bahau (Negeri Sembilan); by the end of 1944, 12,000 settlers had joined, with each household receiving two acres for vegetables and one acre for wet rice (pp. 109–110).
- The Syonan Women's Employment Bureau, established in April 1944, had allocated 370 women to jobs as clerks, typists, nurses, telephone operators, waitresses, domestic servants, and factory workers within three months, with a further 96 placed through informal channels (p. 112).
- The Malay Women's Association was formed on 17 March 1944 with Marquis Tokugawa as patron; the women's section of the Oversea Chinese Association was also formed in March 1944, and the Women's Service Corps (WSC) was established in February 1945 to liaise with the Auxiliary Police (pp. 116–117).
- By the end of 1942, Indian Independence League membership in Malaya stood at approximately 120,000 and Indian National Army membership at 16,000; the Rani of Jhansi Regiment, the INA's women's section, was organized by Captain Lakshmi Swaminathan with a training camp opened in October 1943 (pp. 118–119).
- Johor religious records show a marked increase in divorce petitions filed by women aged 14–30 during 1942, with some women left unsupported by their husbands for as long as three years; the Muar kadi proposed that husbands pay three times the dowry for unacceptable divorce (pp. 106–107).

### Conclusion

Musa concludes that while the early occupation was marked by fear of Japanese brutality across all ethnic groups, the Japanese propaganda machinery gradually succeeded in erasing that fear and mobilizing women into the war effort. Regardless of ethnicity, Malayan women emerged from the war "emancipated and newly confident," and the experience gained through involvement in war efforts, social work, and associations intensified a transformation of women's roles that had already been taking place before the war.

## Context

- Primary archival collections include the Johor Religious Office and Office of the Chief Kadi (Johor Archives), the *Syonan Shimbun* and *Semangat Asia* newspapers, and oral history interviews held at the National Archives of Singapore and the National Archives of Malaysia.
- The article contributes to the historiography of gender and the Japanese occupation by shifting focus from women as victims to women as active agents whose wartime experiences—under both Japanese-sponsored and anti-Japanese frameworks—shaped post-war political participation in Malaya.
