# Gold mines and artefacts

Gold mines and artefacts constitute a significant body of archaeological, economic, and cultural evidence across the Malay Peninsula and Borneo, spanning from the early centuries of the Christian era to the mid-twentieth century. The region's gold deposits, concentrated in the alluvial and lode systems of the Bukit Barisan hinterland of Sumatra, the south-west corner of Borneo, and the auriferous uplifts of the Malayan peninsula, attracted successive waves of indigenous, Chinese, and foreign mining activity over many centuries [[1, pp. 108–110]](#ref-1), [[2, pp. 33–47]](#ref-2). The material record—comprising worked gold objects, burial amulets, inscribed rings, and mining settlements—reveals a complex pattern of cultural contact in which Indian, Javanese, Chinese, and indigenous influences intersected, often in ways that resist simple attribution [[2, pp. 62–71]](#ref-2), [[3, pp. 70–77]](#ref-3).

### Scope and Historical Definition

The term "gold mines and artefacts" in the context of the Malay world encompasses both the physical workings of alluvial and lode gold deposits and the manufactured objects derived from the metal. In West Borneo, Tom Harrisson's 1949 survey traced approximately 115 recorded gold objects (excluding the Sambas hoard), of which about 60 were examined in original form, 16 in replica, and 40 known only by written reference with no surviving specimen [[2, p. 48]](#ref-2). The objects range from simple beads and "bead rings" to elaborate gem-set signet rings, chains, ear and nose ornaments, coat fasteners, and a gold bar or ingot fragment [[2, pp. 53–62]](#ref-2). In the Malay Peninsula, L.C. Isnard's 1890 geological note documented gold-bearing workings at Gemencheh in Negri Sembilan, where Malay miners had sunk 150-foot shafts without tools or pumps, and at Ulu Gedoh, where a syndicate's shallow excavations showed increasing gold richness with depth [[4, pp. 169–170]](#ref-4). Isnard traced a single gold-bearing geological uplift running from the Malacca colony through Gemencheh, the Muar and Serting river valleys, and into Pahang, arguing that the peninsula's gold deposits spanned three states rather than being confined to one [[4, p. 168]](#ref-4).

In Sumatra, the Kota Cina site near Medan, active from the twelfth to the mid-fourteenth century, yielded eight gold fragments from two excavation contexts: a dwelling area with post holes and domestic debris, and a brick complex comprising a roughly square structure (12.5 × 12.5 m), a smaller rectangular building, and a truncated pyramidal hollow shell interpreted as a possible shrine or stupa where the gold may have been deposited as a votive offering [[1, pp. 105–106]](#ref-1). The site's cosmopolitan character is attested by imported Chinese ceramics, coins, jade, Buddha statues of the Cola school, Sri Lankan coins, and Persian Gulf ceramics [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1).

### Economic and Mining Dynamics

The scale of gold production in the region, as recorded in the historical sources, varied enormously by period and locality. Marsden recorded 1,200 gold mining operations in the Minangkabau area alone in the late eighteenth century and estimated annual production of 10,000–12,000 ounces at Padang, of which about half was carried to the eastern side of Sumatra [[1, p. 109]](#ref-1). In West Borneo, Crawfurd (1820) reported 32,000 men and 4,000 women working 13 large and 57 small mines at Montrado, with an estimated total Chinese population of 200,000 in the area; Raffles placed the Montrado–Sambas annual yield at 250,000 ounces in 1812 [[2, pp. 34–35]](#ref-2). The Borneo Company's highest recorded yield at Bau was 68,108 fine ounces in 1907; by 1949, over one million ounces (valued at over $35 million) had been exported from the Bau field, and Lucas estimated over $5,000,000 worth of extractable gold remained in the old tailings alone [[2, p. 44]](#ref-2).

The Chinese dominated organised gold mining in Borneo from the late eighteenth century onward. By about 1760, the "Prince of Mampawah" in the south-west had granted gold concessions to Chinese workers, and "soon after they obtained working rights in Sambas" [[2, p. 33]](#ref-2). The long-time centre of Montrado became the hub of a highly effective Chinese organisation radiating outward; Earl's 1833 visit described an artificial lake about two miles in circumference for gold-working use, and a street three-quarters of a mile long leading to the house of the Chinese "governor" [[2, p. 34]](#ref-2). The Dutch–Chinese antagonism that followed reached grave proportions, culminating in punitive expeditions from the 1820s and the final subjugation of the Chinese in 1854, which Posewitz described as "the ruin of the once flourishing gold-mining industry" [[2, p. 35]](#ref-2).

In Kelantan, the Hakka Chinese gold mining settlement of Pulai in Ulu Kelantan provides a contrasting case of a smaller, more isolated community. By 1902, Waterstradt recorded a Pulai population of only 200, many of whom had already switched to rice farming; by Middlebrook's 1933 visit, the population had recovered to between 700 and 800 Hakka-speaking Chinese [[5, pp. 53–54]](#ref-5). The massacre of Pulai miners under Sultan Muhammad I, probably around 1825, was triggered when the miners killed the Temangong (the Sultan's son) who had enforced a rice monopoly and blocked supply boats up the river; Graham reported that "the river ran red with blood" and the gold mining industry of Kelantan "came to a sudden end" [[5, p. 52]](#ref-5). Sharon Carstens' 1980 study, based on a year of residence in Pulai, argued that the community's survival as a distinct, isolated settlement was determined by its transition from gold mining to subsistence rice farming and the resulting self-sufficiency in food and marriage partners [[5, pp. 50–67]](#ref-5).

### Artefacts and Cultural Significance

The gold artefacts recovered from the region encode significant cultural information beyond their material value. At Kota Cina, sample 1, a piece of gold foil stamped with two Chinese characters, one of which means "gold" (金), implies that the foil was prepared or at least assayed by Chinese craftsmen, raising important questions about the scale of Chinese participation in the Southeast Asian gold trade during the twelfth to fourteenth centuries [[1, p. 107]](#ref-1). The analytical results consistently show very low copper content (all below 1%), which the authors interpret as diagnostic of alluvial gold rather than deliberately alloyed metal; most samples yield total metal percentages near 100%, suggesting the alluvial gold had been refined by smelting to remove silica inclusions [[1, pp. 111–112]](#ref-1).

In West Borneo, the Limbang hoard—comprising 8 rings, 2 chains, 4 ear or nose ornaments, 2–3 coat fasteners, a gold bar or buckle, gold beads, and a fragment of a Persian coin struck in 960 A.D.—represents the most important find apart from the Sambas hoard [[2, pp. 52–53]](#ref-2). The inscribed Limbang ring bears Nagari script dated by Sastri to the 12th–14th century and by Barnett and Coedes to the 14th–15th century; the reading "Araksara" is interpreted as a personal name or office designation [[2, pp. 57–58]](#ref-2). The collared gem ring from Limbang is "very nearly identical" to a ring in the Franks Collection (British Museum nos. 2441–4), listed as "Java" with no date, and the Limbang fish ring closely resembles the goose ring from the Fort Canning hoard, whose bracelets are attributed to "the Majapahit period at its best, i.e. the middle of the 14th Century" [[2, pp. 66–67]](#ref-2).

A particularly significant category of gold artefact is the burial amulet. O'Connor and Harrisson (1971) presented photographic and analytical evidence for a shared mortuary convention across maritime Southeast Asia: the placement of thin, leaf-shaped gold foil over the facial orifices of the dead. The Gilimanuk eye-cover (Bali), excavated by R.P. Soejono in November 1964, dates to approximately 100 B.C.–100 A.D. and is made of silver with a thin gold facing; it was found attached to skeleton No. LX in sector No. XVIII of a bronze-age settlement containing about 80 skeletons [[3, p. 73]](#ref-3). At Santubong, Sarawak, eleven leaf-shaped gold foil pieces with median breach-cuts were recovered from the Jaong site (late T'ang–early Sung period); they range from 52 mm to 20 mm in length, three are elliptical and eight have pointed ends, and three show additional perpendicular cuts [[3, p. 73]](#ref-3). The Oton, Iloilo (Panay) eye covering, dated to the 14th or early 15th century, is decorated with repousse dots and is more elaborate and literal in its representation of the eye than the Sarawak pieces [[3, p. 72]](#ref-3). The authors ultimately decline to resolve the question of whether the practice is of indigenous or externally derived origin, suggesting instead a broadly shared monsoonal Asian context [[3, pp. 74–75]](#ref-3).

The golden keris handle from Balingian, Sarawak, examined by Harrisson in 1966, represents a different dimension of gold's cultural significance. Weighing 34 grams of pure beaten gold and standing 92 mm tall, the handle depicts a fanged, bearded figure holding a human skull, with a fleur-de-lys phallic motif and elaborate pleated hair [[6, p. 177]](#ref-6). Claire Holt of Cornell University identified the figure as a fanged demon associated with Tantric Bhairavist cults in vogue during the reign of Kirtuma-Gura, the last king of Singhasari, murdered around 1292; Wendy Sorensen of the Asia Society, New York, placed the piece in the 13th century or possibly the 10th or late 9th century, and attributed it to the Kingdom of Madura or Kediri [[6, pp. 175–176]](#ref-6). The associated Melanau folklore describes a widower confined for seven days in a solitary enclosure following his wife's death—a custom Harrisson links to the broader Bornean tradition of mourning segregation—and in each story, a fairy figure instructs the widower to escape into the jungle and kill a stranger; upon striking the victim with his spear, the man finds the golden statue at its tip [[6, pp. 176–177]](#ref-6). The handle was worn suspended around the neck by important Melanau shamans in healing ceremonies; it was surrendered to the museum because no shaman was considered sufficiently powerful in the ancient tradition to continue using it [[6, p. 178]](#ref-6).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on gold mines and artefacts reveals a progression from brief geological and administrative notes to comprehensive synthetic surveys. The earliest contribution in the records is Isnard's 1890 geological note on Gemencheh, a short piece by a French civil mining engineer describing the gold-bearing geology of the district and its place within a larger auriferous uplift [[4]](#ref-4). This was followed by Harrisson's 1949 monograph-length survey, which synthesised approximately 115 recorded gold objects, numerous stone and ceramic artefacts, and roughly 50 golden legends to argue that West Borneo was a major early gold-producing region whose material record reveals a complex pattern of Indian and Javanese cultural contact [[2]](#ref-2). Harrisson's work was explicitly prompted by the find of objects now in the possession of Mr Y. S. Tan in Singapore, believed to be from near Sambas, and it corrected the prevailing assumption—exemplified by Coomaraswamy's 1947 survey, which confined itself exclusively to East Borneo—that the western half of the island was archaeologically insignificant [[2, pp. 33]](#ref-2).

The 1960s and 1970s saw a shift toward focused studies of individual objects and sites. Harrisson's 1966 article on the Balingian keris handle drew on specialist consultation in the United States and unpublished Melanau folklore to position a single object within a broader framework of Tantric Buddhist cultural transmission [[6]](#ref-6). O'Connor and Harrisson's 1971 article on gold-foil burial amulets addressed a conspicuous gap in the archaeological literature—the near-total absence of systematic discussion of this practice in Southeast Asia—and provided the photographic documentation and wider comparative context that their earlier 1970 data paper could not [[3]](#ref-3). Treloar's 1968 note on the chemical analysis of metal objects from Chandi Bukit Batu Pahat in Kedah represented an early application of compositional analysis to suggest origin and date for objects from northern Peninsular Malaysia [[7]](#ref-7).

The 1980 volume of the journal marked a further diversification. Edwards, Manning, and Treloar's 1980 study of the Kota Cina gold fragments represented one of the earliest applications of atomic absorption spectroscopy to Southeast Asian archaeological gold, and it challenged the prevailing view (articulated by Harrison and O'Connor) that Sumatra was "not rich in gold" compared to Borneo, arguing instead that transinsular portages from the Bukit Barisan hinterland made the east coast a viable export route [[1, pp. 108–110]](#ref-1). Carstens' 1980 article on Pulai contributed to the study of overseas Chinese communities by documenting a case that defied the typical pattern of acculturation observed in Trengganu and northern Kelantan, and by demonstrating the value of oral history as a complement to the sparse written record for understanding the social and cultural life of isolated Chinese mining settlements [[5]](#ref-5).

A recurring historiographical tension runs through these studies: the question of whether gold objects found in a given locality were made there or imported. Harrisson was particularly insistent on this point, arguing that "the finding of a certain object at a certain point" does not automatically provide a system of dating the presence of whatever culture is thereby indicated, and that "culturally, things are often much more mobile than people" [[2, pp. 62–63]](#ref-2). This caution against over-interpretation of single finds, combined with the acknowledged poverty of the Borneo archaeological record—Basil Gray's 1949 observation that "our archaeological material from Borneo is practically non-existent" remains apt—continues to shape the interpretive framework within which new discoveries are assessed [[2, p. 34]](#ref-2).
