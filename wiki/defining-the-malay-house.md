# Defining the Malay house

Roger N. Hilton's "Defining the Malay House" (1992), drawing on his residence in the Malay Peninsula from 1948 to 1963 and a synthesis of architectural scholarship spanning four decades, argues that the Malay house constitutes a sophisticated modular architecture whose defining structural characteristics can serve as an ethnographic tool for identifying "Malayness" with the same authority as language or religion. The paper establishes a typological definition grounded in the incontrovertibly Malay areas of the Peninsula and then tests it against house forms across the Indo-Pacific, from Japan to Madagascar.

## Summary

Hilton's central analytical move is to separate the *type* of the Malay house from its regional *styles*. He demonstrates that all Peninsula Malay houses, regardless of state or dialect group, reduce to a modular plan based on a minimum of nine, twelve, or sixteen foundation points, with the sixteen-point plan offering the greatest scope for variation while remaining analytically tractable (pp. 41–44). The construction is post-and-lintel throughout: strong corner posts connected by horizontal beams that carry the floors and form lintels, with walls entirely non-load-bearing. The joints are wedged mortises, not nails or lashings, which imposes precise dimensions on every member and permits the entire frame to be dismantled and re-erected elsewhere (pp. 45–47). A critical distinction Hilton insists upon is that the posts rest on masonry foundation stones — making them stilts, not piles — a feature he argues has been systematically ignored in the literature, including by Nguyen Van Huyen's influential 1934 monograph (p. 46).

The paper then surveys regional variation across four Peninsula zones — North West, Central West, East Coast, and Southern — using the eleven full-scale houses at Mini Malaysia, Air Keroh, as a primary reference collection (pp. 52–57). Despite the region's extraordinary immigrant diversity, including Minangkabau, Javanese, Bugis, and Banjarese settlers, the house type remains remarkably unified; migrants adopt the local Malay form rather than importing their homeland styles (pp. 53, 57). Hilton extends the analysis beyond the Peninsula to the Acehnese, Gayo, Batak, Minangkabau, Javanese, Iban, Bugis, Toraja, Cambodian, Thai, Burmese, and Japanese houses, applying his definition as a diagnostic test (pp. 58–65).

The evolutionary argument culminates in a proposal that the Malay house derives from heavily built "megalithic" ancestors — specifically the Batak and Minangkabau houses of Sumatra — which in turn trace to the Dong Son piled dwelling of North Vietnam. The sway-backed roof, found in Toba Batak, Toraja, and certain Negeri Sembilan houses, is identified as the key linking feature, carrying a shared folklore of ancestral arrival by boat (pp. 47–48, 60, 66). The Japanese house, while non-Malay in every cultural respect, shares the modular post-and-lintel logic and posts-on-stones construction, suggesting a common Dong Son ancestry that diffused eastward to Japan and southward through the archipelago (pp. 64–65).

### Key Findings

- The fundamental plan of the Malay house is reducible to modules of 9, 12, or 16 posts; the 16-point plan (8 tall, 8 lower) is the most versatile and is used as the primary analytical model throughout the paper (pp. 41–44, 67).
- Posts stand on masonry foundation stones (stilts), not sunk into the ground (piles); this distinction is the single most reliable initial test for "Malayness" and has been overlooked in prior scholarship, including Nguyen Van Huyen (1934) (p. 46).
- Standard post size is 5 inches dressed all round (125 mm), with a standard span of 9 feet (2.70 m) between post centres, dimensions established by trial and error over centuries; deviation produces structural failure, as Hilton witnessed in a 4-inch Negeri Sembilan house that "shook frighteningly" (p. 45).
- The wedged mortise joint system makes the house fully dismantlable and transportable; in Northern Malaya whole houses are still moved in this manner, while in the South the roof and cladding are removed and the frame knocked apart (p. 47).
- A Burmese Buddhist house at Nyaung U near Pagan, encountered by Hilton in 1963, conformed in every respect to the Malay definition, including posts on stones, demonstrating that the architectural type can exist independently of Malay language or Islamic religion (p. 64).
- The Javanese house, despite sharing a basic 16-point plan and mortised construction, is classified as non-Malay because its short piles deny the utilisable under-floor space that is essential to the Malay type (p. 60).

### Conclusion

Hilton's definitive takeaway is that architecture should be recognised as an ethnographic tool of equal standing to language and religion in defining Malay identity. Because groups change their architecture more slowly than their speech, house type can identify Malay affiliation in cases where linguistic or religious criteria are ambiguous or contested — for instance, the Acehnese, Bugis, and Minangkabau, who would be classified as Malay by house type but not by language (p. 67). The Malay house is thus not merely a dwelling but a cultural technology of extraordinary sophistication: a modular, prefabricated, transportable system that anticipated modern architectural principles by centuries and whose unity across the Peninsula testifies to the assimilative power of Malay culture.

## Context

- The Mini Malaysia collection at Air Keroh, Melaka, constructed as a tourist development by the Malacca State Development Corporation (1987), provided Hilton with an unrivalled three-dimensional reference of eleven Peninsula house styles plus Iban and Dusun examples, each representing a comparatively well-to-do villager's dwelling (p. 40).
- The article builds directly on Hilton's own earlier 1956 paper "The Basic Malay House" (JMBRAS 29:3) and incorporates subsequent studies by Sheppard (1969), Wardi (1981), Dall (1982), Seow (1983), Lim (1987), and Gibbs (1987), positioning itself as a synthetic and comparative advance over these individual regional treatments (p. 40).
