# Defining the Malay house

Dr. R. Hanitsch published this documentary article in 1913, presenting four letters by Nathaniel Wallich that trace the brief establishment of Botanical Gardens in Singapore in 1822. The piece reconstructs, from primary correspondence held in Calcutta, how a visiting botanist's informal recommendation to Stamford Raffles led to the creation of a 48-acre experimental garden on Fort Canning Hill — an institution that lasted only seven years before being discontinued in 1829.

## Summary

The article is fundamentally a source publication: Hanitsch transcribes four letters from the Library of the Royal Botanical Gardens, Calcutta, with editorial commentary that situates them within the early history of Singapore. The first letter (19 July 1822) is Wallich's application for six months' leave to travel to China for his health, in which he requests permission to bring two apprentices and the garden's head overseer, George Porter, on the voyage. The second is a joint committee report (23 October 1822) by Wallich, Dr. James Lumsdaine, and Captain Francis Salmond, appointed by Raffles to assess the suitability of the southern bank of the Singapore River for warehouse construction — a document that reveals the settlement's still-raw topography and the practical engineering challenges of its first years.

The third letter is the substantive core of the article: Wallich's formal recommendation to Raffles for the establishment of a Botanic and Experimental Garden on the island. Wallich argues from both scientific and commercial premises, citing the island's unmatched botanical diversity, the success of clove and nutmeg trials already undertaken by Lieutenant Colonel Farquhar, and the potential for cultivating teak, mahogany, pepper, gambier, cotton, coffee, and tea. He proposes that the garden's costs could be self-sustaining through spice cultivation and requests that the Supreme Government supply apprentices and gardeners from Calcutta. The fourth letter, a reply to Raffles, provides the site plan: an oblong tract of approximately 48 acres (144 Bengal biggahs) on the eastern slope of Fort Canning Hill, bounded by what is now Selegie Road, with a monthly budget of 60 dollars for ten labourers and one overseer.

Hanitsch's editorial commentary maps the historical geography onto the modern city, identifying the "small rivulet" as the Stamford Road Canal, "Bukit Salegy" as Mount Emily, and the broader garden area as encompassing the old cemetery, the present Raffles Museum site, and surrounding roads. He notes that the gardens were discontinued after 30 June 1829, likely for reasons of economy, leaving Singapore without a botanical institution for thirty years until the Tanglin Gardens were established in 1860.

### Key Findings

- Wallich arrived in Singapore towards the end of August 1822, having originally intended only a six-month health leave to China, and returned to Calcutta soon after 21 November 1822 (pp. 39–40).
- The proposed garden site comprised approximately 48 acres (144 Bengal biggahs) on the eastern slope of Fort Canning Hill, bounded by Selegie Road, a cantonment road, and the road around Singapore Hill (p. 47).
- The monthly operating budget was set at 60 dollars, allocated entirely to the support of ten labourers and one overseer (p. 48).
- The committee report on the southern river bank (23 October 1822) confirmed the site was "fully practicable" for warehouses, noting the concave bank received the greatest tidal force and was therefore better suited for mercantile purposes than the northern side (p. 42).
- Wallich identified five distinct wild species of nutmeg in the immediate vicinity of the Cantonments alone, and reported that clove and nutmeg trials by Lieutenant Colonel Farquhar had already proven successful (p. 45).
- The gardens were discontinued after 30 June 1829; Singapore had no botanical gardens until the Tanglin establishment in 1860 (p. 48).

### Conclusion

Hanitsch presents the episode as a brief but significant chapter in Singapore's institutional history: a garden conceived in the settlement's fourth year, proposed by a visiting scientist, funded at a modest level, and abandoned within seven years — likely due to cost overruns against the original $60 monthly vote. The article's implicit argument is that this early experiment, though short-lived, established the precedent and geographic memory for the world-famous Tanglin Gardens that followed three decades later.

## Context

- The primary sources are drawn from the Library of the Royal Botanical Gardens, Calcutta, copied at the request of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society with the permission of Major A. T. Gage, I.M.S., Superintendent (p. 39).
- Hanitsch writes as a contemporary archivist-historian with direct access to the Calcutta collection; his commentary reflects the administrative and topographical knowledge of early twentieth-century Singapore, including the recent reclamation of Mount Wallich into the Teluk Ayer area (p. 39).
