# Shamanism in Perak

W.E. Maxwell, a British colonial official in Perak, published this first-hand ethnographic account in 1883, documenting a shamanic healing ceremony he witnessed in a Malay village. The article's central concern is the persistence of pre-Islamic animist belief and practice among professedly Muslim Malays in the Perak interior, where disease is routinely attributed to supernatural agency and professional *pawangs* (medicine-men) are summoned in preference to, or alongside, any form of medicine.

## Summary

Maxwell opens by situating Malay shamanism within a broader framework of medical and religious belief. He notes that while Arabic medical texts translated into Malay contain learned dissections of the body "of the age of Galen and Aristotle," the popular explanation for unexplained illness remains demoniacal possession—a relic of the spirit-worship of the Malays' "primitive ancestors" (p. 222). He distinguishes between the more Semitic-influenced demonology of coastal and European-settled areas, where spirits are *sheitan* or *jin* and Arabic formulae serve as charms, and the "downright heathenism" of inland *kampongs*, where demons possess distinct personalities and must be countered by other demons or propitiated by bloody sacrifice (pp. 222–223). He also identifies traces of Hindu-Brahminical influence in the chants of *pawangs*, suggesting a historical layering of belief systems.

The bulk of the article is a detailed narrative of a single ceremony Maxwell attended in a Perak village for a young married woman whose first baby was only days old and who lay insensible—what Maxwell himself suspects were "paroxysms of puerperal fever" (p. 224). Two *pawangs* take turns: Che Johan, a specialist in tiger-spirits (*hantu bian*), who is possessed by "Bujang Gelap" (the dark dragon) and speaks in a feigned voice laced with Sakai aboriginal words; and an older man who invokes the spirits of the Sungkei river. The first diagnoses the affliction as a "dumb *langsuyar*" (a banshee-type spirit), a finding the assembled villagers accept because the patient had been silent for two days. The second *pawang* proves a failure, being possessed successively by four named Sungkei spirits without producing any useful diagnosis (pp. 226–228).

When both *pawangs* have exhausted their efforts, a *balei berpusing*—a "revolving hall"—is constructed: a miniature model of a Perak mosque mounted on a pivot, fringed with coconut-leaf streamers called *jari lipan* ("centipede's legs"), and filled with propitiatory offerings of four kinds (*lemak, manis, masam, pedas*). The *pawangs* wave the demons into the model, which is then carried to the river and set adrift (pp. 229–231). Maxwell closes with the patient's death within twenty-four hours, a quiet but pointed acknowledgment that the elaborate ritual machinery had failed to save her.

### Key Findings

- The patient was a young married woman, her first baby only a few days old, who had been insensible for two days; Maxwell identifies the probable cause as puerperal fever (p. 224).
- The *pawang* Che Johan was possessed by "Bujang Gelap" (the dark dragon), a tiger-spirit, and spoke in a feigned voice incorporating Sakai aboriginal words and phrases unintelligible to most Malays present (p. 226).
- The *bidu* (chanting woman, often the *pawang*'s wife) performed a sustained vocal performance at the top of her voice without apparent fatigue, demonstrating "marvellous memory" for very long invocations (p. 227).
- The *balei berpusing* was a miniature Perak mosque on a single bamboo pivot, fringed with *jari lipan* (coconut-leaf streamers), and filled with a sacrificed fowl (blood, feathers, entrails, and joints) plus sweet offerings of *wajil, dodul, tebu,* and *pisang* (pp. 230–231).
- The Sungkei-river *pawang* was possessed successively by four named spirits—"Panglima Raja," "Anak Janggi," "Hulubalang Raja," and "Mambang Dundang"—yet was unable to declare anything useful (p. 228).
- The patient died within twenty-four hours after the *balei berpusing* was cast adrift on the Perak river, despite the efforts of additional *pawangs* retained the following night (p. 232).

### Conclusion

Maxwell's definitive takeaway is implicitly critical: the elaborate ritual apparatus of Perak shamanism, for all its internal coherence and the genuine conviction it commands among participants, is powerless against what is in fact a medical emergency. His closing line—that the patient "never recovered consciousness and died within four and twenty hours"—serves as an unadorned verdict on the efficacy of the practice, delivered without overt polemic but with the quiet authority of a witness who has seen the whole sequence from beginning to fatal end.

## Context

- Maxwell was a British colonial official in Perak during the 1880s, writing from the position of an administrative observer with direct access to Malay domestic life. His account is framed by a colonial ethnographic sensibility that treats the subject as a curiosity to be documented and, implicitly, as a survival to be superseded.
- The article draws on no archival collection; it is a single first-hand observation supplemented by comparative references to Ceylonese and Laotian practices (citing the *Mahawangso* and a French account of Laotian medicine). Its historiographical value lies in providing one of the earliest detailed Western descriptions of a Perak *pawang* ceremony, including the *balei berpusing* rite, which is rarely documented in the colonial record.
