# Isabella Bird’s visit to Malaya: a centenary tribute

J.M. Gullick's 1979 centenary tribute examines Isabella Bird's five-week visit to the Malay States in early 1879, using the original letters she wrote to her sister Henrietta (preserved by her publisher John Murray) to illuminate the composition of *The Golden Chersonese* (1883) and to recover the franker, more politically charged observations that Bird suppressed in the published text. The article is both a character study of Bird as a traveller and a source-critical exercise in comparing manuscript to print.

## Summary

Gullick situates Bird's Malayan journey within the broader arc of her restless, unconventional life: a clergyman's daughter educated at home in close observation, frustrated by mid-Victorian domesticity, who undertook successive long journeys to Australia, Hawaii, the American West, Japan, and Iran. Her 1879 trip to Malaya was an impromptu detour, suggested by Cecil Clementi Smith in Singapore, and she accepted within five minutes. The route took her through Malacca, Sungei Ujong, Klang, Penang, and into Perak, where she spent the most substantial time with the Resident Hugh Low at Kuala Kangsar. Gullick argues that the Perak portion of the journey was the one in which Bird genuinely "escaped from civilisation," moving without European company and even visiting Kota Lama — still regarded as too dangerous for a European — before Low's return.

The article's distinctive contribution is its close comparison of the surviving original letters with the published text of *The Golden Chersonese*. Gullick demonstrates that Bird was a practised editor of her own first impressions: she rearranged, re-drafted, and in several important cases omitted material that was too frank about her hosts or too politically sensitive to publish. The Selangor letters were lost entirely because they contained severe criticism of the Resident Bloomfield Douglas. The Perak letters, by contrast, survive in complete form (29 sheets, each of four sides), and Gullick reproduces key passages showing what was cut — including Low's private assessment that Raja Dris had been suspected of abetting Birch's murder, and his prediction that Douglas "would be assassinated before he had been a week in Perak."

Gullick also recovers minor personal details omitted from the book, such as Bird's near-starvation at Maxwell's austere household in Taiping, and her unflattering private characterisations of James Innes and Paul Swinburne. The article closes by noting that the Malayan experience contributed to what her biographer Anna Stoddart called "immense intellectual and spiritual increase," and that in this sense Malaya "paid its debt" to Bird for the book.

### Key Findings

- Bird arrived in Singapore on 18 January 1879 at age 48, standing 4 feet 11½ inches, and was persuaded by Colonial Secretary Cecil Clementi Smith to extend her journey into the Malay States (p. 113).
- During an elephant ride between Taiping and Kuala Kangsar she identified 126 trees and shrubs, 53 trailers, 17 epiphytes, and 28 ferns (p. 114).
- Hugh Low, aged 54 with 30 years' experience in the East, worked 14 hours out of 24, lived in a two-roomed house, and spoke Malay "like a native"; he was Pope-Hennessy's father and a long widower (p. 113, Sheet 17, p. 2).
- Approximately two thousand Malays had migrated from Selangor into Perak to escape Bloomfield Douglas's "reign of terror" (Sheet 17, p. 2).
- The Selangor letters did not survive because they contained very harsh comments on Douglas; the Sungei Ujong letter (describing the visit with the Misses Shaw) is also missing (p. 116).
- The Perak letter comprises 29 complete sheets (each folded to four sides); a footnote in the 1883 edition claiming a missing page describing Low's apes is incorrect (p. 117).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that *The Golden Chersonese* is best understood not as a straightforward transcription of Bird's letters but as a carefully edited literary product in which the author suppressed politically dangerous and socially unflattering material. The surviving original letters, particularly the Perak correspondence, reveal a sharper and more politically engaged observer than the published text allows, and it is this gap between manuscript and print that constitutes the article's principal historiographical value.

## Context

- The primary archival source is the collection of Bird's original letters to Henrietta held by John Murray (the publisher), which Gullick was permitted to re-read and photocopy (p. 119).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its source-critical method of comparing manuscript letters against the published text, a technique that recovers suppressed political commentary on the Perak and Selangor administrations of the 1870s.
