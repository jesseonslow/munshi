# The old cemetery on Fort Canning, Singapore

H.A. Stallwood's 1912 register of the Old Cemetery on Fort Canning, Singapore, was compiled at Government order following C.B. Buckley's suggestion in his *Anecdotal History of Singapore* that a new record be made after the loss of the original. The work documents the interments of roughly 280 individuals buried between 1822 and 1868, offering a prosopographical snapshot of the colonial Settlement's earliest European, Eurasian, and Chinese Christian communities.

## Summary

The cemetery, situated on the slope of Fort Canning Hill, was opened in 1822 to replace the first Christian cemetery near the present flagstaff site, and was closed in 1865 when the Bukit Timah Road cemetery opened. The ground was divided by a wall: the southern half for Anglicans (Section A) and the northern half for other Christian denominations (Sections B, C, and D). Stallwood's register records names, descriptions, birth and death dates, and ages where legible, drawing on inscriptions in English, Portuguese, and Chinese. The Chinese inscriptions were deciphered with the assistance of the Hon'ble C.J. Saunders, Secretary for Chinese Affairs, and his clerk Yung Sz Meng, who also supplied notes on calendrical conversion difficulties for pre-1834 dates (pp. 73–74).

The interments reveal a community shaped by the hazards of colonial service and tropical climate. Many Civil Service members died young, and the United Services contributed a significant share of names, including naval officers of HMS *Niger*, HMS *Spartan*, and HMS *Spiteful*. Notable burials include Captain William Scott, Harbour Master and cousin of Sir Walter Scott; Sir Joze D'Almeida, Portuguese Consul-General knighted in 1842; and George Dougald Coleman, Superintendent of Public Works responsible for laying out Singapore's main roads (p. 73). The Chinese Christian burials in Section B are predominantly from the Teochiu community, with Christian names of Portuguese origin reflecting the Catholic mission's influence.

Stallwood notes that the tombs are mostly brickwork plastered over, with no architectural pretension, and that many had fallen into disrepair by 1912. He explicitly appeals to descendants to assist in repairing the ruined monuments, framing the register as both a historical record and a practical instrument for preservation (p. 75).

### Key Findings

- The oldest tomb is dated 1821 (John C. Collingwood, ship *Susan*, aged 30), presumably transferred from the earlier cemetery; the latest is dated 1868 (Marie Dominica Scott, aged 2), interred after the cemetery's official closure in 1865 (p. 72).
- The cemetery was divided into four sections: A (Anglican, south), B (other denominations, north), C, and D, with a wall marking the Anglican/non-Anglican boundary (p. 72).
- Section B contains the largest concentration of Chinese Christian burials, predominantly Teochiu, with Portuguese-derived Christian names (e.g., Pedro, Paulo, Joaquim, Francisco), indicating Roman Catholic missionary influence (pp. 74, 111–121).
- A monument near the southern gateway commemorates comrades of HMS *Niger* who fell in action, died of disease, or were drowned in the exercise of their duties (p. 73).
- No English-Chinese calendar was available for years before 1834, forcing reliance on the Chinese reckoning for early interments and creating ambiguity in some date assignments (p. 74).
- The register omits tombs with illegible or absent inscriptions, accounting for gaps in the consecutive numbering on the plan (p. 74).

### Conclusion

Stallwood's register stands as the sole surviving systematic record of the Fort Canning cemetery's interments, compiled at a moment when the physical monuments were already decaying and the original register had been lost. Its value lies not in argument but in preservation: it fixes the names, dates, and social identities of a founding generation of Singapore's colonial community—British civil servants, naval officers, Portuguese-Eurasian merchants, and Chinese converts—before their material traces could be entirely erased.

## Context

- Compiled under Government order and published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, the work reflects the early twentieth-century colonial impulse to document and preserve the material heritage of the Settlement's formative decades.
- The primary evidential basis is the physical tombstones themselves, supplemented by C.B. Buckley's *Anecdotal History of Singapore* and the Chinese-language notes supplied by the Secretary for Chinese Affairs.
