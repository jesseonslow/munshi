# The romance of Tok Janggut: a Kelantan folk hero

Cheah Boon Kheng's 1999 article, published in the John M. Gullick Festschrift of the JMBRAS, examines the Kelantanese folk legend of Tok Janggut — the 1915 rebel who defied both the Sultan of Kelantan and the British colonial administration — and argues that the legend functions as a subversive text through which ordinary Kelantanese people injected anti-royalty and lese-majesty elements to challenge the official palace and British narratives of the rebellion. The article traces the legend's textual history across multiple published versions, revealing how political pressure from the Kelantan palace shaped what could and could not be written about the event.

## Summary

The article's central concern is the gap between official British and Malay palace accounts of the 1915 Tok Janggut rebellion and the richer, more sympathetic folk versions that circulated among Kelantanese communities. Cheah argues that the folk legend belongs to the tradition of Malay oral literature — the domain of the lower classes — in which rulers are depicted as greedy or wicked and the peasant hero comes fully into his own, in contrast to the court *hikayat* tradition that marginalizes the commoner. By framing Tok Janggut's rebellion as *derhaka* (treason against the ruler) and attributing part of the responsibility for the uprising to the Sultan's own failings, the Kelantanese people effectively defied the ruling authorities' version of events. The legend thus operates as a form of popular political commentary, sustained across generations.

Cheah traces the textual afterlife of the legend through a series of published works, each revealing the political constraints under which Kelantanese writers operated. Yahya Abdullah's 1955 book *Peperangan Tok Janggut, atau Balasan Derhaka* was the most daring early account, published just two years before Malayan independence, and it included episodes of lese-majesty involving Tok Janggut's father and the Sultan. A revised edition appeared in 1957 under a different compiler's name, with the anti-royalty passages excised and replaced by material on Tok Janggut's invulnerability cult. Subsequent versions by Ariffin bin Abdul Rashid (1960), Sa'ad Shukri Haji Muda (1962, revised 1971), and Tan Ban Teik (1982) each modified, garbled, or omitted the lese-majesty elements in different ways, while university scholars who consulted the same sources selectively used biographical details but dismissed the anti-royalty stories as incredible. The novelist S. Othman Kelantan's account of his own censorship experiences — a drama script banned by police, a novel never published — provides vivid evidence of the ongoing political sensitivity of the legend well into the 1990s.

The article also addresses the question of why Tok Janggut joined the rebellion in the first place. The folk legend implies a personal motive rooted in the murder of his elder brother by a *budak raja* (royal slave) of Sultan Senik Mulut Merah and the subsequent killing of his father, Panglima Monas, who had committed lese-majesty against the feudal lord of Jeram. This personal grievance narrative coexists with the official explanation of tax resistance, and Cheah shows how different versions shift the blame between the Sultan of Kelantan and the local feudal lord, creating a confusing but politically charged body of testimony.

### Key Findings

- Yahya Abdullah's *Peperangan Tok Janggut, atau Balasan Derhaka* was first published in 1955 by Muslim Printing Press, Kota Bharu, under the pen name 'Y. Abdullah Kelantan'; a revised edition appeared in 1957 under the name Haji Abdullah b. Amirah Seling Kelantan, published by Sinaran Brothers, Penang, with the first 17 pages of lese-majesty episodes deleted (pp. 51–52).
- British officials were aware of Tok Janggut's invulnerability cult: Colonial Secretary W. G. Maxwell noted in a confidential report that Tok Janggut 'had the reputation of being invulnerable,' and District Officer W. E. Pepys recorded that his body was carried to Kota Bharu because 'people in the capital would not have believed a mere report of his death' (pp. 50–51).
- The Sultan of Kelantan ordered Tok Janggut's corpse to be displayed publicly around Kota Bharu in a bullock-cart as a warning against rebellion; the body was raised three times, suspended head-down from a wooden bar, before being buried in Pasir Pekan (pp. 55, 59–61).
- In 1970, a 96-year-old man appeared in Kuantan claiming to be a fugitive freedom fighter named Mat Kelantan who had participated in Tok Janggut's struggle; the Kelantan palace promptly denounced him as a fraud (p. 58).
- S. Othman Kelantan's drama script *Awang Teleng*, scheduled for staging in Kuala Krai, was banned by police authorities because the programme synopsis mentioned the history of Tok Janggut; his novel *Tok Janggut* (completed 1957) was never published, and he instead wrote *Perwira* (1980) about a fictional follower to circumvent the political constraints (pp. 59–60).
- W. E. Pepys recorded that the rebellion added a new chronological marker to the lives of Pasir Puteh residents, who had previously dated events by the 'Great Wind' of c. 1880, and from 1915 onwards said 'At the time of Tok Janggut I was so and so' (p. 62).

### Conclusion

Cheah's definitive takeaway is that the Tok Janggut legend endures as a living, politically charged narrative in Kelantanese culture precisely because it encodes a popular critique of royal authority that the palace has consistently sought to suppress. Despite attempts by Malay-Islamicist commentators to tarnish Tok Janggut's hero status by emphasizing his personal vices (cock-fighting, violence), the legend remains robust: the PAS Mentri Besar of Kelantan, Dato Haji Nik Aziz, officially opened a 1996 seminar on Tok Janggut and hailed him as a freedom fighter against colonial rule, demonstrating that the folk hero's symbolic power transcends even the political factions that once opposed him.

## Context

- The article draws on a range of primary sources including Yahya Abdullah's 1955 and 1957 publications, Ariffin bin Abdul Rashid's 1960 *Peristiwa-peristiwa di Kelantan*, Sa'ad Shukri's *Sejarah Kelantan* (1962) and its 1971 revision, Tan Ban Teik's 1982 academic exercise, and S. Othman Kelantan's 1996 seminar paper, alongside British official documents (CO 273/426/29843, *The Straits Times* report of 29 May 1915) and a photograph from the A. G. Morkill Collection at the Rhodes House Library, Oxford.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the study of popular history and subaltern agency in colonial Malaya by demonstrating how oral folklore functioned as a counter-narrative to official colonial and palace historiography, and by documenting the mechanisms of political censorship that shaped what could be published about the 1915 rebellion in post-independence Kelantan.
