# On the transliteration of Malay in the roman character

W.E. Maxwell published this philological treatise in 1882 in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 9), offering a systematic critique of the government-mandated spelling conventions for Malay names in the Straits Settlements and proposing a more scholarly alternative grounded in the principles of transliteration rather than phonetic approximation. His overarching thesis is that a sound system must faithfully reproduce the native Perso-Arabic script, respect the etymological integrity of Sanskrit and Arabic loanwords, and remain internally consistent—qualities he finds lacking in the systems of Crawfurd, Keasberry, and the official Spelling Committee.

## Summary

Maxwell opens by distinguishing two separate objectives that the existing government system conflates: faithful transliteration of the Malay character (letters before sounds) and guidance for English pronunciation (sounds before letters). He argues that the Straits Settlements' official system, adopted by the SBRAS Council, fails on both counts because it was designed for administrative uniformity rather than philological rigour. The article then proceeds to lay out a complete proposed system covering vowels, diphthongs, the semivowels *y* and *w*, consonant clusters involving liquids, the full consonant inventory, and the specific rules governing Arabic loanwords.

A central argument concerns the so-called "indefinite" short vowel (the *fathah* sound in syllables like *ber-*, *ter-*, *sembah*). Maxwell contends that introducing a special diacritic for this sound, as Crawfurd and Keasberry did, is unnecessary and produces inconsistency; a plain *a* or *e* suffices for transliteration purposes. He documents the practical failures of existing systems with concrete examples—Crawfurd spelling the same word four different ways (*saparti*, *saparti*, *sapurti*, *sapurti*), the Government Committee writing *Selangor* but *Sarawak*, and rendering the historically established name *Patani* as *Petani* in official publications.

Maxwell also devotes significant attention to the agglutinative morphology of indigenous Malay, arguing that consonant clusters before liquids (e.g., *plandok*, *pluru*) are unscholarly because they obscure the underlying particle-plus-root structure (*pe-landok*, *pe-luru*). He demonstrates that restoring the elided vowel reveals etymological connections, such as the shared root linking *belanga* (cooking pot) with *hangat* (hot) and *hangus* (burnt).

### Key Findings

- The Arabic alphabet, as adapted for Malay, is fundamentally inadequate for representing Malay vowel sounds: most Malay words end in consonants (unlike Arabic), short vowels are unwritten, and the *fathah* must serve double duty for both short *a* and the indefinite *e* sound (pp. 143–145).
- Crawfurd's system is internally inconsistent: the word *saparti* appears in four different spellings across his dictionary, and Sanskrit words like *srigala* and *sloka* are distorted into *sarigala* and *saloka* (p. 146).
- The Government Spelling Committee's rendering of *sembilan* (nine) as *se* rather than *sa* is etymologically wrong, since the word derives from *sa-ambil-an*, "one taken away (from ten)" (p. 146).
- The name *Patani* has been spelt thus since the reign of James I, and the native historical text *Sajarah Malayu* confirms the derivation *Pa-Tani* ("Tani's father"), making the government's "Petani" indefensible (p. 146).
- Consonant clusters before liquids in indigenous Malay words (e.g., *plandok*, *pluru*) result from the elision of the vowel in agglutinative prefixes (*pe-*, *ber-*, *ter-*); restoring the vowel reveals etymological relationships invisible in the surface form (pp. 148–149).
- The Arabic definite article *al-* assimilates before fourteen "solar" consonants and does not before the remaining "lunar" consonants, a rule Maxwell specifies with full examples for correct transliteration of Arabic loanwords in Malay texts (pp. 151–152).

### Conclusion

Maxwell's definitive takeaway is that no system of Romanised Malay can be considered satisfactory unless it subordinates phonetic convenience to the twin imperatives of scriptal fidelity and etymological transparency. He does not claim to have solved the problem entirely—acknowledging that Marsden, Crawfurd, and Logan all failed to do so—but he demonstrates that the existing government system is neither uniform in practice nor sound in principle, and that a system built on the three rules he proposes (follow native spelling, supply vowels from educated pronunciation, defer to Sanskrit/Arabic etymology where the native script is silent) offers the most defensible path forward for scientific and literary use.

## Context

- The article responds directly to the 1879 "Malay and English Spelling" report of the Straits Settlements Spelling Committee, which the SBRAS Council had adopted as its official convention (p. 141).
- Maxwell's work sits within a broader colonial-administrative need for consistent spelling in Blue-books, parliamentary papers, and official correspondence, a tension between bureaucratic utility and philological accuracy that the article makes explicit throughout.
