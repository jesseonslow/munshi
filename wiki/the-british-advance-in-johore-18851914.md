# The British advance in Johore, 1885–1914

Keith Sinclair, writing in 1967, traces the progressive dismantling of Johore's independence between 1885 and 1914, culminating in the imposition of a General Adviser whose advice the Sultan was obliged to accept. His central argument is that the primary driver of British intervention was not moral objection to Sultan Ibrahim's conduct but the structural need to integrate Johore into the expanding political and economic system of British Malaya, particularly through the railway linking the Federated Malay States to Singapore (pp. 93–94, 118–119).

## Summary

Sinclair begins by establishing that Johore's independence, though real, was always qualified by the treaties of 1819 and 1824, which restricted the ruler's diplomatic and concession-granting powers. The 1885 Agreement, negotiated by the Maharaja Abu Bakar in London, tightened British links with the state—guaranteeing its security, prohibiting foreign treaties and concessions to non-Asian Europeans, and providing for a British agent with consular functions—yet Abu Bakar successfully resisted the appointment of a full Resident and preserved a working autonomy for another decade. Sinclair emphasises that Abu Bakar was a skilled bargainer who exploited his personal connections in London and the preferences of the Singapore business community to maintain a degree of self-government (pp. 94–96).

The critical shift came under Ibrahim, who succeeded in 1895. Sinclair argues that the railway was the decisive instrument of control: Swettenham insisted that the F.M.S. construct and operate the line through Johore, and the Sultan's attempt to build it with private English contractors was overruled. Equally significant was the Colonial Office's persistent hostility to private capital entering Johore. When the Johore State Corporation was proposed in 1905 with a twenty-year monopoly on economic development, the Office blocked it, reasoning that successful economic development might prevent the extension of British control. The Sultan was thus denied the principal contemporary means of development—first the railway on his own terms, then a developmental corporation—and was left increasingly dependent on British administrative assistance (pp. 97–101).

By 1910, the rubber boom was transforming Johore's economy and creating administrative demands that the existing government could not meet. The Sultan himself requested a British adviser, and Douglas Campbell was appointed as General Adviser in 1909. Over the following years Campbell built up an extensive administrative apparatus, but the Sultan resented the constraint and the two men grew increasingly hostile toward each other. The final break was engineered through the prison scandal: conditions in Johore Bahru gaol—iron fetters, inadequate medical treatment, the employment of freed convicts on the Sultan's estate—provided the pretext for the 1914 Agreement that made the General Adviser's advice binding on all matters except Malay religion and custom. Sinclair's pointed observation is that the British had tolerated similar conditions since at least 1903 and could "control their perturbation about such conditions in a prison in an Asian state until they intended to take it over" (pp. 102–118).

### Key Findings

- The 1885 Agreement prohibited the Sultan from negotiating treaties with foreign states or granting concessions to non-Asian foreigners without British permission, and provided for a British agent with consular powers who was never actually appointed (pp. 95–96).
- The proposed Johore State Corporation Ltd. (1905) was to have initial capital of £110,000, a twenty-year monopoly on economic development, and a government-guaranteed 5% dividend on preference shares; the Colonial Office blocked it because its existence would hinder future British control (pp. 99–100).
- Sultan Ibrahim's personal allowance was Straits $240,000 in 1910, compared with $52,000 for the Sultan of Perak from a revenue four times as great; it was halved in 1911 to $148,000 and then doubled again by the State Council in 1913 (p. 117).
- Rubber exports from Johore rose in value from Straits $1.5 million to $8.2 million between 1910 and 1914, while government revenue rose from $3.3 million to $4.3 million over the same period (p. 115).
- The 1914 Agreement required the Sultan to accept the General Adviser's advice on all matters except those "touching Malay Religion and Custom"; the Sultan secured safeguards including Johore uniform for British officers, English and Malay as official languages, and preference for educated Johore Malays in civil service appointments (pp. 117–118).
- A prison commission under the Singapore Chief Justice confirmed that convicts wore iron fetters that almost prevented walking; a number of prisoners died of beriberi in 1914 (pp. 117–118).

### Conclusion

Sinclair rejects the Hobson–Lenin thesis that imperialism was merely the agent of bankers and monopolists, and instead applies the Robinson–Gallagher model: imperialism as "a sufficient political function of the process of integrating new regions into the expanding economy." The expanding economy of British Malaya pressed on Johore, and the British economy operated only at one remove. The Sultan's relative inefficiency and uncooperativeness stood in the way of the economic development and political integration of the peninsula, and the minimum requirements of British policy were not being met. The prison scandal was, in Sinclair's judgment, "no more" than an excuse—a "sudden and implausible humanitarian concern" deployed at the moment the British decided to complete the take-over (p. 119).

## Context

- Primary archival collections include the Colonial Office files (C.O. 273 series), the Johore State Secretariat Letter Books and General Adviser's files (Johore Archives, State Secretariat, Johore Bahru), and the Governors' Letter Books (National Library, Singapore).
- The article challenges the prevailing historiography of Rupert Emerson and C.D. Cowan, which attributed Johore's quasi-independence to the personal political talents of its rulers, by relocating the explanation in the structural dynamics of British imperial consolidation and the railway-driven integration of the peninsula.
