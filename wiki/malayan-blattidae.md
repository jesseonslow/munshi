# Malayan Blattidae

R. Hanitsch, Director of the Raffles Museum, Singapore, published this comprehensive taxonomic monograph on the cockroaches (Blattidae) of the Malayan region in 1915. Working from collections in Oxford, London, Kuching, and Singapore, and supplemented by his own fieldwork at Bukit Kutu, Selangor (April 1915), Hanitsch consolidated scattered descriptions from over fifty prior publications into a single systematic reference, describing nine new species and analysing the biogeographical distribution of 184 species across nine sub-families.

## Summary

This monograph addresses the fundamental problem of taxonomic fragmentation in Malayan entomology. By 1915, descriptions of Malayan cockroaches were dispersed across the works of Burmeister, Serville, Saussure, Brunner von Wattenwyl, F. Walker, Stål, Krauss, Kirby, Rehn, and Shelford, each employing different terminological conventions and diagnostic criteria. Hanitsch's method was to compile these descriptions, substituting revised accounts by Brunner von Wattenwyl and Shelford where the originals were inadequate, while acknowledging that full standardisation would require re-examination of type specimens held in Vienna, Paris, Stockholm, and elsewhere (p. 17). The work follows Brunner von Wattenwyl's classification of eleven sub-families, of which nine are represented in the Malayan region; the Nyctiborinae and Blaberinae are restricted to the New World (p. 19).

The systematic treatment proceeds sub-family by sub-family, providing for each genus a diagnostic definition, a key to species where applicable, and for each species a full synonymy, description (often in the original Latin or French of the describing author), measurements, and locality data. Hanitsch's own field observations, particularly from Bukit Kutu at 3,000 feet in Selangor, yielded several of the nine new species, including *Epilampra dytiscoides*, which he notes bears a "striking resemblance to *Dytiscus marginalis*" (p. 83). The work also incorporates material from the Robinson and Kloss expedition to Sumatra (1914) and from the Rev. G. Dexter Allen's collections at Banting, Sarawak, broadening the geographical coverage beyond the Malay Peninsula.

The concluding section on geographical distribution represents the work's most original analytical contribution. Hanitsch tabulates the occurrence of all 184 species across four sub-regions (Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Java, Borneo) and identifies 131 as endemic to the Malayan region. He then examines relationships with neighbouring faunas, finding considerably closer affinities with the Asian mainland (30 shared species) than with the islands to the east (14 with Celebes, Amboina, New Guinea, Australia; only 6 with the Philippines). This analysis is explicitly framed in relation to Wallace's biogeographical sub-regions and the validity of "Wallace's Line," with Hanitsch citing van Kampen's work on fish, amphibian, and mammal distributions as evidence that no single line adequately demarcates faunal boundaries in the Archipelago (pp. 167–168).

### Key Findings

- The Malayan region contains 184 species of Blattidae, of which 131 (71%) are endemic to the region; 11 are cosmopolitan (p. 167).
- Nine new species are described: *Pseudothyrsocera moultoni*, *Phyllodromia 4-punctata*, *Rhabdoblatta obtecta*, *Epilampra circumdata*, *Epilampra dytiscoides*, *Periplaneta robinsoni*, *Methana semi-marginalis*, *Corydia maxwelli*, and *Corydia forceps* (p. 17).
- Species counts by sub-region: Borneo 93, Malay Peninsula 67, Java 62, Sumatra 32 — the disparity reflecting the intensity of prior collecting rather than true faunal differences (p. 168).
- The Corydinae show the highest endemism: 8 of 9 species are peculiar to Malaya, and all are "very striking forms, several of which are brightly coloured" and "of extreme rarity" (p. 168).
- The Phyllodromiinae, Epilamprinae, Oxyhaloinae, and Perisphaerinae each contain 70–85% endemic species, while the Blattinae and Panchlorinae contain the fewest characteristic forms due to anthropogenic dispersal (p. 168).
- Only 6 species are shared between Malaya and the Philippines, leading Hanitsch to question Wallace's inclusion of the Philippines in the Indo-Malayan sub-region (p. 167).

### Conclusion

Hanitsch's definitive takeaway is that the Malayan Blattid fauna is overwhelmingly endemic and shows a considerably closer biogeographical affinity with the Asian mainland than with the insular regions to the east. The Philippines, in particular, exhibit no closer relationship to Malaya than to any other neighbouring area, with 55 of their 65 recorded species being peculiar to those islands. This pattern, Hanitsch argues, undermines the utility of Wallace's Line as a single demarcation and supports the view that faunal boundaries in the East Indian Archipelago are complex and taxon-specific rather than following any single geographic line (pp. 167–168).

## Context

- Hanitsch wrote as Director of the Raffles Museum, Singapore, the principal natural history institution of the Straits Settlements; the work draws heavily on his own twenty years of collecting in the Malay Peninsula, Borneo, Java, and Christmas Island, as well as on material loaned by the Sarawak Museum and the Hope Department, Oxford (p. 17).
- The monograph represents the standard colonial-era museum-based faunal survey: a single authoritative compilation produced by the resident scientific officer, synthesising European taxonomic literature with locally collected material, and published through the colonial learned society (the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society).
