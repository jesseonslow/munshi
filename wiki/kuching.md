# Kuching

Kuching, the capital of Sarawak on the northwest coast of Borneo, was founded in the early nineteenth century as a small administrative centre for antimony mining under Brunei suzerainty and transformed within two decades into a multi-ethnic trading port under the Brooke Rajahs. The urban patterns, ethnic residential segregation, and institutional structures established during the formative period of 1841 to 1857 proved remarkably durable, shaping the city's character well into the twentieth century [[1, pp. 107–126]](#ref-1).

### Origins and Founding

Although the Sarawak River basin had been settled for centuries, Kuching itself was established only between 1824 and 1830 by Pengeran Makota, a Brunei prince who took up residence in the district following the discovery of antimony ore in the upper reaches of the river around 1824 [[1, pp. 106–107]](#ref-1). The site was chosen for its defensibility, proximity to both the river mouth and the mines, and to avoid the jealousy of the established Malay elite at Lidah Tanah, the traditional trading post ten miles upriver [[1, p. 107]](#ref-1). In 1839, the estimated population of the entire basin numbered about 8,000, mostly Dayak with perhaps 1,500 to 2,000 Malays and a few Chinese [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1). A civil war erupted in 1835 or 1836 between Makota's Brunei followers and the local Malays of Lidah Tanah, who allied with Dayak communities. The Sultan of Brunei dispatched his uncle, Raja Muda Hashim, whose arrival with a large entourage brought Kuching's population to approximately 1,500 by 1839 [[1, pp. 108–109]](#ref-1).

### Demographic Transformation under Brooke Rule

James Brooke's installation as Rajah in 1841 inaugurated a new era in which Kuching developed from a small river port into the political, social, and economic capital of a growing state [[1, p. 109]](#ref-1). Population growth was rapid: the settlement may well have reached 6,000 or 8,000 by the late 1840s, and between 6,000 and 10,000 by the early 1850s [[1, p. 110]](#ref-1). Before 1857, Malays constituted eighty to ninety per cent of the population, with significant immigration from adjacent districts such as Sadong and Samarahan, as well as from Sumatra, Java, and Brunei [[1, pp. 110–111]](#ref-1). The Chinese community, numbering no more than two or three hundred before 1850, grew substantially after 1,850, when four to five thousand Hakkas fled the Chinese-Dutch war at Pemangkat; nearly 800 of these refugees settled in and around Kuching [[1, p. 111]](#ref-1). Moplah Muslims from the Malabar Coast of India formed a distinct quarter, and a negligible number of Europeans staffed the developing administration [[1, pp. 111–112]](#ref-1).

Residential segregation followed a clear pattern: Chinese and Indians occupied the *pasar* (bazaar or commercial district) on the south bank, each in a separate quarter; Malay *kampongs* (neighbourhoods or wards) concentrated upriver from the bazaar and later extended to the north bank; and Europeans lived apart, concentrated around the *Astana* (palace) and in a hilly area downriver [[1, p. 112]](#ref-1).

### Administrative and Economic Structures

Brooke governed through a system of indirect rule, delegating authority to leaders of the various ethnic communities [[1, p. 112]](#ref-1). The Malay *datus* (chiefs), particularly the Siniawan chiefs restored to office after the civil war, formed a "symbiotic" relationship with the Rajah that endured for a hundred years [[1, p. 113]](#ref-1). The introduction of regular salaries for the *datus* and the establishment of *tua kampong* (village headmen) in 1854 gradually diluted traditional chiefship, a reform that provoked the dismissal of Datu Patinggi Abdul Gapur in the same year [[1, pp. 117]](#ref-1).

Economically, Malay *nakodas* (ship captains) had traditionally monopolised coastal shipping and upriver trade, but by the 1850s Chinese and Indian merchants were encroaching on this domain [[1, pp. 123–124]](#ref-1). By 1850, three or four Chinese and Indian-owned schooners had entered the carrying trade, and a Chinese firm built the first sago processing factory in Kuching in 1856 [[1, p. 124]](#ref-1). The English-operated Borneo Company Ltd., established in 1856 with Brooke's encouragement, obtained a monopoly over Upper Sarawak's antimony mines and opened a steamer service between Kuching and Singapore [[1, p. 124]](#ref-1).

The first phase of Kuching's development ended with the Kuching-Bau War of 1857, in which the town was occupied and partly destroyed. Under Charles Brooke, increased Chinese immigration from the 1870s transformed Kuching from a mostly Malay town into a predominantly Chinese city, yet the fundamental urban patterns of the early Brooke period remained [[1, pp. 125–126]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The principal scholarly treatment of Kuching's formative period is Craig A. Lockard's 1976 article in the *JMBRAS*, which derives from his 1973 doctoral dissertation at the University of Wisconsin–Madison [[1]](#ref-1). Lockard's study represents an early attempt at a social history of Kuching's formative decades, drawing extensively on contemporary European travel accounts including Hugh Low's *Sarawak* (1848), Spenser St. John's *Life in the Forests of the Far East* (1862), and Keppel's *Dido* (1846) and *Meander* (1853), supplemented by Brooke's private correspondence [[1]](#ref-1). The article positions Kuching within the broader historiographical shift toward community-level analysis of Southeast Asian urbanization, arguing that the institutional and spatial arrangements of the 1840s and 1850s constituted a durable template for the city's subsequent development [[1, pp. 107]](#ref-1).
