# Malay manuscripts in New Zealand: the ‘lost’ manuscript of the Hikayat Abdullah and other Malay manuscripts of the Thomson collection

This 1917 article by R. O. Winstedt surveys the provision of Malay-language instruction in European institutions, arguing for the value of preliminary training in the home country before deployment to the East.

## Summary

Winstedt opens by rebutting the common assumption that Oriental languages are best learned in situ, drawing on Sir Charles Lyall's 1907 memorandum to the committee on oriental studies in London, which highlighted the commercial success of German and French agents trained at home and the pedagogical limitations of local *munshi* teachers (p. 2). He then reports on the establishment of the School of Oriental Studies at Finsbury Circus, opened by the King-Emperor in February 1917, with C. O. Blagden appointed as its first Lecturer in Malay (p. 3).

The article provides brief accounts of Malay instruction at Paris and Leiden. At the Ecole Speciale des Langues Orientales Vivantes, 24 regular students studied Malay in 1906–1907, using texts such as the *Pancha-Tandaran* and *Cherita Jenaka* alongside Skeat, Wilkinson, and Winstedt's own grammar (p. 3). At Leiden, courses in Malay served three distinct audiences—administrative civil servants, magistrates, and doctoral candidates in comparative linguistics (p. 3). Winstedt closes by urging commercial firms, estates, and civil service cadets to use the London School for preliminary training, and by asserting that a genuine standard of scholarship exists in Malay studies, dispensing with "the hallowed notion that the highest authority on Malay matters is a *kampong* elder" (p. 4).
