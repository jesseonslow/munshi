# Magic

### Scope and Historical Definition

Magic in the Malay Peninsula constitutes a complex and syncretic system of belief and practice that has been documented, debated, and reinterpreted by successive generations of scholars from the late nineteenth century to the present. The term itself carries a heavy historiographical burden: as Farish A. Noor has argued, the very act of labelling indigenous Malay knowledge as "magic" was a colonial epistemic operation that systematically devalued local epistemologies and relegated them to a secondary register of "non-knowledge" [[1, pp. 97–98]](#ref-1). The practice encompasses a wide range of activities—from rice ceremonies and healing rites to black magic, divination, and spirit invocation—and is distributed across the Malay Peninsula, with particular concentrations documented in Perak, Selangor, Negri Sembilan, Kelantan, Trengganu, and Pahang [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3), [[4]](#ref-4), [[5]](#ref-5).

The earliest substantial Western ethnographic treatment of the subject is W. E. Maxwell's 1883 account of a shamanic healing ceremony in Perak, which documented the persistence of pre-Islamic animist belief among professedly Muslim Malays, where disease was routinely attributed to supernatural agency and professional *pawang* (medicine-men) were summoned in preference to, or alongside, any form of medicine [[3, pp. 222–223]](#ref-3). Maxwell distinguished between the more Semitic-influenced demonology of coastal areas, where spirits were *sheitan* or *jin* and Arabic formulae served as charms, and the "downright heathenism" of inland *kampongs* (villages), where demons possessed distinct personalities and must be countered by other demons or propitiated by bloody sacrifice [[3, pp. 222–223]](#ref-3). This distinction between coastal and inland practice, and between Islamic and pre-Islamic layers, would become a recurring analytical framework in subsequent scholarship.

Walter William Skeat's *Malay Magic* (1900) represented the most comprehensive attempt to catalogue the full range of Malay magical practice, drawing on fieldwork conducted in Selangor and the border regions of Pahang and Perak [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1). The work was dedicated to Sir Cecil Clementi Smith, the thirteenth Governor of the Straits Settlements, and its preface, written by C. O. Blagden, explicitly justified the study as a tool for governance, arguing that "an understanding of the ideas and modes of thought of an alien people in a relatively low stage of civilisation facilitates very considerably the task of governing them" [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1). Skeat's interpretive framework, drawn explicitly from James George Frazer's *The Golden Bough*, identified Malay ritual practices as instances of sympathetic magic, in which the *padi* (rice) was induced to bear by pretending it had already borne a child, and the *pawang*'s eye-rolling and throat-clicking were homoeopathic imitations of rice-whitening and slow eating [[2, pp. 1–41]](#ref-2).

### Ritual Practice and Ceremonial Complexity

The ceremonial life of Malay magic, as documented in the archival record, reveals a system of considerable elaboration and internal logic. Skeat's 1898 first-person account from Negri Sembilan provides one of the earliest verbatim records of Malay ritual language, transcribed by a literate outsider [[2, pp. 1–41]](#ref-2). The most extensively treated ceremony in that account is the *padi* rite, in which the first seven ears of a selected sheaf are ritually extracted, swathed in cloth, placed in a basket-cradle with an egg, a jungle-nut, a shell, and an iron nail, and carried home as a "rice-child" to be treated with the same taboos as a newborn for three days [[2, p. 3]](#ref-2). The "Ten Prayers" (*doa sapuloh*) recited by the *pawang* without taking breath invoke, in sequence, Allah, Muhammad, the five daily prayers, the Five Indras, the opening of the door of sustenance, seven ranks of exaltation, the gate of Rengkiang, the gate of Paradise, the child in the mother's womb, and the child made by Allah, concluding with the names Isa, Karun, and Daud [[2, p. 6]](#ref-2). This sequence illustrates the syncretic character of the practice, blending Islamic formulae with Hindu-Brahmanical references in a single invocation.

Healing ceremonies display a comparable complexity. In Maxwell's 1883 Perak account, two *pawang* took turns attending a young married woman whose first baby was only days old and who lay insensible—what Maxwell himself suspected were "paroxysms of puerperal fever" [[3, p. 224]](#ref-3). The first, Che Johan, a specialist in tiger-spirits (*hantu bian*), was possessed by "Bujang Gelap" (the dark dragon) and spoke in a feigned voice laced with Sakai aboriginal words; the second invoked the spirits of the Sungkei river and was possessed successively by four named spirits without producing any useful diagnosis [[3, pp. 226–228]](#ref-3). When both had exhausted their efforts, a *balei berpusing*—a miniature model of a Perak mosque mounted on a pivot, fringed with coconut-leaf streamers called *jari lipan* ("centipede's legs"), and filled with propitiatory offerings—was constructed, the demons were waved into the model, and it was carried to the river and set adrift [[3, pp. 229–231]](#ref-3). The patient died within twenty-four hours [[3, p. 232]](#ref-3).

In Kelantan and Trengganu, the *Main Peteri* seance represents a more elaborate and publicly performed form of shamanistic healing. Carol Laderman's 1987 synopses of three performances, conducted under the auspices of the Institute for Medical Research, document the full arc of the ritual from elaborate preparations and invocations through spirit dialogues, divinations, bargaining, and the final release rituals [[4, pp. 55–71]](#ref-4). The ceremony concludes with the use of *pelepas* (palm-frond strips tied in double slipknots), which the shaman unties while reciting "One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, you are released," thereby freeing all participants from the spiritual vulnerability inherent in dealings with the spirit world [[4, pp. 61]](#ref-4). Laderman's central argument is that the *Main Peteri* embeds a sophisticated psychotherapeutic theory of personality centred on "Inner Winds" (*angin*)—talents, drives, and creative energies that, if thwarted and shut up within the breast, produce emotional and physical illness [[4, p. 57]](#ref-4). This challenges the prevailing scholarly assumption that shamanistic healing operates solely through external projection of illness onto spirits [[4, pp. 55–57]](#ref-4).

Black magic (*ilmu jahat*) occupies a distinct register. A. E. Coope's 1933 article presents a detailed transcription and translation of a pocket book of Malay black art recovered from the belongings of a celebrated Kelantan burglar, offering one of the few published examples of a working criminal's grimoire from colonial Malaya [[6, pp. 264–272]](#ref-6). The invocations address "Royal Jins of Islam, ye Seven Brethren" and "Mighty Powers of the Seven Worlds," blending orthodox Islamic formulae with commands to lock the "seven doors" and "nine doors" of a named victim, pressing down his organs until he is "as dead as a corpse in the grave" [[6, p. 264]](#ref-6). The bullet charm is addressed to the *Hantu Raya* (Great Demon) in its threefold manifestation—Father of the jungle, Mother of the sea, Son of the plain—and adjures the gun and bullet by naming their supposed origins [[6, p. 270]](#ref-6). Coope's closing observation is characteristically wry: the criminal burgled successfully on a Friday night after midnight (a "good" time), the bullet charm "worked" when the detective's pistol missed fire, he was arrested on a Monday at dawn (when the *ketika* prescribes anxiety), and on the 19th of a month—when the *bintang chelaka* ("bad luck compass") dictates that one should not go out at all—he appeared before a judge who sentenced him to seven years [[6, p. 272]](#ref-6).

### Syncretism and Provenance

A central scholarly debate in the literature concerns the provenance of specific magical practices and formulae: whether they are genuinely indigenous, derived from Hindu-Brahmanical tradition, or imported with Islam from the Semitic magical tradition of Arabia and Mesopotamia. R. O. Winstedt's 1925 survey, drawing heavily on his own monograph *Shaman, Saiva and Sufi*, argued that much of what earlier scholars attributed to Hindu influence is in fact of Semitic or Muslim origin [[7, pp. 6–21]](#ref-7). He is particularly insistent on the last point: the use of dough figures as substitutes in healing, the catalogue-style exorcism that disclaims the magician's own power, the philtre of black cat ashes, and the wax-image curse all find their closest parallels in Assyrian and Hebrew magical texts rather than in Indian sources [[7, pp. 6–21]](#ref-7). The Kelantan magician's philtre for seeing through a brass dice-box, for instance, combines the Semitic ingredient of black cat ashes with the indigenous addition of tiger's eyes, because killing a cat was "sinful" to the Malay and carried the karmic penalty of carrying logs of wood "to the number of the hairs on the beast's body" in the next world [[7, pp. 14–15]](#ref-7).

Winstedt also documents the persistence of aboriginal belief beneath the surface of Muslim practice. The Mantra (a Proto-Malay group) who "remained heathen" nonetheless cut the rice-soul with the *Basmala* and invoked Allah and Muhammad, and Muslim Malays sacrifice a pig on the seashore when all other offerings to secure a catch have failed [[7, pp. 6–21]](#ref-7). The afterlife geography shared by the Peninsula's aboriginal peoples—the westward orientation of the soul's journey, the seven-day haunting of graves, the canoe-ditch around burial mounds, and the soul-ladders and soul-huts that facilitate the dead's departure—further illustrates the deep stratification of belief [[7, p. 20]](#ref-7). A Proto-Malay chief's grave in Johore (1879) included a ditch for the spirit's canoe, four horizontal beams forming an enclosure containing a coconut shell, torch, axe-handle, and cooking-pot, a shoulder-basket for firewood, and "soul-steps" (upright sticks) to enable the spirit's departure, with the body's feet pointing west [[7, p. 20]](#ref-7).

The economic dimensions of magical practice are also attested in the record. Skeat acknowledged that the Malay *pawang* could extract "a hundred or perhaps two hundred dollars" from Chinese tin-mining *towkay* for prospecting advice, yet immediately framed this as evidence of Malay superstition rather than local geological knowledge, contrasting the Malay "nose for tin" with the European reliance on "boring tools" [[1, p. 18]](#ref-1). H. Marriott, writing in 1903, argued that the *pawang* exploited the community's fear, extracting payment on the threat of bewitching children, and explained the self-reinforcing logic of the belief: if a person was thought to have offended the *pawang*, the next illness in the household would be attributed to supernatural causation, ruling out ordinary remedies in favour of incantations alone [[8, p. 210]](#ref-8).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Malay magic spans nearly a century and reveals a clear evolution in method, perspective, and analytical framework. The earliest contributions—Maxwell (1883), Skeat (1898), Marriott (1903)—are first-hand ethnographic accounts by colonial administrators who documented ceremonies they personally witnessed, framing the subject as a curiosity to be catalogued and, implicitly, as a survival to be superseded [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3), [[8]](#ref-8). Skeat's *Malay Magic* (1900) consolidated this material into a comprehensive reference work, but its interpretive framework was explicitly Eurocentric: the Malay mind was positioned as fundamentally unscientific, and all Malay cultural practice was thereby rendered "magical" [[1, pp. 97–119]](#ref-1). Noor's 2021 analysis identifies a genealogical shift in colonial Malay stereotypes running from the "piratical" Malay of the early period to the "superstitious agrarian" Malay of the later period, a transition that he links to the spread and development of British colonial power across the Peninsula [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1).

The interwar period saw a shift towards comparative and etiological analysis. Winstedt's 1925 notes, though still produced within the administrative-ethnographic tradition of the colonial service, introduced a more rigorous provenance-based methodology, systematically re-attributing numerous practices from a Hindu to a Semitic origin [[7, pp. 6–21]](#ref-7). Coope's 1933 article, while still framed as a police curiosity, preserved a complete annotated transcription of a criminal's working manuscript in Jawi script, a genre of source material otherwise extremely rare in the published record [[6, pp. 264–272]](#ref-6).

The post-independence period marks a decisive break with the colonial interpretive framework. Laderman's 1987 study, grounded in sustained participant observation conducted under the Institute for Medical Research, challenges the dominant transcultural psychiatry framework that positions shamanistic healing as inferior to Western psychotherapy, demonstrating instead that the *Main Peteri* contains an indigenous theory of personality operating on the patient's own psyche [[4, pp. 55–71]](#ref-4). Her work represents the most significant methodological departure in the literature: where earlier scholars observed from the outside and interpreted through Frazer or Thompson, Laderman was informally adopted by the shaman Pak Long Awang bin Ali and became a regular attendant at his performances, granting her access to the colloquial, often bawdy register in which the spirits actually speak [[4, pp. 55–56]](#ref-4). The 2005 reprint of Skeat's *Malay Magic* by the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, with an introduction by J. M. Gullick and an appendix of Skeat's later notes, made the foundational text available as an Open Access publication on JSTOR, ensuring its continued availability for critical re-engagement [1, p. 97, n. 5].
