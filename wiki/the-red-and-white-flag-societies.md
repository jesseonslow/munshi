# The red and white flag societies

W.G. Stirling's 1925 article in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the Red and White Flag Societies, Malay-dominated secret organizations that operated in the Straits Settlements and Native States during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Stirling's central thesis is that these societies were not independent Malay movements but deliberate instruments created by the Chinese Ghee Hin and Ghee Hok Triad Societies to extend their reach into Malay administrative and police structures.

## Summary

Stirling opens with a detailed account of a Malay circumcision ceremony, establishing the religious and mutual-aid character that the Red and White Flag Societies originally claimed before degenerating into instruments of intimidation. He argues that the White Flag Society was established first, with the Red Flag following shortly after, and that both were initially mutual benefit organizations admitting only men of "deserving character." Over time, however, they lost their religious purpose and became vehicles for blackmail, violence, and the protection of criminal members, mirroring the trajectory of their Chinese parent bodies (p. 58).

The bulk of the article is devoted to the societies' internal mechanics: pass grips, secret signs, oath rituals, entrance fees, and the elaborate system of coded communication used to request assistance for crimes ranging from grievous assault to murder. Stirling documents how Chinese members used queue-based signals and how Malay members adapted equivalent gestures using cloth, illustrating the hybrid cultural character of these organizations. He also describes the initiation oaths, which for Chinese candidates involved the pricking of a finger, the decapitation of a white cock, and the drinking of a blood-mixed cup, while for Malay candidates the Koran was placed on the head and an oath of entrails-devouring betrayal was sworn (pp. 59–60).

Stirling's most significant analytical contribution is his identification of the structural relationship between the Flag Societies and the Triad parent organizations. He asserts that Chinese Triad headmen deliberately inserted spies into the Flag Societies, and that the societies' very nomenclature — "Red" for righteousness and "White" for loyalty — was borrowed directly from Triad watchwords. The triangular red and white flags themselves were chosen because such colours were already familiar in Muslim votive contexts, providing a plausible cover (p. 60).

### Key Findings

- Entrance fees were $1.25 for the Red Flag Society and $3.25 for the White Flag Society; members were additionally charged 30 cents per head for feasts or legal defence, generating sums of $300–$400 per incident (p. 58).
- The Ghee Hin, Ghee Hok, and all associated Triad Societies were declared unlawful and dangerous in 1890 and compelled to surrender their seals and insignia to the Government; the Red and White Flag Societies were never formally registered or recognized (p. 60).
- The White Flag Society's diploma was an oblong piece of yellow silk bearing a diamond device and the oath of secrecy; the Red Flag diploma was a ticket on yellow or red paper bearing crossed triangular flags and a facsimile of the Eight Diagrams (*Pat Kwa*), evidencing direct Chinese Triad influence (p. 61).
- The societies' membership comprised Malays, Javanese, Boyanese, Tamils, and a minority of Chinese whose explicit function was to spy on their "Muhammadan sworn brethren" (p. 58).
- Stirling concludes that by 1925 the societies had become "insignificant" and "no longer exist," having been sustained only by the knowledge of the Triad Society's organizational backing (p. 61).

### Conclusion

Stirling's definitive takeaway is that the Red and White Flag Societies were never autonomous Malay secret societies but subordinate appendages of the Chinese Triad network, created to handle local Malay police and government employees in ways the Triads themselves could not. Their brief existence and rapid decline after the 1890 suppression of the Triads confirm their derivative character, and Stirling judges that the formation of such secret societies was fundamentally alien to the Malay character absent a genuine religious motive.

## Context

- The article draws on Stirling's own administrative experience in the Straits Settlements police or colonial service, giving it the character of an insider's ethnographic report rather than a purely archival study.
- Historiographically, the piece is one of the earliest systematic descriptions of the interface between Chinese secret societies and Malay social structures in the Straits Settlements, predating later scholarly treatments of Triad-Malay relations by several decades.
