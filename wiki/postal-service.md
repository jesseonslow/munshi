# Postal service

The postal service in Singapore, from its inception in 1819 to the formal separation of postal and maritime functions in 1858, represents a case study in how colonial administrative structures struggled to accommodate commercial expansion. The coupling of the Post Master and Master Attendant roles—initially a practical arrangement for a small military outpost—became progressively dysfunctional as mail volume and port traffic grew, and the eventual decoupling was the product of sustained local pressure rather than imperial initiative [[1]](#ref-1).

## Origins and Early Administration

The settlement's postal functions began under military administration. For the first four years, the Cantonment Adjutant handled the mails under orders from Major William Farquhar, who served as both Resident and Commandant [[1, pp. 2–3]](#ref-1). The first civilian Post Master, Charles Ryan, was appointed in February 1823, but his tenure was brief; he resigned from the Resident's Office in March 1824 following severe criticisms of his book-keeping, and his postal duties ceased on 1 May of that year [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). Captain William Lawrence Flint, Master Attendant and Raffles's brother-in-law, then assumed the postal role, receiving an allowance of fifty dollars a month for the additional work [[1, p. 4]](#ref-1).

During this period there was no internal postal service. Letters for transit away from the settlement were handed in at the post office, incoming letters were sorted and delivered to addressees, and until September 1845 the mails were despatched by any suitable vessel. All captains were obliged to accept post office packets and ensure their delivery at destination ports, with failure to comply rendering them liable to a fine [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1). The Master Attendant's boat was used to land incoming mail when it returned from meeting the vessel, and outgoing mail was sent on board with the port clearance [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1).

## Commercial Growth and Administrative Strain

The Opium War of 1840–42 and the opening of five treaty ports on the Chinese coast transformed Singapore's commercial profile. Postal revenue grew from Rs 1,812 in 1833 to Rs 8,516 in 1841 and Rs 18,740 in 1844, while the total staff salary remained at Rs 1,680 per year—less than a tenth of the 1844 takings [[1, pp. 7–8]](#ref-1). In 1844, 744 square-rigged vessels entered the harbour, an average of just over two per day including Sundays and public holidays [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1).

The physical accommodation was wholly inadequate. The post office occupied half of a single small room at the front of John Argyle Maxwell's house, the other half being shared by the Master Attendant's marine office and the clerk to the registrar of imports and exports [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1). The despatch boat was so antiquated that in 1843 a marine sentry on the *Cornwallis* dropped an 18-pounder shot into it, mistaking it for an old sampan [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1).

The crisis came to a head in September 1845, when the first west-bound P. & O. S. N. Company paddle-steamer arrived from Hongkong carrying over 4,750 covers. The mail was loaded unsorted and half an hour late, prompting a public outcry. The *Straits Times*, founded by Robert Carr Woods, devoted extensive space to condemning the inefficiency of the establishment, which consisted of a Post Master who "does nothing" and a deputy who "does most of the duties of the office but receives little" [[1, pp. 11–12]](#ref-1). Governor Butterworth, who had been pressing Bengal for increased staff and a younger officer to replace the ageing William Scott, was forced to act unilaterally, renting new premises and rearranging the staff [[1, pp. 13–14]](#ref-1).

## Separation and Consolidation

The formal separation of the Post Office from the Master Attendant's department was approved by the Secretary to the Government of India on 29 October 1858 and announced in Singapore on 4 December of that year [[1, p. 17]](#ref-1). William Cuppage, who had served in the postal department since the late 1820s, was elevated to Post Master of an independent department [[1, p. 17]](#ref-1). The *Straits Times* editor, though welcoming the change, noted that the distribution of subordinates was unequal, with the Post Office receiving fewer hands than the marine department despite heavier workloads [[1, pp. 17–18]](#ref-1).

By 1854 the English mail was delivered in under thirty-four days from closure in London, compared with roughly one hundred days for the Thames-to-Batavia run during Raffles's occupation of Java [[1, p. 14]](#ref-1). In 1867 the P. & O. S. N. Company signed a new contract under which it agreed to maintain a weekly schedule and cut the transit time to twenty-six days [[1, p. 14]](#ref-1). Cuppage continued as Post Master until his retirement in 1871, after nearly forty-two years of service; from 1869 his appointment was designated acting Postmaster-General at a salary of $300 per month [[1, p. 19]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

Gibson-Hill's 1958 article remains the definitive study of Singapore's early postal administration, drawing extensively on East India Company Records held at the India Office, particularly series L, M, N, Q, R, S, and Z, supplemented by contemporary newspapers including the *Straits Times*, *Singapore Free Press*, and *Singapore Chronicle* [[1]](#ref-1). The article complements his separate study of the Master Attendants (published in *JMBRAS* 33, 1960) and provides the postal-specific dimension of Singapore's administrative history, a topic previously treated only in passing in general settlement histories such as T. A. Melville's *One Hundred Years of Singapore* (1921) [[1]](#ref-1). The work is notable for its use of primary correspondence between local governors and the Supreme Government at Fort William, which reveals the protracted and often frustrating process by which local administrative needs were met—or not—by imperial authority.
