# A descriptive catalogue of the books relating to Malaysia in the Raffles Museum & Library, Singapore

## Abstract

For well over a century, close personal ties underlay relations between the rulers of Kedah and Siam. In the latter part of the nineteenth century, Kedah emerged as Siam’s most favoured Malay tributary, and the state’s rulers pursued parallel agendas of socio-economic and administrative modernization. That special tie ended abruptly in 1909 when Siam signed a treaty ceding the Kedah to Great Britain, but Tunku Badlishah, a key figure in the Kedah administration, was a naturalized citizen of Siam and had close personal ties with King Vajiravudh. After the latter’s death in 1925, the relationship faded away, but Badlishah’s Thai background continued to influence his career in various ways even after he became Sultan in 1943. His reign coincided with Malaya’s push for independence, and he became a key figure in pressing for a continuing leadership role for Malaya’s sultans and a retention of traditional authority.

## Summary

This article by Kobkua Suwannathat-Pian examines the life of Tunku Badlishah (r. 1943–58), the twelfth son of Sultan Abdul Hamid Halim Shah of Kedah, whose unique biography embodies the long history of personalized relations between the Kedah and Siamese ruling houses. The author situates Badlishah's story within a broader narrative of Kedah-Siam relations stretching from the 1802 succession crisis—when Tunku Pangeran (later Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin Halim Shah) submitted himself to King Rama I's suzerainty to secure the Kedah throne—through the Siamese occupation of 1821–41, the restoration of tributary relations, and the gradual deepening of personal bonds between the two royal families that made Kedah Siam's most favoured Malay tributary by the mid-nineteenth century.

The article's central argument is that Badlishah's Thai upbringing, education, and naturalization as a Siamese citizen shaped his distinctive approach to rulership during a critical transitional period in Malayan history. Raised from around age seven in the household of Crown Prince Vajiravudh (later King Rama VI), educated at King's College in Bangkok and Wadham College, Oxford, and serving in the Siamese Privy Purse Department, Badlishah returned to Kedah in 1925 only after his father's formal request. His reign coincided with the most contentious phase of Malaya's path to independence, during which he became the most outspoken defender of the Malay rulers' traditional authority against both British colonial encroachment and the rising political power of UMNO. The author draws extensively on Thai archival correspondence—particularly letters between Sultan Abdul Hamid, King Vajiravudh, and Prince Damrong (Minister of the Interior)—to reconstruct the personal dynamics of the Bangkok-Kedah relationship and the circumstances of Badlishah's return.

The article also addresses the complex family politics surrounding Badlishah's accession, including his strained relationship with his half-brother Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra (later the first Prime Minister of Malaya), and the role of the influential State Secretary Haji Shariff Osman in deepening rifts within the Kedah royal family. The author argues that Badlishah's Thai background, while giving him a distinctive perspective and boldness, did not prepare him for the polygamous family dynamics and socio-political labyrinth of Kedah's court.

### Key Findings

- Tunku Badlishah was born on 17 March 1894 and was the only member of the Kedah royal family to become a naturalized Siamese citizen, receive a Thai noble title (Phra Si Surintharawiwongsa), and enter the Thai civil service (p. 124).
- The 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty ceded Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan, and Trengganu to Great Britain in exchange for Britain's cancellation of the 1855 Anglo-Siamese Treaty and favourable loan conditions for a Siamese railroad to the south (p. 123).
- King Vajiravudh's letter of 6 August 1925 to Sultan Abdul Hamid, agreeing to "lend" Badlishah to Kedah while keeping his post open in Siam, is preserved in the Thai National Archives (Documents Reign 6, B.3.1/4 126/30) (p. 127).
- In 1946, Sultan Badlishah wrote to the Colonial Office in London asserting that his signature on the Malayan Union agreement had been obtained under duress, and he invoked the 1923 Anglo-Kedah Treaty as his right to appeal directly to the British monarch (p. 130).
- In February 1948, two senior members of the Kedah administration refused to perform the oath-taking ceremony pledging fealty to Sultan Badlishah, arguing he was not ruling "by the will of the people" (p. 132).
- The Conference of Rulers, with Sultan Badlishah as a leading voice, rejected UMNO's proposal to appoint a Malay Deputy High Commissioner at the Federal level, a decision that deeply affected Dato' Onn Jaafar's attitude toward royalty (p. 132).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian presents Sultan Badlishah as the last and most significant exemplar of the personalized Bangkok-Kedah relationship that predated the modern era. His extraordinary circumstances—raised as an adopted son of a foreign king, educated in Siam and Britain, and the first Malay ruler to attend Oxford—moulded a bold and outspoken leadership style that proved both an asset and a liability. His reign marked a transitional moment in which traditional royal authority was tested against the emerging forces of colonial modernization and Malay nationalism, and his personal reconciliation with Tunku Abdul Rahman after the latter's 1955 electoral triumph symbolized the eventual accommodation between traditional and modern sources of political legitimacy in independent Malaya.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the Thai National Archives (Documents Reign 6, B.3.1/4), the UK National Archives (CO 273, CO 535, CO 717, CO 537), and the Rhodes House Library, Oxford (MSS. Ind. Ocn series), alongside published memoirs by Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra.
- The study contributes to the historiography of Kedah-Siam relations by foregrounding the personal and extra-diplomatic dimensions of tributary ties, and by using Thai-language archival sources to illuminate the internal dynamics of the Siamese court's relationship with its Malay tributaries.
