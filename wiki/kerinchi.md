# Kerinchi

Kerinchi (also spelled Kerinci) is a highland region in central Sumatra whose name, languages, and people have attracted sustained scholarly attention for their bearing on the linguistic prehistory of the Malay world and the demographic movements of the nineteenth century. The Kerinchi dialects, spoken across a cluster of localities in the highlands of West Sumatra, exhibit a set of phonological developments that E.O. Van Reijn demonstrated to be parallel to those found in Mon-Khmer languages of Further India and in the languages of the aboriginal peoples of the Malay Peninsula, pointing to a substratum of considerable antiquity [[1, p. 136]](#ref-1). The Kerinchi people themselves are documented as gold-miners, traders, and bearers of an indigenous phonetic script, characteristics that brought them into contact with the Malay Peninsula from at least the eighteenth century onward [[2, pp. 83–84]](#ref-2). The region's most populous area is itself called Rawang, a fact that has generated a persistent historiographical confusion between the Kerinchi and the people known in Peninsular sources as *orang Rawa* [[2, p. 83]](#ref-2).

## Linguistic Profile and the Mon-Khmer Substratum

The Kerinchi dialects form a continuum across fourteen documented localities: Semurup, Kemantan, Sungai Tutung, Koto Payang, Koto Lanang, Rawang, Koto Baru Rawang, Dusun Baru, Sungai Penuh, Kumun, Tanjung Pauh, Pulo Tengah, Tanjung Tanah, and Hiang [[1, p. 138]](#ref-1). Van Reijn's comparative analysis, drawing on his own field recordings and transcriptions from these sites, identifies six distinct categories of sound-shift that are shared with Mon-Khmer languages and with certain other Sumatran languages including Achinese, Minangkabau, Redjang, Pasemah-Serawai, and Lampong [[1, p. 130]](#ref-1).

The first and most striking of these shifts involves the reduction of originally bisyllabic words to monosyllabic forms. The vowel in the first syllable is reduced to zero, stress falls heavily on the final syllable, and the resulting initial consonant cluster is resolved into a preglottalized implosive stop [[1, p. 130]](#ref-1). Thus Kerinchi (Tanjung Tanah) *'rau^N* corresponds to Malay *harum* "sweet-smelling," and Kerinchi (Kumun) *'xahh* to Malay *kɛras* "hard" [[1, p. 130]](#ref-1). Parallel developments are attested in Cham, Bahnar, Thai, and the Besisi of Sepang, confirming that this is not an isolated innovation [[1, p. 130]](#ref-1).

A second regular shift is the development of final *-s* to *-h*, often accompanied by diphthongization of the preceding vowel. Kerinchi (Koto Lanang) *alauh* corresponds to Malay *halus* "soft, delicate," and Kerinchi (Kumun) *siblahh* to Malay *sɛbɛlas* "eleven" [[1, p. 131]](#ref-1). The same pattern appears in Kelantan, Cham, Achinese, and Minangkabau [[1, p. 131]](#ref-1).

A third shift involves the loss of final *-l*, again with frequent diphthongization: Kerinchi (Tanjung Pauh) *kumpau* for Malay *kumpul* "to gather," and Kerinchi (Kumun) *bataw* for Malay *batu* "stone" [[1, p. 131]](#ref-1). Van Reijn notes that in the Sumatran languages listed, as well as in Further India, final *-r* often becomes zero in a parallel fashion, though this development also occurs in other Indonesian languages such as Javanese [[1, p. 131]](#ref-1).

The fourth category concerns systematic diphthongization of vowels in closed or open syllables, following characteristic patterns: *u* > *au* (Kerinchi (Kemantan) *bataw* < Malay *batu*); *i* > *a* or *ɛ* (Kerinchi (Kemantan) *giga* < Malay *gigi* "tooth"); *i* > *u* or *ɔ* (Kerinchi (Koto Payang) *padu* < Malay *padi* "paddy"); *a* > *a* (Kerinchi (Rawang) *muda* < Malay *muda* "young"); *a* > *au* (Kerinchi (Rawang) *lalau* < Malay *lalat* "fly"); and *a* > *ea* or *ɛa* (Kerinchi (Koto Payang) *tibɛa* < Malay *tiba* "to arrive") [[1, pp. 131–133]](#ref-1).

The fifth category is the reduction of original or secondary diphthongs in the final syllable to simple vowels, including *au* > *u* (Kerinchi (Semurup, non-pausal) *danu* < Malay *danau* "lake") and *au* > *a* (Kerinchi (Sungai Penuh) *lima* < Malay *limau* "lemon") [[1, p. 133]](#ref-1). Van Reijn observes that developments such as *au* > *a* beside *a* > *au*, and *a* > *i* beside *i* > *a*, are presumably due to hypercorrection [[1, p. 134]](#ref-1).

The sixth and most complex category involves the development of final unvoiced stops into preglottalized nasals, which are then interchanged with other preglottalized nasals not homorganic with the original stops. From these nasals, new unvoiced stops may develop, or the glottal stop may assimilate the nasal element, reducing it to zero [[1, pp. 134–135]](#ref-1). The full phonological chain for Malay *bukit* "hill" is documented across multiple Kerinchi dialects: Kerinchi (Rawang, pausal) *bukuʔŋ* < *buku1ʔŋ* < *buku1ʔn* < *bukIʔn* < *bukIt* [[1, p. 135]](#ref-1). Comparable chains are shown for Malay *gigit* "to bite" and *lalat* "fly" [[1, p. 135]](#ref-1).

A further point of significance concerns the distinction between pausal and non-pausal forms in Kerinchi. Pausal forms, used when a word is pronounced in isolation or at the end of a sentence, often show loss of final-syllable vowels and consonants that are preserved in the non-pausal forms [[1, p. 130]](#ref-1). This phenomenon was first noted by Voorhoeve for Redjang, where pausal forms are used when chanting legends and when reviling people, particularly by women [1, p. 130, n.1].

Van Reijn's conclusion is that the striking parallelism between the sound-shifts in certain Sumatran languages and in the Mon-Khmer family, including the dialects of the aboriginal peoples of the Malay Peninsula, cannot be due to mere chance but points to the existence of a Mon-Khmer substratum in the highlands of West Sumatra [[1, p. 136]](#ref-1). He further argues, drawing on Kuiper (1948) and Haudricourt (1950), that the intervocalic *m* found in Achinese, Cham, Redjang, and Batak dialects where Malay has *mb* is not the result of nasal assimilation but the outcome of an original implosive voiced stop *b, which differentiated into the variants *b*, *mb*, and *m* [[1, pp. 136–137]](#ref-1).

## The Kerinchi in Peninsular History: The Rawa Question

The Kerinchi people are documented in nineteenth-century sources as a distinct community within the Malay Peninsula, characterized by religious zealotry, gold-mining, and possession of an indigenous phonetic script [[2, p. 83]](#ref-2). McNair (1878) described the Kerinchi in Perak as "a purer race of Mahomedans" who "dress always in white garments" and hold aloof from ordinary Malays [[2, p. 83]](#ref-2). Valentijn (1726) recorded that Kerinchi gold had the reputation of being the best in Sumatra, which explains why Kerinchi migrants pursued gold-mining in the Peninsula [[2, p. 83]](#ref-2). Barnes (1818) described the Kerinchi as entrepreneurs in east-west coast trade, and van Langen (1888) recorded Kerinchi settlements as far north as Aceh [[2, pp. 83–84]](#ref-2).

A persistent confusion in the historical literature concerns the identity of the people called *orang Rawa* (and its dialect variants Rao, Rawang) who appear in records of local disputes and migrations in the Malay Peninsula. A.G. Milner (1978) had accepted the suggestion that *orang Rinchi* referred to followers of the Padri leader Tuanku nan Renceh, and had treated the terms *orang Rawa* and *orang Rinchi* as synonymous from the early nineteenth century onward [[2, p. 82]](#ref-2). C.W. Watson, working from his own field research among the Kerinchi people of central Sumatra, challenges this identification on linguistic grounds, noting that Malay idiomatic usage would not naturally produce such a construction to mean "followers" [[2, p. 82]](#ref-2).

Watson revives Crawfurd's (1856) earlier derivation of "Rinchi" as an abbreviation of "Korinchi," the mountain region to the southern borders of Minangkabau, and supports this with Bickmore's independent observation that the Padri reformers originated from that area [[2, p. 82]](#ref-2). The core of Watson's argument rests on a convergence of characteristics: the three features Milner attributes to the Rawa/Rinchi—religious zealotry, gold-mining, and possession of an indigenous phonetic script—match precisely what is documented for the Kerinchi [[2, p. 83]](#ref-2). Crucially, Watson identifies that the most populous area within Kerinchi is itself called Rawang, and that its inhabitants are locally known as *orang Rawa*, providing a direct etymological link: the name "Rawa" in the Peninsula most likely derives from Rawang in Kerinchi rather than from the generic Malay word for swamp-land [[2, p. 83]](#ref-2).

Watson then proposes a more speculative hypothesis to explain how Kerinchi people came to be associated with the Rawa region in the northern Mandailing-Minangkabau borderland. Drawing on evidence of Kerinchi trading networks that stretched from Aceh to Patani, and on an 1878 newspaper report (cited by Castles) placing Kerinchi traders in south Tapanuli whose "general manner and pleasant bearing were winning converts," he suggests that Kerinchi pilgrims and traders passing through the upper Rekan area as early as the eighteenth century established settlements there, made converts among the local population, and gave the place the name "Rawa" after their home village of Rawang [[2, p. 84]](#ref-2). The nineteenth-century Rawa who migrated to the Peninsula would then be descendants of this mixed community [[2, p. 84]](#ref-2). Marsden (1811) observed that the inhabitants of the Rao country wore the same "peculiarity of dress" as the Korinchi people, which Watson interprets as evidence that the Rao people were Kerinchi settlers [[2, p. 85]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Kerinchi, while limited in volume, addresses two distinct but complementary questions: the linguistic prehistory of the region and its demographic footprint in the Malay Peninsula. Van Reijn's 1974 article represents a substantial contribution to the former, providing the first systematic comparative phonological analysis of the Kerinchi dialect continuum grounded in field data from fourteen localities [[1, p. 138]](#ref-1). The article builds on earlier work by Kuiper (1948) on Munda-Indonesian connections and by Haudricourt (1950) on preglottalized consonants in Indochina, while extending the argument specifically to the Kerinchi dialects and their relationship to the Mon-Khmer substratum hypothesis [[1, pp. 136–137]](#ref-1). The primary data consist of Van Reijn's own field recordings, supplemented by published comparative wordlists including Skeat and Blagden's *Comparative Wordlist*, Jaspan's *Redjang Ka-Ga-Nga-Texts* (1964), and Pinnow's *Versuch einer historischen Lautlehre der Kharia-Sprache* (1959) [[1, p. 136]](#ref-1).

Watson's 1982 note, though shorter in scope, addresses a historiographical problem that had persisted since the early nineteenth century: the identity and origins of the *orang Rawa* in Peninsular records [[2, p. 82]](#ref-2). Watson's intervention is significant because it challenges the prevailing identification advanced by Milner (1978) and proposes instead a direct etymological and demographic link between the Rawa and the Kerinchi, a connection obscured by the polysemy of the term "Rawa" across Sumatra and the Peninsula [[2, p. 85]](#ref-2). His argument draws on his own fieldwork among the Kerinchi people of central Sumatra, supplemented by nineteenth-century European travel accounts by Barnes, Bickmore, Crawfurd, McNair, Marsden, and Valentijn, and more recent scholarship by Tugby (1977), Radcliffe (1969), and Castles (1972) [[2, pp. 82–85]](#ref-2).

Together, these two contributions illustrate the shifting priorities in the Society's documentation of Kerinchi: from the structural-linguistic question of substratum influence in the 1970s to the historical-demographic question of migration and identity in the 1980s. Both remain, by their own authors' admission, incomplete. Van Reijn notes that much further research still has to be done, and that the hypothesis can be used even at the present imperfect state of knowledge for the elucidation of certain dialectal peculiarities [[1, p. 136]](#ref-1). Watson's hypothesis regarding the establishment of Kerinchi settlements in the upper Rekan area is explicitly described as speculative, resting on circumstantial evidence from trade networks and a single newspaper report [[2, p. 84]](#ref-2).
