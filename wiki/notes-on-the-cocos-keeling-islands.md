# Notes on the Cocos-Keeling Islands

C.A. Gibson-Hill (1948) presents a comprehensive natural history of North Keeling Island, the uninhabited northern island of the Cocos-Keeling group in the eastern Indian Ocean, based on two field visits made in January and July 1941. The work documents the island's physical structure, vegetation zones, and faunal communities—particularly its sea-bird colonies—while situating them within the longer history of human exploitation that had driven most edible bird species from the main atoll to this isolated refuge.

## Summary

Gibson-Hill describes North Keeling as a true coral island, approximately rectangular in shape and oriented so that its long axis deviates slightly east of north. The island is low and flat, rising steeply from the shore to a peripheral ridge of ten to fifteen feet, beyond which the ground slopes gently down to a large, shallow, sandy-bottomed lagoon occupying most of the interior. Gibson-Hill argues from structural evidence that the island was not originally a single landmass but formed from the union of two smaller islands on the leeward side, a conclusion supported by 1933 echo-soundings showing the submarine bank to be continuous with that of the main atoll (pp. 65–66). The vegetation is divided into four distinct zones: a broad *Pisonia-Cocos* belt running up the west side and round the north end; a *Tournefortia argentea* belt fringing the exposed coasts; clumps of *Pemphis acidula* at the north-east and south-east corners of the lagoon; and several open, grassy spaces. The soil is essentially absent, consisting of worn coral fragments sparingly mortared with decayed vegetable fibres, with no earthworms, termites, or centipedes present (pp. 68–69).

The fauna is dominated by sea-birds and crabs. Thirteen bird species were recorded as breeding on the island in 1941, the most abundant being the Red-footed Booby (*Sula sula rubripes*), with an estimated eight to ten thousand birds of all ages and just under four thousand breeding pairs, and two species of frigate-bird (*Fregata ariel* and *F. minor* subsp.), each maintaining a colony of approximately one thousand to one thousand two hundred nests in the *Pemphis* masses (pp. 77–80). Gibson-Hill documents the breeding seasons of each species in considerable detail, noting that most adhere to a definite, set rhythm—earlier on North Keeling than on Christmas Island, with peak egg-laying for boobies between April and June and for noddies from late December to February. The invertebrate fauna is strikingly impoverished: no rats, no lizards (confirmed), no centipedes, no millipedes, no termites, and no earthworms, in sharp contrast to the main atoll (p. 73). The crabs, however, are abundant and ecologically significant, with *Gecarcinoides humei natalis* performing the scavenging role that earthworms fill elsewhere, and *Coenobita* species swarming over any carrion within hours (pp. 69, 72).

A significant portion of the article is devoted to the historical interaction between the Cocos-Keeling islanders and North Keeling. Regular trips were made from the main atoll to hunt sea-birds—principally boobies and frigate-birds, knocked down with an eighteen-foot bamboo pole fitted with a brass chain—and later to collect *Cordia subcordata* timber for boat-building. These expeditions continued until 1929, when the death of the overseer (a younger brother of George Clunies-Ross) and the failure to find a replacement brought the work to a halt (pp. 67–68). Gibson-Hill notes that the twenty men who accompanied him on his two visits in 1941 took back approximately five hundred *Fregata* and *Sula* carcasses on each occasion, indicating that the pressure on the colonies remained intense (p. 97). The article also includes a detailed appendix on the wreck of the German light-cruiser *Emden*, which was beached on the reef fringing the south coast of North Keeling on 9 November 1914 after her defeat by the Australian cruiser *Sydney* (pp. 93–95).

### Key Findings

- The Red-footed Booby colony comprised an estimated 8,000–10,000 birds of all ages, with just under 4,000 breeding pairs; the two frigate-bird colonies each contained approximately 1,000–1,200 nests (pp. 77–80).
- The White Tern (*Gygis alba monte*) numbered between 200 and 300 pairs; the Masked Booby (*Sula dactylatra bedouti*) was estimated at no more than 50 pairs; the White-tailed Tropic-bird at no more than a dozen pairs (pp. 73–75, 85).
- The German light-cruiser *Emden* (3,592 tons) was beached on the North Keeling reef on 9 November 1914; approximately one-third of the men still aboard when the *Sydney* opened fire were killed or died shortly afterwards, and their remains were later buried on the shingle ridge (pp. 93–95).
- Timber collection (*Cordia subcordata*, *Morinda citrifolia*, *Erythrina variegata*) by Cocos-Keeling islanders ceased in 1929 following the death of the Clunies-Ross overseer; prior to that, gangs of forty to sixty Malays lived on the island for approximately three months each year (pp. 67–68).
- The island's lagoon contains no living coral; its maximum depth at low tide is less than six feet, and the bottom is a fine greyish sandy silt (p. 68).
- No rats, lizards, centipedes, millipedes, termites, or earthworms were found on North Keeling, in marked contrast to the main atoll, where all are present in fair numbers (p. 73).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive takeaway is that North Keeling functions as a critical refuge for sea-bird species that have been driven from the main atoll by sustained human predation. The island's inaccessibility—landing is possible only at restricted monsoon-change periods, and there is no supply of good fresh water—has preserved colonies that would otherwise have been exhausted. Yet even this refuge is under pressure: the 1941 visits alone removed roughly a thousand bird carcasses, and the absence of any natural predators other than crabs means that the colonies' recovery is entirely dependent on the continued difficulty of access rather than on any ecological self-regulation.

## Context

- The article draws on Gibson-Hill's own field observations from two visits in 1941, supplemented by earlier accounts from Guppy (1889) and Wood-Jones (1906, 1912), and by Darwin's 1836 visit to the main atoll. Specimens of the two frigate-birds and the shearwater were sent to Dr. R. C. Murphy at the New York Museum of Natural History for subspecific identification (p. 92).
- The work forms the second part of Gibson-Hill's broader *Notes on the Cocos-Keeling Islands* (JMBRAS Vol. 20, pt. 2, 1947, pp. 140–202), which covered the main atoll's history and description. It represents one of the few comprehensive faunal surveys of a remote Indian Ocean coral island produced during the wartime period, and its preparation was itself interrupted by the Japanese occupation of Singapore, during which the manuscript was preserved by the MBRAS clerk (p. 92).
