# Notes on the Cocos-Keeling Islands

C.A. Gibson-Hill's 1947 monograph "Notes on the Cocos-Keeling Islands" is a comprehensive ethnographic and historical account of the Cocos-Keeling atoll in the Indian Ocean, based on his residence on Pulo Tikus during the first ten and a half months of 1941. The work documents the physical environment, the full history of the settlement from William Keeling's discovery in 1609 through four generations of Clunies-Ross ownership, and the social, economic, linguistic, and cultural life of the Malay community on Pulo Selma as it stood on the eve of the Second World War — a community in prolonged economic decline and social stasis after more than a century of semi-isolation (pp. 133–135).

## Summary

Gibson-Hill traces the islands' history from their first European sighting by Captain William Keeling in 1609 through their permanent settlement in 1827 by John Clunies-Ross and Alexander Hare, who occupied the atoll jointly as a trading depot for eastern spices (pp. 139–143). Hare withdrew to Batavia by about 1832, leaving Clunies-Ross in sole possession. The settlement was formally incorporated into the British dominions in 1857, transferred to Ceylon in 1878, and finally placed under the Straits Settlements in 1903, though in practice the Clunies-Ross family retained effective control as private proprietors throughout (pp. 146–148). The islands' economy was built almost entirely on the coconut palm: at their height in the 1880s they exported roughly 700 tons of copra and 140 tons of coconut oil annually to Batavia, generating an estimated income exceeding £20,000 per year (p. 148).

The economic collapse began with the cyclone of 1909, which destroyed over 90 per cent of the coconut palms, and was compounded by the loss of the trading schooner *Ayesha* to the German cruiser *Emden* in 1914 and the post-war collapse in copra prices (pp. 154–155). By 1941 the annual export had fallen to just over 600 tons, yielding less than $10 per ton after freight and commission, against a population that had grown to over 1,450 (p. 156). The owner, J.S. Clunies-Ross, was drawing on his Christmas Island Phosphate Company dividends and borrowing against his life insurance to purchase rice (p. 155). The community was paid in a token coinage redeemable only at the proprietor's store, and an edict forbidding the return of any Malay who had once left the islands had been in force for nearly half a century (pp. 156–157).

Gibson-Hill's detailed observations of the 1941 kampong reveal a society of 243 identical houses arranged in parallel rows, governed by a local legal code that persisted long after formal incorporation into the Straits Settlements (pp. 156, 169). The inhabitants spoke a modified form of Batavian Malay with distinctive phonological shifts and a small number of words derived from English and Dutch (pp. 162–163). Their material culture — houses of local timber with coconut-frond thatch, *Dukong* sailing boats, and a diet centred on rice, fish, and coconut — reflected both the ingenuity of the community and the severe constraints of their environment and economic position (pp. 171–188).

### Key Findings

- The Cocos-Keeling Malay community in 1941 numbered over 1,450 people, descended largely from Javanese and Sumatran men brought as labour from the late 1820s onward, with a smaller admixture of Chinese, Papuan, Zulu, and European stocks traceable to Alexander Hare's harem and to visiting vessels (pp. 134, 158–160).
- The settlement's economy had deteriorated from an estimated annual income of over £20,000 in the 1880s to a position where the owner received less than $10 per ton of copra in 1941, and was forced to borrow against his life insurance to feed the population (pp. 148, 155–156).
- The 1909 cyclone destroyed approximately 800,000 coconut palms (or 200,000 by a more conservative estimate), and the resulting overgrowth of immature palms from sprouted nuts on fallen trees meant that most islands were so thick with non-bearing palms that yields remained depressed for decades (pp. 154, 177).
- The community's Malay exhibited systematic phonological changes from standard Batavian Malay, including initial consonant shifts (e.g., *ker* to *ger* in some words but not others), and had incorporated a small vocabulary of English and Dutch loanwords related to sailing and introduced customs (pp. 162–163).
- Rats introduced from the wrecked American schooner *Robert Portner* in 1878 had spread across all islands except Pulo Luar and were feeding on the stalks of growing coconut nuts, causing a high proportion to fall prematurely at about the size of an apple; by 1941 two men and eight dogs were employed to kill approximately 100 rats per week (pp. 149, 177–178).
- The kampong's social structure was characterised by a near-total absence of theft, no prostitution, and a strong norm of redistribution of surplus (fish, cigarettes, credit balances), with the worst social stigma being *sekikir* (meanness); the last murder had occurred nearly thirty years before Gibson-Hill's visit (p. 157).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive historical takeaway is that the Cocos-Keeling Islands in 1941 represented a private estate in advanced decline — a community that had once been prosperous and self-sufficient but had been reduced, by the combined effects of cyclone, war, falling commodity prices, and a century of isolation, to a state of "colourless, listless content" in which the best members would have escaped had they dared (p. 158). The work is explicitly framed as a record of "a world before the deluge came," anticipating that the military occupations of 1942–1944 and the death of the owner in 1944 would bring irreversible changes to a society that had developed in relative isolation since 1827 (pp. 135, 158).

## Context

- Gibson-Hill's original manuscript and a book-length study of the islands were lost from the Raffles Museum during the Japanese occupation of Malaya; this article was reconstructed from surviving notes and is therefore a partial but irreplaceable record of the pre-war settlement (p. 135).
- The article includes an appendix listing all de facto owners of the islands from 1825 to 1944, a selected bibliography of primary and secondary sources, a record of cyclones over the preceding century, and two word lists documenting local Malay terminology for dress and house construction (pp. 189–194).
