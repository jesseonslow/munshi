# Early Indian influence in Malaysia

D. F. A. Hervey published this translation of Valentyn's account of Malacca in 1884, presenting a comprehensive Dutch colonial-era description of the city, its governance, and its Malay inhabitants, supplemented by a lengthy genealogical narrative of the Malay kings from their legendary origins through the Portuguese conquest of 1511. The work draws on rare Malay manuscripts in Arabic script to reconstruct the pre-colonial political history of the Straits of Malacca region.

## Summary

Hervey's article is a translation (by Mr. Muller, Government Translator) of a section from Valentyn's multi-volume work on the Dutch East Indies, covering the territory of Malacca under Dutch VOC administration. The first portion describes the physical layout of the town—its crescent shape, the Portuguese-era fortress on the central hill, the two islets (Ilha das Naos and Ilha das Pedras) where carracks anchored, and the Chrysorant river—along with observations on the local economy, the Malay language as a lingua franca of the Eastern archipelago, and the social character of the Malay people as perceived by a seventeenth-century Dutch observer (pp. 1–8).

The second and more substantial portion is a genealogical and political history of the Malay kings, drawn from three rare Malay manuscripts in Arabic script: the *Tadjoo Esslatina* (Makota Segalla Radja), the *Misa Gomitar*, and the *Kitab Hantoowa* (Soolalet Issalathina). Valentyn traces the Malay people from their origin on the river Malayoo in Sumatra, through the founding of Singapura, the migration to the site of Malacca in 1253 under Siri Iskander Shah, the conversion to Islam under Sultan Mohammed Shah in 1276, and the successive reigns through to Sultan Mahmud Shah (pp. 15–22). The narrative culminates in a detailed account of the Portuguese arrival, the failed assassination plot against Jacob Sequeira in 1509, and Albuquerque's conquest of Malacca in 1511, after which Sultan Mahmud fled to found the new kingdom of Johor (pp. 23–29).

The article also includes extensive lists of VOC governors, supercargos, captains, shahbandars, attorney-generals, barristers, and treasurers of Malacca from 1641 to 1717, providing a valuable administrative record of the Dutch period (pp. 8–14).

### Key Findings

- The gross revenue of Malacca in 1669, consisting of 10 per cent import duty, 3 per cent export duty, and other small taxes, amounted to 74,958 guilders; 116 Javanese vessels arrived that year in addition to Danish, Portuguese, and Moorish shipping (p. 4).
- The town's population had declined from 12,000 souls in former times to no more than 200 or 300 families by Valentyn's time (c. 1669–1717) (p. 4).
- The tin trade at Perak was conducted at a rate of fifty Rix dollars per buhar; the VOC factory there was broken up after the murder of all its personnel in 1651 (p. 8).
- Valentyn records that the Portuguese captured 9,000 brass guns at Malacca in 1511, and that one-fifth of the total booty (the Crown's share) amounted to 200,000 ducats (p. 28).
- The genealogical narrative places the founding of Malacca at 1253 by Siri Iskander Shah, the conversion to Islam at 1276 under Sultan Mohammed Shah, and the Portuguese conquest at 1511, completing a period of 252 years under Malay kings (pp. 19, 29).
- Sultan Mansor Shah's reign is given as 73 years (1374–1447), the longest of any Malaccan king, during which Malacca rose to first rank among the great cities of the region, surpassing Pasi and Haru (pp. 21–22).

### Conclusion

Hervey's translation preserves a unique seventeenth-century Dutch perspective on Malacca that combines administrative record-keeping with ethnographic observation and a reconstruction of Malay political history from indigenous sources. The work's principal value lies in its transmission of the *Kitab Hantoowa* genealogy and its detailed account of the Portuguese conquest, both of which were otherwise inaccessible to English-speaking readers at the time of publication.

## Context

- The translation was made by Mr. Muller, Government Translator, and edited by D. F. A. Hervey, who notes that a partial translation had previously appeared in Logan's *Journal* (Vol. IV) but was incomplete and unreliable (p. 2).
- The article draws on Valentyn's access to three rare Malay manuscripts in Arabic script (*Tadjoo Esslatina*, *Misa Gomitar*, and *Kitab Hantoowa*), which Valentyn describes as "full of fictions and useless stories" yet "the best historical descriptions written in the Malay language" available to him (p. 15).
