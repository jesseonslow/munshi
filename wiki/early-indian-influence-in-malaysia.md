# Early Indian influence in Malaysia

R.J. Wilkinson, a senior colonial administrator writing in 1935, surveys the Indian cultural and political imprint on the Malay world from the first-century *Periplus* through the fourteenth-century Majapahit conquests, arguing that Indian influence arrived not through mass migration but through trade, religious conversion, and the arrival of the Shailendra dynasty, whose "coming" constituted the single most consequential event in pre-modern Malay political history (pp. 1–2, 8–9).

## Summary

Wilkinson opens by tracing the earliest external references to the Malay archipelago—the Greek *Periplus*, Ptolemy's geography, and the Sanskrit *Ramayana*—and uses the etymology of *Java-dvipa* ("land of millet") to illustrate how Northern Indian sailors projected their own agricultural vocabulary onto distant islands they knew only vaguely (p. 1). He then establishes that Indian traders dealt in jungle products—ivory, rhinoceros-horn, camphor, benzoin—gathered by Proto-Malay forest-nomads and bartered at river-mouth settlements such as Selensing, Palembang, and Jambi, while learned Brahmins and Buddhist monks gradually introduced Sanskrit and Hindu-Buddhist religion into local life without flooding the vernacular with Prakrit vocabulary (pp. 1–2). The middle section reconstructs the political geography of the fifth through seventh centuries: the Sumatran kingdom of Kandali (later identified with Sri Vijaya), the ancient state of Langkasuka in northern Malaya, and the parallel development of Java under the Sivaite Sanjaya and the Buddhist Shailendra (pp. 3–7).

The article's centrepiece is the Shailendra dynasty, which Wilkinson dates to between 741 and 775 A.D. and characterises as a conquering, sea-borne dynasty that imposed *nagari* script, Sanskrit as the official language, and Mahayana-Tantric Buddhism across Sumatra, Java, and the Malay Peninsula (pp. 8–9). In Java the Shailendra produced a brief "Golden Age" of monumental architecture—Kalasan, Borobodur, Prambanan—before being overthrown within a century; in Sumatra they became the long-ruling Maharajas of Sri Vijaya, a thalassocratic confederation of trading ports that controlled the Malacca Straits, the Sunda Strait, and the Kra land-route (pp. 9–10). Wilkinson draws on Arab geographers (Ibn Hordazbeh, Sulaiman, Ibn Rosteh, Abu Zaid) and Chinese Sung-dynasty records to depict a wealthy, lightly taxed, and relatively prosperous state whose kings derived revenue from maritime tolls rather than direct extraction of their subjects (pp. 10–12).

The final section narrates Sri Vijaya's decline through three successive shocks: the Javanese invasion under Dharmawangsa (c. 992–1007), the Chola raid by Rajendra-cola (c. 1024–1028) which destroyed Gangga-negara and Lenggiu and forced the capital's transfer from Palembang to Melayu (Jambi), and the Majapahit armadas of Hayam Wuruk (c. 1361–1377) which shattered the remaining polities and reduced Palembang to a pirate lair (pp. 13–15). Wilkinson closes by noting chronological discrepancies between Chinese and local sources for both the Chola War and the Majapahit invasion, underscoring the fragility of the evidential base (p. 16).

### Key Findings

- The name *Java-dvipa* derives from *yava* (sorghum), a Northern Indian grain, and was originally a generic term for the gold- and grain-rich islands beyond the Ganges before being narrowed to the island of Java proper (p. 1).
- The Kota Kapur inscription (686 A.D.), found on Bangka Island, records the suppression of a revolt in Java "which had not been loyal to Sri Vijaya" and invokes the gods as protectors of the state, confirming Sri Vijaya's suzerainty over Java at that date (p. 7).
- The Shailendra name appears in only four primary sources—two Central Javanese inscriptions (778, 782 A.D.), a ninth-century copper plate from Bengal, and a Negapatam inscription of 1008 A.D.—yet survived in the *gamelan salendro* orchestra and in the dynastic claims of every southern Malay sultanate (p. 8).
- Arab sources from the early tenth century describe the Maharaja of Zabaj (Sri Vijaya) as the wealthiest ruler in the Indies, who symbolically threw gold nuggets into a palace lagoon as a debt to the Ocean, and whose domain included Barus, Kedah, and numerous island trading ports (pp. 10–11).
- The Chola invasion of c. 1024–1028, recorded in Rajendra-cola's Tanjore inscription of 1030 A.D., captured Kedah, Sri Vijaya, Melayu, Tumasik (Singapore), and the Dindings, and destroyed Gangga-negara and Lenggiu permanently, after which Sri Vijaya sent no tribute missions to China for thirty years (pp. 13–14).
- The transfer of the capital from Palembang to Melayu (Jambi) at an uncertain date in the eleventh or twelfth century is the event by which the term *Melayu* became the national name for the entire Malay world, a shift confirmed by Marco Polo's 1292 reference to the "Melayu country" with no mention of Palembang (p. 14).

### Conclusion

Wilkinson's definitive takeaway is that the Shailendra "coming" was the one great formative event in Malay political history: it created the institution of royalty (*raja*) where previously only non-royal chiefs (*yang-dipertuan*) had existed, established the Buddhist-Mahayana religious and administrative culture that coloured all subsequent Malay statecraft, and produced the thalassocratic model of governance that defined Sri Vijaya's seven-century span (pp. 8–9). The subsequent decline was not the result of internal decay but of Sri Vijaya's structural vulnerability as a dispersed confederation of ports, which successive external powers—Java, Chola, Siam, and finally Majapahit—exploited in turn until the old order was irrecoverably broken (pp. 12–15).

## Context

- Wilkinson was a senior colonial official (C.M.G.) and the author of the standard *A History of the Malay Peoples* (1920, revised 1933); this article draws heavily on his own earlier identification of Sri Vijaya with Malay history, first published in 1908 (p. 6).
- The article relies on the foundational scholarship of George Coedes (particularly his 1918 monograph on Sri Vijaya) and H.G. Quaritch Wales's predecessor Col. J.R. Logan, as well as the epigraphic work of H.G. Krom; it represents the state of Sri Vijaya studies as understood in the mid-1930s colonial academy (pp. 6–7).
