# An old minute by Sir Stamford Raffles

H.A. O’Brien’s 1891 article in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society presents a critical transcription of a foundational 1823 Minute by Sir Stamford Raffles. Set against the backdrop of early colonial Singapore, the document outlines a legal philosophy that prioritizes moral obligation and local customs over rigid European formalities. O’Brien argues that Raffles’ views on deportation and the Malay character remain as deserving of study in 1891 as they were in the Settlement’s infancy (p. 2).

## Summary

Raffles’ Minute establishes a fundamental principle that laws must align with moral obligation rather than mere legal formalities, ensuring justice is not inconsistent with the benevolence of British rule (p. 3). He argues that while individuals may protect themselves, redress for wrongs must be administered solely by Government through appointed Judicial and Executive Officers to prevent private revenge (p. 4). This system aims to secure the safety of person and property without enduring local usages that endanger liberty, ensuring legal and moral obligations never vary (p. 3).

Specific regulations were proposed to suppress temptations to crime, including the prohibition of habitual weapon-wearing by Malays and the suppression of gaming and cockfighting (p. 4–5). Intoxication was to be managed by constraining public inebriates and fining vendors, while prostitution was regulated to prevent third-party profiteering (p. 5–6). Furthermore, the Minute mandated standard weights and measures, a general Registry Office for written agreements, and the registration of all house-holders to facilitate fraud detection and property security (p. 7).

In defining rights and punishments, Raffles affirmed the absolute rights of security, liberty, and property for all classes under the British Flag (p. 8–9). He advocated for a penal code that avoided degrading punishments, respecting the Malay sensitivity to shame, and emphasized compensation to injured parties alongside offender punishment (p. 9–11). The document concluded with instructions for Magistrates to deliberate on a graduated Scale of Crimes and Punishments to be established as a Code of Laws for the Settlement (p. 11–12).

### Key Findings

*   Raffles proposed banning the habitual wearing of weapons like the kris to reduce violence (p. 4).
*   Gaming and cockfighting were to be suppressed as they incited fraud and robbery (p. 5).
*   Intoxication laws included fines for vendors and constraint for public inebriates (p. 5–6).
*   A general Registry Office was proposed for written agreements to prevent fraud (p. 7).
*   Punishments were to be graduated, with death reserved for murder and banishment as the next severe penalty (p. 11).
*   The Minute was dated June 1823 and instructed Magistrates to frame a Code of Laws for the Settlement (p. 11–12).

### Conclusion

Raffles’ Minute establishes a paternalistic yet liberal legal framework that sought to harmonize British rule with local moral sensibilities. It prioritized social stability and victim compensation over punitive severity, laying the groundwork for Singapore’s early legal identity.
