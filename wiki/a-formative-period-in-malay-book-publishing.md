# A formative period in Malay book publishing

I. Proudfoot (1986) examines the transformation of the Malay-language book publishing industry in the Straits Settlements and peninsular Malay states between 1887 and 1920, arguing that a vigorous indigenous printing trade was progressively displaced by well-capitalized European firms whose dominance was secured through the colonial education system and government cultural policy.

## Summary

Proudfoot approaches Malay publishing as an industrial activity—using particular technologies to produce literary goods for consumers—rather than through the more familiar lenses of literary history or biographical study. Drawing on Government Gazette registration data from the Straits Settlements and Federated Malay States, supplemented by physical examination of surviving legal deposit copies, the article identifies a three-tiered structure: a concentrated, capital-intensive European sector employing letterpress; a more dispersed indigenous sector relying on lithography and the jawi script; and a small, under-capitalized Chinese sector producing serialized Baba-language romances. By measuring output in terms of bulk (pages multiplied by copies) and value (price multiplied by copies) rather than mere edition counts, the analysis reveals a hierarchy far steeper than edition numbers alone would suggest.

The central narrative traces how the indigenous industry, initially dominant in the General and Kitab categories, was undermined by a confluence of structural changes. The colonial government's expansion of European-style vernacular schooling created a captive textbook market that fell to European firms, while the promotion of Romanized script in education effectively closed off an entire field of publication to indigenous printers, who had never produced rumi books and whose lithographic technology could not accommodate them. Simultaneously, the rising production costs of the transition from lithography to letterpress—driven partly by inflation, partly by the technology shift itself—eroded the indigenous printers' cost advantage. An economic slump in the mid-1890s exposed the sector's vulnerability to market forces, but it was the more durable changes of the 1910s that proved fatal.

As the book trade passed into European hands, the periodical press emerged as an alternative outlet for Malay literary creativity. Newspapers adopted letterpress from the outset and depended on school subscriptions and the expanding postal infrastructure for survival. The article thus documents a formative period in which the fundamental characteristics of the modern Malay publishing industry—its technology, script, institutional relationships, and commercial structure—were settled, and in which the colonial schooling system became the decisive force shaping the industry's composition.

### Key Findings

- The sample comprises 459 editions of Malay-language books (1887–1920), with General books (39%), Kitab (17%), and Mission (16%) the largest categories by edition count; by bulk, General (36%) and School (22%) dominate, while by value, General (29%) and Bilingual (23%) lead (pp. 104–107).
- European printers (principally the Methodist Publishing House and Kelly & Walsh) accounted for 64% of bulk output and 67% of value, while indigenous printers managed only 26% of bulk and 16% of value, despite being nearly equal in edition count (42% vs. 47%) (pp. 107–108).
- The Malay Literature Series, launched in 1906 with government assistance, produced typeset rumi editions at a weighted average price of 0.40 cents per page (1906–1920), compared to 1.61 cents for other rumi General books in the open market—a disparity reflecting the guaranteed school market (p. 120).
- Indigenous printers' weighted average price per page rose by 96% over the period, compared to +37% for European and +51% for Chinese printers; the Kitab and General categories saw increases of +49% and +67% respectively, far exceeding the ~30% currency devaluation (p. 121).
- The 1896–1900 slump saw indigenous printers retreat into smaller, less efficient editions (lower capital per edition, higher cost per page), with several printers closing down; General-category newspaper circulation fell to its lowest point for the entire 1886–1920 span, with no issues recorded for 1898 and 1899 (pp. 116–117).
- By 1920, periodical production had grown to approximate the bulk of book publication; Utusan Melayu and Lembaga Melayu provided a combined circulation of 1,500–2,000, suggesting the emergence of a self-supporting press, whereas earlier titles like Jawi Peranakan and Sekola Melayu depended critically on government school subscriptions of only 250 copies each (pp. 124–126).

### Conclusion

Proudfoot's definitive historical takeaway is that the indigenous Malay book-printing industry, which flourished under the commercial stability of early colonial rule, was ultimately destroyed not by market failure per se but by the combination of colonial government cultural policy and the superior capitalization of European enterprises. The expansion, centralization, and manipulation of the vernacular school system, with its "increasingly voracious appetite for prescribed teaching materials," determined the commercial environment of the book's decline and the newspaper's birth. The links forged between the printing industry and the school system during this transitional period became a persistent structural feature of the publishing industry that endured well beyond 1920 (p. 128).

## Context

- Primary source base: Government Gazette registration data (SSGG from 1887; FMSGG from 1915) cross-referenced with physical examination of surviving legal deposit copies to determine printing technique (letterpress vs. lithography) and script (jawi, rumi, pegon), since the Gazettes record neither.
- Historiographical contribution: Shifts the study of Malay publishing from literary-historical and biographical approaches to an industrial-economic analysis, establishing the three-tier model and demonstrating how colonial education policy structurally determined the industry's composition—a framework that subsequent scholarship on Malay print culture has built upon.
