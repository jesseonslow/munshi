# Two Sea-Dayak legends. {With a note H.N. Ridley

Rev. Edwin H. Gomes published these two Sea-Dyak legends in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1904, presenting oral traditions collected among the Sea-Dyak people of Borneo that function as aetiological myths explaining core cultural practices. The overarching thesis is that these narratives—believed by the Dyaks to be historically true rather than merely fabulous—encode the origins of paddy cultivation, bird-omen divination, and the taboo against consuming unclaimed jungle fruit. A brief note by H. N. Ridley appends the text, raising questions about the tiger motif's possible Malay provenance.

## Summary

Gomes opens by dividing Sea-Dyak oral literature into two classes: purely entertaining animal tales (featuring the *Plandok* and *Kekura* as trickster figures analogous to Brer Rabbit) and narratives the Dyaks regard as true accounts of their gods and preternatural beings, which constitute their mythology proper (p. 1). The two legends presented here belong to the second class, specifically to the subset that provides causal explanations for observed customs.

The first legend, "Danjai and the Were-Tiger's Sister," narrates how the great chief Danjai's young wife, unaware of a Were-Tiger's territorial claim on jungle fruit, eats rambutan left on a tree stump and is consequently beheaded by the creature. Danjai tracks the tiger to its longhouse, where the tiger's sister warns him of three successive assassination attempts (poisoned rice, a sword stroke at night, repeated over three nights) and ultimately instructs him to kill the Were-Tiger with the latter's own sword, to which he is otherwise invulnerable. In exchange for the tiger's head, Danjai receives his wife's head and the sister's hand in marriage, along with a hair-charm for invisibility in warfare (pp. 2–11). The legend explicitly states its purpose: it explains why Dyaks to this day dare not eat fruit found lying on stumps or rocks in the jungle (p. 11).

The second legend, "The Story of Siu," is a far more elaborate cosmogonic narrative. Siu, a handsome chief's son, accidentally stumbles upon the longhouse of Singalang Burong, Ruler of the Spirit World, whose inhabitants can transform into birds. Siu marries Singalang Burong's youngest daughter, Bunsu Burong, who exacts a promise that he never touch or kill a bird. When Siu breaks this promise by stroking a caged bird, his wife vanishes back to the spirit world. Their son Seragunting—growing at a supernatural rate—travels to find her, aided by a spider and a fly, and proves his divine lineage before his uncles (the sons-in-law of Singalang Burong) through ordeals involving tops, wrestling, and a boar hunt. Singalang Burong then instructs Siu and Seragunting in paddy cultivation, warfare, fishing, and the reading of bird omens, presenting them with seed paddy and protective charms before they return to the mortal world (pp. 12–28). The legend concludes with an explicit statement of its aetiological function: it marks the transition from a primitive existence on jungle fruits and yams to an agricultural civilisation guided by divine omens (p. 28).

### Key Findings

- Gomes identifies a clear typology in Sea-Dyak oral tradition: Class I (fabulous animal tales with *Plandok*/*Kekura* trickster pairs) and Class II (mythological narratives believed true, including the adventures of the hero *Klieng* and his wife *Kumang*, and the two legends presented here) (p. 1).
- The Were-Tiger legend encodes a specific behavioural taboo: Dyaks must never consume fruit found on stumps, rocks, or pathsides without verifying its origin, under threat of death or misfortune (p. 11).
- The legend of Siu provides the mythological foundation for the Dyak practice of reading bird omens, naming seven sacred birds—*Katupong, Beragai, Bejampong, Papau, Nendak, Pangkas,* and *Embuas*—after the sons-in-law of Singalang Burong, whose cries are interpreted as divine commands governing all activities from farming to warfare (p. 27).
- Singalang Burong is explicitly identified as "the Ruler of the Spirit World" and "God of War," requiring offerings before combat and feasts, establishing a structured system of reciprocal obligation between humans and the spirit realm (p. 27).
- The Dyak custom of headhunting to end mourning is embedded in both legends: in the first, Danjai must bring a woman's head to the Were-Tiger's sister to end her mourning; in the second, the men of Singalang Burong's house depart on a raid to obtain heads to end their own mourning (pp. 10, 18).
- Ridley's appended note highlights that the tiger is "quite unknown in Borneo," suggesting possible Malay origin for the first legend, though Gomes counters by citing the tree-tiger (*Felis marmorata*), known to Dyaks as *Kemaung dau* or *Kemaung raras*, and references A. H. Everett's earlier paper on the subject (p. 29).

### Conclusion

Gomes presents these legends not merely as folklore curiosities but as the living mythological substrate of Sea-Dyak culture, each narrative performing a specific aetiological function: one explains a forest taboo, the other the entire agricultural and divinatory system. The definitive historical takeaway is that the Sea-Dyaks understood their civilisational transition—from foraging to paddy cultivation, from blind existence to omen-guided action—as a divine gift mediated through the figure of Singalang Burong, and that this origin story remained actively believed and narrated in the early twentieth century.

## Context

- The publication appears in Volume 41 of the JSBRAS (January 1904), a period when the Society was actively collecting and publishing indigenous oral traditions from across the Straits Settlements and Borneo, often contributed by missionaries and colonial administrators with direct field access.
- Ridley's note situates the text within a broader comparative framework, drawing on Everett's 1890s work on Bornean fauna and mythology, and comparing the tiger motif to traditions among the Mias (Mawas) of the Malay Peninsula, reflecting the era's interest in tracing cultural diffusion across the archipelago.
