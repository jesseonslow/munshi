# Malay precedence and the federal formula in the Federated Malay States, 1909 to 1939

Philip Loh Fook-Seng's 1972 article examines the tension between British rhetorical commitments to Malay sovereignty and the practical centralization of power in the Federated Malay States between 1909 and 1939, arguing that the "Malay precedence" formula was simultaneously a genuine policy constraint and a strategic instrument for managing ethnic politics. The study demonstrates that the failure to federate all nine Malay States was not a matter of British incapacity but a deliberate preference for persuasion over force, rooted in Indirect Rule ideology and personal relationships with Malay royalty.

## Summary

The article traces how the F.M.S. federal structure, established in 1895, progressively eroded the precedence of the Malay Sultans relative to their counterparts in the Unfederated Malay States, particularly Johore. The 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty completed British control over the Peninsula, but the U.M.S. rejected federal membership, preferring the Johore model of a British Adviser without a federal bureaucracy. The British response was not coercion but a sustained, nearly thirty-year search for a "looser" federal formula that would reassure the reluctant states. This search was shaped by the persistent official language of Malay sovereignty—articulated first by Swettenham's "heir to the inheritance" concept and reinforced by Treacher's policy of encouraging Malay entry into the administrative service—which created expectations that the Sultans then tested against the reality of a centralized federal administration dominated by the Chief Secretary in Kuala Lumpur.

The Guillemard-Maxwell conflict of the 1920s emerges as the central drama of the article. Guillemard's decentralization program was entangled with his personal rivalry with Maxwell, the Chief Secretary, whose centralized management of the F.M.S. served British commercial interests effectively but contradicted the official narrative of Malay sovereignty. The Sultan of Perak's 1924 trip to London crystallized the Rulers' grievances: the richest state in the Federation could not spend its own revenues without federal approval, and its State Council had become a body "with practically no work to do." Clementi's more aggressive decentralization in the early 1930s was explicitly motivated by the need to use revitalized Malay Sultanates as a "buffer" against Chinese political agitation, a rationale the Colonial Office found troubling but ultimately endorsed in principle.

The article's analytical payoff lies in its demonstration that "Malay precedence" was neither mere illusion nor straightforward reality but a productive ambiguity. The British used the language of sovereignty to justify intervention in Malay internal affairs (as in the Selangor Succession of 1936, where the Colonial Office overrode the Sultan's preference for his heir), while simultaneously deploying the same language to constrain the pace of constitutional change and to manage the anxieties of a non-Malay population that feared discrimination under a devolved system. Loh Fook-Seng concludes that this formula "has filtered through to become, in our time, a major theme in the fabric of Malaysian politics."

### Key Findings

- The 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty transferred suzerainty over Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan, and Trengganu from Siam to Britain, completing formal British control of the Peninsula; the U.M.S. subsequently rejected federal membership in favour of the Johore Adviser model (pp. 29–30).
- The Malay College Kuala Kangsar was founded in 1905 with federal funds; its "Malays only" admission policy became restrictive from 1909, limited to sons of Rulers and Chiefs until 1920, when a 50% quota for non-aristocratic Malays was introduced. The Malay Administrative Service, established in 1910, recruited only 3–4 candidates per year through 1927 (pp. 30–31).
- The 1931 census recorded the F.M.S. population as 41.5% Chinese, 34.7% Malay, 22.2% Indian, and 1.6% other—a demographic reality that underpinned both the "buffer" rationale for preserving Malay Sultanates and the non-Malay fear of discrimination under devolved state governments (p. 47).
- Clementi's 1932 decentralization programme proposed transferring Agriculture, Education, Electricity, Forests, Medical Services, Mines, Public Works, and Veterinary Services from federal to state control, along with the abolition of the Chief Secretary post; in practice, only the expansion of State Councils and the abolition of the Chief Secretary were achieved, with education and agriculture remaining federally financed (pp. 43–48).
- In the Selangor Succession of 1936, the Colonial Office removed Resident Theodore Adams from Selangor to preserve the Sultan's dignity but upheld his "advice" to install the third son as Raja Muda over the Sultan's preferred candidate, with Under-Secretary Edward Gent minuting that "the ultimate power exercisable in Selangor through the British Resident in that State is now open to question" was not admissible (pp. 49–50).
- Clementi was "ordered to go on pension" before completing his term in 1934 after the Secretary of State, Cunliffe-Lister, instructed him not to establish an Executive Council in the F.M.S.; the Colonial Office's marginal comment on his assertion that Britain was "bound in honour" to adopt his policy read: "He has no earthly right to say this" (pp. 44–47).

### Conclusion

Loh Fook-Seng's definitive takeaway is that the "Malay precedence" formula was a structurally ambiguous instrument: it constrained British administrative centralization enough to preserve the political legitimacy of the Sultanates, yet it was flexible enough to permit the Colonial Office to override Malay sovereign prerogatives whenever British interests demanded. The 1936 Selangor Succession resolved any residual illusion of genuine sovereignty, yet the formula's persistence into the twentieth century ensured it became a foundational element of post-independence Malaysian constitutional politics.

## Context

- The article draws extensively on Colonial Office archives (C.O. 717 and C.O. 273 series), including secret despatches, private correspondence between Guillemard and the Permanent Under-Secretary, and minutes from the 1920s and 1930s, much of which had not been previously published in scholarly form.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the early literature on Malayan constitutional history by foregrounding the internal British administrative conflict (Guillemard vs. Maxwell, Clementi vs. the Colonial Office) as the engine of constitutional change, rather than treating Malay Ruler resistance as the primary explanatory variable.
