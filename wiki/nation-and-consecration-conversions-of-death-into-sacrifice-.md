# Nation and consecration: conversions of death into sacrifice in Singapore

## Abstract

In an era of increasing scepticism of racism and primordialism, national narratives have urgent reasons to move from arbitrariness to motivation. Memorials to blood sacrificed have long valorized many nations, and sanctified their senses of destiny. In Singapore, prosperity and growth have fuelled the nation, but claims of sacrifice motivate and even justify its independent existence. Lee Kuan Yew was a principal architect of the national narrative, including the creation of a monument (1967) memorializing the civilians killed by Japanese occupiers during World War II—not only the thousands of Chinese caught up in a purge early in the occupation but also the Malays, Indians, and Eurasians who perished, a fourfold sacrifice for the nation. Following his death in March 2015, the Singaporean public viewed Lee’s own life as a sacrifice to them, creating a debt that they must redeem by maintaining the nation he helped to build, a nation established not by race, heritage, accident, or exclusion but by duties embraced within mutual commitment.

## Summary

John Kelly examines how Singapore converts death into sacrifice to motivate and justify its national existence, reading across three ritual forms that converged in 2015: the funeral and memorial tributes to Lee Kuan Yew following his death in March, the SG50 national anniversary celebrations, and the September 2015 parliamentary general election. Because Singapore explicitly abjures claims of nationhood by blood, kinship, or ethnic heritage, its stories of blood sacrificed carry particular weight in consecrating the nation and its future. Kelly argues that the public's response to LKY's death—expressed as personal debt and obligation—demonstrated what he calls "civic sincerity," a mode of political engagement that overtopped state orchestration and revealed the genuine motivational force of sacrifice narratives in a prosperous, technocratic state (pp. 84–85, 99–100).

The article traces the layered history of Singapore's war memorials to show how different groups' wartime deaths have been included, excluded, or transformed in the national narrative. The Cenotaph (1922) honoured only European dead; the Kranji War Cemetery memorializes Commonwealth forces with a heavy South Asian presence; the Civilian War Memorial (1967) was designed with a four-fold parallel structure representing Chinese, Eurasian, Indian, and Malay civilian suffering under Japanese occupation. Meanwhile, the Syonan Chureito (Japanese war dead) was destroyed by the Japanese themselves before the war's end, and the Indian National Army memorial was demolished by Mountbatten within hours of the British flag being raised over Singapore in September 1945. No memorial exists for Communist or pro-Communist deaths, including those of the over 100 Leftists arrested in Cold Store operations (pp. 86–98).

Kelly situates these consecrations within Singapore's unique property regime, in which the state owned over 90 per cent of the island's land by 2002 and 86 per cent of citizens lived in public housing while 90 per cent were homeowners. This "property state" logic, combined with a trusteeship ideology oriented toward future generations rather than present electoral mandates, creates a political universe in which sacrifice narratives serve to motivate citizens toward maintaining a nation established by mutual commitment rather than accident or exclusion (pp. 94–96, 104–105).

### Key Findings

- The Singapore Cenotaph was unveiled in 1922 in the presence of Prince Edward of Wales, inscribed with the names of 124 European "Singaporeans" who died in World War I; its Chinese-language inscription, selected by bureaucrat S. C. A. Adkins, deliberately rendered "They died that we might live" as a four-character phrase meaning "They sacrificed themselves for the public good" (pp. 95–96).
- The Civilian War Memorial (nicknamed "Chopsticks"), designed by Leong Swee Lim and opened on 15 February 1967, was the tallest structure in its environment at the time and features a four-fold parallel structure representing the four ethnic groups; ashes of Sook Ching victims are buried beneath it, marked by a central urn (pp. 96–97, 98).
- The INA memorial, modelled on the Cenotaph and sited adjacent to it, was demolished by the 74 Field Company at 5:40 pm on 6 September 1945—just over two hours after Mountbatten raised the Union Jack over City Hall at 3:20 pm the same day; Mountbatten ordered it broken into rubble so fine its shape could not be reconstituted (p. 97).
- The Syonan Chureito, a unitary obelisk on the highest point of central Singapore near the Ford Factory surrender site, was constructed in 1942; the Japanese themselves destroyed it before the war's end and moved cremated ashes to the Japanese Cemetery in Hougang (p. 97).
- In the 2015 parliamentary election, the PAP won 83 of 89 seats with 69.9 per cent of the overall vote, rebounding from its independence low of 60.1 per cent in 2011; the Workers' Party held its six seats, five by margins of 51–49 per cent (pp. 92–93).
- By 2002, the Singaporean state owned more than 90 per cent of the island's land; as of 2000, 86 per cent of citizens lived in public housing and 90 per cent were homeowners, a property regime Anne Haila identifies as unique worldwide (pp. 94–95).

### Conclusion

Kelly's definitive takeaway is that Singapore's sacrifice narratives are not merely state propaganda but constitute a genuine social contract in which citizens recognize specific debts and embrace duties toward the nation's future. The overwhelming momentum of grief, nostalgia, and prosperity surrounding LKY's death made the PAP's electoral reversal unsurprising, but the sustainability of Singapore's forms of property and propriety may now hinge on how the state handles the real personal relationships and sincere reflections attracted by its own efflorescence—relationships that cannot be reduced to the impersonal logic of the global market (p. 105).

## Context

- Kelly draws on his own fieldwork observations in Singapore in March–April 2015 (funeral queues, memorial sites, SG50 events) supplemented by archival and secondary sources on Singapore's war memorials, including the work of Pitt and Leong (2012), Blackburn and Hack (2012), and Haila (2016) on Singapore's property regime.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Singaporean nationalism by arguing that the "twilight zone" between memory and history (Hobsbawm's 50–70 year window) makes the politics of 50-year anniversaries particularly revealing for understanding how nations calibrate their narratives of sacrifice and obligation.
