# Dogs

Dogs have occupied a distinctive and enduring position in the domestic, ritual, and ecological landscapes of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo, a presence attested from the Neolithic period through to the mid-twentieth century. The archaeological record reveals a small, barkless, dun-coloured domestic type that persisted in isolated communities for millennia before being progressively diluted by crossbreeding with imported breeds [[1]](#ref-1). In the historic period, dogs served as supplementary food animals in ceremonial contexts, as subjects of genetic study, and as integral components of the material culture of nomadic and semi-nomadic peoples of Borneo [[2]](#ref-2), [[1]](#ref-1). The breadth of evidence—spanning skeletal remains from Sarawak caves, nineteenth-century travel accounts, mounted museum specimens, and twentieth-century fieldwork—establishes the dog as one of the most archaeologically visible domestic animals in the region [[1]](#ref-1).

## Archaeological Evidence and Ritual Consumption

The most significant archaeological assemblage of domestic dogs in Malaysia derives from the cave sites of Sarawak, where skeletal material from Niah, Lubang Kudih, Lubang Sirih, and the Bau district has been subjected to metrical comparison [[1]](#ref-1). The site of Lubang Kudih, a sepulchral cave used in the late fifteenth century, yielded the remains of 24 individual dogs represented exclusively by lower jaws, alongside 94 pigs [[1, pp. 17–18]](#ref-1). Lord Medway interprets this pattern—by analogy with the pig remains from the same site—as evidence that dog meat was ceremonially consumed at funerary feasts, indicating that the Kudih people constituted a sophisticated settled culture for which the dog was a supplementary food animal alongside domestic pigs [[1, pp. 17–18]](#ref-1).

The Kudih dogs were notably smaller than the Neolithic specimens from Niah West Mouth, which date to approximately 4000–250 B.C. and exhibit lower-jaw measurements falling in the upper range or above those of the Kudih homologues [[1, p. 24]](#ref-1). This metric difference suggests that the Kudih type was a later, more specialised derivative of the earlier Niah form. The Kudih dogs displayed extreme dental crowding, with overlapping crowns of the fourth premolar and first molar, and in two specimens the second premolar was congenitally absent—traits Medway reads as products of prolonged domestication rather than natural variation [[1, p. 18]](#ref-1). These features, taken together, indicate a population under sustained selective pressure over many generations.

## The Ancient Domestic Type and Its Survival

The central question addressed in the literature is one of continuity: whether the small dog type identified in Bornean archaeological deposits persisted into the historic period or vanished without trace. Tom Harrisson had previously noted that the only dogs found in Bornean archaeological deposits were a small form resembling Japanese Neolithic types, and that this type "has vanished from the Bornean and Southeast Asian scene without a trace" [[1]](#ref-1). Medway challenges this conclusion by assembling all known skeletal material and comparing it metrically with historic museum specimens and living dogs examined in the 1960s [[1]](#ref-1).

For the historic and recent period, the evidence draws on nineteenth-century travel accounts by Beccari, Lumholtz, and St. John, early twentieth-century photographs, mounted specimens in the Taiping museum, and Medway's own fieldwork among the Semai and Semelai of Pahang [[1]](#ref-1). The Taiping museum specimens, identified as "Sakai dogs" from the turn of the twentieth century, measured 66½–73 cm in head-and-body length with shoulder heights of 26–31½ cm, confirming the small size attested in Senoi oral tradition [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1). The breed is classified within the "pariah group" of dogs originally distributed from the Indochinese peninsula to the Middle East, with close relatives documented in Java, Sumatra, and Thailand [[1, p. 25]](#ref-1).

Linguistic evidence provides independent support for the great antiquity of dog domestication among the Orang Asli. Dentan observed that all Senoi dialects retain a non-Malay cognate root for "dog" while borrowing Malay terms for other domestic animals, a pattern consistent with a long and independent history of dog keeping predating the adoption of other livestock from Malay sources [[1]](#ref-1).

## Genetic Confirmation and the Telomian Dogs

The existence of a discrete breed rather than a random mongrel population was confirmed through genetic study. The Telomian dogs, sent to the Jackson Laboratory in Bar Harbor, Maine, in 1963, proved genetically uniform across first- and second-generation crosses, demonstrating that the population constituted a genuine breed with a stable genetic identity [[1, p. 23]](#ref-1). This finding resolved a question that had persisted since the early twentieth century, when the small dogs of the Malay Peninsula were variously described by travellers but never subjected to systematic genetic analysis.

The primary material for these comparisons derives from the Sarawak Museum excavation collections at Niah, Lubang Kudih, and Lubang Sirih, the British Museum (Natural History) osteology collection—including Everett's 1878–79 Paku material and the skeleton of a specimen registered as 'Manggis' (reg. no. 71.753)—and the Taiping museum mounted specimens [[1]](#ref-1). The metrical framework employed by Medway, comparing mandibular and dental measurements across time periods, provides the first comprehensive quantitative basis for assessing the continuity of the Malaysian domestic dog type from the Neolithic to the present [[1]](#ref-1).

## Dogs in the Context of Nomadic Communication Systems

Beyond the archaeological and zoological dimensions, dogs feature in the ethnographic record of Borneo's nomadic peoples as participants in complex social and communicative systems. Tom Harrisson's 1965 study of three "secret" communication systems among Borneo nomads includes dogs as integral elements of these systems, suggesting that the animals were not merely utilitarian companions but active participants in the social architecture of mobile communities [[2]](#ref-2). This dimension of the dog's role—embedded in the communicative and ritual life of nomadic groups—complements the archaeological evidence of ceremonial consumption and the genetic evidence of breed stability, together painting a picture of an animal whose significance extended well beyond simple domestication.

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on dogs in the Malay Peninsula and Borneo reflects a progression from incidental observation to systematic investigation. Early contributions, such as W.G. Maxwell's 1906 note on the etymology of *kun* and *payah kun* in *JSBRAS*, demonstrate that even the linguistic and religious vocabulary surrounding domestic animals attracted scholarly attention in the Straits Branch period, though the note concerns the *pawang* (shamanic practitioner) tradition rather than the animal itself [[3]](#ref-3). The substantive archaeological and zoological investigation began in earnest with the excavation of Sarawak cave sites in the early twentieth century, which produced the skeletal material later analysed by Medway [[1]](#ref-1).

The pivotal shift occurred in the 1960s, when Harrisson's ethnographic work on nomadic communication systems brought dogs into the frame of social and cultural analysis [[2]](#ref-2), and Medway's metrical comparison of ancient and modern specimens established the continuity of the domestic type [[1]](#ref-1). The 1963–67 genetic study at the Jackson Laboratory provided the definitive confirmation that the Telomian dogs constituted a discrete breed [[1, p. 23]](#ref-1). Together, these contributions transformed the dog from a marginal footnote in the archaeological record into a subject of sustained interdisciplinary inquiry, spanning osteology, genetics, linguistics, and ethnography. The historiographical significance of Medway's 1977 article lies in its provision of the first comprehensive metrical comparison of ancient and modern Malaysian dogs and in its establishment of the Kudih site as evidence of ritual dog consumption in a fifteenth-century Bornean culture [[1]](#ref-1).
