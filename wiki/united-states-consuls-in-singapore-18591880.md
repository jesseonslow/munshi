# United States consuls in Singapore, 1859–1880

Pamela Sodhy's 1983 article examines the four United States consuls who served in Singapore between 1859 and 1880—John P. O'Sullivan, Isaac Stone, James Grey Jewell, and Adolphus G. Studer—drawing primarily on consular despatches held at the National Archives in Washington. The article argues that these consuls personified the dual character of the United States in this period: a commercial power seeking to protect and expand trade in a strategically vital free port, and an aspiring political power whose imperial ambitions were frustrated by the dominance of European colonial powers in Southeast Asia.

## Summary

Sodhy situates the Singapore consulate within the broader hierarchy of American diplomatic posts, noting that Southeast Asia was regarded as a backwater compared to European capitals, with Siam ranking as the most important post in the region. The consuls were political appointees of independent means, often with personal connections to the President or State Department officials, and they operated under severe constraints: low salaries, minimal staffing (typically just the consul and a peon), slow communications, and the necessity of conducting relations through British colonial authorities rather than directly with local governments. The despatches reveal consuls who were overworked, underpaid, and frequently frustrated, yet who nonetheless carried out a wide range of duties including commercial reporting, settling disputes among American sailors, attending to shipwrecks and piracy, managing estates of deceased Americans, and handling diplomatic contacts with other consular representatives.

A significant thread in the article concerns the consuls' attempts to elevate American prestige in the region. Jewell and Studer both advocated for the establishment of a naval base in Borneo, with Jewell proposing the purchase of land at the mouth of the Brunei River from the Sultan for $50,000. Studer expressed the hope that the United States would gain "a foothold, a naval station or colony, somewhere in these regions," a remark that Sodhy notes was marked with parallel lines in the margin by a State Department official, suggesting it was considered significant. Neither proposal was acted upon, and the despatches make no further mention of a naval base after 1880.

The consuls also served as informal observers of Singapore's social and commercial life, providing Washington with detailed accounts of the city's cosmopolitan population, its free-port status, and the political developments in the Malay States that would lead to British intervention. Their reports on the tin-mining conflicts in Perak, Selangor, and Larut, as well as on the German commercial competition noted in the *Straits Times*, offer a contemporary American perspective on the events that culminated in the Pangkor Engagement of 1874.

### Key Findings

- The Act of August 18, 1856 (effective January 1, 1857) raised the consul's salary from $1,000 to $2,500 per annum and prohibited private trade, ending the earlier practice of consuls supplementing their income through commerce (p. 4).
- In 1858, 108 American vessels totalling 81,241 tons arrived at Singapore; by 1859 this had fallen to 92 vessels and 74,330 tons (p. 10).
- Jewell proposed in 1870 that the United States purchase a ten-mile-square tract at the mouth of the Brunei River from the Sultan of Brunei for $50,000 to establish a naval station (p. 16).
- The State Department finally allocated $1,500 for a clerk in 1878, after persistent efforts by Studer; before that, consuls paid for clerical help from their own salaries (p. 6).
- Singapore's population was approximately 125,000, with the Chinese constituting one-half to three-fourths of the total (p. 18).
- General Ulysses S. Grant visited Singapore from April 1–9, 1879, accompanied by his wife, son, former Secretary of the Navy, private secretary, and a physician (p. 25).

### Conclusion

Sodhy concludes that the U.S. consuls in Singapore embodied the tension between America's commercial interests and its political aspirations in the late nineteenth century. The consuls' proposals for a naval base and increased naval presence reflected a desire for greater political prominence, but the Eurocentric orientation of American policymakers, the low status accorded to the post, and the entrenched dominance of British, French, and Dutch colonial powers in the region prevented any meaningful American expansion. The United States would not gain a foothold in Southeast Asia until those European powers eventually withdrew, a process that carried "somewhat horrendous repercussions."

## Context

- The primary source is the *Despatches from United States Consuls in Singapore 1833–1906* (microfilm, 26 reels), held at the National Archives, Washington; Sodhy used reels six through thirteen (p. 12, n. 1).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Anglo-American relations in Southeast Asia by using consular despatches as a lens on both the internal workings of the American consular service and the broader geopolitical dynamics of the region during the pre- and post-Civil War era.
