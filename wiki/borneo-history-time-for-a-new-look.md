# Borneo history: time for a new look?

## Abstract

The present article re-assesses key episodes before and during the period of colonial rule in northern Borneo. Historical accounts of this region generally present colonial rule as benevolent state paternalism, echoing official narratives from the colonial era. Community and political leaders in the Borneo states have often called for historical accounts that reflect the vibrancy of indigenous aspirations and eschew the historiographical legacy of colonialism. The response has been muted, and consequently the historical significance of events in Brunei, Sabah and Sarawak tends to be ignored or misrepresented in wider-ranging accounts. Recently released ‘migrated archives’ from British official sources can facilitate such a re-assessment, but it cannot be confined to the period of formal colonial rule, or constricted by traditional academic boundaries.

## Summary

The article is a historiographical intervention arguing that the dominant narrative of British colonial rule in northern Borneo — Brunei, Sarawak, and North Borneo — remains an unchallenged colonial construction presenting the period as benevolent paternalism progressing smoothly toward independence. Phillips contends this narrative was deliberately manufactured by colonial publicists and Cold War propaganda machinery, and that it has persisted into post-independence historiography, marginalizing indigenous political agency and the complex social forces that actually shaped the region's modern history. He identifies several "hinges of history" — the fourteenth-century collapse of Brunei's commercial power, the Tambora eruption and its aftermath, the mid-1850s counter-insurgency campaigns, the Japanese occupation, and the post-war "second colonial occupation" — that have been either ignored or misrepresented because they do not fit the colonial narrative of orderly progress. His central contention is that the period 1937–47, rather than the arrival of James Brooke in 1839, represents the true turning point between the world of the jungle frontier and the modern state.

The article draws heavily on the 'migrated archives' — nearly 9,000 files retained by British colonial authorities on the eve of independence and only released by the Foreign & Commonwealth Office in 2011 — to demonstrate that colonial administration was far more reactive, improvised, and politically compromised than the official record suggests. These documents reveal the flimsy evidentiary basis of the 1960 *Subversion in Sarawak* white paper, the absence of any credible evidence for Indonesian or communist involvement in the 1962 Brunei rebellion, and the extent to which the colonial narrative was a product of deliberate public relations strategy rather than historical fact. Phillips argues that the Borneo states' history has been systematically underplayed in wider accounts of British decolonization, and that the "one-handed clapping" of a historiography that treats Malaya and Malaysia as interchangeable terms obscures an intrinsic component of the history of colonialism and state-building in the region.

### Key Findings

- In 2011 the Foreign & Commonwealth Office revealed the existence of almost 9,000 files retained by British colonial authorities on the eve of independence, now accessible at the National Archives in Kew; many others had been destroyed (p. 55).
- The 1960 *Subversion in Sarawak* white paper, which purported to reveal a centrally-directed communist conspiracy, was based on "a toxic network of police informers whose stock in trade was *kedai* rumour and malicious gossip" and a "wilful misunderstanding of seized documentation produced for the ideological needs of the moment" (p. 58).
- British military intelligence found no evidence of Indonesian or communist involvement in the 1962 Brunei rebellion; interrogations identified rural neglect, administrative breakdown, youth unemployment, and mismanaged development schemes as the causes most frequently cited (p. 62).
- The 1902 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* claimed that "Borneo has no proper history"; in over 700 pages of *The Oxford History of the British Empire* the Borneo territories receive less attention than St Kitts-Nevis or Somaliland (pp. 46, 48).
- Wartime plans for a centralized administration of the Borneo states with a Governor-General and Central Borneo Council based in Labuan were revived post-war but disowned by government ministers in London, partly because they were discredited by the experience of the Central African Federation (p. 60).
- The 1973 Sri Aman peace accord, which neutered an armed communist insurgency with origins in the nature of colonialism, effectively marked the true conclusion of British colonial rule in Borneo rather than the 1963 formation of Malaysia (p. 63).

### Conclusion

Phillips' definitive takeaway is that the colonial legacy in the Borneo states was not a benign inheritance of constitutional democracy and social welfare but a fragile, reactive, and often improvised arrangement sustained by propaganda, surveillance, and political compromise rather than by the consent of the governed. The "second colonial occupation" after 1945 created the modern state apparatus, but it did so by imposing novel concepts of loyalty and prescribed cultural values while remaining dependent on traditional authority structures and the Chinese commercial class. The road to genuine economic and social progress was only opened after independence, under leaders like Abdul Rahman Ya'kub, who implemented measures of popular mobilization and development administration that had their origins in the thwarted ambitions of the colonial state itself.

## Context

- **Primary archival collections:** The 'migrated archives' at The National Archives, Kew (FCO and CO series), released by the FCO in 2011; the article also draws on Colonial Office policy documents (e.g., CO 825/43/22) and Cabinet papers (CAB 134/1555–56).
- **Historiographical contribution:** The article positions itself as a corrective to what Phillips terms "colonial records history" — the tendency to base historical narratives exclusively on official papers, government press releases, and administrators' memoirs — and calls for a synthesis that intermeshes the study of popular movements, aspirations, and livelihoods with the formal apparatus of colonial authority.
