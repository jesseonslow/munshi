# Cards

## Cards

Playing cards constitute a significant element of Malay recreational and gambling culture across the Straits Settlements and the Federated Malay States, as documented in the ethnographic literature of the early twentieth century. The games played with cards in the region draw on both European card conventions—standard decks, pip values, and suit structures—and indigenous Malay terminology, ritualised procedures, and regional rule variations that distinguish local practice from the European models from which they derive. The principal games recorded in the archival literature include *main chabut*, a vingt-et-un variant; *daun tiga lei* (also known as *pakau*), a three-card hand-ranking game; *main trop*, a trick-taking game with a complex strategic vocabulary; and *main daun cheki*, a game with its own complete nomenclature for individual cards. These games were played in Perak, Selangor, and Singapore, and their study provides a window into the social, linguistic, and economic dimensions of Malay leisure in the colonial period [[1]](#ref-1).

### Scope and Historical Definition

The earliest systematic treatment of Malay card games in the Society's literature is found in W.W. Skeat's *Malay Magic* (1900–1901), which provided a foundational account of the games and their associated terminology. R.O. Winstedt, a British colonial administrator serving in Perak, published a corrective supplement in 1906, drawing on his direct observation of play in Perak, Selangor, and Singapore to clarify points where Skeat's description was imprecise or incomplete [[1]](#ref-1). Winstedt's article is a compact ethnolinguistic record rather than a theoretical study, and its primary aim is to document local rule variations and unrecorded Malay terminology across four distinct games [[1]](#ref-1).

The games themselves are characterised by a mixture of European structural conventions and distinctly local features. *Main chabut* is a vingt-et-un variant played to either twenty-one or thirty-one pips, in which the ace (*sat*) is flexible—worth one, ten, or eleven pips at the player's discretion—except that two aces in a thirty-one game must each count as one pip [[1]](#ref-1). Winstedt corrected Skeat's claim that court cards are excluded from the twenty-one version, reporting that they are in fact used in both the twenty-one and thirty-one games, counted as ten pips each [[1]](#ref-1). The phrase *jumpa di-jalan, diadu kalah, dichabut mati*, which Skeat had recorded but not adequately interpreted, was reinterpreted by Winstedt as describing the loser in a pip-total tie: the player who first reached a given total (for example, twenty-nine) wins by right of precedence, and the second player, reluctant to draw and risk going *mati* (bust), cannot compete [[1]](#ref-1).

*Daun tiga lei* (or *pakau*) is a three-card game whose hand rankings vary significantly by region. Winstedt documented three distinct hierarchies: in Perak the best hand is three aces, in Selangor three threes, and in Singapore three court cards (*tiga kuda*) [[1]](#ref-1). In Singapore, three aces are the worst possible hand, scoring only three pips, while in Perak they constitute the best [[1]](#ref-1). The game features a ritualised card-revealing process known as *mengurut daun*, accompanied by the associated vocabulary *minta kosong*, *minta isi*, and *minta penoh*, used during the suspenseful examination of the middle card [[1]](#ref-1).

*Main trop* is a trick-taking game for which Winstedt supplied terms absent from both Skeat and Wilkinson's *Dictionary of the Malay Language*, including the suit names *batu Malacca* (or *dobin*/*jubin*) for diamonds, *payong* for spades, *klawer* for the suit with the flying animal, and *bibi* for the Queen [[1]](#ref-1). The strategic vocabulary includes *kiler* (concede the remaining tricks) and *kot* (attempt to take all tricks, risking *kena kot* if a trick is lost), which govern endgame decisions [[1]](#ref-1). Winstedt also described a set of covert hand signals used by colluding partners, which he wryly noted are "masonic signs accounted by Malay gamblers proper and clever, but which we should call cheating" [[1]](#ref-1).

*Main daun cheki* is treated more briefly, with Winstedt providing a complete list of card names in the local dialect, including *iyu merah besar*, *sah waji*, *si lebai*, *chek laier*, *peh pichak kapala*, and *ji gendang*, and identifying two species of the game: *cho'it* and *chochot* [[1]](#ref-1).

### Regional Variation and Linguistic Documentation

A defining feature of the documented card games is the sharp regional variation in rules and hand rankings, which Winstedt's fieldwork brought into clear relief. The most striking example is the treatment of three aces in *daun tiga lei*: the same hand is the best possible in Perak, the worst in Singapore, and of intermediate value in Selangor, where three threes hold the top position [[1]](#ref-1). This variation suggests that the games were not transmitted as fixed rule-sets but were subject to local negotiation and adaptation, with each community developing its own conventions.

The linguistic record is of particular value for the study of Malay vocabulary as applied to a European-derived object. The suit names in *main trop*—*batu Malacca*, *payong*, *klawer*—represent a hybrid nomenclature in which Malay words are applied to the standard European suits, while the card names in *main daun cheki* constitute a fully indigenous naming system for individual cards [[1]](#ref-1). The strategic terms *kiler* and *kot* in *main trop* similarly reflect a localised vocabulary for game-specific concepts that have no direct equivalent in English card terminology [[1]](#ref-1). Winstedt's work thus functions as a partial lexicon of Malay gaming language, supplementing the broader lexicographical tradition represented by Wilkinson's *Dictionary of the Malay Language* [[1]](#ref-1).

### Social and Economic Context

The card games documented by Winstedt were embedded in a social context that included both legitimate recreation and gambling. The presence of covert hand signals among colluding partners in *main trop* indicates that the games were not merely pastimes but also venues for organised cheating, a practice that Winstedt observed with the detached amusement of a colonial administrator [[1]](#ref-1). The gambling dimension of card play is further suggested by the broader pattern of animal contests and games in the region, where heavy betting was a consistent feature. In Kelantan, for example, the Sultan's pre-war ban on bull-fighting and other animal contests was motivated not so much by concerns over brutality as by the encouragement they gave to gambling [[2]](#ref-2). While the card games themselves are not described in the same terms of economic significance, their association with betting and the social networks of collusion implies a comparable, if less visible, economic undercurrent.

The games were played in a range of settings, from the *kampong* (village) to urban centres such as Singapore, and their regional variation reflects the degree of social and commercial connectivity between these centres. The fact that Winstedt could observe the same game—*daun tiga lei*—being played with different rules in Perak, Selangor, and Singapore within a short period suggests a degree of mobility among players and a shared cultural framework within which local variation was both expected and accepted [[1]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Malay card games is concentrated in a relatively narrow period, with the principal contributions appearing in the first decade of the twentieth century. Skeat's *Malay Magic* (1900–1901) established the foundational account, and Winstedt's 1906 supplement in the *JSBRAS* provided the most detailed ethnolinguistic record of the games, drawing on direct observation in Perak, Selangor, and Singapore [[1]](#ref-1). Winstedt's article is explicitly positioned as a corrective to Skeat, addressing specific points of imprecision and supplying terminology that was absent from both Skeat and Wilkinson's Dictionary [[1]](#ref-1).

The post-war literature, as represented by Hill's 1952 survey of Kelantan games and entertainments, does not address card games directly but provides valuable contextual information about the broader landscape of Malay recreation in the period [[2]](#ref-2). Hill's account of bull-fighting, kite-flying, the Tiger Game (*main rimau*), and the shadow play (*wayang kulit*) demonstrates the diversity of recreational pursuits available to the Malay population and the intensity with which they were pursued, offering a comparative framework within which the significance of card games can be assessed [[2]](#ref-2). The absence of card games from Hill's survey may reflect either their lesser prominence in Kelantan specifically, or the fact that they were already well documented in the earlier literature and did not require further ethnographic attention [[2]](#ref-2).

The overall record suggests that the study of Malay card games was a relatively specialised interest within the Society's broader ethnographic programme, attracting the attention of colonial administrators with a particular aptitude for linguistic documentation rather than the wider community of scholars who contributed to the literature on Malay culture, religion, and history. The games themselves, as Winstedt's observations make clear, were a living and variable practice, subject to regional adaptation and social negotiation, and their documentation in the Society's journals represents a valuable snapshot of a recreational culture that has since been transformed by the spread of modern entertainment and the changing social conditions of the post-colonial period.
