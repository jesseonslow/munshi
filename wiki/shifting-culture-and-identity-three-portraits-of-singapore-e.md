# Shifting culture and identity: three portraits of Singapore entrepreneur Lee Kong Chian (1893–1967). Il

Huang Jianli's 2009 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 82, Part 1) examines the shifting cultural and identity constructions surrounding Singapore entrepreneur Lee Kong Chian (1893–1967) across the decades of high nationalism, decolonization, and Cold War politics. The article's central thesis is that three dominant portraits of Lee—Nanyang capitalist-philanthropist, diasporic patriot, and local "virtuous pioneer"—are not fixed historical truths but contingent products of changing circuits of power, each emerging in response to specific political and economic conjunctures in Singapore, China, and the broader Chinese diaspora.

## Summary

The article undertakes a critical review of the Chinese-language print culture surrounding Lee Kong Chian, demonstrating that the vast majority of substantive writings on him trace back to a single master account: the 28-page biographical sketch by his friend Tan Ee Leong, completed just before Lee's death in June 1967 and published in revised form in 1969. Huang shows how this template was reprinted, adapted, and expanded across successive waves of publication interest—the 1980s "Asian values" discourse, China's post-1978 market reforms and renewed interest in overseas Chinese leaders, the 1993 centenary celebrations in Nan'an, and the 1997 book-length biographies by Zheng Bingshan and Li Yuanrong—each layer adding new materials while remaining structurally dependent on Tan's original narrative.

The three portraits are then examined in turn. The first, Lee as Nanyang capitalist and philanthropist, is identified as the most historically grounded and least "constructed," drawing on the well-documented trajectory of his business empire from a small rubber operation through the Great Depression and Korean War to a transnational enterprise spanning Singapore, Malaya, Thailand, and Indonesia. The second portrait, Lee as representative patriot of the Chinese diaspora, is shown to be a post-1978 PRC construction, built around his educational investments in Fujian province and selectively framed to align with Beijing's evolving overseas Chinese policy. The third, Lee as local "virtuous pioneer," emerged in Singapore from the early 1980s as the PAP government rehabilitated the Chinese past under the discourse of Asian values, using the deliberately ambiguous label to skirt political sensitivities around Lee's associations with Chinese-medium education and the Left.

Huang's method is primarily historiographical rather than archival: he reads the published literature as a body of cultural production, tracing how each portrait serves the interests of particular political actors at particular moments. Where new primary materials do appear—such as Zheng Bingshan's discovery of a short autobiography reportedly written by Lee in December 1961, or Li Chengfeng's frank 1993 conference testimony that Lee had planned to evacuate his family from Singapore before the Japanese invasion—they are treated as evidence of the gaps and silences in the dominant narrative rather than as the foundation of a new account.

### Key Findings

- Rubber prices fell from $0.48 per pound in 1928 to $0.07 in 1932 during the Great Depression, then surged to $2.41 by February 1951 during the Korean War; Lee's business empire by the early 1940s encompassed approximately 30,000 hectares of rubber and pineapple plantations, 2,000 administrative personnel, and over 30,000 labourers across Singapore, Malaya, Thailand, and Indonesia (pp. 78–80).
- Tan Ee Leong's 1969 biographical account (28 pages, published in *Xingma renwuzhi*) became the master template for virtually all subsequent Chinese-language writings on Lee, being reprinted verbatim in 1987 (Kuala Lumpur), 1989 (Beijing), 1993 (Nan'an), and 1998 (Singapore Nan'an Association) (pp. 72–75).
- The Lee Foundation was established on 29 March 1952; its S$60 million donation to build the new National Library headquarters (reopened July 2005) represented one of its largest single gifts, echoing Lee's personal laying of the foundation stone for the old National Library on 16 August 1957 (pp. 82–83, 89).
- Lee's Guoguang Education Village in Nan'an, Fujian, was expanded in the early 1950s to a campus of more than 500 *mou* of land with a built-up area of 50,595.9 square metres, encompassing kindergarten through high school; it was renamed Guangqian Education Village in 1993 and endowed with a bronze statue (pp. 85–86).
- The 1993 centenary commemoration in Nan'an attracted over 200 academic participants and a 10,000-strong street procession, producing three major publications and marking the institutionalization of Lee's "patriot" portrait within PRC discourse (p. 74).
- The Sun Yat-sen Villa in Singapore, purchased by Lee and five other merchants in 1937 and donated to Kuomintang China, underwent an S$7.5 million renovation funded by the Lee Foundation and was formally reopened by Lee Kuan Yew in 2001 (pp. 84, 88).

### Conclusion

Huang Jianli's definitive takeaway is that the three portraits of Lee Kong Chian are best understood as mutually reinforcing yet politically contingent constructions, each serving the needs of a different governing authority or community at a different historical moment. The "virtuous pioneer" label in Singapore, the "patriot" framing in China, and the "capitalist-philanthropist" image in the diasporic business world all converge on a single figure precisely because they are sufficiently ambiguous to allow each audience to project its own values onto him. The article thus contributes to the broader historiographical project of interrogating how national and transnational histories are scripted through the selective remembering and strategic forgetting of public figures.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on published Chinese-language secondary literature (biographies, conference proceedings, commemorative volumes, and newspaper serializations) rather than on archival collections; the only reference to primary source materials is the Lee Kong Chian Papers (NA 1269) at the National Archives of Singapore, cited for individual cases of philanthropy (p. 82, fn 48).
- Historiographically, the article extends Huang's own earlier work on the scripting of Singapore's national history (co-authored with Lysa Hong, 2008) by applying the same analytical framework to a single biographical subject, and it complements Lim How Seng's 1987/1995 study as the most sustained English-language engagement with the Chinese-language literature on Lee.
