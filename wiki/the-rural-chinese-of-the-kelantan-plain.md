# The rural Chinese of the Kelantan plain

Robert L. Winzeler's 1981 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* presents a detailed ethnographic study of two rural Chinese villages in Pasir Mas District, Kelantan, based on systematic household surveys conducted in 1975. The central argument is that these long-settled, locally acculturated Chinese—referred to in Malay as *cina kampung*—constitute a distinct cultural formation whose patterns of bilingualism, bilateral kinship, mixed architecture, and village-god ritual differ significantly from both the Straits Chinese babas and the more recently arrived immigrant Chinese, and whose continued viability is now threatened by the political-economic transformations of post-independence Malaysia.

## Summary

Winzeler situates the rural Chinese within the broader history of Chinese settlement in Kelantan, a state that never experienced the massive tin- and rubber-driven immigration that transformed the western peninsula. The Chinese population, numbering approximately 36,000 at the time of writing, is one of the smallest in Malaya, and its growth has been modest because the Kelantan plain was already well populated by Malay peasant agriculturists and because no large-scale plantation or industrial mining economy developed there (pp. 3–4). A fundamental division exists within the state's Chinese community between an older, more acculturated rural sector settled along the Kelantan River and a newer, less acculturated urban sector concentrated in market towns. Unlike the babas of the Straits Settlements or the peranakans of Java, who lost Chinese language entirely, the Kelantan rural Chinese became bilingual or even trilingual (Hokkien, Malay, Thai) without abandoning Chinese (p. 6). Their acculturation also involved a significant relationship with the Thai, reflected in intermarriage, the presence of Thai Theravada Buddhist temples, and the use of Thai cultural forms in village festivals (p. 6).

The two villages studied—designated North Village and South Village—exhibit a distinctive material culture. Nearly all of the over sixty houses are built in a traditional style mixing Malay and Chinese architectural elements: fine hardwood construction, saddle-shaped roofs, stilted central halls with household altars, and large verandas, with kitchen and casual living areas at ground level (pp. 7–8). This adherence to traditional building patterns is noted as uncharacteristic of both the Malays (who prefer brightly painted modern houses) and the urban Chinese (who prefer modern styles), and the neatness of house yards is described as more characteristic of Malay than Chinese practice (p. 8). The agricultural economy is based primarily on horticulture—mixed orchard and garden cultivation similar to surrounding Malay *dusun*—supplemented by wage labour, with tobacco growing for local *rokok daun* (pandanus-leaf cigarettes) being a notable cash crop, particularly in North Village (pp. 10–11).

In the domain of social organization, Winzeler documents a significant departure from traditional Chinese patrilineal norms. No true patrilocal extended families were found; the most common household type is the simple nuclear family (47.5%), with stem and joint families making up the remainder (Table 5, p. 16). Kin terms have been simplified from the elaborate traditional Chinese system to one closer to the bilateral Malay pattern, and no corporate patrilineal descent groups of any social, economic, or ritual nature could be identified (pp. 19–20). Land inheritance shows a clear male bias—48 men own some land versus 23 women, while 36 women are landless compared to only 11 men (Table 8, p. 18)—yet the overall kinship system operates in a broadly bilateral direction. Community-level organization centres on a "rotating temple" system in which village dieties (small painted wooden statues called *tokong*) are housed in individual homes and take turns hosting three-day birthday festivals involving medium trances, *manora* or shadow-play performances, and large feasts (pp. 21–22).

### Key Findings

- The 1970 census records 36,668 Chinese in Kelantan, with Hokkien speakers constituting the majority in all districts except Ulu Kelantan, where Hakka and Cantonese speakers predominate, reflecting the historical split between coastal (Fukien/Hokkien) and interior (Kwangtung) settlement patterns (Table 1, p. 3).
- Chinese sex ratios in Kelantan were markedly skewed: 362 females per 1,000 males in 1911 and 355 in 1921, though improving to 404 by 1941—still below the all-states mean of 447 (Table 2, p. 4).
- Average total landholding across both villages is 2.19 acres per household, with garden land (1.17 acres) exceeding rice land (0.75 acres) and rubber (0.26 acres); only 53% of village-born men continue to work within the village, reflecting acute land shortage (Table 3, pp. 9, 13).
- Nuclear families account for 47.5% of all 59 households; stem families (married son or daughter) account for approximately 18.6%; joint families (brother-brother or brother-sister) for about 10.2%; average household size is 5.8 persons (Tables 5–6, pp. 16–17).
- Land ownership is strongly gendered: 48 men own some land versus 23 women, and 36 women own no land versus only 11 men, indicating a patrilineal bias in inheritance even within an otherwise bilateral kinship system (Table 8, p. 18).
- The village-god festival system involves a *tokong tua* (elder god) that rotates among households annually; the host household's male head organizes and finances a three-day celebration during which delegations from surrounding villages bring their own elder gods, mediums perform in trance, and a new host is selected by divination using horn-shaped oracle boards (*bow*) (pp. 21–22).

### Conclusion

Winzeler concludes that the rural Chinese of the Kelantan plain represent one of the larger surviving concentrations of locally acculturated Chinese in Malaya, having developed a coherent and distinctive cultural adaptation over several centuries. However, their way of life faces converging pressures: they share with urban Chinese the limitations of non-Malay status in a Malay-controlled state without the latter's business capital and networks, and they share with rural Malays the problems of a shrinking land base without access to the *bumiputra* privileges of the New Economic Policy in government land schemes, licences, loans, and education. The survival of this "rather unique, rich and truly Malaysian local culture" is, in Winzeler's assessment, not completely assured (p. 23).

## Context

- The article forms part of Winzeler's broader study of ethnic adaptation among non-Malay groups in the Kelantan plain and contributes to the small body of scholarship on long-established acculturated Chinese communities outside the Straits Settlements, a topic for which, as he notes, "little is known" (p. 2).
- The fieldwork sites and the broader Kelantan plain context intersect with the archival and ethnographic record of Chinese presence in northern Malaya and southern Thailand, including the late 18th-century Chinese visitor account (Hsieh) and British colonial-era observations by Graham (1907) and Norman (1895), all of which Winzeler draws upon to reconstruct settlement history (pp. 2–3).
