# Johore 1901–1914: the railway concession; the Johore Advisory Board: Swettenham’s resignation and the first General Adviser

J. de V. Allen's 1972 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the turbulent period of Sultan Ibrahim's early rule in Johore (1901–1914), arguing that the state's resistance to British absorption into the Federated Malay States was driven less by principled constitutionalism than by a personal and financial rivalry between Sultan Ibrahim and Sir Frank Swettenham, in which railway concessions and land speculation were the true currency of diplomacy.

## Summary

The article traces how Sultan Abu Bakar's death in 1895 left his son Ibrahim vulnerable to British pressure to accept a Resident or Agent, a threat that recurred throughout the 1900s. Swettenham, who became Governor of the Straits Settlements in 1901, used the railway question as leverage: he threatened to send an Agent unless the Sultan agreed to a new Anglo-Johore Railway Convention, and Allen hypothesises that Swettenham's abrupt resignation in December 1903 was motivated not by health but by the prospect of a lucrative land concession along the railway route that he could only obtain as a private citizen outside the Federation (pp. 12–13). The Sultan, for his part, repeatedly stalled, sought alternative commercial arrangements, and ultimately secured a concession for Rubber Estates of Johore, Ltd. in which Swettenham himself held 10,000 fully paid shares as "Vendor" (p. 19).

The collapse of the Johore Advisory Board in 1905—triggered by the Sultan's attempt to grant a twenty-year monopoly of all natural resources to a "Johore State Development Corporation" backed by members of the Board itself—brought the state to the brink of receiving a British Agent. The Colonial Office, appalled by what it saw as the Sultan "selling his birthright for a mess of pottage," authorised the Board's abolition and prepared to appoint an Agent, but the matter was defused when Lord Elgin ordered the Sultan to return to Johore and the Board resigned *en bloc* (pp. 16–18). In 1909 the Sultan, having lost his Board, voluntarily requested a British Adviser (D.G. Campbell) whose salary Johore itself would pay, a move that placed the state in the category of the Unfederated States rather than the Federation (p. 23).

The final crisis of 1914, in which Governor Young threatened deposition over the Sultan's doubling of his allowance and a prison scandal, was resolved by Sultan Ibrahim's characteristic tactical surrender: he appeared to accept Pangkor Treaty clauses but in fact secured only a "General Adviser" with limited powers, and attached *surat akuan* that preserved his right to recommend the removal of British officials he disliked and to maintain Malay preference in government posts (pp. 26–27).

### Key Findings

- Swettenham's resignation as Governor in December 1903 is interpreted as a strategic move to free himself from land-ownership regulations so he could accept a Johore railway concession; the Colonial Office noted his departure with only "restrained expressions of regret" (pp. 12–13).
- The Rubber Estates of Johore, Ltd. concession of November 1905 granted 25,000 acres with Swettenham as a Director receiving 10,000 fully paid £1 shares, a fact that the Colonial Office found so compromising that it could not formally object without revealing its own motive (p. 19).
- The Johore State Development Corporation scheme of 1905, which would have granted a twenty-year monopoly of all natural resources to a company backed by Advisory Board members and the Amsterdam banking group of Erlanger, was killed by the Colonial Office's refusal to "countenance or recognise" it (pp. 16–17).
- By paying Campbell's salary and providing his house, Sultan Ibrahim placed Johore in the "Unfederated States" category, where the British Adviser was answerable only to the Governor rather than to the Resident-General of the Federation—a far cheaper price than Federation membership (p. 23).
- The 1914 Treaty incorporated only Articles vi, viii, and x of the Pangkor Treaty (not vi–x as Young initially reported), and the *surat akuan* preserved the Sultan's right to recommend removal of any British official with whose work he was displeased (pp. 26–27).
- Allen notes that three major deposition crises in twentieth-century British Malaya (Johore 1914, Kedah 1913, Trengganu 1919–20) arose from the Ruler's decision to increase his allowance in retaliation to British pressure (p. 25, n. 84).

### Conclusion

Allen's definitive takeaway is that Johore's survival as an independent state through this period was not the product of constitutional principle or popular support but of a sustained personal contest between two formidable individuals—Sultan Ibrahim and Swettenham—conducted through the medium of commercial concessions, social manoeuvring in London, and the strategic use of the Colonial Office's own regulatory constraints. The Sultan's "invincible charm" and tactical flexibility, combined with the structural ambiguity of the 1885 Treaty, allowed him to extract a form of British involvement (the General Adviser) that was far less intrusive than the Resident system imposed on the Federated States, at the modest cost of a salary and a house (pp. 26–27).

## Context

- The article draws extensively on Colonial Office files (CO 273 series) at the Public Record Office, London, and on the Swettenham Collection, making it a primary-source study of Anglo-Johore relations during the consolidation of British control over the Malay Peninsula.
- Allen's work complements E. Thio's *British Policy in the Malay Peninsula, 1880–1910* (1969) and W.G. Maxwell & W.S. Gibson's *Treaties and Engagements affecting the Malay States and Borneo* (1924), both cited throughout.
