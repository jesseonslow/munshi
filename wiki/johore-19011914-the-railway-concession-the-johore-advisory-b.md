# Johore 1901–1914: the railway concession; the Johore Advisory Board: Swettenham’s resignation and the first General Adviser

W. George Maxwell published "Mantra Gajah" in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society in 1905, presenting a literal translation and linguistic analysis of a Malay manuscript of elephant mantras collected in Perak. The article argues that the core of Malay elephant lore—its mantras, words of command, and training techniques—is of Siamese rather than Indian origin, challenging the prevailing scholarly consensus derived from Crawfurd.

## Summary

Maxwell's article centres on a small Malay manuscript in his possession, a collection of mantras used in the capture, training, and medical treatment of elephants. The manuscript, committed to writing by Toh Sarif Aman from the oral tradition of Tunku Mantri Ibrahim bin Jaffar, traces its lineage through the Datohs Sri Adika Raja of Ulu Perak. Maxwell's primary intellectual task is to determine the linguistic and cultural provenance of the non-Malay words that constitute the majority of the mantras. He demonstrates that these words are corrupt Siamese, a conclusion supported by the unanimous testimony of Malay elephant drivers who attributed their entire knowledge of the *elmu gajah* ("science of elephants") to Siam, and by the partial recognition of Siamese sounds by a native speaker to whom Maxwell read the text aloud (pp. 1–9).

The article simultaneously documents an extraordinary religious syncretism within the mantras. Hindu deities (Betara Guru, Betara Kala, identified with Vishnu and Shiva), Islamic formulae (*Bismillah*, *la-ilaha illa-llah*), references to the Biblical Azazel, and animist forest spirits all coexist in a single ritual corpus. Maxwell does not attempt to resolve this "extraordinary jumble of religions" but presents it as evidence of layered cultural accumulation (pp. 2–3). He supplements the translation with a legend from the *Sejarah Melayu* (thirteenth *cherita*) about Sri Rama, a Kshatriya elephant headman at the Malacca court, and Maharaja Dewa Sura of Pahang, arguing that the story preserves a memory of two distinct traditions of elephant knowledge—one Indian, one indigenous—converging in the Peninsula (pp. 5–6).

The appendices provide a comparative list of words of command used in Perak and Kedah, a glossary of technical terms for the training stocks (*chelong*) and associated equipment, and a botanical identification of the plants prescribed in the medicinal sections, compiled with the assistance of H. N. Ridley of the Singapore Botanic Gardens (pp. 41–53).

### Key Findings

- The manuscript was committed to writing in the 27th day of Rajab 1296 AH (1879 CE), making it approximately twenty-six years old at the time of Maxwell's publication (p. 7, p. 39).
- Wild elephants required forty to a hundred days in the *chelong* (training stocks) for domestication, compared with seven to ten days for village-reared animals; a full year (*pulang pohun*) was expected to elapse before the elephant was first harnessed with a *rengka* (pannier) (pp. 41, 46).
- The words of command differ markedly between Perak and Kedah and are not Malay in origin; for example, "Go to the right" is *Umbû* in Perak but *Dao* in Kedah, and "Kneel down" is *Tērum* in both but "Get up" is *Tah* in Perak versus *Puan* in Kedah (pp. 42–44).
- The mantras contain Siamese terms such as *rengab* (antidote, from Siamese *ra-ngab*), *chengrai* (ill fortune, from Siamese *chang-rai*), and *chang* (elephant), all of which had been absorbed into Malay usage (p. 8).
- The medicinal prescriptions include items such as rhinoceros navel skin, fish, prawns, oxide, arrack, and water from a pig's wallow, the last being described by Maxwell as "most extraordinary" given Malay Islamic sensibilities (pp. 10, 38).
- Maxwell identifies the *lumbong siam* (Siamese mines) tradition as parallel to the *katurunan deripada siam* (descended from Siam) attribution of the mantras, though he notes that scientists attributed the old mining shafts to Mon-Khmer or Indonesian rather than Thai peoples (p. 7).

### Conclusion

Maxwell's definitive takeaway is that the Malays' knowledge of elephant management was acquired from the Siamese, not from India as Crawfurd had argued on the basis of Sanskrit loanwords. The Sanskrit vocabulary (gajah, gambala, kosa) reflects an earlier Indian influence on terminology, but the practical art—the mantras, the words of command, the training methods—was transmitted from Siam. The legend in the *Sejarah Melayu* of two distinct traditions (the Hindu Kshatriya Sri Rama and the indigenous Pahang king Maharaja Dewa Sura) may preserve a genuine memory of this dual inheritance, with the Siamese-derived system ultimately prevailing in the Peninsula.

## Context

- The article draws on a private manuscript held by Maxwell, oral testimony from Malay elephant drivers in Perak, and consultation with a Siamese speaker; the botanical appendix was compiled with H. N. Ridley of the Singapore Botanic Gardens.
- The study contributes to the historiography of Malay-Siamese cultural transmission and challenges Crawfurd's Indian-origin thesis, which had been only partially modified in Maxwell's own earlier *Manual of the Malay Language*.
