# Recent archaeological discoveries in Malaysia 1962–1963: Malaya

B.A.V. Peacock, writing in 1966 as Director of the National Museum, reports two major archaeological discoveries from Malaya in 1965 — a pair of Dong Son bronze drums from Kuala Trengganu and a hoard of polished stone implements from Penang Island — both of which substantially revise the known geographic distribution and material character of prehistoric culture in the region.

## Summary

The principal focus of the article is the recovery of two Dong Son bronze drums from a sandy strand line at Batu Burok, just south of Kuala Trengganu on the east coast. The drums were destroyed by heavy earth-moving machinery during road improvements in 1965, and by the time Peacock visited the site in July of that year, the find spot had already been sealed beneath the new metalled surface. The account therefore relies heavily on witness testimony, particularly that of Enche Mohammed Ali bin Abdullah, a local medical attendant who undertook his own investigations in the road bed. The drums are identified as Heger's Type I, bearing the characteristic Dong Sonian motifs of zig-zag saw-tooth patterns, ladder designs, linked circles, and conventionalized birds in flight, with a boat scene on the surviving fragment of the smaller drum's body. Their significance lies in the fact that they are the first Dong Son bronze objects recorded from the east coast of Malaya, disrupting the previously assumed concentration of such material along the west coast (pp. 198–200).

The article draws careful parallels with the Kampong Sungai Lang site in Selangor, excavated in 1964, where four drums had been recovered under controlled conditions. At both localities the drums were buried inverted, and at Kuala Trengganu there is evidence they had been used as containers, with traces of iron rust and the imprint of coarsely woven matting preserved in the patina. A socketed iron spearhead found adhering to the inner surface of the large drumhead provides further confirmation of the association between socketed iron implements and Dong Son bronzes in Malaya (p. 199). The pottery recovered from Kuala Trengganu, however, differs markedly from the Kampong Sungai Lang assemblage: it comprises light reddish brown sand-tempered earthenware and a small quantity of black earthenware tempered with carbonized vegetable matter, the latter being a type not previously recorded from any Malayan archaeological site (p. 199).

The second discovery, from Glugor on Penang Island, consists of a hoard of polished stone implements struck at a depth of ten to fifteen feet during building foundation work in December 1965. The hoard includes ten axes, three beaked adzes, a stone bracelet, and a perforated disc with ten projections. Several of the axes are exceptionally large, thin, and finely polished in a marble-like material, with one measuring 30 centimetres in length. Peacock argues that their fragility and the evident care of their manufacture indicate a ceremonial rather than utilitarian function, making them unique in the Malayan archaeological record (p. 200).

### Key Findings

- The two Kuala Trengganu drums are Heger's Type I: the larger measures 28½ inches in diameter with a central twelve-pointed star surrounded by eight concentric decorative zones; the smaller measures 21½ inches with a ten-pointed star and seven bands (p. 200).
- A socketed iron spearhead was found adhering through corrosion to the inner surface of the large drumhead, confirming the association of socketed iron with Dong Son bronzes in Malaya (p. 199).
- Approximately forty pottery sherds were recovered, including a previously unrecorded type of black earthenware tempered with carbonized vegetable matter (chopped straw or husk) (p. 199).
- Seven glass beads were recovered: five pale translucent blue, one deep purple, and one yellow — none of the opaque terracotta red type found at Kampong Sungai Lang (p. 199).
- The Penang hoard comprised ten axes, three beaked adzes, one stone bracelet with a flanged central hole, and one perforated stone disc with ten projections, found at a depth of 10–15 feet (p. 200).
- The largest Penang axe measures 30 cm in length, is made of a marble-like material, and is highly polished; its thinness and fragility suggest ceremonial function (p. 200).

### Conclusion

Peacock's definitive takeaway is that the Kuala Trengganu discovery fundamentally challenges the established model of a predominantly west-coast distribution of Malayan bronze and iron antiquities, opening the east coast as a new area promising rich returns from future intensive archaeological investigation. The inability to obtain a radiocarbon date for the drums — unlike the Kampong Sungai Lang site — is noted as a significant limitation, particularly because the drum style might typologically suggest an earlier period than the four previously known Malayan examples (p. 200).

## Context

- Peacock was Director of the National Museum (Muzium Negara) at the time of writing; the article draws on his own field observations, witness testimony, and examination of recovered objects rather than controlled excavation data.
- Historiographically, the article extends the known geographic range of the Dong Son tradition in Malaya and introduces new ceramic and lithic types to the regional prehistoric record, building directly on Peacock's own 1965 publication on the Kampong Sungai Lang drums in *Malaya in History*.
