# Recent archaeological discoveries in Malaysia 1964: Malaya

B.A.V. Peacock, writing in 1966 for the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, reports on two significant archaeological discoveries made in Malaya during 1965: a pair of Dong Son bronze drums from Kuala Trengganu on the east coast and a hoard of polished stone implements from Penang Island. The overarching argument is that both finds substantially revise the known geographic and typological distribution of prehistoric material culture in the Malay Peninsula, challenging assumptions about a predominantly west-coast concentration of bronze-age antiquities and establishing Penang as a new locus for Neolithic research.

## Summary

The Kuala Trengganu discovery, made at Batu Burok during road improvements in 1965, yielded a pair of Dong Son bronze drums that had been buried inverted in a sandy strand line at the foot of a rocky outcrop. The site was tragically destroyed by heavy earth-moving machinery before Peacock could conduct a controlled excavation, and only fragments of the drums and a small number of associated objects survived. Nevertheless, the evidence is rich: the inner surfaces of the drums bore traces of iron rust and the imprint of coarsely woven matting, indicating they had been used as containers, and a socketed iron spearhead was found adhering through corrosion to the inner surface of the larger drumhead. This last find provides further confirmation of the association between socketed iron implements and Dong Son bronzes in Malaya, a pattern first established at Kampong Sungai Lang in Selangor. The drums were clearly buried together as a pair, mirroring the large-and-small pairing observed at the Selangor site (pp. 198–200).

The associated pottery and beads, though recovered under compromised circumstances, introduce new typological categories. Approximately forty small sherds of light reddish-brown sand-tempered earthenware and a few pieces of black carbon-tempered earthenware were collected; the latter type had not previously been recorded from any Malayan archaeological site. Three decorative techniques are identified: cord-wrapped beater impression, diamond lozenge paddle impression, and fine point impressions in wave or spiral form. Seven tiny glass beads (five pale translucent blue, one deep purple, one yellow) were also recovered, though their direct association with the drums cannot be proven beyond reasonable doubt (pp. 199–200).

The second discovery, from Glugor on Penang Island, comprised a hoard of polished stone implements struck at a depth of ten to fifteen feet during building foundation work in December 1965. The complete assemblage included ten axes, three beaked adzes, a stone bracelet with a flanged central hole, a perforated stone disc with ten outer projections, and several fragmentary tools and rings. The most remarkable pieces are several very large, thin axes with splayed cutting edges, made of a marble-like material and highly polished; one measures 30 centimetres in length. Their extreme thinness, fragility, and evident craftsmanship strongly suggest a ceremonial rather than utilitarian function. This hoard is the first archaeological discovery recorded from Penang Island, and the large marble axes and knobbed stone ring are unique in the Malayan archaeological record (pp. 200–201).

### Key Findings

- The larger Kuala Trengganu drum measures 28½ inches in diameter with eight concentric decorative zones surrounding a central twelve-pointed star; the smaller drum is 21½ inches in diameter with a ten-pointed star and seven decorative bands. Both are classified as Heger Type I (p. 200).
- A socketed iron spearhead was found adhering through corrosion to the inner surface of the large drumhead, confirming the association of socketed iron implements with Dong Son bronzes in Malaya (p. 199).
- The drums bear the same square holes (normally filled with a soft alloy) observed in all previously known Malayan Dong Son drums and some from Thailand, interpreted as mould-segment peg holes from the casting process (p. 200).
- The Penang Glugor hoard comprises ten axes, three beaked adzes, one stone bracelet, one perforated disc with ten projections, and several fragmentary tools; the largest axe is 30 cm long, made of a marble-like stone, and extremely thin (p. 200).
- The Kuala Trengganu pottery includes a previously unrecorded type: black earthenware tempered with carbonized vegetable matter (chopped straw or husk), alongside light reddish-brown sand-tempered ware (p. 199).
- The drums are the first Dong Son bronze objects recorded from the east coast of Malaya, disrupting the previously assumed predominantly west-coast distribution of Malayan bronze and iron antiquities (p. 201).

### Conclusion

Peacock's definitive takeaway is that the Kuala Trengganu discovery fundamentally challenges the geographic model of Malayan prehistory by demonstrating that the Dong Son bronze tradition extended to the east coast, an area previously considered outside its distribution. The inability to obtain a radiocarbon date for the drums is noted as a significant loss, particularly because their decorative style might typologically suggest an earlier period than the four previously known Malayan drums. Both discoveries, Peacock concludes, point to new areas where intensive archaeological investigation may be amply rewarded.

## Context

- Peacock was Curator of the Muzium Negara (National Museum) and had previously published the controlled excavation report on Kampong Sungai Lang in *Malaya in History* (1965); this article extends that work by reporting the parallel Kuala Trengganu find and the new Penang material.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its disruption of the west-coast distribution model for Malayan bronze antiquities (a position associated with Lowenstein, 1956) and in establishing the first recorded archaeological assemblage from Penang Island.
