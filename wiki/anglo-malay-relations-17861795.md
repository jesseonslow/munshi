# Anglo-Malay relations, 1786–1795

D.K. Bassett's 1965 article examines Anglo-Malay relations during the first decade of British settlement on Penang (1786–1795), arguing that the East India Company's refusal to honour its commitments to Sultan Abdullah of Kedah and other Malay rulers was dictated by broader Anglo-Dutch diplomatic considerations in Europe, and that Francis Light's opportunistic and often dishonest reporting to Calcutta was the principal instrument through which the Company evaded its obligations. Written as a tribute to the late Carl Gibson-Hill, the essay attempts to give equal consideration to the attitudes of indigenous rulers and the Company while acknowledging the complete inequality of power between the parties.

## Summary

The article traces the complex web of negotiations between the British East India Company and the rulers of Kedah, Selangor, Trengganu, and the Riau-Johore empire during the critical years following the Dutch conquest of Riau and Kuala Selangor in 1784. Bassett's central concern is the structural asymmetry between European and Asian actors: the Malay and Bugis rulers could offer the Company trade privileges and political friendship, but they could not compel it to act against its own broader imperial interests. The Company's policy was ultimately shaped by the Anglo-Dutch accommodation in Europe, which rendered political commitments to individual Malay states both unnecessary and potentially counterproductive to the comprehensive agreement that British diplomacy sought with the Dutch Republic.

A major thread of the argument concerns the treatment of Sultan Abdullah of Kedah, who ceded Penang in 1785 on specific conditions including a defensive alliance and annual compensation. Bassett demonstrates that Light, as superintendent of Penang, systematically misrepresented Abdullah's character and intentions to the Bengal government, thereby undermining the sultan's legitimate claims and creating the conditions for the brief war of 1791. The article challenges the traditional hagiography of Light as a champion of Malay interests, showing instead that his reports were coloured by an uneasy conscience at the cavalier manner in which Abdullah had been treated and a desire to protect his own reputation as the founder of Penang.

Bassett also addresses the broader question of whether the Company's non-involvement policy was wise. He concludes that Cornwallis and the home authorities possessed a wider strategic vision than Light, whose advocacy for political alliances with the Malay states served the narrow interests of the British country trading community rather than the Company's or Britain's larger imperial objectives. The Malay rulers' failure to secure British protection in 1786–88 left them no choice but to seek accommodation with the Dutch, who were assured of local political supremacy in the absence of a countervailing European power.

### Key Findings

- Sultan Abdullah of Kedah demanded $30,000 per annum compensation for ceding Penang in August 1785; Light proposed $10,000, and the Bengal government ultimately authorised only $10,000 for a maximum of seven to ten years, after which Penang would become the Company's absolute property (pp. 185, 197).
- Cornwallis formally repudiated the Company's obligation to defend Kedah in January 1788, stating that "any Acts or Promises which may be Construed into an obligation to defend the King of Queda are to be avoided," directly contradicting Macpherson's assurance of April 1786 (p. 197).
- The treaty signed on 1 May 1791, following the British attack on the forts at Prai on 12 April 1791, reduced Abdullah's annual payment to $6,000 and contained no guarantee of British protection by land or sea (pp. 208–209).
- Sultan Mansur of Trengganu offered to supply 6,000 piculs of pepper per annum in exchange for 240 chests of opium; the Bengal government declined to act as principal in the trade, relegating it to the private agency house of Joseph Price, Senior and Junior (pp. 195, 198).
- The comprehensive Anglo-Dutch negotiations of 1787–92 envisaged a British guarantee of the Dutch spice monopoly in exchange for the cession of Riau to the British Company; British ministers Grenville and Dundas were willing in 1790 to give up Penang for this purpose (p. 199).
- Sultan Mahmud of Riau-Johore offered to pay $60,000 for reinstatement at Riau, and the Batavia government approved this in writing on 29 May 1795, but the British capture of Malacca in August 1795 intervened before the arrangement could be implemented (p. 202).

### Conclusion

Bassett's definitive takeaway is that 1786–1795 represents a lost opportunity for British influence in the Malay states, but that this loss was a rational consequence of the Company's correct prioritisation of the Anglo-Dutch alliance over trivial local advantages. The Malay rulers, unable to affect British policy or alter the trajectory of European imperial expansion, were left to accept Dutch hegemony south of Kedah and in western Borneo. Bassett insists on presenting the Asian viewpoint sympathetically while acknowledging that motivation and intention in history are not synonymous with effect: the Malay and Bugis efforts at independence between 1784 and 1788 were ultimately thwarted by the warships of Van Braam and Silvester, and only the coincidence of the British seizure of Malacca in 1795 modified the situation.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the Company's Secretary's State Records (SSR) from Calcutta, Light's despatches and diary, and Dutch sources including Netscher's *De Nederlanders in Djohor en Siak* and Veth's *Borneo's Wester-Afdeeling*. Bassett explicitly acknowledges the one-sidedness of the sources, which favour the Company and Light, and employs internal evidence and measured hypothesis to reconstruct the Malay rulers' perspective.
- Historiographically, the article challenges the older expansionist narrative (exemplified by Clodd's *Malaya's First British Pioneer*) that treated the extension of British dominion in the Malay Archipelago as desirable *per se* and credited the local official with superior judgement over his superiors. Bassett's work represents an early attempt at a balanced "local history" approach that gives equal consideration to indigenous rulers' attitudes while not obscuring the fundamental inequality of power between European and Asian participants.
