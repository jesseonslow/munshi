# Āl al-'Aṭṭās and Ḥaḍramī Arab Migration to the Malay World

## Abstract

Hadrami Arab migration to various Indian Ocean littoral states had been taking place for centuries. Hadramis can be found in eastern Africa, South Africa, India and most of the nations that make up the Malay world, with greater concentrations in Indonesia, and sizeable communities in Malaysia and Singapore. The ancestors of the Hadrami communities in the Malay world originate from the Hadramaut region in the southern part of Yemen, and among families from the Hadramaut that have traversed the Indian Ocean over the centuries, many belonged to the various houses of the Ba ‘Alawi. One such house is the Al al-‘Attas, a clan that has produced generations of Sufis, scholars, entrepreneurs, artists and politicians in the various countries of the Indian Ocean. This article discusses the genealogy of the Al al-‘Attas—their beginnings in the Hadramaut, their migration, and their accomplishments in various fields in Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore.

## Summary

This article traces the genealogy, migration, and multi-generational accomplishments of the Āl al-'Aṭṭās, a branch of the Ba 'Alawi (Hadrami Sayyid) families originating in the Hadramaut region of southern Yemen. It situates the clan within the broader history of Hadrami diaspora across the Indian Ocean and examines how this particular house produced figures in Sufism, scholarship, commerce, and politics across Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore. The author frames the study around a central question: how did a relatively late-emerging clan achieve such breadth of influence across disparate fields while remaining embedded in the Ṭarīqat al-'Alawiyyah Sufi order?

The article draws heavily on primary genealogical and hagiographic sources, particularly the *Kitab al-Qirtas* (a hagiography of the eponymous ancestor Habib 'Umar) and the *Taj al-A'ras*, a 1600-page two-volume work documenting the lives of over two hundred scholars associated with Habib Salih b. 'Abdallah al-'Attas. These are supplemented by the anthropological fieldwork of Abdallah Bujra in Huraydah and Kazuhiro Arai's doctoral dissertation on the family. The author also addresses historiographical debates about the Ba 'Alawi, including whether early members were Imami Shi'ites (inferred from the absence of certain names in early genealogies) and how the relationship between the Sufi order and political authority shifted across distinct historical stages.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to the Malay world, where the Al al-'Attas produced madrasah founders, muftis, reformist merchants, university vice-chancellors, and political writers. The author highlights the intermarriage networks that linked Hadrami Sayyid families to the Johor royal house and the Malay political elite, demonstrating how religious authority and secular power became intertwined through marriage alliances.

### Key Findings

- Imam Ahmad al-Muhajir left Basra in 317 AH (930 AD) due to Karmathian persecution and settled in the Hadramaut in 340 AH (952 AD), initiating the conversion of the region to the Shafi'i school of jurisprudence (pp. 2–3).
- The eponymous ancestor, 'Umar b. 'Abd al-Rahman al-'Attas (992 AH/1584–5 – 1072 AH/1661 AD), was born in Lisik, Hadramaut, and settled in Huraydah in 1037 AH (1627–8 AD); he composed the *Ratib al-'Attas* and instituted the *hawtah* (sacred sanctuary) system in that town (pp. 5–6).
- The Al al-'Aṭṭās comprise fourteen branches designated by the names of sons, grandsons, and great-great-grandsons of Habib 'Umar, plus two lines traced to his brothers (p. 7).
- Habib 'Abdallah b. 'Alawi b. Hasan al-'Attas (1277 AH/1875 – 1333 AH/1915) established the Ṭarīqat al-'Attasiyyah in Calcutta in 1304 AH (1886–7 AD), which subsequently spread to the Indian subcontinent and Burma (p. 11).
- Habib 'Abdallah b. Muhsin al-'Attas (1840/1 – 1933) married Rogayah Hanim, a Circassian woman 'gifted' by Sultan Abdulhamid II to Sultan Abu Bakar of Johor, thereby linking the Hadrami Sayyid community to the Johor royal family; her grandson from a third marriage became Tun Hussein Onn, Prime Minister of Malaysia (1976–81) (p. 15).
- Habib Muhammad b. Salim al-'Attas (1906–1976) established the Ba 'Alawi Mosque in Singapore in 1952, which became a crucial node for the consolidation of the Ba 'Alawi Sufi order in the region (p. 18).

### Conclusion

The author concludes that the Āl al-'Aṭṭās story is, in many ways, a repetition on a smaller scale of the experience of many other diasporic Ba 'Alawi clans across the Indian Ocean. Their distinguishing characteristic is the breadth of their accomplishments across religious, intellectual, commercial, and political domains, but their overall trajectory follows the broader pattern of Hadrami migration, settlement, and integration into local societies.

## Context

- Primary sources include the *Kitab al-Qirtas* (manuscript hagiography of Habib 'Umar, obtained from Kazuhiro Arai), the *Taj al-A'ras* (al-'Attas, 1979, two volumes), and the genealogical work of al-Mashhur (1984). The author also draws on Bujra's (1971) anthropological fieldwork in Huraydah and Arai's (2004) doctoral dissertation, supplemented by personal communications with living family members.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Hadrami diaspora by providing a focused clan-level study that connects genealogical, Sufi, and political histories, and by documenting the intermarriage networks between Hadrami Sayyid families and Malay royal and political elites.
