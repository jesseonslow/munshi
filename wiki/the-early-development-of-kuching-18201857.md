# The early development of Kuching, 1820–1857

Craig A. Lockard's 1976 article traces the transformation of Kuching from a sparsely populated river settlement into a multi-ethnic trading port during the first two decades of Brooke rule (1841–1857). The central argument is that the urban patterns, ethnic residential segregation, and institutional structures established in this formative period proved remarkably durable, shaping Kuching's character well into the twentieth century.

## Summary

Lockard begins by situating the Sarawak River basin as a political and economic backwater before the early nineteenth century, with only two or three Malay villages and a total basin population of roughly 8,000 in 1839. The founding of Kuching is attributed to Pengeran Makota, a Brunei prince who established a new administrative centre between 1824 and 1830 to exploit antimony deposits upriver. This move provoked a civil war (1835–36) between the Brunei-backed settlement at Kuching and the local Malay elite at Lidah Tanah, which James Brooke resolved in 1840–41 by storming the rebel fort at Siniawan in exchange for appointment as governor, later becoming Rajah in 1841.

The bulk of the article examines the demographic, social, and political transformations that followed. Lockard demonstrates how the departure of the Brunei element by 1844 transformed Kuching's Malay community from one dominated by Brunei aristocrats to one in which local Sarawak River origins predominated, and how the Rajah's confirmation of the Siniawan *datus* in their traditional offices created a "symbiotic" political partnership that endured for a century. He then maps the emerging ethnic residential pattern — Chinese and Indians in the waterfront bazaar, Malays in *kampongs* on the south and north banks, Europeans clustered around the *Astana* — and analyses the internal stratification of the Malay community, from the aristocratic *perabangan* and *datus* down through *nakodas*, *orang pereman*, and bound dependents.

The final sections address the political and economic tensions that defined the period's closing years. Brooke's introduction of regular salaries for the *datus* and the establishment of *tua kampongs* in 1854 diluted traditional chief authority, culminating in the dismissal of Datu Patinggi Abdul Gapur. Simultaneously, Chinese and Indian merchants began encroaching on the trading sphere once monopolized by Malay *nakodas*, while the 1850 influx of Hakka refugees from Pemangkat intensified inter-ethnic economic competition. These dynamics set the stage for the Kuching-Bau War of 1857, which Lockard treats as the effective end of the first developmental phase.

### Key Findings

- The total population of the Sarawak River basin was estimated at approximately 8,000 in 1839, comprising mostly Dayaks with perhaps 1,500–2,000 Malays and a few Chinese (p. 105).
- Kuching's population reportedly reached 1,500 by 1839 following the arrival of Raja Muda Hashim and his entourage, and may have grown to between 6,000 and 10,000 by the early 1850s (p. 110).
- The 1850 Hakka refugee influx from Pemangkat numbered 4,000–5,000, of whom nearly 800 settled in and around Kuching, taking up market gardening, carpentry, blacksmithing, and other artisanal work (p. 111).
- The first *Masjid Besar* was completed in 1852, a direct product of a Muslim revitalization spurred by Anglican missionary activity that had begun in 1848 (p. 123).
- The Kwong Wai Siew Association, established by Cantonese settlers in 1853, was the only legally recognized Chinese dialect association in Kuching before 1857 (p. 120).
- The Borneo Company Ltd., established in 1856 with Brooke's encouragement, obtained a monopoly over Upper Sarawak's antimony mines and opened a steamer service between Kuching and Singapore (p. 124).

### Conclusion

Lockard's definitive historical takeaway is that the urban configuration established between 1841 and 1857 — a heterogeneous multi-ethnic population, a pluralistic institutional structure governed through indirect rule, residential segregation between Malay *kampongs* and the Chinese bazaar, and a degree of ethnic occupational specialization — proved remarkably durable. Even after the demographic transformation under Charles Brooke that converted Kuching from a mostly Malay town into a predominantly Chinese city, these foundational patterns continued to structure the settlement's social and spatial organization.

## Context

- The article draws extensively on contemporary European travel accounts and official narratives, including Hugh Low's *Sarawak* (1848), Spenser St. John's *Life in the Forests of the Far East* (1862), Keppel's *Dido* (1846) and *Meander* (1853), Mundy's *Narrative* (1848), and Baring-Gould and Bampfylde's *White Rajahs* (1909), supplemented by Brooke's private correspondence.
- The study represents an early attempt at a social history of Kuching's formative period and derives from Lockard's 1973 PhD dissertation at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, positioning it within the emerging historiographical shift toward community-level analysis of Southeast Asian urbanization.
