# Social memory and Indian women from Malaya and Singapore in the Rani of Jhansi regiment

## Abstract

The Rani of Jhansi Regiment (RJR) is scarcely visible in the social memory of the Japanese occupation in Southeast Asia and the activities of Subhash Chandra Bose on behalf of the Indian nationalist movement. The few memoirs that do exist were written by elite and well-educated RJR veterans, and they celebrate a sense of unity among Indians from various background who supported Bose’s efforts, including soldiers who came to the RJR from working class backgrounds in Malaya and Singapore. Using recollections from witnesses to and participants in the Indian nationalist movement in Japanese-occupied Malaya and Singapore, this article challenges the elite view by exploring alternative perceptions of Bose’s activities. Illustrating the importance of gender and socio-economic boundaries in Bose’s nationalist movement, this article argues that differences in class and caste estranged subaltern Indian women during the Occupation.

## Summary

Arunima Datta's article interrogates the social memory surrounding the Rani of Jhansi Regiment (RJR), the all-women's infantry unit formed by Subhash Chandra Bose in Japanese-occupied Malaya in 1943. The central problem she identifies is that the RJR's historical record is dominated by memoirs and oral accounts from elite, well-educated veterans—such as Dato' Rasammah Bhupalan and Dr Lakshmi Sahagal—who present a unified, celebratory narrative of Bose's emancipatory leadership and the regiment's revolutionary egalitarianism. Datta argues that this narrative obscures the experiences of the majority of RJR recruits: illiterate, second-generation Indian women from the rubber estates of Malaya, whose voices have been systematically excluded from mainstream historiography and media representations alike.

Drawing on personal interviews with both elite and estate women, British intelligence reports, and oral history archives from the National Archives of Singapore, Datta makes two interrelated arguments. First, the RJR was not primarily a feminist or emancipatory project but a politicization of gender in service of Bose's nationalist agenda—women were mobilized as symbolic bearers of the nation and as a psychological weapon against the British Indian Army, not as agents of their own liberation. Second, the socio-economic backgrounds of participants determined their experience within the regiment and their subsequent place in social memory. Class and caste, mediated through language (Bose's imposition of Hindi on a predominantly Tamil-speaking corps) and educational attainment, created internal hierarchies that persisted from recruitment through post-war rewards. Estate women joined the RJR not out of patriotic conviction but to secure regular meals, protection from *kirani* and Japanese soldiers, and a degree of bodily autonomy unavailable on the estates.

The article's methodological strength lies in its treatment of silence as a historical source. Datta reads the gaps in elite memoirs, the brevity of estate women's appearances in media productions, and the absence of post-war provisions for non-elite recruits as evidence of how social memory is manufactured. By juxtaposing the recollections of privileged veterans with those of estate labourers like Pachaimmal and Pushpa, she demonstrates that the "unity" celebrated in RJR narratives was always already fractured by the material conditions of colonial plantation life.

### Key Findings

- Approximately 80 per cent of RJR recruits were from the rubber estates of Malaya; the majority were illiterate and second-generation residents (p. 87).
- The Syonan Rubber Syndicate, formed by 18 Japanese companies by the end of 1942, reduced estate labourers' working days to ten–fifteen per month and cut wages by 30–40 per cent below pre-war rates (p. 83).
- By 1943, the controlled price of one bag of rice was $50, while black-market prices ranged between $100 and $150; a British Intelligence Report stated that "tens and thousands of Indian rubber estate coolies were thrown out of work and many starved to death" (p. 84).
- The Indian population of Malayan estates fell by seven per cent during the Japanese occupation (p. 85).
- Bose's first public call for Indian women in Malaya to join the RJR was made on 12 July 1943 (p. 87).
- After the war, Bose diverted IIL funds to benefit RJR members, but arrangements for boarding school education and post-war support reached only elite recruits; estate women returned to plantation life with no provisions (p. 99).

### Conclusion

Datta's definitive historical takeaway is that the overseas Indian nationalist movement, for all its rhetoric of unity and emancipation, did not subvert the socio-economic hierarchies among its participants. Class and caste determined who joined the RJR, under what conditions, what role they were assigned in the war zone, and—most consequentially—who was remembered and in what capacity after the war. The article calls on historians to resist the temptation of a single, reconciled narrative and instead to present the simultaneous existence of multiple, often contradictory voices as the true texture of the past.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: National Archives of Singapore oral history interviews (including recordings of K. R. Menon, A. K. Motiwalla, Lakshmi Sahagal, Seva Singh, Tan Ban Cheng, and Vilasini Perumbulavil); British Intelligence Reports (CO 273/669 series); the Puan Sri Datin J Athi Nahappan Collection; and the author's own field interviews conducted in Malaysia between 2011 and 2013.
- Historiographical contribution: The article applies Michel-Rolph Trouillot's framework of manufactured silences to the RJR, challenging the celebratory narratives of Lebra (2008) and Sengupta (2012) by foregrounding class, caste, and language as structuring forces within the regiment. It extends the scholarship on gender and nationalism (McClintock, Yuval-Davis, Eisenstein) to the specific context of Japanese-occupied Southeast Asia and the plantation economy of Malaya.
