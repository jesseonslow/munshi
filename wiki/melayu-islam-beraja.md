# Melayu Islam Beraja

Abdul Aziz Umar, Brunei's last Chief Minister before independence in 1983, delivered this practitioner's reflection in 2013 arguing that Melayu Islam Beraja (MIB) is not a static traditional inheritance but the cumulative product of Brunei's socio-political experience — particularly the British intervention of 1904 and the post-war nationalist crisis — that transformed the ancient *negara* into a modern nation-state.

## Summary

Umar's central intervention is to challenge the common historical justification for MIB — the claim that it represents "how we have always been." He argues that MIB is in fact a modern construct, born from the collision between Brunei's pre-colonial political culture and the Western administrative frameworks imposed by the British after 1904. The British demarcation of fixed territorial boundaries, for instance, fundamentally altered the concept of *negara*, which had never been conceived as a geographically bounded entity but rather as a "projection of power" in Clifford Geertz's terms. The introduction of formal education along British lines further eroded hereditary social structures, producing a new class of Bruneian Malay teachers who became carriers of nationalist political ideas in the post-war period.

The article then situates MIB within the broader "problems of political survival" that Brunei faced between 1939 and 1983, a framing Umar borrows from Ranjit Singh. The critical decision point was whether to join the Malaysian Federation in 1963; Umar contends this was driven not by economic or political calculation but by the deep-seated notion of *negara* — Brunei could not be absorbed into a parallel political cosmos without alienating its own identity. By the time of independence in 1983, the challenge had shifted from physical survival to cultural survival against the homogenizing pressures of globalization and Western popular culture. MIB, in Umar's account, emerged as the institutional answer to this second-order threat.

Umar is candid that MIB is "not an exact re-enactment of the traditional Brunei socio-political order" but rather a concept "enhanced with Western, or specifically British, concepts of power." He frames the ongoing internal debate between those who justify MIB through historical continuity and those who argue that "what Brunei has always been is not necessarily what Brunei should be" as a necessary process of "composed progress" that must not be interrupted by external political-cultural intervention.

### Key Findings

- The British Resident Malcolm MacArthur, sent by the Colonial Office in 1905/1906, described Brunei as "a dying nation that needs to be saved" (p. 94).
- The British demarcated Brunei's physical boundaries for the first time, transforming the ancient *negara* into a nation-state after over 600 years (p. 94).
- At least seven ethnic groups — Brunei, Kedayan, Tutong, Dusun, Belait, Murut, and Punan — were confined within the new national boundaries, each with distinct cultural and linguistic identities (p. 94).
- The first vernacular school was established in 1912; by the 1950s a new social class of Bruneian Malay teachers had formed a critical mass capable of carrying nationalist political ideas (p. 95).
- Brunei's total area is just over 2,200 square miles with a population of fewer than 400,000, making ethnic diversity a potentially serious governance challenge (p. 94).
- Umar served as Brunei's last Chief Minister on the eve of independence in 1983 and had been chairman of the National Council for MIB (p. 93).

### Conclusion

Umar's definitive takeaway is that MIB's legitimacy rests not on historical authenticity but on its demonstrated utility: the peace, stability, low crime, and quality of education and healthcare that Brunei enjoys under a multi-racial, multi-religious society operating within a "Malay, Muslim and monarchical" framework. He rejects the premise that non-Western political cultures must justify themselves within a Western moral and ethical arena, positioning MIB as an indigenous conceptual framework that can only be fully appreciated by its own exponents.

## Context

- This is a practitioner's address rather than an archival study; Umar draws on his nearly 40 years of government service and direct mentorship by Sultan Omar Ali Saifuddin III in the 1960s and 1970s rather than on primary documentary sources.
- Historiographically, the piece contributes to the small body of insider accounts of Brunei's political formation, complementing Ranjit Singh's external scholarly framing of the 1939–1983 period as one of "political survival."
