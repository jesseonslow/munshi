# The Hervey Malay Collection in the Wellcome Institute (with a short biography of D.F.A. Hervey). R.F. Ellen, M.B

R.F. Ellen, M.B. Hooker, and A.C. Milner published this catalogue and biographical study in 1981, documenting the Malay manuscript collection of D.F.A. Hervey (1849–1911), a Straits Settlements Resident Councillor, now held at the Wellcome Institute in London. The authors argue that the collection's principal value lies not in its analytical sophistication but in its preservation of the conditions under which colonial ethnographic data was gathered—specifically, the directed relationship between a European official and his Malay informants.

## Summary

The article serves a dual purpose: it provides a detailed inventory of the Hervey Malay manuscripts and printed materials in the Wellcome Institute, and it reconstructs the career and research methods of the man who assembled them. Hervey, the first "cadet" of the Straits Civil Service, spent his administrative career in Malacca from 1880 until his retirement in 1893. The authors portray him as a competent but unremarkable official—bypassed by more dynamic contemporaries like Swettenham and Maxwell, and whose handling of the Tampin succession dispute in 1882 was judged by his superior Cecil Clementi Smith to have been "bungled." Yet his character as a patient, inquisitive collector of data made him well suited to the kind of systematic ethnographic gathering that produced the Wellcome collection (pp. 82–84).

A central argument of the article concerns the methodology of colonial ethnography. The official folders in the collection contain Hervey's minutes directing his Malay writer, Munshi Muhammad Jaafar, to collect information on specific subjects. Hervey's instructions sometimes take a "schoolmasterly tone," and on one occasion he complains that Jaafar has not provided the *sebab* (reason) for a custom (*adat*) relating to a buffalo fight (pp. 85–86). The authors use these documents to raise a broader question: to what extent were Malay manuscripts produced in response to the specific requests of inquisitive European officials, and in what ways did the requirements of a European audience shape the content and form of the texts? This question has implications for the interpretation of all Malay manuscripts of uncertain provenance (p. 86).

The ethnobiological materials—the largest single category in the collection—receive particular attention. The authors note that while Malay folk botany is relatively well documented compared to other non-European cultures, folk zoology and classification systems remain poorly known. The Hervey materials are significant because they record where information was collected, under what circumstances, and for what purpose, enabling scholars to assess variation in terminology across different parts of the Malay peninsula and to identify artifacts introduced by the conditions of collection and presentation rather than reflecting locally significant representations of natural species (pp. 86–87).

### Key Findings

- The Wellcome Institute acquired the Hervey Malay materials at Hodgson's sale rooms, London, on 23 November 1934 for twelve guineas; the full collection was dispersed among the Institute, the London Library, and the booksellers Kegan Paul (p. 82).
- Well. Mal. 1 contains a Malay-language paraphrase of the Minangkabau matrilineal legal system, identified as derived from Maxwell 118a, with the "Twenty Laws" divided into the "Eight" and the "Twelve," and a list of circumstantial evidence for guilt (pp. 87–88).
- The collection includes what may be the only extant copy of the Singapore newspaper *Peridaran alshams wa alkamar*, No. 20, dated 30 August 1877, a fact confirmed by W.R. Roff's bibliography of Malay and Arabic periodicals (p. 92).
- Hervey's grass collection lists (Well. Mal. 8:G) were gathered in April 1891 by four named individuals—Leman of Malaka Pindah, Mata Mata Mahat of Sungei Petei, Mohamat Amin bin Omar of Pangkalan Rama, and Hasan of Selandau—at Hervey's explicit direction to the Assistant Superintendent of Forests (pp. 90–91).
- A medical report at the end of Hervey's career recorded that he had a "defective memory," was "hypochondriacal" and "emotional," and that his family history was "distinctly neurotic" (p. 83).
- The 1904 Annual Report on Education, Straits Settlements, records that a prize at the Malay College in Malacca was named the "Mohamed Jaafar Memorial Prize" in memory of Hervey's Munshi and Malay Writer (p. 92).

### Conclusion

The authors' definitive takeaway is that the Hervey collection functions as a methodological document as much as a source of ethnographic content. Because the official folders preserve the chain of instruction from European official to Malay informant, the collection allows historians to trace how colonial administrative structures shaped the production of knowledge about Malay society. For *adat* scholarship specifically, Hervey's plain, untheorised descriptions—particularly his 1884 account of the Rembau *perpateh* system—provide a valuable baseline against which to measure the "undue (and inaccurate) theorising" that came to dominate the field two decades later (p. 84).

## Context

- The article draws on the Wellcome Institute Oriental Collection (London), the Arkib Negara Malaysia (which has held Hervey's unpublished papers since 1969), and the manuscript holdings of SOAS and the University of Cambridge (pp. 82, 85).
- Historiographically, the study contributes to the emerging critique of colonial ethnographic methodology by foregrounding the conditions of data collection, and it positions Hervey's descriptive work as a counterweight to the later, more theoretically driven *adat* literature.
