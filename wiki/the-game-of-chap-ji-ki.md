# The game of chap-ji-ki

G. T. Hare's 1898 article in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* documents the Chap-Ji-Ki lottery as it operated in Singapore during the mid-1890s, a gambling epidemic that swept through the Straits-born Hokkien and Teochew Chinese trading community before being suppressed by colonial authorities in 1896. Written from the perspective of an official intimately familiar with the game's internal mechanics, the piece serves both as an ethnographic record of a disappearing practice and as an implicit justification for the Government's crackdown.

## Summary

Hare frames the Chap-Ji-Ki lottery as a distinctive mutation of a traditional Chinese card game, transformed by its female operators into a highly organised, mobile, and deliberately evasive gambling enterprise. The original game—played on a fixed board with twelve cards derived from Chinese chess figures—was radically restructured in Singapore: the board was abandoned, the game became exclusively female, and the entire operation was decentralised into a network of collectors who canvassed private households and carried stakes in coded form to avoid police detection. Hare's central analytical interest lies in the ingenuity of these anti-detection measures, which he presents as a testament to the "originality and organising powers" of the promoters.

The article also explores the social dynamics that made the lottery possible. Hare identifies the participants as women of the Chinese trading classes—married women with "much spare time" whose gambling was fuelled by a cultural disposition toward speculation and whose losses were financed by pawning diamond jewellery and clothing. The lottery's solvency was underwritten by the public reputation of wealthy women who guaranteed the syndicates in exchange for partnership shares, creating a self-reinforcing structure of trust and profit. Hare draws a pointed parallel between the intensity of Chinese women's gambling and the famous high-stakes card parties of English ladies at Vauxhall during the Restoration, a comparison that simultaneously exoticises and normalises the phenomenon for his colonial readership.

The piece concludes with a description of the "sub-agencies" that proliferated once the principal lotteries proved profitable—open offices that mirrored the winning card of a parent syndicate and were consequently easier for police to raid. Hare's account thus maps a hierarchy of gambling operations, from the tightly controlled, women-only principal lotteries to the more exposed and commercially ambitious branch firms.

### Key Findings

- The Chap-Ji-Ki lottery was won by guessing one of twelve cards (six "red" and six "black," derived from Chinese chess figures); winners received ten times their stake (p. 4).
- The game first appeared in Singapore around 1893 and was broken up by Government action in 1896 (p. 2).
- Daily total stakes at the three principal lotteries ranged from $300 to $500 or more; sub-agencies handled roughly $100 per day (pp. 9–10).
- Collectors (*phoe-kha*) received a commission of at least 10% on all money won by their clients; if no commission was due, the manager paid a token of about $5 to cover transport (p. 9).
- Households that lent their premises for lottery openings received a fee of $5 to $10 per occasion (p. 8).
- Stakes were limited to $25 or $50 in some lotteries but unlimited in others; lotteries were opened once or twice daily, typically at midday and between 8:30 and 9:30 p.m. (p. 7).

### Conclusion

Hare's definitive takeaway is that the Chap-Ji-Ki lottery represented a uniquely sophisticated form of organised gambling whose longevity owed less to the appeal of the game itself than to the administrative ingenuity of its female operators. The article implicitly argues that only sustained and determined Government intervention could dismantle a system so well insulated from detection by its own structural design—coded stakes, rotating venues, female-only participation, and a culture of mutual trust among collectors.

## Context

- Hare's detailed operational knowledge—down to the specific symbolic notations used on stake packets and the internal communication protocols between managers and collectors—strongly suggests he held a law enforcement or intelligence role within the Singapore police or colonial administration, positioning the article as a professional record rather than a purely scholarly exercise.
- The article's framing of Chinese women's gambling through a Restoration-era English analogy, and its emphasis on the "peculiar form of gambling propensity" among the Chinese, reflects the colonial administrative discourse of the period that treated indigenous gambling as a problem to be managed rather than a cultural practice to be understood on its own terms.
