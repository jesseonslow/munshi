# Molluscs

Molluscs constitute a diverse and ecologically significant component of the Malayan fauna, encompassing both marine and terrestrial assemblages that have attracted sustained taxonomic attention from the early twentieth century onward. The Society's journal literature documents this group through two complementary strands: a vernacular nomenclature study that records the Malay-language names applied to marine species encountered in Singapore, and a systematic checklist of the land and fresh-water molluscs of the Malay Peninsula that addresses the Pulmonata in particular. Together these contributions reveal a fauna that is, in the terrestrial realm, comparatively impoverished relative to neighbouring islands, yet one that harbours cave-adapted specialists, apparently endemic genera, and the consequences of human-mediated introductions [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2).

## Vernacular Nomenclature and Marine Molluscs

The earliest of the two contributions under consideration is a concise note by A. W. Hamilton, published in 1933, which presents a list of twenty-three Malay names for local mollusc species [[1, p. 135]](#ref-1). Hamilton compiled the list during a residence in Singapore, identifying each vernacular term by comparison with named material held in the Raffles Museum [[1, p. 135]](#ref-1). The species represented span bivalves—including *Tridacna ferruginea*, *Pinna vexillum*, and *Solen malaccensis*—as well as gastropods such as *Turbo ticaonicus*, *Murex martinianus*, and *Trochus maculatus*, alongside other groups [[1, p. 135]](#ref-1).

A methodological feature of the note is its cross-referencing with the independent work of Dr. R. O. Winstedt, who had recorded Malay names for mollusc species in his own capacity. Where Winstedt had applied the same vernacular name to the same species, Hamilton appended the letter "W" to the Malay name to indicate this concurrence [[1, p. 135]](#ref-1). A footnote records one instance in which Winstedt had applied the same name to a different species, highlighting the potential for ambiguity in vernacular usage [[1, p. 136]](#ref-1). The note thus serves a dual purpose: it documents the local linguistic vocabulary for marine molluscs and provides a point of comparison between two independent collectors' observations, a practice that strengthens the reliability of the identifications.

## Terrestrial Pulmonate Fauna

The more substantial contribution is F. F. Laidlaw's 1933 checklist of land and fresh-water molluscs of the Malay Peninsula, which constitutes the second part of a survey first begun with the Operculata in 1928 [[2, p. 211]](#ref-2). Laidlaw undertook what he candidly described as a "first attempt" to systematise the Pulmonate fauna, a group he acknowledged remained "very imperfectly known" [[2, p. 211]](#ref-2). The article follows the classification of Thiele (1931) and documents the family-level and generic composition of the group across twelve families of terrestrial pulmonates [[2, pp. 211–234]](#ref-2).

The survey ranges from the highly primitive Rathouisiidae, represented by the genus *Atopos*, which Laidlaw notes are "probably the most primitive of existing land-mollusca", through to the more familiar Achatinidae, Ariophantidae, and Streptaxidae [[2, pp. 211–234]](#ref-2). A recurring theme throughout the article is the contrast between the Peninsula's comparatively impoverished pulmonate fauna and that of neighbouring islands. Of the 159 terrestrial pulmonate species recorded for Java by van Benthem Jutting, only thirteen are shared with the Peninsula, and all of those are species whose range has been extended by human activity [[2, p. 211]](#ref-2). Similarly, the genus *Amphidromus*—richly represented in Borneo, Sumatra, and Java—yields only a handful of forms in Malaya, including a single immature specimen of the section *Syndromus* collected by Dr. Annandale at Patalung [[2, p. 229]](#ref-2).

Laidlaw's work also includes the description of two new species. *Sarika planata*, from the Cameron Highlands, is distinguished from its nearest relative *S. limbata* by radular formula and shell morphology [[2, pp. 218–219]](#ref-2). *Parmarion peilei*, a dark slug from the same locality, is characterised in detail, with its spermatophore structure compared to that of *P. malayanus* [[2, pp. 223–224]](#ref-2). The latter species, described by Collinge from the Cameron Highlands, possesses a spermatophore approximately 6 mm in length (excluding the filiform tail), S-shaped with comb-like chitinous plates along the convex curvature of the upper limb; its radular formula is 98–26–1–26–98 [[2, pp. 223–224]](#ref-2).

A significant portion of the article is devoted to taxonomic revision and synonymy. Laidlaw follows Hoffmann in collapsing several *Atopos* species into fewer taxa, reassigns *Parmarion doveri* back to the type genus on the grounds that the external character used to separate it is not valid, and discusses the uncertain placement of *Megaustenia ophiria* and *Durgella hatchongi* [[2, pp. 212]](#ref-2). He also flags a transposition error in his earlier 1931 paper on the Dyakiinae, correcting the identity of the species from Pulau Aor [[2, p. 234]](#ref-2).

## Cave Assemblages and Endemism

The Batu Caves in Selangor emerge from Laidlaw's survey as a critical locality for Malayan pulmonate malacology, hosting species of *Prosopeas*, *Subulina*, *Opeas*, *Kaliella*, *Microcystis*, *Indoennea*, and *Durgella*, often in association with bat guano as a nutrient medium [[2, pp. 215–216]](#ref-2). The streptaxid *Indoennea ridleyi* Peile is recorded as "abundant" in both the Batu Caves and Gua Tembus, Selangor, indicating a significant cave-adapted component of the local fauna [[2, p. 232]](#ref-2).

Of particular taxonomic interest is the apparently endemic genus *Plicaxis*, known only from the Peninsula. Its two known species, *P. perakensis* and *P. mirabilis*, are recorded from Perak and Kelantan respectively, and the anatomy of the animal remains unknown [[2, p. 216]](#ref-2). The combination of restricted distribution and incomplete anatomical knowledge makes *Plicaxis* a notable gap in the understanding of Malayan pulmonate diversity.

## Introduced Species and Biogeographical Context

The problem of introduced species features prominently in Laidlaw's treatment. *Achatina fulica*, an African species, became established in Kedah and Province Wellesley after accidental introduction, and is noted to be preyed upon by the larva of a glow-worm [[2, p. 215]](#ref-2). This observation links the biogeographical question of how non-indigenous species become established with the local ecological dynamics of predator–prey interaction.

More broadly, the biogeographical pattern that emerges from the checklist is one of faunal impoverishment on the Peninsula relative to the greater Sunda islands. The fact that all thirteen species shared with Java are those whose distribution has been extended by human activity [[2, p. 211]](#ref-2) suggests that the natural faunal overlap between the Peninsula and Java is minimal, and that the two landmasses have been effectively isolated from one another in terms of terrestrial pulmonate dispersal. The sparse representation of *Amphidromus*—a genus that is richly diversified in Borneo, Sumatra, and Java—reinforces this pattern [[2, p. 229]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The documentation of Malayan molluscs in the Society's journal literature reflects a progression from early descriptive work to more systematic taxonomic synthesis. Laidlaw's 1933 checklist explicitly bridges the earlier descriptive contributions of Stoliczka (1873), de Morgan (1885), Collinge (1902–1903), and von Mollendorff (1891, 1902) with the more systematic frameworks of Thiele (1931) and Hoffmann (1924, 1929) [[2, p. 211]](#ref-2). The survey was itself a multi-year undertaking, with the Operculata treated in 1928 and the Pulmonata completed in 1933, indicating the scale of the task and the incremental nature of the work [[2, p. 211]](#ref-2).

Hamilton's 1933 note, by contrast, represents a different mode of contribution: a short, practical compilation of vernacular names that supplements the taxonomic literature with ethnolinguistic data. Its cross-referencing with Winstedt's independent recordings introduces an element of verification that is characteristic of the collaborative culture of Malayan natural history in this period [[1, p. 135]](#ref-1).

The primary material for Laidlaw's checklist was drawn from the F.M.S. Museums (including the Raffles Museum collection), the British Museum, and his own field collections from the Cameron Highlands (1931), Gunong Pulai in Johore, and the Batu Caves in Selangor [[2]](#ref-2). The combination of museum-based taxonomy and targeted fieldwork—particularly the repeated sampling of cave localities—establishes a methodological template that subsequent workers in the field would follow. The candid acknowledgment of gaps, such as the unknown anatomy of *Plicaxis* and the "very imperfectly known" status of the Pulmonata as a whole, underscores that the 1933 checklist was intended as a foundation rather than a definitive account [[2, pp. 211]](#ref-2).
