# The metalliferous formation of the peninsula

D.D. Daly, a British official involved in boundary surveys in the Straits Settlements, presented this paper to the Royal Asiatic Society on 2 September 1878, proposing that the gold and tin alluvial deposits of the Malay Peninsula are detrital products of a single primary lode running along the central mountain spine from Cape Patani in the north to the Kesang River in the south. His overarching thesis is that locating and working this "main reef" with European machinery would transform the peninsula's economy from its current dependence on itinerant Chinese alluvial mining into a more stable and profitable system.

## Summary

Daly's paper is fundamentally a geological prospectus. He traces a line of mineral occurrences from Tanjong Tohor in the south through the Kesang, Segamet, and Pahang rivers to Cape Patani in the north, arguing that the scattered alluvial gold and tin deposits on either side of the central range are all derived from a single primary formation. His methodological model is explicitly borrowed from Sir Roderick Murchison's prediction of the Ballarat gold fields in Australia: by studying the nature, set, and dip of strata, one can deduce the location of the parent lode. Daly supports this with field observations from a boundary expedition between Perak and Siamese territory, where he encountered slate formations cropping out of the granite in a manner he considered analogous to Australian gold-bearing districts, and where local Malays confirmed the presence of coarse, scaly gold dust indicating recent detachment from its matrix.

The paper is also a critique of the political and economic obstacles to proper mining. Native rulers in Pahang, Trenggano, and Kelantan actively prevent European access and the introduction of machinery, while the Sultans of the latter states are described as powerless against their own rajas. Daly frames the current system—Chinese miners gambling on alluvial patches, moving along the hill base with uncertain returns—as a form of "reckless speculation" that will persist until the primary lode is found. He identifies two specific locations where tin is found in large blocks rolled down hillside, suggesting proximity to the main lode and recommending them as starting points for a boring expedition.

### Key Findings

- The alluvial tin deposit on the western side of the central range extends approximately 250 miles in length and ranges from 1 to 12 miles in width, winding northwest to Tongkah and into British Burmah (p. 196).
- The defunct Chindras Gold Company's deepest shaft reached only about 100 feet, which Daly considers insufficient to reach a compact body of stone (p. 195).
- Gold prices varied dramatically by location: $22 per bungkal in Pahang (where the Bendahara controlled sales), $32 in Selangor, and $35–$40 in Singapore (p. 196).
- Two specific prospecting sites are identified as likely close to the main lode: Ulu Slini on the Selangor–Perak border and the Batang Padang District in Perak, where tin occurs in large blocks rolled down hillside (p. 198).
- The Bendahara of Pahang prohibits European visits to Ulu Sungei Lui and prevents Chinese miners from introducing machinery, resulting in gold being worked "most imperfectly" with pestle and mortar (p. 196).
- Coal has been found in the Isthmus of Kra, marking the northern terminus of the metalliferous zone (p. 197).

### Conclusion

Daly's definitive takeaway is that the Malay Peninsula's mineral wealth rests on an unstable foundation of alluvial prospecting, and that the application of European geological science and mechanical mining to the primary lode—once located through systematic boring—would convert the region from a speculative alluvial economy into one of "more certain and legitimate" industrial mining, securing the prosperity of the Straits Settlements on a "more solid basis."

## Context

- Daly's fieldwork was conducted during a boundary-fixing expedition between Perak and Siamese territory, indicating his role as a British colonial official with direct access to interior regions and native informants (p. 197).
- The paper draws on Murchison's Australian gold-field predictions as its methodological template, situating it within the broader Victorian geological discourse on prospecting through stratal analysis (p. 197).
