# The State of Lukut

L.D. Gammans published "The State of Lukut" in 1924 in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, offering a compact administrative and military history of the Lukut settlement on the west coast of the Malay Peninsula. The article traces the state's brief period of semi-independent rule under Selangor-appointed viceroys from the 1830s to its absorption into Selangor following the Sungai Ujong War of 1886–87, and closes with a topographical survey of the Lukut Fort.

## Summary

Gammans' narrative centres on two successive rulers—Raja Jumaat and his son Raja Bot—who governed Lukut as viceroyalties of Selangor but exercised de facto sovereignty, collecting their own revenue and administering justice without reference to the Selangor treasury. The article draws on a 1860 report by Captain Macpherson, Resident Councillor of Malacca, as its principal primary source for the settlement at its zenith, which describes macadamised roads, a uniformed police force, a regulated gambling farm, and a Chinese quarter of over forty brick shop-houses. Gammans uses this to argue that Lukut represented a remarkable experiment in multi-ethnic governance under a single Malay authority, combining Malay, Chinese, and Arab populations under a system that was both pragmatic and, by the standards of the time, orderly.

The second half of the article shifts to the military and political crises that ended Lukut's autonomy. Two local disturbances—a raid by Raja Sulaiman of Sungai Raya and the violent episode involving Yahya, adopted son of the Sultan of Langat, who beheaded a Chinese coolie in the settlement—illustrate the fragility of Raja Bot's position. The decisive blow came with the British military expedition to Sungai Ujong, during which Major-General Anson's forces passed through Lukut and a Bugis gunner was accidentally killed during a salute. The subsequent negotiations saw Lukut formally annexed to Selangor, and Raja Bot retired to Singapore with a solatium before dying at Klang in 1911.

The article concludes with a detailed physical description of the Lukut Fort on Bukit Gajah Mati, noting its rectangular plan, defensive ditch, cannon emplacements, water cisterns, and the remains of Raja Jumaat's daughter's house, several carved panels from which had been transferred to the Kuala Lumpur Museum.

### Key Findings

- Sultan Muhammad of Selangor (r. 1826–1858) annexed Lukut and appointed Raja Jumaat as viceroy; Jumaat extended his authority from the Sepang to the Linggi river (p. 290).
- Raja Jumaat instituted a uniformed police force of 22 Malays with numbered hats and built the Lukut Fort on Bukit Gajah Mati (p. 290).
- The Chinese settlement comprised a double row of more than 40 brick shop-houses, mostly Hylam, whose foundations were still visible in 1924 (p. 290).
- Raja Bot's revenue consisted of a 10 per cent levy on tin exports; he maintained a mercenary force of approximately 30 Arabs who intermarried with local Malays (p. 291).
- During the British expedition to Sungai Ujong, a Bugis gunner was killed when a cannon discharged while he was ramming the charge with a ramrod (p. 292).
- Raja Bot received a solatium of $20,000 upon Lukut's forfeiture to Selangor and died at Klang in 1911 (p. 292).
- The fort's defensive ditch measured 10–12 feet in depth; three large brick cisterns for water supply still remained, and the well at the south-west corner yielded water only in the wettest season (p. 295).

### Conclusion

Gammans presents Lukut as a case study in the transition from indigenous Malay sovereignty to colonial administrative absorption. The state's brief autonomy—sustained by tin revenue, multi-ethnic settlement, and capable local leadership—was ultimately rendered obsolete by the larger geopolitical realignments of the 1880s, and its fort stands as a physical monument to a political order that the British intervention permanently displaced.

## Context

- Gammans writes from the perspective of a colonial-era official or administrator, framing the Lukut rulers in terms that emphasise their orderliness and "civilisation" in contrast to the surrounding jungle, a rhetorical strategy typical of British administrative writing of the period (p. 290).
- The article relies on a single extant primary document—Macpherson's 1860 report—supplemented by local oral tradition and the author's own inspection of the fort; no archival collections are cited.
