# The Pahang capital

Tunku Tan Sri Dato' Shahriman bin Tunku Sulaiman, in this 2003 article for the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, traces the two-stage relocation of Pahang's administrative capital from Pekan to Kuala Lipis in 1889 and then to Kuantan in 1955, arguing that each transfer was driven by a convergence of economic miscalculation, political friction between the Malay ruler and British Residents, and practical infrastructure constraints that made the interim site untenable over time.

## Summary

The article opens with the arrival of John P. Rodger as the first British Resident of Pahang in October 1888, who found the royal capital of Pekan to be a modest settlement of roughly 5,000 people. Two forces made a relocation unavoidable: the opening of gold and tin concessions in Ulu Pahang, which shifted the economic centre of gravity upstream, and the political rupture between Sultan Ahmad al-Mu'azzam Shah and the British, who felt the partnership could not survive proximity. The Sultan withdrew to Pulau Tawar for three and a half years, delegating authority to his son Tunku Mahmud as Regent, while the British established a new administrative headquarters in the interior.

The choice of Kuala Lipis was shaped by Hugh Clifford's familiarity with Ulu Pahang and his relationships with local chiefs, though the site was inaugurated under violent circumstances when the first superintendent, W. C. Mitchell, was killed by the local chief Haji Wan Daud. Intended as a temporary measure, Kuala Lipis nonetheless endured for 64 years. Its decline was gradual: the gold mines closed, the town failed to attract sustained Chinese commercial settlement, and the catastrophic floods of 1926 exposed its vulnerability at the confluence of the Jelai and Lipis rivers.

Despite proposals as early as 1901 to relocate the headquarters, the matter was repeatedly deferred—first pending railway construction, then by the economic slump of the 1930s, and later by the financial pressures of the Malayan Emergency. The decisive catalyst came in 1952 when the Federal Government approved funding for government quarters that could not be accommodated in Kuala Lipis. Between the Temerloh–Mentakab area and Kuantan, the latter prevailed due to its level terrain, water supply, airport, and proximity to Pekan. The transfer was formally executed on 27 August 1955 under the energetic leadership of Menteri Besar Tengku Muhammad and Acting State Secretary Abdul Razak, who overcame significant opposition from state and federal officers.

### Key Findings

- Pekan's population was estimated at 5,000 including surrounding kampungs when Rodger arrived in 1888 (p. 87).
- Sultan Ahmad withdrew to Pulau Tawar from December 1889 to May 1893, a period of three and a half years during which his Pekan residences fell into disrepair (p. 87).
- The British protectorate regime of 1889 divided Pahang into six districts: Pekan, Kuala Pahang, Kuantan, Rompin, Temerloh, and Ulu Pahang (p. 87).
- The land at Kuala Lipis was purchased from the sons of Haji Wan Daud for $200; Mitchell was subsequently killed by the chief himself (p. 88).
- The 1926 floods raised water to near the first-floor level of shophouses and caused the collapse of several buildings (p. 90).
- The final decision to transfer to Kuantan was made on 2 March 1953; the official ceremony took place on 27 August 1955, during which the state had seen four Sultans and sixteen British Residents (pp. 91–92).

### Conclusion

Shahriman frames the 64-year tenure of Kuala Lipis as a testament to the inertia of colonial administration, where a site chosen under urgent political and security pressures became entrenched despite its evident unsuitability. The eventual move to Kuantan, completed just two years before Malayan independence, represented not merely an administrative correction but a symbolic new beginning for the state.

## Context

- The article draws on Pahang Annual Reports (AR Pahang 1889, 1890), W. Linehan's 1936 "A History of Pahang" in JMBRAS, and *Twentieth Century Impressions of British Malaya* (1908), supplemented by personal photographs from the Tengku family and the Tun Razak family.
- As a contribution to local administrative history, the piece fills a gap in the historiography of Pahang's colonial governance by providing a coherent narrative of capital-city selection that had previously been treated only in passing in broader state histories.
