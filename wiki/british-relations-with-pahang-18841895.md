# British relations with Pahang, 1884–1895

John Anderson, Malay Translator to the Penang Government, compiled this two-part work in 1823–24 under the supervision of Governor Robert Fullerton, arguing that Britain was bound by treaty and interest to intervene in Kedah following its Siamese conquest in 1821–22. J. S. Bastin's 1962 introduction to this facsimile reprint corrects the long-accepted narrative that the book was suppressed by government order, demonstrating instead that the suppression story originated in rumour and was categorically denied by Anderson himself in 1841.

## Summary

The work is divided into two distinct sections. Part First (pp. 1–112) constructs a political and legal argument for British intervention in Kedah. Anderson traces the history of Kedah's relations with Siam from the Malayan Annals through the period of the cession of Penang in 1786, contending that the triennial transmission of the *Boonga Mas* (golden and silver flower) was a mere token of courtesy analogous to Siam's own tribute to China, not evidence of feudal subjection. He marshals the correspondence of Captain Francis Light, Captain Glass, and the Governor General to demonstrate that the King of Kedah ceded Penang in expectation of British protection against Siamese aggression, and that Light explicitly promised such assistance. The Siamese conquest of Kedah in November 1821—executed by treachery when the Siamese fleet surrounded the wharf and seized the Malay chiefs—is presented as an act of unprovoked aggression against a sovereign ally. Anderson further argues that the Siamese had already conquered Perak in 1818 at Kedah's behest (itself under Siamese compulsion), and was preparing to move against Selangor, making British intervention a matter of urgency. He proposes a comprehensive scheme: declare Kedah under British protection, extend guardianship to the other Malayan states, establish a post at Pankour, and develop the tin trade of the peninsula.

Part Second (pp. 113–204) is a detailed geographical and economic survey of the tin-producing states on the western coast from Junk Ceylon to Lingi near Malacca. Anderson enumerates 84 rivers, describes the villages and populations along each, records tin production figures and prices, and provides ethnographic notes on the Semang, the Bugis settlers of Selangor, and the administrative structures of the various states. The section draws heavily on his own observations and on the testimony of native informants, and includes a description of the mining and smelting processes.

Bastin's introduction (pp. i–x) addresses the book's publication history and the controversy surrounding its supposed suppression. He shows that Fullerton authorized the printing of 100 copies on his own authority in December 1824, that the Supreme Government received them favourably, and that no action was taken to recall or destroy them. The suppression narrative, Bastin argues, likely originated with James Fairlie Carnegy (Anderson's brother-in-law) and was amplified by J. R. Logan, who had no first-hand knowledge of the period. Anderson's own 1841 statement in the *Asiatic Journal*—that no copies were called in and no pledge was required—effectively demolishes the story, as does the survival of at least ten copies identified by Bastin.

### Key Findings

- Only 100 copies were printed in 1824; Bastin identified ten surviving copies in libraries at Oxford, London, The Hague, Taiping, and Singapore (pp. 12–13).
- The Siamese fleet entered the Kedah river on 12 November 1821; the King fled on elephants and reached British territory at the Prye River after five days (pp. 41–44).
- Kedah's pre-conquest population was estimated at 100,000 within an area of 150 miles by 30–35 miles; the country contained 128 *mukims* (administrative divisions) (pp. 184, 188).
- Perak's annual tin export to the Dutch was approximately 5,000 piculs, delivered at 32 Spanish dollars per *bahar* of 428 lbs; the total produce was estimated at 9,000 piculs (pp. 224–225).
- Junk Ceylon's tin ore assayed at 64.5% fine tin, compared with 53.5% for Penang ore; the island was capable of producing approximately 500 tons annually (pp. 308–309).
- The average tin export from Pinang in the seven years preceding 1822 was 16,300 piculs per annum; the cession of Penang cost the King of Kedah an estimated 20,000 dollars in annual revenue (pp. 136, 189).

### Conclusion

Anderson's definitive historical takeaway is that Britain had both a contractual obligation—rooted in Light's promises of protection at the time of the 1786 cession—and a strategic imperative to check Siamese expansion in the peninsula. The Siamese conquest of Kedah threatened Pinang's food supply, encouraged piracy, and positioned a hostile power at Britain's doorstep. Anderson's work was directly influential on Governor Fullerton's despatch of 19 October 1824 urging a strong anti-Siamese policy, and it informed the negotiations that produced the Anglo-Siamese and Anglo-Perak treaties of 1826 under Burney and James Low.

## Context

- Anderson was the Malay Translator to the Penang Government, a position that gave him access to official records and native informants; the work was compiled "partly from the Records of Government, and partly from those sources of private information to which his official pursuits had afforded him access" (p. 6).
- Bastin's introduction draws on the Straits Settlements Records (India Office Library, London), the Burney Papers (Royal Commonwealth Society, London), the trial transcript of Tengku Mohamed Saad (1840), and the Calcutta press controversy of 1841, providing a historiographical correction to Logan's account that had been accepted by all subsequent commentators.
