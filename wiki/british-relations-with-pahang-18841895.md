# British relations with Pahang, 1884–1895

J. de Silva's 1962 article in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* traces the transformation of Pahang from a semi-independent Malay state into a British-protected territory under the Residential system between 1884 and 1895. Set against the backdrop of the British "Forward Policy" in the Malay Peninsula, the article argues that the introduction of a British Resident was driven less by humanitarian concern than by the imperative to protect British mining capital, and that the resulting 1891–92 rebellion was a direct consequence of the Resident's failure to secure the cooperation of the Sultan and his territorial Chiefs.

## Summary

De Silva begins by establishing the political context: after the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 removed Dutch influence from the peninsula, Pahang's Bendahara increasingly regarded himself as an independent sovereign, and by 1863 Bendahara Ahmad had consolidated his rule following a civil war with his brother Mutahir (pp. 1–2). The British Government recognized Ahmad as de facto ruler, but the state remained outside formal British protection. Through the 1870s, successive Governors—Ord, Clarke, Jervois, and Weld—offered "advice" to the Bendahara, but these overtures were received with politeness and produced no substantive change in Pahang's governance (pp. 3–4). The turning point came in 1884, when the Raja Muda Wan Mansur's failed bid to overthrow the Bendahara prompted the latter to place himself "in Smith's hands," opening the door to formal negotiation (p. 5).

The negotiations of 1884–1887 were marked by the Sultan's reluctance to accept a British Resident and his persistent bartering of mining concessions to European companies, which the Governor repeatedly remonstrated against (pp. 5–7). Hugh Clifford, Weld's nephew, was dispatched as the Governor's representative and eventually secured the Sultan's signature on an Agreement in October 1887, establishing a British Agent in Pahang (pp. 7–8). However, the Agent's status was that of a consul rather than a Resident, and Clifford himself acknowledged he was "no more an adviser of the Sultan than anyone in Singapore was" (p. 9). The murder of the Chinese subject Go Hui in 1888 provided the pretext for Sir C.C. Smith to demand the full Residential system; the Sultan, under pressure, requested a Resident on 20 August 1888, and W.P. Rodger arrived in October of that year (pp. 9–10). De Silva notes that Clifford described the passing of Pahang under British "protection" as "barely distinguishable from annexation" (p. 11).

The period 1888–1891 saw the gradual introduction of administrative structures—a police force, courts, a State Council, and district collectors—but the Sultan's deliberate uncooperativeness (he spent most of this period at Pulau Tawar in the interior) and the Chiefs' resentment at diminished authority created conditions ripe for rebellion (pp. 12–14). The Semantan Rebellion of December 1891, precipitated by the Orang Kaya of Semantan's grievance over an inadequate pension and the injudicious conduct of the Acting Collector, escalated into a wider crisis involving the murder of two Englishmen and a threatened attack on Pekan (pp. 15–20). The rebellion was ultimately suppressed through a combination of Sikh police operations, the Sultan's nominal military expedition, and the Governor's personal intervention in August 1892, which compelled the Sultan to reside permanently at Pekan and publicly express regret (pp. 20–22). The financial unsustainability of Pahang's administration—expenditure running at nearly four times revenue by 1891—ultimately drove the decision to incorporate Pahang into the Federated Malay States through the Treaty of Federation, signed at Pekan on 19 June 1895 (pp. 23–25).

### Key Findings

- The 1887 Agreement establishing a British Agent in Pahang was extracted through a combination of the Sultan's political vulnerability after the Raja Muda's failed coup and the intervention of the Dato Mentri of Johore, who "turned the scale in Clifford's favour" (p. 7).
- Sir C.C. Smith was determined to introduce the Residential system by July 1888; the Go Hui murder was the immediate pretext, but as Sir Frederick Dickson later stated, the "true explanation...was not for the avenging of the life of a British subject, but really for the preservation of British capital" (p. 11).
- Pahang's financial position was dire: by 1891 the State Debt had accumulated to $588,055, with expenditure of $238,178 against revenue of only $77,386, and the Colony of Singapore was advancing the difference at 5 per cent interest (p. 14).
- The Semantan Rebellion was precipitated by the Acting Collector's decision to take three arrested men under guard to Lubok Trua rather than release them on bail, an action Clifford described as "most injudicious" (p. 16).
- The Sultan's commitment to suppressing the rebellion was nominal; despite Rodger's repeated reports of the Sultan's good faith, the Governor concluded by June 1892 that the Sultan was "aware of the situation" regarding the ex-Orang Kaya's escape and was "wishing, if possible, to screen those belonging to it" (p. 21).
- The Treaty of Federation was signed at Pekan on 19 June 1895 after only thirty hours' stay by Swettenham, with the Governor's instructions emphasizing that the rulers were "not in the slightest degree...diminishing the powers and privileges which they now possess"—a claim De Silva notes was contradicted by the reality that the Residents were the de facto rulers (p. 25).

### Conclusion

De Silva's definitive historical takeaway is that the Residential system in Pahang represented, in effect, virtual annexation achieved through legal fiction. The Sultan's "right of self-government" was a nominal concession; in practice, the Resident acting on the Governor's orders was the true administrator. Yet De Silva concedes that despite the rebellion and the coercive circumstances of its introduction, the system "put an end to cruelty and despotism," and by 1893 the condition of the native population had improved markedly (p. 25). The article thus presents a nuanced account in which British imperial expansion was simultaneously an act of economic self-interest and a genuine, if reluctant, improvement in the welfare of the Malay peasantry.

## Context

- The article draws extensively on Secret Despatches to the Secretary of State for the Colonies, Annual Reports for the State of Pahang, and the proceedings of the Singapore Legislative Council, many of which are held in the National Archives of Malaysia and the Public Record Office, London.
- De Silva's work sits within the MBRAS tradition of recovering the administrative history of the Malay States from colonial official correspondence, complementing earlier studies by Winstedt, Linehan, and Wilkinson on Pahang's pre-colonial political history.
