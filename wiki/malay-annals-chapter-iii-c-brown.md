# Malay Annals, chapter III. .C. Brown

C. C. Brown's 1969 translation of Chapter III of the *Malay Annals* (*Sejarah Melayu*) presents the legendary foundation narrative of the classical Malay polities, tracing the descent of Alexander the Great's lineage into the Malay world and culminating in the founding of Singapura by Sri Tri Buana. Published in the Singapore 150th Anniversary Commemorative Issue of the *JMBRAS*, the translation renders the chapter's central thesis: that Malay sovereignty derives from a sacred, divinely sanctioned lineage whose legitimacy is sealed through a mutual covenant between ruler and subject.

## Summary

The chapter opens in Palembang, ruled by Demang Lebar Daun, a descendant of Raja Shulan. Two widows, Wan Empok and Wan Malini, cultivate rice on Bukit Si-Guntang Mahameru, where their paddy miraculously turns to gold, silver, and gold alloy upon the arrival of three princely youths—Bichitram, Paladutani, and Nilatanam—who identify themselves as descendants of Raja Iskandar Dzu'l-Karnain (Alexander the Great) through the lineage of Raja Sulaiman (p. 26). The ruler of Palembang receives the princes and distributes them: the eldest becomes Sang Sapurba of Minangkabau, the second becomes Sang Maniaka of Tanjong Pura, and the youngest remains at Palembang as Sang Utama, with Demang Lebar Daun abdicating in his favour (p. 27).

A figure named Bath, born from the foam of a white sow, reads a *chiri* (coronation formula) that elevates Sang Utama to the title Sri Tri Buana. The king then seeks a consort but finds that thirty-nine princesses are stricken with chloasma after sleeping with him. Demang Lebar Daun offers his daughter Wan Sendari on condition that a mutual covenant be made: the king's descendants must never disgrace the Bendahara's line, and the Bendahara's descendants must never be disloyal to the king's line, even under oppression (pp. 27–28). This covenant, sealed with a solemn oath invoking divine retribution, establishes the foundational principle of Malay political ethics.

After a forty-day lustration ceremony, Sri Tri Buana sails to the coast and, via Bentan (ruled by Queen Wan Sri Benian, who adopts him as her son), reaches Tanjong Bemian. There he witnesses a lion-like creature and, upon seeing the white sands of Temasek across the water, decides to found a city. He names it Singapura, rules for forty-eight years, and is buried on Singapore Hill. His son Paduka Sri Pikrama Wira succeeds him, and the chapter closes with the establishment of the court hierarchy—Bendahara, Perdana Menteri, Temenggong—and the growing fame of Singapura as a great trading city (pp. 32–33).

### Key Findings

- The chapter explicitly links Malay royal legitimacy to the lineage of Alexander the Great (Raja Iskandar Dzu'l-Karnain) through Raja Sulaiman, a genealogical claim that positions Malay sovereignty within a universal, prophetic history (p. 26).
- The covenant between Sri Tri Buana and Demang Lebar Daun establishes that Malay rulers must never publicly shame their Bendahara-line subjects, and that those subjects must never be disloyal to their rulers regardless of injustice suffered—a principle the text presents as divinely ordained and binding for all time (pp. 28–29).
- The *chiri* read by Bath, a being born from the foam of a sow, confers the title Sri Tri Buana and is identified as the origin of the readers of coronation formulas in later times (p. 27).
- The founding of Singapura is triggered by the sighting of a lion-like animal (red body, black head, white breast, larger than a he-goat) at Kuala Temasek, which Demang Lebar Daun identifies as a lion, providing the etymological basis for the city's name (p. 32).
- The court hierarchy established under Paduka Sri Pikrama Wira includes the Bendahara (Tun Perpateh Permuka Berjajar), the Perdana Menteri (Perpatah Permuka Sekalar, a son of Demang Lebar Daun), the Penghulu Bendahari (Tun Jana Buga Dendang), and the Temenggong (Tun Jana Putra), with specific protocols for audience and precedence (p. 33).
- Queen Wan Sri Benian of Bentan is credited with instituting the drum of sovereignty (*drum of kingship*), a practice subsequently adopted by other Malay rulers (p. 30).

### Conclusion

Brown's translation presents Chapter III as the foundational charter of Malay political theology: sovereignty is not merely inherited but is divinely validated through a sacred covenant that binds ruler and subject in mutual obligation. The chapter's ultimate historical takeaway is that the legitimacy of the Malay state rests on this original pact—breach of which by either party invites divine destruction of the kingdom—thereby encoding a constitutional principle of reciprocal duty within a mythological framework that traces Malay kingship to the universal lineage of Alexander and Solomon.

## Context

- This translation appeared in the Singapore 150th Anniversary Commemorative Issue (July 1969) of the *JMBRAS*, reflecting the period's heightened scholarly and public interest in Singapore's pre-colonial origins as narrated in the *Sejarah Melayu*.
- The *Malay Annals* is the principal literary monument of the classical Malay court tradition, compiled in its extant form in the early seventeenth century; Chapter III is among the most frequently cited passages in debates on the historicity of early Singapura and the political ideology of the Malay world.
