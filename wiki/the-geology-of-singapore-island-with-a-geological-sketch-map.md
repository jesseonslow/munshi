# The geology of Singapore Island; with a geological sketch map

J. C. Moulton, accompanied by naturalists H. N. Ridley, E. B. Copeland, J. J. Smith, A. Griffini, and F. F. Laidlaw, published this detailed account of the first successful European expedition to Mount Batu Lawi in the Limbang–Madihit region of Sarawak, Borneo, in 1912. The paper, appearing in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (No. 63), combines a day-by-day travel narrative with historical, ethnographic, and natural-historical observations gathered during a two-and-a-half-month journey in 1911.

## Summary

The expedition was motivated by long-standing rumours that Batu Lawi was a sacred mountain whose very name should not be spoken, and that pointing at it would summon heavy rain. Moulton's account opens with a historical survey of all previous European approaches to the region, establishing that only three Europeans had reached the Kuala Madihit in the preceding twenty years, and that no white man had previously reached the mountain itself. Sir Spenser St. John's 1858 journey up the Limbang remains the principal prior reference point; Moulton carried St. John's book and map throughout the expedition and used them continuously for comparison.

The narrative proper covers the river journey up the Limbang and Madihit (May 2–17, 1911), the overland crossing through the Turan–Derian range (May 19–29), the brief sojourn on Batu Lawi and Selinguid (May 29–June 1), and the return by way of the Seridan, Mago, Tutau, and Baram rivers to Claudetown (June 2–15). Along the way, Moulton records extensive ethnographic detail on the Tabun, Murut, Kalabit, Dayak, Penan, and Kayan peoples encountered: jar burial customs, head-shaving on bereavement, the system of slave-children, omen-reading, and the linguistic relationships (and mutual unintelligibility) between these groups. Natural-history observations, though constrained by the hurried nature of the transit, yielded several new and rare species, including the first recorded observation of a Lycaenid butterfly tending a Homopterous larva in the field.

The paper is structured as a primary-source diary rather than a synthetic argument. Moulton explicitly disclaims authority on native life and frames his contribution as a stimulus for more experienced observers to publish their own records. The concluding section justifies the expedition's modest collecting yield by the logistical constraints of the sparsely populated Madihit district, where no native habitations existed for stretches of four to thirteen days.

### Key Findings

- The base of Batu Lawi stands at approximately 3,500 ft above sea level; the mountain rises a further 2,700 ft, giving a total elevation of roughly 6,200 ft. The higher of the two peaks presents a sheer limestone face of about 600 ft, described as "like a chimney" (pp. 38–40).
- The total journey from Kuching to Kuching lasted two and a half months (May 2 – July 17, 1911); the segment from Limbang to Claudetown took 40 days, of which 31 were spent in actual travelling. The party slept two consecutive nights in the same place on only five occasions (p. 56).
- Temperatures at altitude dropped to 62°F (16.7°C) at 2 p.m. on Mt. Derian (4,700–5,000 ft) and 63°F at dusk on the Selinguid summit (4,850 ft), in marked contrast to the 80–82°F recorded at lower river levels (pp. 33–35, 38).
- Tama Kuling's feast at the Kuala Madihit featured 35 great jars of *tuach* (fermented rice liquor), each holding approximately 23 gallons, totalling some 800 gallons (p. 22).
- The expedition's natural-history collections, though small in volume, were "surprisingly rich in new and rare species," including *Oriolus hosei* (a rare oriole), *Papilio brookeanus*, *Papilio payeni brunei*, *Amblypodia narada*, *Neurobasis chinensis*, and *Allotinus nivalis* observed tending a Homopterous larva (pp. 13, 23, 33–34, 56).
- The vocabulary appendix (Appendix IX) records approximately 150 words in Tabun, Murut, and Kalabit, with cross-references indicating which terms are shared across the three languages, providing one of the earliest comparative lexical datasets for these groups (pp. 100–104).

### Conclusion

Moulton's definitive contribution is the establishment of a reliable physical and geographical baseline for the Batu Lawi region, replacing the "vague rumours" and second-hand reports that had characterised all prior knowledge of the mountain. By documenting the route, altitudes, river conditions, tribal distributions, and the mountain's actual topography, the paper transforms Batu Lawi from a legendary object of native superstition into a mapped and measurable feature of Borneo's interior, while simultaneously preserving a snapshot of Kalabit, Tabun, and Murut material culture at a moment when Sarawak's administrative reach was still consolidating over these border districts.

## Context

- The paper draws on the author's own field diary, St. John's 1858 map and narrative, and reports in the *Sarawak Gazette* and *Sarawak Museum Journal*; no major archival collection beyond these published sources is cited.
- Historiographically, the article is significant as one of the few first-person European accounts of the Upper Limbang–Madihit watershed before the region was more thoroughly surveyed in the 1920s and 1930s, and its ethnographic and linguistic data remain a primary reference for the Kalabit and Tabun languages.
