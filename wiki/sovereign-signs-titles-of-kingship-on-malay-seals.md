# Sovereign signs: titles of kingship on Malay seals

## Abstract

The recent publication of a new catalogue of over 2,000 Malay seals—defined as seals from Southeast Asia, with inscriptions in Arabic script—makes available for the first time a substantial corpus of primary source material from the Malay archipelago, dating from the sixteenth to the early twentieth century. The main function of the inscription on a Malay seal was to identify the seal owner and to locate him or her within their social, political and spiritual universe. This was evoked through an iteration of his/her title and/or personal name, sometimes accompanied by a pedigree, a place name, a date, a religious expression, and an amuletic formula imbued with protective powers. In this article the titles of Malay kingship inscribed on seals will be explored through the various component elements: the prefatory honorifics, the title proper of the ruler, and the sovereign epithets used to confirm and magnify his status.

## Summary

Drawing on the newly published catalogue of 2,168 Malay seals (Gallop 2019), this article analyses the titles of kingship inscribed on sovereign seals from the Malay archipelago, dating from the second half of the sixteenth century to the early twentieth century. Of the 655 sovereign seals in the corpus (30 per cent of the total), the title *sultan* appears on 70 per cent, establishing it as the unifying marker of Muslim kingship across the region. The article examines three structural components of royal inscriptions: prefatory honorifics of Sanskrit origin (*seri*, *paduka*, *duli*), the title proper of the ruler, and sovereign epithets drawn from the caliphal and Persian traditions that served to confirm and magnify the ruler's status (pp. 1–3).

A central argument is that while *sultan* carried enormous prestige and international recognition, it did not always denote the apex of the Malay political hierarchy. The indigenous title *Yang Dipertuan* ('he who is made lord') is identified as the only unambiguous indicator of absolute political authority, and its absence from a seal could signal that a ruler had not undergone formal coronation or held a junior position in a dualistic system of government (pp. 5–10). The article further demonstrates that seals function as stern guardians of official dignity: in several cases the title used in the body of a letter exceeds what the accompanying seal actually claims, revealing a gap between aspirational self-presentation and the more conservative language of the seal itself (p. 8).

The final section traces the dissemination of caliphal epithets—*khalifa*, *khalifat Allah*, and especially *zill Allah fi al-ʿalam* ('the shadow of God in the world')—throughout the archipelago, and gives particular attention to *johan berdaulat*, a compound epithet meaning 'the champion endowed with sovereign power' that originated in Aceh during the reign of Iskandar Muda (r. 1607–36) and was subsequently adopted by all male rulers of that state and, by extension, in Perak and Minangkabau (pp. 10–18).

### Key Findings

- The title *sultan* appears on 462 of 655 sovereign seals (70 per cent), but its adoption was not automatic: in Banten it was granted by the Grand Sharif of Mecca in 1638, in Mataram in 1641, and in Johor only in 1885 after protracted negotiations with the British (pp. 6–7).
- The honorifics *bawah* and *duli* appear exclusively on Brunei sovereign seals (19 and 13 respectively), enabling the identification of the patron of a seal from the chief of Kalis in Kalimantan Barat as Sultan Abdul Mumin of Brunei (r. 1852–85) (p. 4).
- *Zill Allah fi al-ʿalam* appears on 48 seals across the archipelago, most densely on 19 Acehnese sovereign seals, with the earliest documented occurrences in the second half of the seventeenth century (pp. 12–13).
- *Johan berdaulat* is found on 21 seals of male Acehnese sovereigns and is absent from all dirhams issued before Iskandar Muda's reign, confirming its institutionalisation during his rule; the epithet subsequently appears on seals from Perak, Inderapura and Minangkabau, likely through Acehnese influence (pp. 14–17).
- In Johor, following the 1722 Malay–Bugis agreement, three office-holders simultaneously bore the title *sultan*—the Malay Yang Dipertuan, the Bugis Raja Tua, and the Bugis Yang Dipertuan Muda—illustrating the tensions inherent in dualistic or tripartite sovereignty (p. 10).
- By the early twentieth century, the most extravagant caliphal epithets had largely disappeared from seal inscriptions, a shift the author attributes to the puritanical movement that gained momentum in the early nineteenth century (p. 14).

### Conclusion

Gallop concludes that Malay seals provide a uniquely reliable record of regnal titles because they preserve the titles actually in use during a ruler's lifetime, unlike manuscript copies that may post-date composition by centuries. The paramount concern of Malay rulers, as revealed by their seals, was to position themselves within the broader Islamic world community by adopting the most widely recognised sovereign titles and caliphal epithets, while the indigenous title *Yang Dipertuan* remained the sole unambiguous marker of supreme political authority in the Malay pantheon (p. 18).

## Context

- The article is based on the British Library's catalogue project, which documents seals from over seventy public and sixty private collections worldwide, including holdings at the National Archives (UK), SOAS, the British Library, and institutions in Malaysia, Indonesia and Brunei (p. 2).
- The seals originate from present-day Malaysia, Brunei, Singapore, Indonesia, and the southern parts of Thailand, Cambodia and the Philippines, reflecting the extent of the Malay *lingua franca* and Islamic cultural sphere in maritime Southeast Asia (p. 2).
