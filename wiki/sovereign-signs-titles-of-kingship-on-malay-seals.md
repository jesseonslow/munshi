# Sovereign signs: titles of kingship on Malay seals

## Abstract

In the nineteenth century, a small group of scribes in Singapore copied texts by hand in paper manuscripts and prepared texts to be reproduced for mechanical reproduction on lithographic presses. This article discusses the rise of the craft and its decline in the face of technological change. It starts with literacy in centres of religious learning and royal patronage, places that gradually gave way under the pressure of modernity to emerging urban business centres as places where writings were produced. The people of urban conglomerates in the Malay world, home to a budding colonial cosmopolitan culture, needed information, entertainment and moral guidance, and the new print media made it possible to provide and distribute texts to the public in periodicals, books, and treatises.

## Summary

This article traces the evolution of literary production in the Malay world from a predominantly oral, manuscript-based culture to a print-mediated one during the nineteenth century. Van der Putten argues that the written word was long confined to religious and royal centres, guarded by a small privileged class of scholars and scribes, and that the shift toward secular, urban, and commercially distributed texts was driven by colonial modernity, urbanisation, and technological change. The narrative is anchored in the figure of the *penyurat* (scribe) and *penyalin* (copyist), whose professional role was simultaneously enabled and ultimately destroyed by the very printing technologies that expanded access to texts.

The article examines specific individuals who bridged the manuscript and print worlds: Abdullah Munshi, who taught Malay and transferred texts onto lithographic stones for Reverend Keasberry's press at Mount Zion; Husin bin Ismail, a Bugis-descended copyist who may have produced over a hundred surviving manuscripts for the Wilkes expedition and other patrons; and Tuan Siami, a Thai-origin scribe in Raffles's employ who turned to polemical poetry after his dismissal from the colonial administration. These figures are shown to be instrumental in seeding the Muslim lithographic printing industry that flourished in Singapore's Kampung Gelam area in the latter half of the century, before being overtaken by cheaper letterpress imports from Bombay, Mecca, Cairo, and Istanbul.

### Key Findings

- Manuscript and translation work for foreign patrons in 1830s–40s Singapore was remunerated at $3–$30+ per text, with Abdullah Munshi as the highest-paid senior scribe and Husin bin Ismail as the most productive copyist (p. 6).
- The Wilkes expedition (1842) acquired 29 Malay and 16 Bugis manuscripts, now held in the Library of Congress and Houghton Library; Abdullah Munshi copied seven of the Malay texts (p. 6).
- The first known indigenously owned and operated lithographic press in the Malay world produced a Qur'an in Palembang in 1848, with the printer acknowledging Abdullah Munshi as his teacher in the art (p. 8).
- Encik Ibrahim of Riau is connected as copyist to at least 95 different titles—mostly rhymed narratives (*syair*)—produced on lithographic presses in Kampung Gelam from the early 1880s to 1910 (p. 8).
- The *Hikayat Abdullah* (1849) was among the first extended Malay texts printed on Keasberry's lithographic press at Mount Zion, Singapore, and featured chromolithographic decorations (p. 7).
- The Muslim lithographic printing industry declined at the start of the twentieth century as the market was flooded with cheaper, technologically superior letterpress books from Bombay, Mecca, Cairo, and Istanbul (p. 9).

### Conclusion

Van der Putten concludes that the scribes and copyists of the nineteenth century occupied a liminal position: they were the human agents who carried the manuscript tradition into the age of mechanical reproduction, yet their professional identity was rendered obsolete by the very technologies they helped introduce. The *penyurat* and *penyalin* became "quaint remnants of yesteryear," their names buried in obscure manuscripts and lithographs, replaced by technicians, editors, and literary agents of the modern publishing world.

## Context

- The article draws on manuscript holdings in the Library of Congress, Houghton Library, and other collections, as well as published accounts of early Malay printing (notably Proudfoot's provisional account of materials printed in the Singapore-Malaysia area up to 1920).
- The study contributes to the historiography of Malay literary production by centring the labour of individual scribes and copyists rather than treating them as anonymous functionaries, and by linking the missionary printing enterprise directly to the subsequent indigenous Muslim print industry.
