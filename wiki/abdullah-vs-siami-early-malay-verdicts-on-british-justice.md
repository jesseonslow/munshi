# Abdullah vs Siami: early Malay verdicts on British justice

J. M. Gullick's 2007 article reconstructs the history of Ulu Langat, a remote interior district in Selangor, from the early nineteenth century through 1900, arguing that the district's transformation from an isolated, tin-mining backwater into a viable "planting district" was ultimately driven by the arrival of the railway and the rubber boom of the 1890s, which finally overcame the geographical barriers of its difficult river and hilly terrain.

## Summary

Gullick's narrative is shaped by the central problem of accessibility. The Langat River, the traditional route into the interior, had "particularly awkward stretches" in its middle reaches, and until lateral roads were built in the 1880s, it was the only means of exporting tin. In the dry season a heavily laden boat might take thirteen days to travel downstream, and Swettenham's 1875 journey from Bandar Langat to Ulu Langat village covered 93 miles in five days. This isolation meant that Ulu Langat, despite having tin deposits, never achieved the scale of development seen in the upper Klang and Selangor valleys, and it remained a peripheral district in the colonial administrative imagination well into the 1890s.

The article traces the district's political and social evolution through several phases. Early settlement involved Malay communities under the distant authority of the To' Engku Klang, Sumatran (Menangkabau and Mandailing) migrants who came for rattan and tin, and the Orang Asli (Sakai), who were already well absorbed into the Malay community by the 1880s. The Selangor civil war of 1867–73 brought Mandailing warriors like Raja Asal and Sutan Puasa into the district, and the post-war period saw the colonial regime install Raja Kahar, a son of the Sultan, as "Malay Magistrate" until 1883, when Ulu Langat was formally constituted as a separate administrative district. A boundary dispute with Sungei Ujong over the Labu River valley was resolved by treaty in 1878, with Selangor ceding Lukut on the coast in exchange for the upstream territory.

The economic narrative moves from the tin boom of the late 1870s and the catastrophic price collapse of the early 1880s, through a period of stagnation, to the "take off" of the mid-1890s. The extension of the railway to Kajang in 1897 was the pivotal development, enabling European and Chinese planters to establish rubber estates in the district for the first time. Gullick draws on a rich array of primary sources—Swettenham's journals and reports, the Douglas diary, Selangor Government Gazette entries, district annual reports, and the *Selangor Journal*—to build a textured picture of daily administrative life, local disputes, and the slow social integration of a diverse population.

### Key Findings

- The 1884 rough census recorded a district population of 3,695 (1,808 Malays, 1,675 Chinese); by the systematic 1891 census this had risen to 8,015 (3,166 Malays, 4,664 Chinese), reflecting significant Chinese immigration driven by mining (pp. 12).
- By the mid-1880s, approximately 1,500 miners, almost all Chinese, were at work in the district's tin deposits, which lay beneath 5–12 feet of overburden in veins 10–50 feet wide and 6 inches to 2 feet deep (p. 10).
- The railway reached Pudu in 1893, Sungei Besi in 1895, and Kajang in 1897; the first European estate land applications (two of 500 acres each) were approved in 1894, and the Kindersley brothers planted an experimental 5 acres of rubber in 1896, generally credited as the first commercial rubber planting in Malaya (pp. 14–16).
- In September 1891, a tin ore discovery near Kajang generated 44 land applications for 915 acres within ten days, illustrating the speculative energy of the mining economy (p. 14).
- Sutan Puasa, arrested in 1875 on suspicion of planning an uprising, was sentenced to three years' imprisonment but released after nine months on a bond of $5,000 for good behaviour (p. 7).
- The 1878 boundary treaty with Sungei Ujong ceded Lukut to the latter state in exchange for the Labu River valley; the ceremony at Rekoh involved a gun salute, the hoisting of the Selangor flag, and a feast attended by some 200 local people (pp. 9–10).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that Ulu Langat's long period of stagnation—described in 1894 as a "misleading District" and a "struggling" one—was broken not by any single policy intervention but by the convergence of improved transport infrastructure and the global rubber boom. The district's transformation into a "flourishing planting district" by 1900 marked a genuine turning point, ending centuries of geographical marginality and integrating Ulu Langat into the broader economic orbit of colonial Selangor.

## Context

- Primary archival sources include the Douglas diary (1876–1882), Swettenham's Malayan Journals (1874–76) and various reports, Selangor Government Gazette entries, district annual reports, and the *Selangor Journal* (1892–1896).
- The study contributes to the regional history of Selangor's interior districts, an area less thoroughly documented than the Klang and Kuala Lumpur river valleys, and complements Gullick's broader work on colonial governance in the Malay states.
