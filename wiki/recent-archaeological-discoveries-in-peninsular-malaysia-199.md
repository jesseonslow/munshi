# Recent archaeological discoveries in Peninsular Malaysia (1991–1993

Adi Haji Taha, a senior officer in the Antiquity Division of the Museums Department, published this comprehensive field survey in 1993 documenting archaeological discoveries and research activities across Peninsular Malaysia between 1991 and 1993. The article serves as both a progress report on the professionalization of Malaysian archaeology and a catalogue of significant new finds, ranging from the earliest confirmed *Homo sapiens* in the peninsula to votive tablets that provide material evidence for Kelantan's role in the Srivijaya sphere.

## Summary

Taha's report is structured geographically, moving through Kedah, Perak, Selangor, Pahang, and Kelantan, and covers both formal university-led excavations and chance discoveries reported by local communities, loggers, and estate workers. The overarching narrative is one of institutional maturation: the establishment of the Association of Malaysian Archaeologists, the launch of the Young Archaeologists Club in 1992, and the increasing involvement of university teams from Universiti Sains Malaysia, Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia, and Universiti Malaya in systematic fieldwork. Taha positions these developments as marking a transition from the earlier era of colonial-era antiquarian collecting to a more scientific, multi-institutional discipline.

The article addresses several historiographical problems. The discovery of the "Perak Man" at Gua Gunung Runtuh, carbon-dated to approximately 11,000 BP, establishes a new baseline for human occupation in the peninsula and prompts discussion of burial practices and social stratification in early Holocene communities. The finding of a Tang glazed ceramic within a slab grave at Ulu Bernam challenges the conventional assignment of the slab grave culture to the Iron Age (0–4th century AD), suggesting some graves may date to the early historic period—a point Taha supports by analogy with the Pakauman site in Indonesia. Perhaps most significantly, the votive tablets recovered from Gua Berhala in Ulu Kelantan provide the first material evidence corroborating long-standing claims that Kelantan was a major seat of government during the Hindu-Buddhist Srivijaya period, a claim that had previously lacked archaeological support.

Taha draws on a mix of primary field observations, unpublished preliminary reports from colleagues (Tjia, Davison, Zulkifli Jaafar, Leong Sau Heng), and earlier published accounts by Evans, Tweedie, Peacock, Sieveking, and Rentse to contextualize new finds within the existing archaeological record. The article is explicitly framed as preliminary in several cases, particularly regarding the fossil vertebrate at Gua Naga Mas and the ongoing Bujang Valley excavations, and Taha repeatedly directs readers to more detailed publications by his colleagues.

### Key Findings

- The "Perak Man" from Gua Gunung Runtuh, Lenggong, was carbon-dated to approximately 11,000 BP, making him the earliest confirmed *Homo sapiens* in Peninsular Malaysia; he was buried in a foetal position with over 2,000 shell food remains and stone tools, and was estimated at 157 cm in height (pp. 71).
- Four new slab (cist) graves were excavated in the Bernam Valley between late 1992 and March 1993; the second grave at Changkat Menteri contained more than 2,700 red glass beads, and the third grave at Ulu Bernam yielded a Tang glazed ceramic tentatively dated to pre-ninth century AD, the only occurrence of Chinese ceramics in a slab grave in Peninsular Malaysia (pp. 73–75).
- Votive tablets were discovered at Gua Berhala, Gunung Biol, Ulu Kelantan, including a tablet impressed with nine positions of the Buddha and a twelve-armed Avalokitesvara; the tablets are pear-shaped, 6–12 cm long, with an average thickness of 2.5 cm, and bear ash pigments possibly from cremated remains (pp. 77–79).
- Cave paintings made with charcoal were identified at Gua Batu Putih, Kodiac, Kedah (reported May 1993), depicting human figurines (50–60 cm in height), buffalo, a snake approximately 1 metre long, boats with masts, and kites; a separate site at Gua Batu Cincin, Ulu Kelantan, yielded 53 figures on a semi-circular wall (pp. 70, 79).
- A fossilized vertebrate measuring 98 cm was found embedded in the travertine ceiling of Gua Naga Mas, Ipoh, at a height of 5.5 metres above the cave floor; Dr. Geoffrey Davison identified it as a big cat, most likely a Middle Pleistocene tiger (*Panthera palaeojavanica*), while Prof. H. D. Tjia dated it to the Late Pleistocene or Pliocene (pp. 72–73).
- A decorated tripod stand sherd was recovered from Kampung Dusun Raja, Kuala Betis, Ulu Kelantan, extending the known distribution of tripod technology beyond the west coast of the peninsula (p. 77).

### Conclusion

Taha's definitive takeaway is that Peninsular Malaysian archaeology has entered a new era of systematic, multi-institutional research that will yield increasingly rigorous data. The discoveries reported—spanning the deep past (11,000 BP human remains, Pleistocene fauna) through the Iron Age (slab graves, Hoabinhian tools) to the early historic period (Srivijaya-era votive tablets, Tang ceramics)—collectively demonstrate that the peninsula's archaeological record is far richer and more complex than previously documented, and that the trained cadre of university archaeologists now working in the field is well positioned to exploit this potential.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on field observations by the author and his Museums Department colleagues, supplemented by unpublished preliminary reports from Prof. H. D. Tjia (geology), Dr. Geoffrey Davison (paleontology), and Assoc. Prof. Leong Sau Heng (slab grave excavations), none of which had been formally published at the time of writing.
- Historiographically, the article functions as a transitional document: it records the shift from the colonial-era model of antiquarian collection (Evans, Tweedie, Peacock, Sieveking) to a university-driven, scientifically methodological approach, and it provides the first published evidence linking Kelantan to the Srivijaya political sphere through material culture rather than textual inference alone.
