# Gold ornaments dug up at Fort Canning, Singapore

R.O. Winstedt's 1928 article in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* presents a critical edition of a composite Malay legal manuscript obtained from the Dato' Luar of Kelantan, which Winstedt demonstrates to be a forgery or misattribution: the rulers named in its oldest section are in fact Kedah sultans, not Kelantan ones. The article prints the full Malay text of four distinct legal codes and a ceremonial chapter, prefaced by an English outline of the earliest port-laws, and situates the manuscript within the broader corpus of Malay legal literature including the *Undang-Undang Melaka* and the Malacca Maritime Code.

## Summary

Winstedt's central argument is one of textual authentication. The manuscript, presented as ancient Kelantan law, contains four codes and a chapter on the *bunga mas* (golden flower tribute to Siam), royal installation, and betrothal customs. The oldest code, dated 1650 A.D., names Sultan Rijalu'd-din Muhammad Shah "at Naga" and Sultan Muhiyyu'd-din Mansur—rulers Winstedt identifies as belonging to Kedah, not Kelantan, thereby "exploding the authenticity" of the document as a Kelantan text (p. 2). The manuscript was subsequently deposited at the School for Oriental Studies, London.

The legal content itself is of considerable interest for the economic and administrative history of pre-colonial Kedah. The 1650 port-laws (Bab I) constitute a detailed tariff and regulatory code governing the entry, inspection, and departure of foreign vessels—particularly from Kalinga (India) and Gujarat—specifying duties in gold (*kupang*, *tahil*, *emas*), compulsory purchase of tin at below-market rates, and the ceremonial protocol for receiving diplomatic letters from Siam, Patani, Perak, Jambi, and the Dutch Company at Malacca. The second code (1667 A.D., Bab II) shifts to rural governance: zakat collection, buffalo fencing and liability, night curfew enforcement, and the authority of the *kweng* (village headman). The third code (Bab III) addresses the powers of the Temenggong (police chief), the inviolability of royal language and dress, and the hierarchical precedence of court officials. The final chapter (Bab IV) describes the physical construction of the *bunga mas*, the regalia carried at a royal installation, and the graded etiquette of betrothal gifts according to social rank.

Winstedt notes that the maritime section of the manuscript is substantially identical to the Malacca Maritime Code as edited by van Ronkel (1919) and translated by Dulaurier (1845), and that a superior version exists in an unpublished manuscript formerly held by E.A.G. Stuart. He also identifies a parallel Achinese copy of the same maritime code dated 1222 A.H. in the Batavian Society collection, suggesting a shared textual tradition across the Malay world.

### Key Findings

- The 1650 port-laws impose a duty of 400 *emas Patani* on ships from Kalinga and 600 *emas* on ships from Gujarat, with the amount adjusted according to vessel size and cargo (pp. 4, 7).
- Tin from Perak was subject to a compulsory purchase: of every six *bahara* (a unit of weight) carried, two *bahara* were bought by the state at a rate one *kupang* of gold below the market price, because tin was "a very important article of merchandise" demanded by Gujarati ships (p. 5).
- The *bunga mas* tribute to Siam weighed seven *ringgit* of gold for the golden flower and its two spear-shafts, and twelve *ringgit* of silver for the silver equivalent; in the time of Sultan Mohamed Jemiwa, ten *mas* were added to its weight (p. 38, n. 13).
- The 1667 code prescribes that a stranger entering the country pays 6 *kupang* in gold for a permit; export of buffaloes is taxed at 6 *kupang* per head, elephants at 1 *paha* of gold, and slaves at 1 *emas* each (p. 4).
- The manuscript's maritime section was copied in 1222 A.H. (1707 A.D.) under Sultan Jamal al-'Alam Badr al-munir of Aceh (r. 1703–1726), and an identical Achinese copy of the same year is held by the Batavian Society (p. 2).
- Royal installation regalia included six swords (one gold-hilted, one *suasa*-hilted, four carried by *mata-mata*), sixteen *tombak chabang* (branched spears) with yellow covers, sixteen umbrellas in yellow, red, green, and white, and twenty *tunggul* (standards) (p. 42).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that the manuscript, though misattributed to Kelantan, is a genuine Kedah legal compilation of considerable antiquity and value, incorporating the Malacca Code and Maritime Code alongside locally specific port regulations and ceremonial protocols. Its existence confirms the continuity of Malay legal tradition from the Malacca period into the eighteenth century and provides rare documentary evidence of the economic relationships—particularly the tin trade and the Siamese tribute system—that structured Kedah's pre-colonial polity.

## Context

- The primary source is a single Malay manuscript obtained through W.E. Pepys of the Malayan Civil Service from the Dato' Luar of Kelantan; Winstedt deposited it at the School for Oriental Studies, London.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malay legal literature by establishing the Kedah provenance of a text previously assumed to be Kelantan, and by cross-referencing it with van Ronkel's edition of the *Undang-Undang Melaka* (1919), Dulaurier's translation of the Maritime Code (1845), and de Hollander's *Handleiding* (1893).
