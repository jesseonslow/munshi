# The camphor tree and the camphor language of Johore

H. Lake and H. J. Kelsall, accompanied by a contribution from H. N. R., published this short note in 1894 documenting the presence of the Bornean camphor tree (*Dryobalanops aromatica*) in the Indau district of Johore and recording the specialized "camphor language" (Pantang Kapur) used by the Jakun people during their seasonal hunts for the resin. The article serves both as a botanical record—filling a gap in the literature by confirming the tree's occurrence on the Malay Peninsula—and as an ethnographic account of Jakun camphor-gathering practices, beliefs, and a partially obsolete dialect.

## Summary

The article addresses two interrelated problems: the absence of any prior botanical record of *Dryobalanops aromatica* from the Malay Peninsula, and the near-extinction of the Jakun camphor vocabulary in the Indau and Sembrong districts of Johore. Lake and Kelsall, during a recent expedition across Johore, collected specimens of the tree and transcribed as much of the camphor language as they could from the Jakun hunters themselves. The botanical description is brief but precise, noting the tree's height (100–150 feet), stem diameter (about 3½ feet at the base), slow growth rate, and its distribution limited to North Borneo, Labuan, North-West Sumatra, and the Madek and Kahang rivers in Johore.

The historical section draws on a long passage from Garcia de la Huerta's *Historia Aromatum* (1593), which provides a detailed sixteenth-century account of Borneo camphor's trade, grading system (head, breast, legs, feet), pricing, and adulteration. The authors use this to argue that Bornean camphor was known and highly valued long before Chinese camphor (*Cinnamomum camphora*) entered European commerce, and that the Sumatran locality of Barus was the principal source referenced by medieval Arabian writers under the names Fansur, Kansur, or Kaisur. The ethnographic portion, contributed by H. N. R., describes the Jakun belief in a "bisan" spirit (identified by the authors as a cicada) that must be propitiated before hunting, the dietary taboobs observed during the hunt, and the practical method of testing trees by cutting the bark and smelling for a faint camphor odour before felling.

The linguistic contribution notes that the camphor language is composed largely of Malay or Malay-origin words but retains a significant number of non-Malay terms presumed to be remnants of the original Jakun dialects, which are otherwise nearly obsolete in the region.

### Key Findings

- *Dryobalanops aromatica* is confirmed as a native of the Madek and Kahang rivers in Johore, with no prior record of its occurrence anywhere else on the Malay Peninsula (p. 35).
- The tree reaches 100–150 feet in height with a stem about 3½ feet in diameter at the base; it is a very slow-growing Dipterocarp (p. 35).
- Borneo camphor was known in the sixth century CE, referenced in the poems of the Arabian prince Imru-l-kais, and was always more expensive than Chinese camphor (p. 36).
- Garcia de la Huerta (1593) records that "head" grade Borneo camphor sold for 80 pardans per pound (a pardan being an Indian gold coin worth 10 Castilian Rials), compared to 20 for "breast," 12 for "legs," and 4–5 for "feet" (p. 36).
- In 1894, camphor was sold to Chinese traders at Kwala Indau at prices ranging from $15 to $40 per katti, depending on quality (pp. 38, 40).
- Only a very small percentage of camphor trees actually contain camphor; hunters test by making a deep cut in the bark and checking for a faint odour before felling the tree (p. 39).
- Jakun camphor parties remain in the jungle for three to four months at a time, and all members of the community—men and women at home as well as those in the field—must observe the Pantang Kapur dietary restrictions (p. 40).

### Conclusion

The authors' definitive takeaway is twofold: the Bornean camphor tree, long known from Borneo and Sumatra, is confirmed as a native of Johore and thus extends the known range of *Dryobalanops aromatica* to the Malay Peninsula for the first time in the botanical literature; and the camphor language, though largely composed of Malay elements, preserves a stratum of archaic Jakun vocabulary that is otherwise nearly lost, making its documentation urgent.

## Context

- The article is a product of the colonial-era Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society and reflects the administrative and scientific interests of the Johore government in cataloguing the natural products and indigenous knowledge of its territory.
- The primary sources are the authors' own field observations during a 1893–94 expedition across Johore and the Jakun informants' oral testimony; the historical section relies on Garcia de la Huerta's *Historia Aromatum* (1593) and references to Marsden's *Sumatra*.
