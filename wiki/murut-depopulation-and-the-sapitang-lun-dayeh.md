# Murut depopulation and the Sapitang Lun Dayeh

Jay B. Crain's 1972 article, published in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, addresses the long-standing "Murut Problem" in Sabah—the apparent demographic collapse of the Murut population between the 1921 and 1951 censuses—and argues that the phenomenon was substantially a statistical artifact produced by the conflation of ethnically distinct groups under a single census category. Drawing on his 1968–69 fieldwork among the Lun Dayeh of Sipitang District, Crain demonstrates that the Lun Dayeh, a people with linguistic and cultural affinities to central and southern Borneo rather than to the northern Murut, were enumerated as "Murut" in official tallies, thereby distorting population trends and obscuring the true dynamics of both groups.

## Summary

The "Murut Problem" was first flagged by Evans (1922) and Rutter (1929) and subsequently investigated by a succession of medical specialists—Ride, Copeland, Shircore, Clarke, Regester, and Polunin—each proposing different causal mechanisms: malaria, gonorrhoea, malnutrition, inbreeding, acute infectious disease, and the psychological effects of culture contact. Crain's central critique is methodological: none of these investigators treated the "Murut" as a homogeneous breeding population, and none made systematic use of the well-known cultural, linguistic, and geographic differentiation among the groups lumped under that label. Clarke (1951) is cited as typical of the era's dismissiveness toward such distinctions, noting that divisions among Murut groups were "convenient but of no great significance" (p. 111).

Crain's argument pivots on the anomalous position of Sipitang District. Jones (1966), the Superintendent of Census for the 1951 and 1960 enumerations, had already observed that Sipitang Murut fertility was the highest of any district—nearly matching that of the Bruneis, whose fertility was recorded as the highest in Asia—and that the Sipitang population had recovered far more sharply than the Murut population as a whole. Jones attributed this to lower malarial exposure in coastal areas and to the infusion of "new blood" from other tribes. Crain accepts the directional finding but rejects the explanation: the Sipitang "Murut" were in fact predominantly Lun Dayeh, a group that Harrisson (1950) had noted was multiplying in Kalimantan even as Sarawak populations of the same stock were declining. The Lun Dayeh's numerical superiority over the Tagal (the true northern Murut of Sipitang) at a ratio of at least two to one, combined with ongoing in-migration from Kalimantan, accounts for the district's unique demographic trajectory (pp. 113–114).

Crain supplements the census data with a four-village census of Lun Dayeh settlements (Kampongs Samin, Kawang, Ranau, and Mendulong) conducted in October–November 1968, yielding a sample of 811 individuals. The resulting age structure, fertility tables, and child-survival data confirm a highly fertile, growing population whose demographic profile is markedly different from that of the total Murut aggregate. The article thus reframes the Murut depopulation narrative not as a story of biological or cultural decline but as a cautionary case in the perils of applying census categories to ethnically heterogeneous populations.

### Key Findings

- Total Murut population of Sabah: 30,355 (1921) → 24,444 (1931) → 18,724 (1951) → 22,138 (1960), representing a cumulative decline of 38.3% from the 1921 peak before a partial recovery (p. 108).
- Sipitang Murut population: 4,864 (1931) → 5,013 (1951) → 7,246 (1960), with a percentage change of −21.57% (1931–1951) and +53.69% (1951–1960), compared to the total Murut change of −23.40% and +18.23% respectively (p. 112, Table 3).
- Sipitang Murut constituted only 9.97% of the total Sabah Murut population in 1960 yet accounted for 22.58% of the total Murut numerical increase between 1951 and 1960 (p. 114, Table 7).
- District Office and village headman estimates for 1968–69 indicate the Lun Dayeh outnumber the Tagal in Sipitang District by at least two to one (p. 113).
- The 1968 four-village Lun Dayeh census (n = 811) recorded an average of 4.4 live children per mother across all ages, compared to 3.6 for the total Murut in 1960 and 3.8 for Sipitang Murut specifically (pp. 116–117, Tables 9–12).
- Sipitang Murut fertility, measured by child/woman ratio based on district of birth, was 130% higher than that of Pensiangan, the district with the most homogeneous northern Murut population (p. 112).

### Conclusion

Crain's definitive takeaway is that the "Murut depopulation" narrative, as constructed by medical specialists and census analysts over four decades, was fundamentally compromised by the failure to distinguish between ethnically distinct populations enumerated under a single administrative label. The apparent recovery of the Murut after 1951 was not a genuine reversal of demographic decline among the northern Murut but the statistical signature of a different, more fertile population—the Lun Dayeh—being counted within the same category. The article stands as an early and pointed demonstration that census categories, when applied to linguistically and culturally heterogeneous groups, can produce misleading demographic signals that in turn generate spurious medical and social explanations.

## Context

- Crain's primary empirical base is his own 1968–69 fieldwork in Sipitang District (funded by Cornell University and the U.S. Public Health Service), supplemented by the unpublished 1960 census district reports held in the Sipitang District Office files and Jones's (1962a) published census report (pp. 114–115, fn. 8).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Bornean demographic anthropology by foregrounding the problem of ethnic boundary confusion in colonial census practice—a concern that would later become central to the critique of "tribal" categories in Southeast Asian population studies.
