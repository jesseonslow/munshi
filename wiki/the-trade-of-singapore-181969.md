# The trade of Singapore, 1819–69

R. S. Shelford's 1900 account documents a zoological collecting expedition to Mt. Penrissen (4,800 ft) in southern Sarawak, undertaken jointly with E. A. W. Cox of the Sarawak Government service between 5 May and 4 June 1900. The expedition was motivated by the fact that although the mountain had been previously visited by Beccari, Everett, Haviland, and Henlerson, it had never been systematically collected from a zoological standpoint. The article combines a detailed travel narrative with ethnographic observations of the Land Dyak communities along the route and a preliminary assessment of the mountain's fauna and flora.

## Summary

Shelford's account traces the expedition's progress up the Sarawak River from Kuching to the village of Sennah, the last settlement before the mountain. The journey itself is described in considerable detail: the difficulties of poling heavily laden boats through shallows and rapids, the exchange of Malay crews for Land-Dyak polers who proved superior in the art, and the limestone scenery of the upper river. At Sennah, Shelford provides a vivid ethnographic sketch of Land Dyak village life — the stilted houses on precipitous river banks, the women's brass arm and leg rings (noting the progressive distortion of the calf in girls who wore them from a young age), the beadwork "Bok tumbis" hat worn during harvest dances, and the general absence of local manufacture of cloth, weapons, or pottery, all of which were purchased from Malay and Chinese traders.

The scientific results were mixed. Mammals and birds proved extremely scarce — only two kinds of monkey were seen, and no game birds were procured — a deficiency Shelford attributes to the heavy gun-carrying by the local Dyak population (estimating 60 per cent armed) and to the possibility that southern Sarawak is less faunistically rich than the north. The invertebrate fauna, by contrast, was "extremely rich," and Shelford expressed confidence that entomologically the mountain had been worked more thoroughly than any other in Borneo. Three fish species were captured in a mountain stream using *Tuba* poison. The botanical collections, though limited by time, proved sufficiently interesting to prompt a planned return expedition in October 1900 dedicated to botany.

The appendices, contributed by specialists, add taxonomic value. Bishop Hose's fern list records several species new to Borneo, including *Polypodium hirtellum* and *Polypodium obliquatum*, and a potentially new species in the sub-genus *Goniophlebium*. Mosses and hepatics collected by A. H. Everett were named by F. Brotherus, yielding at least one new species (*Thuidium everetti*).

### Key Findings

- The summit of Mt. Penrissen (4,800 ft) was found to be a flat plateau approximately half a mile long by 200 yards broad, covered in pandanus and rotan with hundreds of large trees in various stages of decay (p. 6).
- Only two species of monkey were observed during the entire expedition; pigs, deer, *kijang*, and *pelandok* were "noticeable only by their absence," and no game birds were procured or seen (p. 8).
- Shelford estimated that 60 per cent of the Dyaks in the district were armed with guns, which he identified as the primary cause of mammal and bird scarcity (p. 8).
- Bishop Hose's fern list records *Polypodium hirtellum* Bl. and *Polypodium obliquatum* Bl. as new to Borneo, previously known only from Java, Perak, Ceylon, the Philippines, and China (p. 25).
- F. Brotherus described *Thuidium everetti* as a new moss species from the collections made by A. H. Everett on the mountain (p. 26).
- A trogon, *Harpectes dulitensis*, and a giant earthworm nearly eighteen inches long were sent down from the summit by Cox on 14 May (p. 6).

### Conclusion

Shelford's definitive assessment is that while the expedition was "a great success" overall, the mountain's vertebrate fauna was disappointingly depauperate — a condition he linked to intensive local hunting pressure rather than to any inherent biological poverty of the site. The real scientific yield lay in the invertebrates and the flora, the latter being sufficient to justify a dedicated botanical return.

## Context

- Shelford was a naturalist operating under the auspices of the Sarawak Government; his "formidable looking surat" from the Resident facilitated the arrangement of carriers and local cooperation (p. 3).
- The article draws on the comparative work of John Whitehead and Charles Hose on Mts. Kina Balu and Dulit as the benchmark for Bornean mountain faunistics, situating Penrissen within an existing but incomplete regional framework (p. 8).
