# Recent archaeological discoveries in Malaysia 1962–1963: Borneo,

Barbara Harrison and B. A. V. Peacock published this 1964 field report in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, documenting archaeological work carried out in 1962–1963 across Sarawak and the Malay Peninsula. The article's central argument is that west Borneo—particularly the Niah Caves and the Sarawak River Delta—was a far more significant centre of prehistoric human activity and long-distance maritime trade than the scholarly consensus of the time allowed, with direct implications for the timing of *Homo sapiens* in Southeast Asia.

## Summary

The Borneo section, written by Harrison as Curator of the Sarawak Museum, is dominated by the long-term Niah Caves programme. The 1962 season focused on the Painted Caves, where nearly 200,000 pieces of prehistoric material had been properly excavated by year's end. Harrison's key interpretive achievement was linking the red haematite wall paintings to the death-ship coffins on the cave floor: by brushing the under-soil, she established post-holes beneath the murals into which upright posts fitted, and onto which the death ships settled, surrounded by bone, teeth, and ornaments. This demonstrated a ritual secondary-burial complex. Equally significant was the discovery of a polished "dancing floor" at the cave mouth, overlaid with thousands of prehistoric earthenware sherds and early Chinese Yueh wares—some of which, following a 1962 symposium at Hawaii, were re-dated to a period earlier than the Tang Dynasty, pushing back the evidence for high-quality ceramic trade in the region.

The deeper Niah work produced what Harrison calls the most striking result to date: a skull from the "Hell" section, reported by Dr. Don Brothwell of the British Museum, which is typical *Homo sapiens* and the earliest "modern man" yet proved in Southeast Asia. This challenged the prevailing assumption that anatomically modern humans originated further west and spread eastward only in later periods. Harrison also presents a preliminary eight-phase phaseology for Niah, spanning from a Late Middle Palaeolithic horizon estimated at 40,000–50,000 B.C. to a Chalcolithic phase around 250 B.C.

Beyond Niah, the article covers the Sarawak River Delta trading stations (nearly forty sites located, six excavated), the Kelabit upland megalithic survey of 1962, and the 1963 laboratory work on Chinese ceramics and glass beads. The Malaya section, by Peacock, reports on the discovery of a Hoabinhian-type stone-working floor at Gua Chawan in Ulu Kelantan, a probable pottery kiln at Gua Jaya, and a trial trench at Batu Ner yielding a pecked quadrangular adze and decorated pottery comparable to Sieveking's Gua Cha assemblages.

### Key Findings

- Nearly 200,000 pieces of prehistoric material had been properly excavated from the Painted Cave system by the end of 1962; the main fieldwork was nearly complete, with a full monograph planned for publication as a special volume of *Artibus Asiae* (p. 193).
- The Niah deep skull, reported by Dr. Don Brothwell (*Sarawak Museum Journal*, IX, 15–16, pp. 322–350), is typical *Homo sapiens* and the earliest "modern man" yet proved in Southeast Asia, suggesting sapient man may have been in far Asia far longer than previously thought (pp. 194–195).
- The preliminary Niah phaseology spans eight phases from a Late Middle Palaeolithic "Mid-Sohan" flake horizon at 40,000–50,000 B.C. to a Chalcolithic phase at c. 250 B.C., with C-14 dating supporting phases 2, 3, 4, and 8 (p. 195).
- Chemical analysis of Kelabit upland glass beads demonstrated more than 5% lead oxide (PbO), in sharp contrast to leadless monochrome beads from the Sarawak River Delta, Kuala Selinsing, and Kakao Island, indicating a different source and date for the Kelabit material (p. 200).
- At Gua Chawan, Ulu Kelantan, 34 chipped stone tools were collected from a surface working floor, the majority bi-facially flaked core tools of Hoabinhian type, suggesting the possible survival of a stone industry formally indistinguishable from the Hoabinhian into recent historical times (pp. 201–203).
- The Gua Jaya cave in Ulu Kelantan yielded over 1,500 pottery fragments from a homogeneous ash-and-sherd deposit interpreted as a pottery kiln, including a hollow pottery cylinder possibly functioning as a bellows nozzle and a decorated flat clay tablet of unknown purpose (pp. 204–205).

### Conclusion

Harrison's definitive takeaway is that the cultural and economic importance of west Borneo, and south-west Sarawak in particular, "may well prove to have been much greater than is generally supposed, at least during those periods prior to the advent of the first western influences," and that the scale of the prehistoric sites makes it "difficult to regard this area as other than a major trading centre for the whole of South-east Asia" (p. 198).

## Context

- The article draws on the Sarawak Museum's excavation archives and reference collections (including a ceramic reference collection of over 2,000 pieces acquired over sixty years), as well as comparative material from the British Museum and international exchanges (pp. 193, 200).
- Historiographically, the piece is significant for challenging the west-to-east diffusion model for *Homo sapiens* in Southeast Asia and for arguing against Sieveking's position that no contact between the Hoabinhian and the Malayan Neolithic could be accepted, proposing instead the contemporaneous coexistence of distinct technological traditions in the same ecology (pp. 194–195, 203).
