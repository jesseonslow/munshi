# The progress of Malaysian law. Annual lecture delivered on 6 December, 1994

J.P. Mead's 1914 romanized transcription of the *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai*, published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (No. 66), presents the earliest known Malay chronicle of the Pasai sultanate in north-east Sumatra, copied from the Raffles manuscript (Royal Asiatic Society, London, MS No. 67). The text narrates the legendary founding of Pasai, its conversion to Islam, and its eventual conquest by Majapahit, serving as a foundational source for the historiography of the Malay world.

## Summary

The *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai* is a genealogical chronicle that traces the origin of the Pasai royal house from two brother-kings, Raja Ahmad and Raja Mohammad, who established settlements in the forested region of Semerlanga. Each brother discovers a miraculous child—Raja Mohammad finds Putri Betong inside a hollow tree, while Raja Ahmad encounters a child carried on the head of an elephant named Bujang Sakalis. The children marry, produce two sons (Merah Silu and Merah Hasum), and are killed in a fratricidal war between their fathers. The surviving sons flee to Beruana, where Merah Silu discovers gold through a fish trap and tames wild buffalo, eventually establishing himself at Buloh Telang under the patronage of Megat Iskandar.

The narrative's central pivot is the Islamization of Merah Silu, who becomes Sultan Malik al-Salleh after a dream in which the Prophet Muhammad instructs him to recite the shahada. A ship from Mecca, commanded by Shaikh Ismail, arrives bearing royal regalia and a "fakir" (a descendant of Abu Bakar) who confirms the conversion and establishes Islamic practice in Semudera Daru'l-Islam. The chronicle then follows the dynastic succession through Sultan Malik al-Tahir (who founds Pasai proper, named after his hunting dog Si-Pasai), his sons Malik al-Mahmud and Malik al-Mansur, and Sultan Ahmad Permadala Permala, whose reign is marked by the extraordinary exploits of his son Tun Beraim Bapa and ultimately by patricide and the arrival of Putri Gemerincang from Majapahit.

The text concludes with the Majapahit conquest of Pasai under Sina Pati Anglaga, followed by Gajah Mada's subsequent campaigns of subjugation across the Strait of Malacca, Borneo, and the eastern islands. A final episode narrates the legendary buffalo-fight at Minangkabau, in which Javanese envoys are poisoned during a feast, providing an aetiology for the name "Minangkabau" (winning the fight). The chronicle closes with a colophon dated to 21 Muharram and two appended lists of place names.

### Key Findings

- The manuscript is the Raffles collection MS, now Royal Asiatic Society (London) No. 67; Mead notes it "bears traces of an ignorant scribe" with a few doubtful transcriptions (p. 1).
- The text contains no mention of Malacca, distinguishing it from the *Sejarah Melayu*, which references Pasai numerous times (p. 1).
- The colophon dates completion to "hari isnin kapada hari dua-puloh satu kapada bulan muharram sanat tujoh hijratul-nabi" (Monday, 21 Muharram, year seven of the Hijra), a date that is almost certainly a scribal corruption (p. 54).
- The chronicle provides aetiological legends for at least a dozen place names, including Pasai (from the dog Si-Pasai), Semudera (from a giant ant), Padang Maya (from the burial of Said Semayam al-Din), Padang Pauh Bengkok (from Javanese spears bending a tree), and Minangkabau (from the buffalo fight) (pp. 9, 24, 48–53).
- The Majapahit conquest narrative lists 27 states that submitted to Majapahit after the fall of Pasai, including Timbalan, Siatan, Jemaja, Riau, Bantan, Sambas, Jambi, and Palembang (pp. 54–55).
- The text preserves the proverb "badak makan anaknya" (the rhinoceros eats its own young), used by a fisherman to encode the news that Sultan Ahmad had killed his own sons (p. 45).

### Conclusion

Mead's edition provides the first accessible romanized text of the *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai*, a document of paramount importance for understanding the self-conception of the Pasai sultanate, the process of Islamic conversion in the Malay world, and the geopolitical relationship between Pasai and Majapahit. The text functions simultaneously as royal genealogy, conversion narrative, and regional political history, and its aetiological legends remain the primary source for the toponymy of north-east Sumatra.

## Context

- J.P. Mead was a British colonial administrator in the Straits Settlements; his edition was produced in the standard colonial-era scholarly idiom of the Straits Branch RAS, with minimal critical apparatus beyond a brief preface and occasional footnotes referencing Shellabear's romanized *Sejarah Melayu* (p. 9).
- The Raffles manuscript (RAS 67) is the sole witness to this text and represents the earliest stratum of Pasai historiography, predating the *Sejarah Melayu* in its treatment of the region's pre-Islamic and early Islamic past.
