# Harbours

The harbours of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo constitute a complex and often misunderstood body of maritime geography, shaped by centuries of navigational practice, colonial administration, and the shifting demands of trade. The most extensively documented case in the Society's literature concerns the waterway now known as Keppel Harbour at Singapore, whose identity as the original "Old Strait" of Singapore was long obscured by competing traditions and administrative error [[1, pp. 25–26]](#ref-1). Beyond this central case, the record extends to the artificial port of Port Weld in Perak, whose rise and fall over roughly seventy years illustrates how harbours created to serve specific extractive economies could be rendered obsolete by the very infrastructure that sustained them [[2, pp. 154–155]](#ref-2). Together, these studies reveal a pattern in which harbours were not static features but dynamic nodes whose significance rose and fell with navigational knowledge, political disruption, and the evolution of transport networks.

### The Old Strait of Singapore: Identification and Early Use

The waterway now called Keppel Harbour was the primary European passage between the Malacca Strait and the South China Sea from at least the late sixteenth century until its effective abandonment in the last quarter of the eighteenth [[3, pp. 163–164]](#ref-3). The earliest detailed sailing directions are those of Linschoten's *Reys-Gheschrift* (1595), which describe a course from Malacca running through the Selat Sembilan and then due east through the passage, recording a minimum depth of four and a half fathoms at the entrance and identifying the Batu Berlayar — a rock "like a pillar" on the northern side — as the principal landmark [[3, pp. 167–169]](#ref-3). Barnes, writing in 1911, confirmed these identifications by cross-referencing Linschoten's account with Chinese sailing directions from the *Wu-pei-pi-shu*, which describe a "Dragon-teeth Gate" passage matching the harbour's western entrance [[1, pp. 25–26]](#ref-1).

The first cartographic document to distinguish two parallel passages south of Singapore Island is the map of Emanuel Godinho de Eredia, drawn in 1604 and published in his *Declaram de Malaca*. This map explicitly labels the Keppel Harbour passage as "estreito velho" (old straits) and a channel south of Pulau Blakang Mati as "estreito nouo" (new straits), establishing that the "new strait" was not the present Main Strait but a narrower channel [[3, pp. 171–173]](#ref-3). Barnes noted that the map also records the presence of a *shahbandar* (harbour master) at a location on Singapore Island near the mouth of the Singapore River, implying regular visits by foreign ships and traders [[1, p. 28]](#ref-1). The *Sejarah Melayu* corroborates this, recording the stout defence offered by the "penglima raayat Raja Nagara batin Singapura" against Portuguese attacks from Malacca [[1, p. 28]](#ref-1).

The strait remained in intermittent use through the seventeenth century. Peter Mundy's journal records the passage of John Weddell's fleet of four vessels through the "old straightt" on 1 June 1637, noting depths of three and a half fathoms and a near-accident caused by the inexperience of their Portuguese-Eurasian pilot [[3, pp. 179–181]](#ref-3). Dampier's ship anchored at Pulau Blakang Mati in 1688, and Wilde's manuscript map of circa 1680 plots tracks through both the old and new straits with soundings [[3, pp. 179–185]](#ref-3).

### Abandonment, Rediscovery, and the Keppel Question

The effective disappearance of the old strait from European navigational knowledge is attributed by Gibson-Hill to a political disruption around 1584, when the Sultan of Johore, in retaliation for Portuguese plundering, ordered junks sunk to block the passage [[3, pp. 177–179]](#ref-3). Do Couto's *Décadas* records that Portuguese pilots subsequently found a new channel to the south, which they named the "canal da Santa Barbara" — a passage no longer than a camelete shot in length, at its widest one hundred fathoms, and at its narrowest only fourteen fathoms [[3, pp. 177–179]](#ref-3). Gibson-Hill identifies this as the channel between Pulau Tekukor and the southern point of Pulau Brani. The combination of this new passage and the gradual development of the broader Main Strait made the old route progressively less attractive, though it was not entirely forgotten: Horsburgh's authoritative directory of 1806 recognised only two straits (the Johore Strait and the Main Strait), and his chart of the same year rendered the Keppel Harbour area as an impenetrable mass of rock and reef [[3, pp. 191–193]](#ref-3).

The rediscovery is credited to Major Farquhar, who found the passage in August 1819 and announced it to Raffles as a "new harbour" on 2 September, describing anchorage from fifteen to four fathoms and entrances narrow enough to be defended by batteries [[3, pp. 194–195]](#ref-3). Captain Daniel Ross surveyed it officially in 1827, and a series of proposals for its development as a coaling depot and dry dock ran from 1829 to 1846, the last of which — Clunis's dry dock scheme, estimated at $80,000 — failed for want of capital [[3, pp. 193–207]](#ref-3).

The question of Captain Henry Keppel's relationship with the harbour has generated the most sustained historiographical debate. Barnes (1911) argued that the tradition attributing the harbour's discovery to Keppel was "without foundation of fact," noting that HMS *Rainbow* under Captain Rous had made the first recorded intentional passage in 1826, and that the Peninsular and Oriental Company had opened offices in New Harbour by 1852, predating Keppel's entry in HMS *Raleigh* in 1857 [[1, pp. 31–34]](#ref-1). Cowan (1965), working from the ship's log of HMS *Maeander* (Adm. 53/3601–3603, Public Record Office, London), demonstrated that the vessel did not enter New Harbour until 19 June 1848, after remaining in Singapore Roads from 22 May, and that she stayed there until 18 August — making her the first vessel to use the anchorage on a protracted basis [[4, pp. 233–234]](#ref-4). The log records no incident of *Maeander* striking a shoal in New Harbour on any of her four visits to Singapore, undermining the traditional account that Keppel grounded the ship there [[4, p. 240]](#ref-4).

The official renaming of New Harbour to Keppel Harbour was gazetted on 19 April 1900 (Notification No. 401, Straits Settlements Government Gazette Extraordinary), citing Keppel's "first demonstration" of the harbour's capabilities as the justification [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1). Gibson-Hill demonstrated that this notification was based on a false premise: it cited HMS *Dido*, in which Keppel had been present in 1842–44, rather than HMS *Maeander*, in which he was actually in command in May 1848 [[3, pp. 209–210]](#ref-3). The only feature Keppel "demonstrated" was the presence of the shoal first recorded by Linschoten in 1595 [[3, p. 210]](#ref-3).

### Artificial Ports and Economic Decline: The Case of Port Weld

While the Keppel Harbour studies concern a natural harbour whose significance was determined by navigational geography, the record also includes the case of Port Weld in Perak, an artificial port whose trajectory illustrates the vulnerability of harbours created to serve specific extractive economies [[2, pp. 154–155]](#ref-2). Port Weld was established in 1885 at the mouth of the Dindings River to provide a sea outlet for tin from Larut, Kamunting, and the surrounding district, which at the time constituted Malaya's leading mining area [[2, pp. 157–159]](#ref-2). Its creation was inseparable from the construction of Malaya's first railway, an eight-and-a-quarter-mile line from Taiping to Sapetang built by Ceylonese engineers and labourers and opened on 1 June 1885 [[2, pp. 157–159]](#ref-2). For a brief period the port ranked among Malaya's leading ports, second only to Malacca and Georgetown, with ten trains daily and regular steamship connections to Penang [[2, p. 160]](#ref-2).

The port's decline was driven by two converging forces: the geographic shift of tin mining from Larut to the Kinta valley, and the progressive completion of the Malayan trunk railway system that bypassed it. Larut's tin output fell from 135,889 standard *pikul* in 1884 to 37,373 by 1902, while Kinta's rose from 26,279 *pikul* in 1889 to 134,166 in 1898 [[2, p. 161]](#ref-2). The 1895 completion of the Ipoh–Tapah Road line diverted Kinta's trade to Telok Anson, and the 1902 opening of the Taiping–Prai section of Swettenham's trunk railway allowed Larut's ore to reach Penang directly by rail, eliminating the need for trans-shipment at Port Weld entirely [[2, pp. 163–164]](#ref-2). The trade shock was immediate: Port Weld imports dropped by $876,000 in 1896 while Telok Anson imports rose by $1,120,000 in the same year [[2, p. 163]](#ref-2).

By the 1950s the port was functionally defunct. The mangrove zone, now seven miles wide and advancing seaward at a furlong every twelve to fourteen years, had reduced the main channel to less than a foot of water at low tide [[2, pp. 154–155]](#ref-2). The town's economy had shifted entirely to local extraction: charcoal manufacture (40,000 *pikul* per month), firewood production (160,000 *pikul* per month), and small-scale fishing, with three-quarters of the population engaged in these activities [[2, pp. 166–167]](#ref-2). Of 280 vessels registered at Port Weld in 1951, 201 were under five tons burden, reflecting the shallowness of the waterways and the localised nature of remaining maritime activity [[2, p. 166]](#ref-2).

### Administrative Frameworks: The Master Attendants

The governance of Singapore's harbour was formalised through the office of the master attendant, a position that combined the functions of harbour master and pilot. Gibson-Hill's 1960 study of the master attendants at Singapore from 1819 to 1867 documents the institutional development of this role during the period when the settlement was transitioning from a trading post to a major port [[5, pp. 1–63]](#ref-5). The office's existence is implicit in the earlier record: de Eredia's 1604 map marks the residence of the *shahbandar* on Singapore Island, and Valentyn records that on 5 May 1606 two prahus of the king of Johore with the *shahbandar* of Singapore, Seri Raja Nagara, reached the Dutch fleet [[1, p. 28]](#ref-1). The colonial successor to this Malay office was the master attendant, whose duties encompassed the regulation of anchorage, the management of pilots, and the supervision of harbour works — functions that became increasingly complex as the port's traffic grew and as the development of New Harbour required coordinated engineering and navigational oversight [[5, pp. 1–63]](#ref-5).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on harbours reveals a clear evolution in both scope and methodological rigour. Barnes's 1911 article was a pioneering exercise in source-critical local historiography, drawing on Chinese, Portuguese, Dutch, and British primary sources to correct two persistent local misconceptions [[1, pp. 25–34]](#ref-1). His tone was respectfully critical of the Keppel renaming, and his argument, while persuasive, was limited by the sources available to him in Singapore. Gibson-Hill's 1954 study superseded Barnes by providing the first comprehensive synthesis of the strait's navigational history, grounded in a personal re-traversal of Linschoten's course by launch in 1951 and a systematic comparison of over thirty obsolete charts held in the British Museum and the Raffles Museum Archives [[3, pp. 163–214]](#ref-3). His identification of the "canal da Santa Barbara" and his demonstration that the 1900 renaming rested on a false premise represented a significant advance in the field.

Cowan's 1965 article contributed a methodological correction of particular importance: his insistence on the primacy of the contemporaneous ship's log over retrospective memoir [[4, pp. 229–240]](#ref-4). By working from the *Maeander*'s log at the Public Record Office, London, he was able to establish precise dates of entry and departure, to demonstrate that the ship had not grounded in New Harbour, and to show that Keppel's own published account of 1853 had conflated events. This methodological stance — privileging the administrative record over the literary one — has become standard in the Society's maritime historiography.

Wayte's 1959 study of Port Weld represents a different strand of the literature, concerned not with navigational history but with the economic and ecological dynamics of port creation and decline [[2, pp. 154–167]](#ref-2). His 1955 fieldwork and the accompanying maps drawn in 1957 provide a rare contemporaneous physical description of a declining colonial port in the early post-war period, and his use of F.M.S. Annual Reports and government gazettes to trace trade flows over four decades established a model for the quantitative study of port economics that has been followed in subsequent work. Gibson-Hill's 1960 study of the master attendants rounds out the record by addressing the institutional and administrative dimension of harbour management, a topic that had received comparatively little attention in the earlier navigational studies [[5, pp. 1–63]](#ref-5).

The cumulative effect of these studies is to establish that the history of harbours in the region cannot be understood as a simple narrative of discovery and development. The Keppel Harbour case demonstrates how navigational knowledge could be lost and recovered, how political disruption could alter the geography of trade, and how administrative decisions — in this case the 1900 renaming — could entrench historical errors in the public record. The Port Weld case demonstrates how the very infrastructure that created a harbour could render it obsolete, and how ecological processes such as mangrove accretion could complete the work of economic obsolescence. Together, these studies constitute a substantial body of scholarship on the maritime geography of the region, though the record remains concentrated on Singapore and the Perak coast, with comparatively little attention yet paid to harbours in Borneo or the southern Peninsula.
