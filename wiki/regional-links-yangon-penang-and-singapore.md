# Regional links: Yangon, Penang and Singapore

Daw Win and Loh Wei Leng (2009) examine the commercial, shipping, and social networks that bound Chinese entrepreneurs across Yangon (Rangoon), Penang, and Singapore during the British colonial period, arguing that these three port cities formed an integrated regional economy in which Penang functioned as both a commercial gateway and a social bridge—supplying not only goods and capital but also Nonya brides to Burmese Chinese merchants. The article, published in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* as part of a special issue on Peranakan Chinese in Penang and the region, draws on trade statistics, shipping directories, and biographical records to reconstruct the flows of people, commodities, and capital along this northern littoral axis.

## Summary

The authors situate their analysis within the broader history of Southeast Asian maritime trade, noting that Chinese merchants had held a comparative advantage in Burmese commerce since the Pagan era, when they served as agents of the crown controlling lac, cotton, and other agricultural products (p. 67). The British conquest of Lower Burma in 1852 and the subsequent opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 accelerated trade volumes, transforming Rangoon into the "Rice City" and creating a vast free-trade area across the region. The article's central argument is that the Penang–Rangoon–Singapore triangle was not merely a subset of the better-known Straits-to-China shipping lane but a distinct regional circuit with its own logic, driven by the rice trade, kerosene distribution, coolie transport, and the personal networks of individual entrepreneurs (pp. 68–70).

The narrative is organised around two case studies of prominent Burmese Chinese tycoons—Lim Chin Tsong (1867–1923) and Teoh Eng Hock (1867–1942)—whose business empires spanned all three cities. Lim, who married a Penang Peranakan woman and operated his trading house Chop Lim Soo Hean from China Street, Rangoon, diversified into kerosene distribution (as Burmah Oil Company agent from 1891), steamship ownership (the Seang Line), and mining, while maintaining agents in Penang (Kim Keng Leong & Co.) and Singapore (Giang Hoe Company) (pp. 70–72). Teoh, who migrated from Amoy via Penang to Rangoon and became a British subject in 1885, leveraged that status to establish a shipping agency in Amoy during the late Qing period, a privilege unavailable to Chinese citizens (p. 72). The rice trade section documents how multiple prominent Rangoon rice merchants—Lee Ah Phoy, Koh Ban Pan, Eng Foke Sein, Peh Beng Teng, Tan Sew Him, Chan Ma Phee, and Khoo Kin—had first settled in Penang before moving to Burma, and several married Nonya women from Penang, reinforcing the social dimension of these commercial ties (pp. 73–74).

The appendix extends the analysis to Penang- and Singapore-based shipping companies that operated scheduled services to Rangoon, including the Wee Bin Line (est. 1856), Koe Guan Company (16 steamers, 18-day Penang–Rangoon rotations), Beng Brothers, Heap Eng Moh (later acquired by Oei Tiong Ham in 1912), Ho Hong Steamship Company (est. 1904), and the Tan Kim Tian Line (which purchased SS *Rangoon* from the British India Steam Navigation Company in 1871) (pp. 76–78). Together, these firms demonstrate a dense web of inter-firm relations—what the authors, following Hamilton and Chen, term "firm networks"—that sustained regular cargo and passenger traffic across the region.

### Key Findings

- Penang's trade with Moulmein and the Coast peaked at 957,048 rupees in 1847/48, while trade with "British Burma" (post-1852) saw Penang's imports from Burma reach 2,260,368 Spanish dollars in 1879, indicating substantial growth after the Second Anglo-Burmese War (pp. 69).
- Lim Chin Tsong's Seang Line steamers each carried approximately 2,000 coolies from China for employment in Burma's agricultural and industrial sectors; the SS *Seang Leong* was lost in a typhoon in 1902 and the SS *Glenogle (1)* sank in 1919 (p. 71).
- Lee Ah Phoy's Wor Lee Lone Rice Mill on Kanounto Creek produced 1,700 bags of rice per day, illustrating the scale of individual rice-milling operations in colonial Rangoon (p. 73).
- Koe Guan Company of Penang operated sixteen steamers on a scheduled 18-day rotation between Penang and Rangoon via Ranong, Victoria Point, Mergui, and Tavoy, alongside weekly Penang–Singapore and 28-day Penang–Amoy services (p. 77).
- In 1826, John Crawfurd insisted in a trade treaty with the Burmese court that British vessels under 50 tons receive the same tax exemptions as Chinese junks, reflecting the dominance of Chinese junk trade in the region prior to European steamship competition (p. 70).
- The SS *Shropshire (1)* and SS *Cheshire (1)*, owned by Lim Chin Tsong, were requisitioned by the Royal Australian Navy during World War I and renamed SS *Seang Bee* and SS *Seang Choon* respectively (p. 71).

### Conclusion

The authors conclude that while contemporaneous commentary such as the *Hong Kong Free Press* credited the Straits Peranakan as the pre-eminent Chinese shipowners in the region, the Burmese Chinese entrepreneurs' ability to develop and exploit the economic links between Yangon, Penang, and Singapore deserves equal recognition. The article positions these merchants as active agents in a regional system that was neither simply a branch of the Straits economy nor a passive recipient of Chinese capital, but a distinct commercial circuit sustained by personal networks, marital alliances, and the specific commodity flows of rice, kerosene, and coolie labour (p. 76).

## Context

- Primary sources include the *Straits Settlements Blue Books* (1877–1880), the *Tabular Statement of the Commerce and Shipping of Prince of Wales Island, Singapore and Malacca* (1843–1853), the *Singapore and Straits Directory* (1921), Pearn's *A History of Rangoon* (1939), and Crawfurd's *Journal of an Embassy* (1829), supplemented by *Straits Times* advertisements and shipping columns.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Burmese Chinese economic history by shifting focus from the well-documented Straits-to-China axis to the Penang–Rangoon–Singapore triangle, and by foregrounding the role of Penang as a social and commercial intermediary rather than merely a terminus.
