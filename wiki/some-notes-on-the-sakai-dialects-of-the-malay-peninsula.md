# Some notes on the Sakai dialects of the Malay Peninsula

Hugh Clifford's 1891 article in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* presents the earliest systematic comparative study of Sakai dialects in the Malay Peninsula, arguing that the Sen-oi and Tem-be' languages are varieties of a single common tongue, that the Sakai are the more ancient inhabitants of the Peninsula, and that Malay did not derive its elementary roots from Sakai.

## Summary

Clifford's central problem is the relationship between the scattered Sakai dialects of the Peninsula and the question of linguistic priority between the aboriginal and Malay languages. He distinguishes two distinct aboriginal races—the Sakai (light-skinned, wavy-haired, house-dwelling, semi-agricultural) and the Semang or Pangan (true Negritos, short, dark, purely hunter-gatherers)—and focuses his analysis on the Sakai, specifically the Sen-oi and Tem-be' dialects spoken in the large interior tract of "Sakai country" comprising the headwaters of the Jelai, Telom, and Serau Rivers in Pahang, the ulu of the Batang Padang, Bidor, Kampar, and Plus Rivers in Perak, and the Galas and Nenggiri Rivers in Kelantan. He argues that only in this interior zone, where Malay influence has been minimal, can the pure Sakai language be observed, as the scattered coastal clans speak bastardised forms heavily contaminated by Malay vocabulary.

His method is comparative lexicography. He presents parallel vocabularies demonstrating that Sen-oi and Tem-be' share a core of elementary words (e.g., *cha'* for "to eat," *ois* for "fire," *te* for "earth") while diverging in other common terms (e.g., *eng* vs. *yeh* for "I"), and extends the comparison to the Blanja and Slim dialects to show a common origin across all hill Sakai. He then describes the phonology of Sen-oi in detail—five vowels each with four length/quality variants, nasal finals (*kn*, *pn*, *tn*), and two distinct rhotics—and outlines a simple grammar with no noun inflection or verb conjugation, cases marked by prepositions, and word order governed by fixed rules.

The article's most significant historiographical contribution is its rebuttal of two contemporary theories. First, he rejects Vaughan Stevens's claim that Sakai is connected to Tibetan, demonstrating that the two languages share no root or grammatical form. Second, and more substantially, he refutes W. E. Maxwell's hypothesis in the *Malay Manual* that certain Malay roots (notably *tang* "hand") derive from Sakai. Clifford's evidence is threefold: the Sakai word for "hand" is *terkn* (Sen-oi) or *pih* (Tem-be'), not *tang*; the Malay compound words for indigenous fruits (*duri-an*, *rambut-an*, *pulas-an*) show that the Malay language was already fully formed with its productive suffix *-an* before the Malays reached the Peninsula; and the stone implements found in the interior bear Sakai names (*jek*, *i-ôdz*) while the metal tools introduced by the Malays bear Malay names (*ku'-pak* for *kapak*), proving the Sakai were the original tool-users and the Malays the later arrivals who brought metallurgy.

### Key Findings

- The Sen-oi dialect is spoken by approximately 6,000 people in the lower portion of Sakai country; the pure Sakai interior spans the headwaters of the Jelai, Telom, Serau, Batang Padang, Bidor, Kampar, Plus, Galas, and Nenggiri rivers (pp. 15, 20).
- No pure Sakai can count above three in their own language; a word meaning "many" (*kêrpn* in Sen-oi, *cha'-tukn* in Tem-be') covers all higher numbers. Only one exception was found: a seven-member Semang clan near Sadang on the Perak River (1885) possessed a word for "four" (*sa-bêh*) (p. 18).
- Sen-oi possesses five vowels (*a, e, i, o, u*), each with four variants (medium, long, short, abrupt), except *u* which lacks the abrupt form; it also has diphthongs *ai*, *au*, *oi*, and distinctive nasal finals (*kn*, *pn*, *tn*) (pp. 20–22).
- The Sakai recognise only three colour terms: *re-ngah* (black, covering all dark colours), *bi-ug* (white, covering green, blue, mauve), and *cheng-ul* (red, covering crimson, orange, yellow, brown) (p. 25).
- Stone implements found in the interior—axes, spear-heads, and choppers—are called by Sakai names (*jek*, *ta-rok*/*bê-lush*, *i-ôdz*) while the metal hatchet, which has no stone equivalent, is called by the Malay name *ku'-pak* (from *kapak*), demonstrating that the Sakai preceded the Malays in the Peninsula and that the Malays introduced metallurgy (pp. 27–28).
- Five phonetic rules govern the adoption of Malay words into Sen-oi, including the pronunciation of otherwise-silent final *k* (e.g., *kapak* → *ka'-pak*), which Clifford explains as a systematic euphonic necessity rather than evidence of an archaic Malay pronunciation (pp. 28–29).

### Conclusion

Clifford concludes that all Sakai dialects descend from a single common language, that the Sakai are the more ancient inhabitants of the Peninsula, and that the Malay language owes nothing in its elementary roots to Sakai. He expresses hope of eventually connecting the Sakai tongue to Papuan or Dyak languages, or to the dialects of the Andaman Islanders, whose physical resemblance to the Sakai he notes as striking.

## Context

- Clifford was a young Straits Settlements civil servant conducting fieldwork in the interior of Perak and Pahang; the article represents early colonial-era linguistic documentation of aboriginal peoples, framed by the racial typologies and evolutionary assumptions of the period (e.g., the Sakai are described as "degenerate" when they adopt Malay clothing, p. 15).
- The study draws on Clifford's own field vocabularies collected in the interior Sakai country and among scattered coastal clans, and his work on the Sadang Semang dialect (1885), which he notes will be "the only record that such a dialect was ever spoken by man" (p. 18).
