# The fishing economy of Malaysia 1955–1975

G. Sivalingam's 1986 article examines the transformation of Malaysia's fishing economy between 1955 and 1975, tracing how successive waves of technological change—from lift and gill-drift nets to purse seines and finally trawlers—restructured the organization of production and progressively worsened income distribution among fishing communities.

## Summary

The article addresses a central question in development economics: how does the introduction of more capital-intensive technology affect the social organization and income distribution of a traditional fishing economy? Sivalingam contrasts four technologies along a capital-intensity spectrum and demonstrates how each successive innovation displaced the previous one, not through policy mandate but through comparative productivity advantages. The shift from labor-intensive to capital-intensive gear created a new class of non-operator capital owners—fish dealers, finance companies, and government cooperatives—who displaced the traditional owner-operator *juruselam* (fishing expert) and his cooperative crew structure.

The organizational analysis reveals a clear trajectory from informal, kinship-based production relations with shared decision-making toward formalized, hierarchical wage-labor arrangements. As capitalization increased, the *juruselam* was reduced from owner-operator to employee, crew members lost their participatory role in decision-making, and marketing channels became concentrated in fewer hands. The author draws on Firth's ethnographic fieldwork, Department of Fisheries statistics, and government cooperative reports to document these shifts at both village and national levels.

The income distribution analysis constitutes the article's most significant contribution. Sivalingam demonstrates that while total industry output and per capita income rose substantially over the period, the gains were concentrated among capital owners, and the labor share of proceeds declined progressively with each technological upgrade. Displaced fishermen who could not access the new capital-intensive gear were pushed into subsistence fishing in remote villages or out of the industry entirely.

### Key Findings

- Trawlers were non-existent in 1955 but by 1975 accounted for 3,823 boats on the West Coast and 1,922 on the East Coast, generating over 51% of total value added in the fishing industry (pp. 51–52).
- The Marine Fisheries Regulation of 1967 classified trawlers into three groups by tonnage and horsepower with distance-from-shore limits of 3, 7, and 12 miles; enforcement was weak due to shortage of police vessels (p. 56).
- In 1940, 60% of lift net fishermen owned boats and net components; by 1963 under purse seine dominance, only 10% owned both boats and nets, and 62.29% owned neither (pp. 71–73).
- The 1970 Census reported that more than 62% of all fishery households had no boats (p. 75).
- Total industry income rose from $105.5 million in 1960 to $541.9 million in 1975, while total employment fell from 125,231 to 116,889, a decline of 7.1% (p. 78).
- The labor share of cash proceeds declined from approximately 63% on lift nets to 45% on purse seines and to roughly 30% on large trawlers (38–56 tons) (pp. 71–77).

### Conclusion

Sivalingam's definitive takeaway is that technological modernization in Malaysia's fishing economy produced a paradox: aggregate output and average incomes rose substantially, yet the distribution of gains became more unequal over time. The capital-intensive technologies displaced poor fishermen and crew who depended on less efficient gear, and while some displaced labor was absorbed into the new technologies, a significant portion was forced into subsistence fishing in remote villages or out of the industry altogether. The net effect was a progressive concentration of wealth and a widening income gap between capital owners and laborers.

## Context

- Primary sources include R. Firth's ethnographic fieldwork (*Malay Fishermen, Their Peasant Economy*, 1966), Department of Fisheries *Annual Fisheries Statistics* (1955–1975), the American Peace Corps *Report on Cost and Earnings Survey of Malaysian Trawl Fisheries* (1969–1970), and the 1970 Population Census.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malaysian economic development by applying a political economy framework (drawing on Leonief's Marxian analysis) to the fishing sector, bridging the gap between Firth's earlier ethnographic work and the quantitative policy debates of the 1970s.
