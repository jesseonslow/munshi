# Notes on the remains of some ancient brick structures in Pekan district

H. Goring Dalton's "A Visit to some Islands off the East Coast of Johore and Pahang," published in 1928, is a first-person travelogue documenting an eleven-day expedition in May 1918 to a chain of islands in the China Sea, combining natural-history observation with ethnographic and administrative notes on the Malay communities encountered.

## Summary

Dalton, a government official based at Mersing, undertook the voyage with a motor-launch, a cargo prau, and a crew of ten Malays, visiting in sequence Pulau Sribuat, Pulau Tiuman, Pulau Pemanggil, Pulau Aur, Pulau Tinggi, and Pulau Babi. The narrative is organised around the practicalities of the journey—anchorage, provisioning, engine trouble, and the physical toll of sunburn—rather than a systematic scientific survey, yet it yields valuable incidental observations on coral morphology, shell assemblages, butterfly species, and the marine ecology of the islands.

A significant portion of the account is devoted to the human geography of the islands. Dalton records the sparse Malay settlements, the role of Chinese shopkeepers as middlemen in the copra and shell trade, and the administrative presence of customs officers and penghulus. The most extended ethnographic episode concerns a festival at Pulau Aur, held roughly every eight or nine years to propitiate evil spirits, during which the party was effectively confined to the island and forbidden from collecting specimens or killing animals. Dalton also notes the discovery of four cannon and a Dutch silver coin dated 1792 on Pulau Aur, suggesting a shipwreck of considerable antiquity on a route between Singapore and China.

The article closes with a brief economic survey of island products—copra, dried fish, edible birds' nests, special woods such as kayu kemuning and kayu penaga, and various fruits—establishing the islands' place in the broader trade network of the east coast.

### Key Findings

- The expedition covered approximately 188 miles, with a maximum distance of 37 miles from the mainland, over eleven days from 21 May 1918 (p. 95).
- Pulau Aur had approximately 200 inhabitants at the time of visit; Pulau Tinggi about 100 regular residents (pp. 90, 92).
- The motor-boat consumed 60 gallons of kerosine and 4 gallons of benzine over the entire trip (p. 95).
- Four cannon and a Dutch silver coin dated 1792 were found on Pulau Aur, the latter sold by the Penghulu to Dalton; the wreck was estimated at roughly 100–200 years old (p. 91).
- The ghost-ship festival at Pulau Aur, involving a sago-palm boat laden with gifts set adrift to carry away the island's troubles, occurs approximately every 8 to 9 years (pp. 89–90).
- Edible birds' nests were collected and exported from Pulau Aur, Pulau Tinggi, and Pulau Babi; copra and dried fish formed the main livelihood of island populations (p. 95).

### Conclusion

Dalton's account, though framed as a personal holiday rather than a formal survey, provides a useful snapshot of the social, economic, and natural conditions of the east-coast island chain in the immediate post-war period, and its incidental observations on the ghost-ship festival, the cannon and coin at Pulau Aur, and the island trade in copra and shells remain of historical interest.

## Context

- The author was a British government official stationed at Mersing, Johore, and the expedition was undertaken on leave with a government motor-launch; the administrative framing (customs officers, rest houses, the Sultan's boats) reflects his official vantage point.
- The article draws on no archival collections; its value lies in direct field observation and oral testimony from island inhabitants, customs officers, and the Penghulu of Pulau Aur.
