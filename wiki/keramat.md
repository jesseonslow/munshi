# Keramat

The *keramat* is a sacred site or person venerated in the Malay archipelago, encompassing graves, natural objects, and living individuals believed to possess supernatural power. Derived from the Arabic *karamah*, the term designates a *wali*—a close friend of God or pious person—whose sanctity persists after death and whose intercession is sought by ordinary believers [[1, pp. 31–32]](#ref-1). The practice is attested across Singapore, Malaysia, Brunei, Indonesia, the southern Philippines, and southern Thailand, where it functions as a focal point for lay religious life, folk medicine, and the negotiation of power between communities [[1, pp. 29]](#ref-1). Its significance extends beyond the purely spiritual: *keramat* sites have shaped land-use patterns, influenced economic activity in the timber and construction industries, and served as markers of ethnic identity and inter-communal accommodation in multi-religious societies [[1, pp. 35–37]](#ref-1).

### Scope and Historical Definition

The earliest systematic treatment of the subject in the scholarly record is Richard Winstedt's 1924 paper "Karamat: Sacred Places and Persons in Malaya," which catalogued fifty-one sites across the Malay Peninsula and classified them into six typological groups: natural objects such as rocks, hilltops, capes, and whirlpools; sacred tigers and crocodiles; graves of magicians; graves of settlement founders; graves of Muslim saints; and living Muslim saints [[2, p. 264]](#ref-2). Winstedt argued that the study of *karamat* illuminated both the indigenous nature-worship and ancestor-worship of the pre-Islamic Malays and the "facile canonization" of Muslim saints inherited from the Indian subcontinent, with syncretism rendering the original kernel of many traditions difficult to isolate [[2, p. 264]](#ref-2). Walter W. Skeat, writing in *Malay Magic*, observed that the reverence paid to *keramat* sites "savours a good deal too much of ancestor-worship to be attributable to an orthodox Muhammadan origin," and noted that many of these sites were not graves at all but ancient locations of primitive nature-worship or the adoration of spirits of natural objects [[1, p. 29]](#ref-1).

In the Malay tradition, *keramat*-worship is understood as a legacy of early Sufi Islam, or *tasawuf*, which played an important role in the spread of mystical teachings through Islamic movements [[1, p. 31]](#ref-1). The deceased *datuk*—a term for a respected elder or founder—is believed to manifest as an invisible spirit in particular localities, ensuring the general well-being of residents who worship them [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1). Persons who have done good deeds and contributed to the peace and prosperity of the community may also be remembered and honoured as *keramat* after their demise [[1, p. 33]](#ref-1). The concept thus exists in two forms: material (*kasar*), referring to the bodily tenement or receptacle containing the soul of the departed saint or ancestor, and non-material (*halus*), denoting the sanctity and miraculous power attributed to that soul [[1, p. 33]](#ref-1).

Islamic theologians and jurists were forced by popular consensus to recognize saints and acknowledge their miracles, yet they did not condone pilgrimages to saints' graves or the practice of cultic activities there, since the doctrine of *tauhid*—the absolute oneness of God—repudiates any association of other pseudo-divinities with the one true God [[1, p. 33]](#ref-1). Some Malays nonetheless justify their belief by invoking the concept of *ikhtiar* (effort permitted by God) or *wasilah* (intermediary in approaching Allah), arguing that humans are frail and unworthy of direct contact with the divine [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1). The tombs of Habib Noh in Singapore, Dato' Machap in Malacca, Mahsuri in Langkawi, and Sunan Kalijaga in Indonesia remain visited and worshipped by Malays despite frequent pronouncements against such practice by Muslim scholars [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1).

### The Sinicization of the Keramat

The most extensive modern study of the *keramat* in the Society's literature is Cheu Hock Tong's 1998 article, which documents how Chinese communities in Malaysia adopted and reinterpreted the Malay *keramat* tradition as the *Datuk Kong* or *Nadugong* cult [[1, pp. 29–31]](#ref-1). The term *Datuk Kong* is composed of two synonyms—*Datuk* (Malay) and *Kong* (Chinese), both meaning "grandfather"—the second being redundantly used as an honorific title for the Malay holy person worshipped as a *keramat* or saint [[1, p. 35]](#ref-1). The *Datuk Kong* is thus a generic term for the cult of a venerated deceased person, usually of Malay or native origin, or the spirit being guarding a particular sacred place [[1, p. 35]](#ref-1).

Cheu Hock Tong's fieldwork, conducted between 1977 and 1998 in Peninsular Malaysia, Sabah, and Singapore, revealed that the *Datuk Kong* cult was in a state of ascent among the Chinese even as it declined among Muslims under the impact of the *Dakwah* movement [[1, pp. 35]](#ref-1). In the late 1970s, the ratio of *Datuk Kong* shrines to Chinese houses was 1:10 in villages and 1:100 in towns; by the early 1990s this had shifted to 1:500 in new housing estates and 1:1,000 in urban centres, reflecting the cult's migration from rural to urban settings [[1, p. 41]](#ref-1). The invocation of *Tangfan Nadugong* became common only after the May 13, 1969 riots; before that date, most shrines were based on natural earth outgrowths with minimal structure [[1, pp. 45–46]](#ref-1).

The earth mounds central to the cult typically measure two to three feet high by two to three feet in diameter, though some in Penang, Kedah, and Perak reach five to six feet [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1). Cheu Hock Tong identified these mounds as colonies of giant termites—specifically *Macrotermes carbonarius*, *M. malaccensis*, *M. gilvus*, and *M. ghmadi*—rather than supernatural entities, noting that the "red and white spirits" believed to reside in them are in reality red and black termites [[1, p. 47]](#ref-1). Some farmers and rubber tappers dig the mounds not for spiritual encounter but to obtain queen termites, which they prize as an aphrodisiac [[1, p. 47]](#ref-1).

The *Datuk Kong* differs from the Malay *keramat* in organizational form: the former adopts traditional Chinese spirit tablets and sometimes idols, while the latter avoids such representations, retaining features characteristic of the Malay tradition [[1, p. 37]](#ref-1). The *Datuk Kong* is worshipped as a "local" deity believed to control mysterious forces within a particular jurisdiction; a *Datuk Kong* who reigns supreme in the east is considered powerless in the west, and vice versa [[1, p. 37]](#ref-1). This locality principle accounts for the spread and configuration of shrines in the west coast states of Peninsular Malaysia, where Chinese predominantly reside in towns and suburban villages, and for their subsequent extension to Sabah and Sarawak following Chinese migration after 1963 [[1, p. 37]](#ref-1).

### Material Culture and Sacred Sites

The physical manifestations of the *keramat* cult are diverse. Winstedt recorded diagnostic physical marks associated with sanctity: in the living, a hairy uvula, dwarfism, a partially black tongue, and short-sightedness; in the dead, saffron-like fairness of the corpse and the mysterious separation of head and foot stones of a grave [[2, pp. 264]](#ref-2). At Ketapang, Pekan, Pahang, the head and foot stones of the grave of the pious unmarried Malay Jaafar (*To' Panjang*) separated to a length of fifteen feet after his death, confirming his saintly status; a parallel case at Kampong Marhum, Kuala Pahang, records a grave that parted to thirty feet after an angelic figure confirmed its sanctity [[2, p. 275]](#ref-2). At *Karamat Tangga China* near Ampang, the animals of a pious Sumatran immigrant—his pony, cow, and buffalo—refused food and died at his grave in succession, prompting the community to recognize him as a saint; rich Chinese subsequently built a shrine over the grave [[2, p. 274]](#ref-2).

In Singapore, P. J. Rivers documented a rich but vanishing landscape of *keramat* sites. The *Batu Berlayar* ("Lot's Wife"), a natural rock pillar venerated by the Orang Laut and known to navigators for "many hundreds of years," was blown up in 1848 as an obstruction to shipping [[3, p. 102]](#ref-3). An engraved sandstone block found at the mouth of the Singapore River—ten feet high, two to five feet thick, nine to ten feet long, bearing approximately fifty lines of illegible inscription—was blasted to pieces by 1835 under Superintendent of Public Works George Coleman to make way for a bungalow [[3, p. 104]](#ref-3). The *Keramat Iskandar Shah* at Fort Canning was restored in 1990 at a cost of nearly S$150,000, incorporating twenty wooden pillars carved by Malaccan craftsmen; a 1984 excavation by John Miksic uncovered pottery fragments dating to the fourteenth century, raising the possibility that the grave belongs to Sri Tri Buana (c. 1299–1347), founder of the Singapore dynasty, rather than to Iskandar Shah (d. 1413) [[3, pp. 104–105]](#ref-3). The mausoleum of Habib Noh (d. July 1866), erected c. 1890 by Syed Mohammed bin Ahmed Al-Sagoff, underwent a 1980s renovation costing S$800,000 [[3, p. 107]](#ref-3). By the 2000s, only four *keramat* sites remained in Singapore—Habib Noh, Iskandar Shah, Radin Mas, and the Nagore Durgha—while the *Keramat Habib Syed Ismail* at the eighth milestone, the *Keramat Panjang* in Ringwood Road, and the Lorong 40 long grave had all been destroyed by development [[3, pp. 115–116]](#ref-3).

Rivers distinguished between "true" *keramat*—sites that acquire power independently of the person interred—and the far more numerous pseudo-*keramat*, which are essentially "no cure, no pay" arrangements where devotees make offerings in exchange for specific results such as lottery numbers or cures [[3]](#ref-3). This distinction explains how Singapore, in less than two centuries of modern settlement, could generate so many saints: the sites were often accidental, the graves convenient, and the names apocryphal [[3]](#ref-3).

### Administrative and Legal Frameworks

Official Islamic orthodoxy has periodically moved against *keramat* practices. In May 1996, the Islamic religious authorities in Malaysia took the Shi'ites to task by excluding them from the Sunni Muslim community for adding to the *azan* the clause that "Ali is the Friend of Allah" [[1, p. 33]](#ref-1). A 1990 Sabah State Fatwah prohibited the construction of structures at graves and the placement of articles "such as flags, mosquito nets, lamps, umbrellas, milk bottles, pillows and portraits," reflecting the campaign against *keramat* practices [[3, p. 99]](#ref-3). In some urban centres, *Nadugong* or *Tangfan Nadugong* shrines have been demolished to give way to development, including the construction of roads and buildings; in isolated cases, shrines were razed for encroaching on state land or for bearing Muslim names [[1, p. 55]](#ref-1). Conversely, numerous stories circulate of local authorities being prevented by guardian spirits from demolishing certain shrines, which explains why some continue to occupy strategic positions in urban centres [[1, p. 55]](#ref-1).

The economic dimensions of the cult are notable. In Tawau, Sabah, black fowls used for sacrificial rituals before major lumbering, mining, and construction projects fetched between RM100 (US$40) and RM1,000 (US$400) each, owing to the difficulty of obtaining wholly black specimens [[1, p. 42]](#ref-1). Indonesian and local workers in Keningau, Sandakan, and Tawau have been known to refuse to clear jungle or begin work at new timber camps or construction sites until their employers secured a *pawang* to perform supplicatory rituals at the sites [[1, p. 35]](#ref-1). During the New Economic Policy era, full-sized *Datuk Kong* temples were constructed, including the Stone Datuk Temple at Broga, Selangor (two acres, with plans to expand by a further three acres), the *Datuk Kong* Temple at Tuaran, Sabah (one acre), and the *Datuk Acheh* Temple at Jalan Weston, Penang (less than one acre) [[1, p. 56]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The scholarly record on the *keramat* in the Society's literature spans nearly eight decades, from Winstedt's foundational typology of 1924 to Rivers' elegiac survey of Singapore's vanishing sacred landscape in 2003. Winstedt's paper, published in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, established the first systematic classification of *karamat* sites and persons and explicitly invited further contributions [[2, p. 279]](#ref-2). For over seventy years, no further report on the *keramat* appeared in the journal, a gap Cheu Hock Tong noted in 1998 [[1, p. 29]](#ref-1). His article represented a significant shift in analytical focus: rather than treating the Malay *keramat* as an object of ethnographic description, he examined its reception and transformation within Chinese folk religion, thereby contributing to the literature on religious syncretism and locality cults in Southeast Asia [[1, pp. 29–31]](#ref-1).

Between Winstedt and Cheu Hock Tong, the Society's record includes only a brief note by W. Linehan on the *Keramat Seri Benian* (1951), which documents a specific sacred site in the Malay Peninsula [[4]](#ref-4). The historiographical trajectory thus reveals a pattern of initial colonial-era cataloguing, a long period of relative silence, and a late twentieth-century resurgence driven by the observable expansion of the *Datuk Kong* cult among Chinese communities and the concurrent decline of *keramat*-worship among Muslims. Rivers' 2003 article, drawing on fieldwork conducted in the 1950s and supplemented by a postscript from the 2000s, provides the most detailed account of the material fate of individual *keramat* sites over a fifty-year span, documenting their destruction by urban development and the persistence of a small number of major shrines [[3]](#ref-3). The combined evidence suggests that the *keramat* remains a living institution whose future is shaped by the competing forces of Islamic revival, urbanization, economic development, and inter-ethnic religious accommodation.
