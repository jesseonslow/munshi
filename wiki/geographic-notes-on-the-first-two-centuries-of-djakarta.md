# Geographic notes on the first two centuries of Djakarta

James L. Cobban's 1971 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the historical geography of Djakarta (Batavia) across the two centuries of Dutch East India Company sovereignty, arguing that the city's form, population, and economic hinterland were shaped by the interplay of maritime trade, defensive imperatives, and the gradual agricultural exploitation of Java. The study draws extensively on Dutch archival sources—Company resolutions, the *Dagh-Register*, and the *Nederlandsch Indisch Plakkaatboek*—to reconstruct the physical and social evolution of the settlement from its founding in 1619 to the European abandonment of the old city at the close of the eighteenth century.

## Summary

Cobban frames Djakarta as a city of foreign creation imposed upon an indigenous landscape of coastal trading ports and inland *kraton* capitals. The Dutch introduced a European concept of urban form—canalized, walled, rectangular—that was alien to Javanese settlement patterns and which transformed the physical environment of the Tjiliwong river mouth. The article traces how the settlement evolved from a small trading post (the stone warehouses Nassau and Mauritz) into a fortified city of sixteen canals and fifty-six bridges, shaped by successive Governors-General who responded to defensive threats from Bantam and Mataram while simultaneously developing the infrastructure of a commercial *rendez-vous* for the Company's Asian trade network.

A central theme is the tension between the Company's dual role as sovereign and merchant. The free citizenry (*vrijburgers*) that Coen envisioned as the backbone of a prosperous Dutch settlement was systematically undermined by the Company's monopolistic trading policies, which reserved the most profitable commercial activities for itself while leaving the *burgers* with limited agricultural and artisanal opportunities. The Chinese, by contrast, filled the economic niches the Company neglected—market gardening, inland trade, sugar production, and *arak* distillation—becoming the dominant non-European population and the engine of the city's domestic economy. Cobban shows how this demographic and economic structure, combined with the malarial environment of the low-lying coastal site, ultimately drove the European population inland to Weltevreden, leaving the old city to decay.

The article also reconstructs the development of the agricultural hinterland, demonstrating how sugar cultivation, rice production, and forest clearance extended the city's economic reach from a few miles in the seventeenth century to the entire island of Java by the eighteenth. The sugar industry, in particular, is treated in considerable detail as the principal economic activity of the *Ommelanden*, its rise and crisis driven by fuelwood availability, government regulation, and competition from Caribbean production.

### Key Findings

- The 1610 agreement between the Dutch and the Pangeran of Jacatra granted the Company a site in the Chinese quarter on the east bank of the Tjiliwong for a payment of 1,200 reals of eight (approximately 3,000 guilders), with the first stone building (Nassau) constructed on the parcel nearest the indigenous village (pp. 110–111).
- By 1624, Governor-General Carpentier reported the population at 6,425, composed of Hollanders, Mardijkers, slaves, prisoners of war, Chinese, Japanese, English, and other foreigners; by 1724, Valentijn recorded additional groups including Armenians, Persians, Arabs, Bengalese, Siamese, and peoples from Timor, Makassar, Ambon, Banda, Ternate, Bali, Sumatra, and Borneo (pp. 121–122).
- Sugar mill numbers in the environs of Djakarta fluctuated dramatically: 22 mills by 1648, declining to 8–10 by 1659 after the Bantam raid of 1656, recovering to 131 by 1710 (49 west, 82 east, all but five Chinese-owned), then restricted to 80 by Governor-General Mossel in 1750 and falling to 55 by 1779 (24 European, 26 Chinese, 5 Javanese) (pp. 131–132).
- The canal system expanded from three east-west canals (Markgracht, Oude Kerkgracht, Leeuwengracht) by the early 1620s to a complete network of sixteen canals and fifty-six stone bridges by the 1670s, with the Tijgersgracht added in 1622 and a defensive canal 300 feet wide and 10 feet deep constructed by 1627 (pp. 114–115, 119).
- In 1623, Coen recorded the arrival of three Dutch ships from Coromandel carrying approximately 1,500 slaves, mostly children; by the end of the eighteenth century, large landed proprietors commonly owned over 300 slaves and modest European householders around 60 (pp. 127–128).
- The European abandonment of the old city began under Governor-General van Overstraaten (1796–1801) and was completed under Daendels, who demolished the fort, city walls, and exterior fortifications; by 1816 the old city was inhabited mostly by Chinese, Portuguese, and Asians (pp. 147–148).

### Conclusion

Cobban's definitive takeaway is that Djakarta's early history was one of a trade port that gradually became a colonial capital through reluctant political engagement with the Javanese interior. The city's primacy in Indonesia was secured not by the natural products of Java—which remained negligible for Company trade until coffee was introduced to the Preanger in 1707—but by its strategic position on international shipping routes and its function as the Company's *rendez-vous*. The decision to retain the site despite its notorious unhealthiness, moving the European population to the higher ground of Weltevreden rather than abandoning the location entirely, insured Djakarta's continued dominance as Indonesia's largest city.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the *Dagh-Register* of Batavia (1624–1682), the *Nederlandsch Indisch Plakkaatboek*, the multi-volume work of J.K.J. de Jonge (*De Opkomst van het Nederlandsch Gezag in Oost-Indie*), and the writings of Jan Hooyman (1781) and Andreis Teisseire (1792) on the *Ommelanden*, supplemented by the maps of Clemendt de Jonge (1650) and the *Nederlandsch Indie Platen Atlas* (1926).
- Historiographically, the article fills a gap in English-language scholarship on Djakarta's early urban development, building on the Dutch archival tradition of Brandes, de Haan, and van der Chijs while applying a historical-geographic framework that integrates physical morphology, demographic composition, and economic land use into a coherent spatial narrative.
