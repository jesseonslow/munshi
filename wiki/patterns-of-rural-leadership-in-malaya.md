# Patterns of rural leadership in Malaya

S. Husin Ali, a Malaysian sociologist, published this study in 1968 to examine how rural Malay leadership in Malaya was being restructured by the cumulative impact of colonial administration, economic modernisation, and post-war political change. Drawing on comparative fieldwork in two rural *mukim* and one urban settlement, the article argues that traditional leadership—rooted in kinship authority, magico-religious expertise, and hereditary political office—was being progressively absorbed into a bureaucratic framework, while new dominant leaders centred on formal education and economic wealth were emerging to fill the resulting vacuum.

## Summary

Ali's central problem is to identify who the leaders in rural Malay communities actually are, how their positions are determined, and how the nature of their authority has shifted as the village itself has been transformed from a simple, kinship-based, subsistence economy into a stratified, cash-crop-dependent society integrated into the national administrative and political system. The study is designed as a controlled comparison: two rural *mukim* (Kerdau in Pahang and Bagan in Johore) are examined in depth, with an urban Malay settlement (Kampung Baru, Kuala Lumpur) serving as a contrast case to illuminate what has been lost or displaced in the rural setting.

The core argument is that the transition from traditional to modern leadership is neither complete nor uniform. In the rural areas, traditional and modern forms coexist along the same institutional line: the *penghulu* retains the formal trappings of the old hereditary village head but now functions as a salaried government servant whose authority derives from the District Office rather than the *sultan*; the *imam* of the government-built mosque is a bureaucratised religious official distinct from the *surau* imam who still operates within a community-consent framework. New dominant leaders—school teachers and wealthy landlords—have risen not by displacing the traditional categories but by occupying the key posts in the proliferating committees and organisations that the modern administrative and political order has introduced into the village. Ali demonstrates that the *penghulu* is caught in a structural dilemma: his effectiveness as a community leader depends on his personal qualities and his willingness to identify with his *anak buah*, yet his position as a government servant pulls him toward the role of *orang kerajaan* (government man), eroding the trust that made the traditional head indispensable.

The empirical base consists of participant observation, structured questionnaires, and interviews conducted over multiple field seasons, supplemented by a corpus of student academic exercises on the *penghulu* system across several states. These sources allow Ali to trace the succession of village heads through genealogical records, to quantify the distribution of committee posts by social category, and to document the specific mechanisms—election, bureaucratic selection, inheritance—by which leadership positions are now filled.

### Key Findings

- The first *penghulu* elections were held in Kerdau in 1954 and Bagan in 1956, replacing hereditary succession; in Pahang the method subsequently shifted back toward bureaucratic selection by the Public Services Commission, while Johore continued to use election consistently (pp. 126–127).
- School teachers dominated key committee posts: in Kerdau, 7 teachers held 10 of 53 posts (4 secretaries, 2 chairmen); in Bagan, 10 teachers held 13 of 56 posts (7 secretaries, 3 chairmen), despite constituting only 10.8% and 10.1% of household heads respectively (pp. 134–135).
- Bagan's class structure was markedly more polarised than Kerdau's: non-owning peasants comprised 44.3% of Bagan households versus 3.2% in Kerdau, while landlords made up 13.4% versus 5.4% (p. 106, Table I).
- The Bagan *penghulu*, a former school teacher and ex-UMNO local secretary, maintained effective community leadership; the Kerdau *penghulu*, with only minimum elementary education, was perceived by villagers as a mere *orang kerajaan* who spent his evenings at the mahjong table (pp. 131–132).
- Rubber was introduced to Bagan in 1910 and Kerdau in 1915; by the time of the study, 87.6% of Kerdau and 83.2% of Bagan household heads remained dependent on agriculture, confirming the villages' continued rural character (pp. 97, 103).
- In Kampung Baru (Kuala Lumpur), the *penghulu* was virtually unknown to residents; dominant leaders were English-educated government or ex-government servants with monthly incomes of $300–$700, and the settlement's management committee was directly responsible to the state Chief Minister rather than to the community (pp. 138–141).

### Conclusion

Ali's definitive historical takeaway is that rural Malay leadership is fundamentally instrumental rather than transformative: village leaders function as channels through which decisions made at supra-village levels are implemented locally, with initiative flowing consistently from the top downward. The coexistence of traditional and modern leadership in rural areas reflects the incomplete nature of social transformation—traditional values and structures remain strong enough to sustain the old categories alongside the new—whereas in urbanised settings like Kampung Baru, traditional leadership has been effectively displaced by a modern, achievement-based elite drawn from the government middle class.

## Context

- Primary data derive from participant observation in Kerdau (two months, 1965) and Bagan (four months, c. 1959, with follow-up visits 1965), supplemented by twelve student academic exercises on the *penghulu* system across Pahang, Johore, Kedah, Perak, Selangor, and Kuala Lumpur (pp. 96–98, 145).
- The article constitutes the first systematic study of Malay rural leadership as a distinct subject, moving beyond the indirect references in Gullick's *Indigenous Political Systems of Western Malaya* (1958), Burridge's articles on Johore (1957), Downs on Kelantan (1960), and Swift on Jelebu (1965) (p. 95).
