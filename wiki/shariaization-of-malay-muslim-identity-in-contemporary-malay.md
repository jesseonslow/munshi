# Shariaization of Malay-Muslim identity in contemporary Malaysia

## Abstract

The importance of Gua Kajang (Kajang Cave) as a prehistoric archaeological site in Peninsular Malaysia has been recognized since the early 1900s and was designated as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2012. Over the past 10 years, archaeological excavations and research in this cave have produced evidence of prehistoric occupation that broadens the understanding of prehistoric cave archaeology in the Lenggong Valley. This article presents the early lifeways of prehistoric people of Gua Kajang between 11,000 and 4,000 years ago based on two human burials and associated finds, including stone artefacts, fauna remains and earthenware pottery. The material indicates that the early humans of Peninsula Malaysia were cave-dwellers or hunter-gatherers who depended heavily on forest resources.

## Summary

The article addresses a long-standing problem in Malaysian prehistoric archaeology: the unresolved chronology and cultural sequence of Gua Kajang, one of the earliest investigated cave sites in Peninsular Malaysia. Despite being known since Evans's 1917 excavations, the cave had never been securely dated, and earlier investigations by Evans, Williams-Hunt, and Chia were hampered by poor documentation, methodological limitations, and site disturbance from guano digging and illegal excavation. The 2007 excavation, conducted in the only remaining undisturbed area of the front cave, was designed to resolve these chronological ambiguities and rescue material from ongoing destruction (pp. 1–5).

The core argument is that Gua Kajang was intermittently occupied by hunter-gatherer populations from the Late Pleistocene through the Holocene, with the site serving both as a short-term camp and a burial ground. The two human burials (GK 1 and GK 2) and their associated mortuary assemblages—stone tools, fauna remains, and riverine shells—provide the primary evidence for reconstructing lifeways. The flexed burial position of GK 1 aligns with a broader pattern of Late Pleistocene mortuary practices across Mainland Southeast Asia, while the presence of haematite-stained limestone in GK 2's association signals a shift toward Holocene burial traditions (pp. 8–13).

The article draws on the full stratigraphic sequence (1.6 m of cultural deposits across six layers), radiocarbon determinations from shell and organic sediment samples, osteological analysis of the skeletal remains, and comparative typological analysis of the stone assemblage to argue for continuity in subsistence strategies across the Late Pleistocene–Early Holocene, with the appearance of earthenware pottery marking a technological transition in the later Holocene (pp. 13–15).

### Key Findings

- The 2007 excavation yielded 15,329 cultural artefacts from a 1.6-metre-thick anthropic layer, including 700 stone artefacts, 14,592 fauna remains (predominantly 14,255 riverine shells), and 37 pottery sherds (p. 6, Table 1).
- Four radiocarbon dates establish occupation from approximately 11,000 to 7,000 years ago: Layer 6 at 10,820 ± 60 BP and 10,000 ± 60 BP (shells), Layer 5 at 10,470 ± 60 BP (organic sediment), and Layer 3 at 7,890 ± 80 BP (shells) (p. 7, Table 2).
- GK 1, a flexed burial at 120–140 cm depth, was identified as an adult female approximately 155–163 cm tall, buried with 35 stone tools, 47 animal bones, and 1,411 riverine shells (pp. 8–10).
- GK 2, at 60–80 cm depth, was associated with a haematite-stained limestone slab overlaying the foot bones, 8 stone tools, 67 shells, and 7 animal bones; the haematite pigmentation is characteristic of Holocene burial culture in the region (pp. 10–11).
- The stone assemblage is dominated by flake tools and 'Sumatralith' pebble tools made from locally available quartz, quartzite, and limestone, with no significant typological variability across stratigraphic layers (pp. 13–14).
- Earthenware pottery (plain, cord-marked, incised, and red-slipped) appears only in the upper undated layers, provisionally dated to 3,000–4,000 years ago, marking the Neolithic transition (pp. 14–15).

### Conclusion

The authors conclude that early humans in the Malay Peninsula were hunter-gatherers dependent on forest and riverine resources who practised mortuary rituals involving the interment of used stone tools and fauna remains with the dead. The site was not permanently occupied but repeatedly used for short-term habitation and burial across the Late Pleistocene and Holocene, with no strong evidence for continuous sequential occupation throughout the Holocene (p. 15).

## Context

- The study draws on the 2007 excavation archive at Gua Kajang (Lenggong Valley, Perak), building on earlier collections from Evans (1917), Williams-Hunt (1945–1952), and Chia (1995).
- Historiographically, the article resolves the chronological gap left by earlier investigations and contributes to the broader Southeast Asian framework for Late Pleistocene–Holocene cave occupation and burial practices, linking Gua Kajang to comparative sites in Thailand, Java, and Vietnam.
