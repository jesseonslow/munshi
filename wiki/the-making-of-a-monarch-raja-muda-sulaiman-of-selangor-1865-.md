# The making of a monarch: Raja Muda Sulaiman of Selangor 1865-98

J.M. Gullick's 2008 article reconstructs the formative years of Raja Sulaiman (1865–1898), tracing his development from a child displaced by the Selangor Civil War into the Raja Muda who would eventually succeed Sultan Abdul Samad. Set against the backdrop of Selangor's transition from a small, tin-exporting sultanate to a British-administered state within the Federated Malay States, the article argues that Sulaiman's path to the throne was neither straightforward nor predetermined, shaped as it was by his eccentric father's failures, the political threat of rival claimants, and the demands of an emerging colonial bureaucracy.

## Summary

Sulaiman was born in 1865 at Kuala Selangor, the eldest son of Raja Muda Musa, who held the coastal fief as a nominal royal representative. The death of Sultan Ibrahim in 1826 had set in motion a long succession of political instability, culminating in the civil war of 1867–73, during which Musa withdrew his family to Singapore. There Sulaiman received a disjointed education at Raffles Institution before the family returned to Kuala Langat, where the Sultan had established his ancestral stronghold. At the small Malay vernacular school in Kuala Langat, the young Sulaiman was noted by the district officer's wife, Emily Innes, as a "bright, gentle-mannered boy" who could read and write Malay fluently—an unusual accomplishment for a boy of his rank (pp. 2–3). His father Musa, described by contemporaries as "mad" yet revered for his industriousness, broke aristocratic convention by sending Sulaiman to school and apprenticing him on a sugar estate in Province Wellesley to learn cultivation techniques (pp. 3–4).

Musa's sudden death in July 1884 left the nineteen-year-old Sulaiman to manage his father's agricultural enterprises, including a sugar plantation and coconut groves totalling some 1,100 acres. The venture collapsed within months: his only sales contract was for 12 pikuls of sugar monthly to a Cantonese headman, and the coconut plantation was soon overgrown and neglected (pp. 6–7). The more pressing question was political. Raja Mahmud, a charismatic but volatile kinsman who had been deputising at Kuala Selangor, immediately enquired about the vacant Raja Muda post. British officials, unwilling to elevate such an "awkward customer," managed to deflect him by sending him to Pahang as adviser to Hugh Clifford. Sulaiman was appointed Raja Muda in 1887, though his installation was further delayed by the death of Mahmud's father, which allowed Mahmud to return with the consolation of his father's title on the state council (pp. 7–8).

As Raja Muda, Sulaiman occupied a dual position: representing his aged grandfather at Jugra while participating in the English-language bureaucracy at Kuala Lumpur. He initiated the Malay Raja School in 1890, secured the appointment of Selangor's first state kadi, and presented a pamphlet on matrimonial law to the colonial government in 1894 (pp. 8–11). He was also a pioneer of Malay girls' education, establishing schools at Kuala Langat and Klang with curricula limited to reading, writing, religious instruction, and traditional handicrafts, and even providing curtained bullock carts to overcome parental objections to sending girls out of seclusion (pp. 11–12). His 1891 marriage to Tunku Maharum, daughter of the former regent Tunku Kudin, was a politically arranged union that produced five children in eight years, though it would later be strained by Sulaiman's second marriage (pp. 12–14).

### Key Findings

- Sulaiman's inheritance of his father's 1,100-acre plantation at age nineteen ended in commercial failure within a year, with only a 12-pikul monthly sugar contract and a neglected coconut grove, confirming he was "not gifted as a man of business" (pp. 6–7).
- The Raja Muda succession was resolved not by genealogy alone but by British political management: Raja Mahmud was deflected to Pahang, and the appointment was further delayed by the death of his father, which gave Mahmud a consolation title on the state council (pp. 7–8).
- Sulaiman's 1890 request for an English tutor directly led to the foundation of the Malay Raja School in Kuala Lumpur, of which he was one of a handful of pupils (pp. 8–9).
- He secured the appointment of the first state kadi, Haji Jafar, and introduced registers and standard certificates for marriage and divorce records, addressing what officials described as "a state of chaos" in native affairs (pp. 10–11).
- His 1894 pamphlet on matrimonial law, presented to the colonial government, was "thoughtful rather than radical" and met with a "tepid and non-committal" official response, with its minimalist views eventually finding expression in the FMS Mohamedan Laws Enactment of 1904 (p. 11).
- Sulaiman was a talented wood carver and designer who personally designed the carved wooden chair, pillar mouldings, and stained glass windows for a new mosque he built near the astana at Kuala Langat (pp. 9, 14).

### Conclusion

Gullick presents Sulaiman as a figure who "acquitted himself well but it was not always plain sailing" (p. 1). The article's definitive takeaway is that Sulaiman's value to the Selangor state lay not in commercial acumen or military prowess but in his capacity as a cultural and institutional mediator—bridging traditional Malay royalty and the colonial modern state through his work in Islamic law reform, education, and the arts. He was heir presumptive rather than heir apparent, and his standing at the moment of his grandfather's death would ultimately determine the succession, a condition that kept him perpetually performing for an audience that included both the Sultan and the Resident.

## Context

- The article draws heavily on the Kuala Langat Journal (1882–1885), the Douglas diary, Selangor Government Gazette reports, and the *Selangor Journal*, supplemented by Emily Innes' *The Chersonese with the Gilding Off* (1885) for contemporary observations of court life at Kuala Langat.
- Gullick's broader research programme on Selangor's colonial period, including his multi-volume *A History of Selangor* (1990s), provides the institutional framework within which this biographical study is situated.
