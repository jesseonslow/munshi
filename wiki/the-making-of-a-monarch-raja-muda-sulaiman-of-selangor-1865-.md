# The making of a monarch: Raja Muda Sulaiman of Selangor 1865-98

Bob Reece's 2008 article examines the semi-official correspondence between Rajah Charles Brooke and his Sarawak Treasurer, F. H. Dallas, written primarily from the Rajah's Gloucestershire estate between 1902 and 1917. The letters reveal the inner workings of Brooke's governance, his family conflicts, and his territorial ambitions during the final decades of his rule over Sarawak.

## Summary

Reece draws on a previously underutilized collection of letters held at the National Archives, Kew, to reconstruct the character and administrative priorities of the second White Rajah during his final fifteen years. The article argues that these private communications offer a far more nuanced portrait than the official histories or the hostile memoirs of his daughter-in-law Sylvia Brooke, presenting a man who was simultaneously a meticulous administrator, a radical liberal in his political views, and a deeply troubled patriarch. The correspondence was written mostly from Chesterton Lodge, the Rajah's country estate near Cirencester, where from 1903 he spent the English autumn and winter, with a handful of letters dispatched from Sarawak outstations or aboard ship.

The letters illuminate three interlocking themes. First, Charles's determination to maintain personal control over Sarawak's government despite his annual absences in England, using Dallas as his principal agent and check on his son Vyner, whom he regarded as pessimistic, undisciplined, and financially irresponsible. Charles explicitly instructed Dallas that he remained responsible for carrying out the Rajah's orders "whatever order the Rajah Muda may give you" (p. 5). Second, his single-minded campaign to acquire Brunei and eventually British North Borneo, pursued through press lobbying, political cultivation, and direct negotiation with the Chartered Company of North Borneo, only to be blocked by the Foreign Office's supplementary treaty with the Sultan of Brunei in December 1905. Third, the domestic financial and emotional crises caused by his estranged wife Ranee Margaret's spending, his son Harry's debts, and the catastrophic marriage of Vyner to Sylvia Brett in 1911, which Charles correctly predicted would destabilize the succession.

Reece also uses the letters to explore Charles's personal life at Chesterton Lodge — his hunting with the Gloucestershire Hunt, his voracious reading, and his devotion to French and Italian opera — and his political views, which were notably anti-imperialist and liberal for a colonial ruler of his era. He deplored the Boer War, supported Irish Home Rule, and criticized the "de facto Lords of Creation" of London's financial city. The article closes with the Rajah's death in May 1917 and the vindication of his fears about Vyner's incapacity, which set Sarawak on a path toward stagnation and eventual British annexation.

### Key Findings

- The primary source collection comprises thirteen chronologically ordered files at the National Archives, Kew (PRO 30/79/1–13), supplemented by PRO 30/79/14 (Dallas's business correspondence) and PRO 30/79/15 (Vyner Brooke's letters to Dallas, 1907–1912) (p. 4, n. 16).
- Charles Brooke purchased Chesterton Lodge in late 1902 for £10,000 and opened a public reading room and museum for his Sarawak artefact collection there in September 1904 (pp. 7, 11).
- The acquisition of the Lawas district from the British North Borneo Company was finalized in December 1904, which Charles described as "a good bargain for us and a stepping stone to get Brunei" (p. 16).
- The British government negotiated a supplementary treaty with the Sultan of Brunei in December 1905, providing for a Malay States-style Resident and effectively blocking Charles's annexation plans (p. 16).
- The Ranee's annual allowance was increased by £500 (to £2,500), and Charles ultimately paid out £4,000 to settle his son Harry's debts from the failed newspaper *The Planet* (p. 13).
- Charles died of pneumonia at Chesterton Lodge on 17 May 1917, days before his 89th birthday; his body was buried at Sheepstor churchyard in Devon on 12 June 1919 (pp. 24–25).

### Conclusion

Reece's definitive historical takeaway is that Charles Brooke was the most significant of the three White Rajahs — the builder of Sarawak's modern boundaries, its rational bureaucracy, and its economic foundations — yet his inability to secure a competent succession meant that his life's work was squandered by Vyner's misrule. The Advisory Council and Committee of Administration that Charles devised as safeguards were scrapped immediately after his death, and the state slid toward "bankruptcy of policy, stagnation of government," cession of sovereignty in February 1946, and British annexation in July 1947 (p. 25).

## Context

- Primary archival collections: National Archives, Kew (PRO 30/79/1–15; CO 531/19) and Rhodes House Library, Oxford (MSS Pac. s. 83; MSS Pac. s. 90).
- Historiographical contribution: The article provides the first serious engagement with the Dallas correspondence, offering a corrective to both the hagiographic official history (Baring-Gould and Bampfylde, 1909) and the hostile personal memoirs (Sylvia Brooke, 1936 and 1970), and fills a gap left by Colin Crisswell's 1978 biography, which made no use of the Rhodes House primary sources.
