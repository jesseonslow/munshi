# The ritual of the rice-field

R. O. Winstedt published "The Ritual of the Rice-Field" in 1929, presenting a Perak manuscript that documents the full ceremonial cycle prescribed by the Malay *pawang* (medicine-man) for rice cultivation, from jungle clearing through to grain storage. The article situates this ritual within the broader syncretic framework Winstedt had already outlined in *Shaman, Saiva and Sufi*, showing how Islamic formulae, animist spirit-veneration, and agricultural pragmatism are interwoven in a single operational text.

## Summary

The article is essentially a philological and ethnographic presentation of a longer Perak manuscript supplied by two headmen, which Winstedt reproduces in full without translation, noting that Malay scholars will not need one and that English readers can consult the paraphrase in chapter VI of *Shaman, Saiva and Sufi*. The manuscript traces the *pawang*'s involvement at every critical juncture of the wet-rice cycle: the initial consultation before clearing, the burning of the fallow, the sowing of seed, the "taking of the rice-soul" (*mengambil semangat padi*), the harvesting, threshing, winnowing, and final storage in the granary.

The core argument, implicit in Winstedt's framing, is that the rice-field ritual constitutes a coherent, internally consistent system in which the *pawang* mediates between the cultivator and the spirits of the land, the rice-plant, and the village. The ritual is not a collection of isolated superstitions but a structured sequence of prohibitions, offerings, and invocations that governs the temporal and spatial organisation of agricultural labour. Islamic elements—*Bismillah*, salutations to the Prophet, the *imam*'s prayers, the obligation of *zakat*—are embedded within a framework that also invokes *dato'* (ancestral spirits), *dewa*, *mambang*, *jin*, *peri*, *langsuir*, and *bota raya*, demonstrating the layered religious character of Perak Malay agrarian practice.

The manuscript also provides valuable lexicographical material. Winstedt flags several terms absent from contemporary Malay dictionaries, including *nasi mura* (cooling rice-water), *gedebong* (Javanese for banana stem), *menyang manggi* (a variety of rice), *katam* (a slice of sugar-cane), and *tengkelangkan* (to cross the hands). He further corrects and extends Skeat's account of rice-ear varieties in which the rice-soul resides, identifying ten types against Skeat's fewer, and offering an etymological note on *Tongkat Mandah* via Von de Wall's identification of *mandah* as "rice-chest."

### Key Findings

- The manuscript prescribes a ten-type hierarchy of rice-ears in which the rice-soul (*semangat padi*) may reside, ranked from *Raja Padi* (white ear, yellow grain, yellow stem, white leaves with yellow stripes) down to *Padi Di-sarang oleh burong untong* (a rice-ear nested in by a bird at its base); if none of the ten is found, the taking of the rice-soul should not be attempted (p. 444).
- The *nasi mura* preparation—rice-water infused with *perut kundor*, banana stem, *pisang tasbeh*, *rotan dini*, *sambau dara*, *setawar benar*, and *setawar hutan*—must be sprinkled on the burnt clearing before dawn ("before the flies fly") to prevent *Nenek Kemang* from sowing her own seeds of weeds and grasses (p. 439).
- The *pawang* must circle the field three evenings before entering it on the third evening to locate and mark the rice-soul plant; the seven stalks are bound with twisted *terap* and *ijok*, five-coloured thread, and a nail, then cut in a single stroke while the *pawang* holds his breath with eyes closed and teeth clenched (pp. 444–445).
- A three-day prohibition (*pantang*) follows the taking of the rice-soul: no fire may be lit, food must not be finished in the pot, no one may shout from house to field or field to house, and no one may sleep in the field; during the first three days of harvesting, the cutter must not cast a shadow, look to the sides or behind, or speak to anyone (p. 446).
- The *zakat* obligation is specified at one-tenth: for every 500 *gantang* of paddy, 50 *gantang* must be distributed to the poor, the *imam*, and village officials, accompanied by a feast slightly larger than the preceding one (p. 447).
- The *pawang* selects the *ibu tukal* (mother dibber) from *senyanya* or *dada* wood growing on a termite mound or ant's nest; if neither is available, any wood may be used, but the preferred species are specified as approximately forearm-thick at the base (p. 441).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that the Perak rice-field ritual, as preserved in this manuscript, represents a complete and self-contained body of prescriptive knowledge in which the *pawang* functions as the indispensable intermediary between the human cultivator and the spiritual forces governing the land and the crop. The text demonstrates that Malay agricultural ritual is neither a residual pre-Islamic survival nor a mere collection of folk beliefs, but a living, structured system in which Islamic and indigenous elements are functionally integrated at every stage of the rice cycle.

## Context

- The manuscript was supplied by two Perak headmen and is printed in full without translation; Winstedt's earlier paraphrase appears in *Shaman, Saiva and Sufi* (1925), chapter VI.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malay religion by providing a primary-source operational text that complements and in places corrects Skeat's *Malay Magic* (1900–1901), particularly on the taxonomy of rice-soul varieties and on the role of the *pawang* in the agricultural calendar.
