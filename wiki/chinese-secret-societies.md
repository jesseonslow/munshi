# Chinese secret societies

Geoff Wade's 2005 article, published in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 78, Part 1), offers a revisionist reassessment of the early fifteenth-century Ming maritime voyages commanded by the eunuch Zheng He. Wade argues that these missions, long celebrated in Chinese popular memory as "voyages of friendship," were in fact aggressive instruments of the Yong-le emperor's (r. 1403–24) southern expansion policy, constituting a form of maritime proto-colonialism aimed at establishing a *pax Ming* across Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean.

## Summary

Wade situates the Zheng He voyages within a broader tripartite strategy of southern expansion pursued by the Yong-le emperor, which also encompassed the land-based colonization of the Tai polities in Yun-nan and the military invasion and occupation of Đai Viet (1406–28). By placing the maritime missions alongside these two better-recognized colonial ventures, Wade demonstrates that the eunuch-led fleets shared the same strategic logic: the projection of military power to compel submission, extract tribute, and secure economic advantage. The voyages were not diplomatic goodwill tours but armed expeditions crewed predominantly by military personnel, equipped with the most advanced firearms of the era, and tasked with enforcing Ming suzerainty over the polities of the maritime realm (pp. 39–45).

The article draws heavily on the *Ming shi-lu* (Veritable Records of the Ming), supplemented by the *Wu-bei-zhi* military compendium, Ma Huan's *Ying-yai sheng-lan*, and a range of secondary scholarship including Mills' translation of Ma Huan, Whitmore's work on the Ming-Vietnamese war, and Pearson's analysis of the Portuguese maritime empire. Wade catalogues specific acts of violence and coercion—attacks on Old Port (1407), Java (1407), Sri Lanka (1411), and Samudera (1415), threats against Burma (1409), and the razing of a stupa in Ayudhya—to demonstrate that the fleets functioned as gunboat diplomacy in practice. He further argues that the establishment of military depots (*guan-chang*) at Malacca and Samudera, and the Ming role in fostering the rise of the port-state of Malacca, reveal a deliberate strategy of controlling the Straits of Malacca and the trade networks linking the Middle East to East Asia (pp. 47–51).

In the article's final section, Wade engages with the historiography of colonialism, drawing on definitions by Armitage, Osterhammel, Emerson, and others to justify the label "maritime proto-colonialism" for the Ming voyages. He distinguishes this from the full territorial colonialism seen in the occupation of Đai Viet and the Yun-nan Tai polities, arguing that the maritime missions controlled nodes and networks rather than territory, and that their cessation after the Yong-le emperor's death prevented their evolution into a more formal colonial system (pp. 52–55).

### Key Findings

- The Yong-le emperor ordered the construction of 137 ocean-going ships in 1403, approximately 400 additional ships from various military units the same year, 1,180 ships from Zhe-jiang and other commissions in 1405, and 48 "treasure-ships" (*bao-chuan*) by the Ministry of Works in 1408 (pp. 45–46).
- The Ming invasion of Đai Viet in 1406 mobilized an official figure of 800,000 troops (though Whitmore estimates 215,000 as more realistic); by 1408 the Chinese had established 472 military and civilian offices in the occupied province of Jiao-zhi, and 7,600 captured tradesmen and artisans were deported to Nanjing (pp. 42–43).
- In the 1407 attack on Old Port (Sumatra), the Ming fleet reported 5,000 persons killed, 10 ships burnt, and 7 captured; the polity was subsequently reorganized as a "pacification superintendency" with a likely Ming-appointed ruler, a configuration that persisted until 1430 (p. 47).
- After 170 Chinese troops were killed in Java in 1407, the Ming demanded 60,000 *liang* of gold in compensation from the Western king, explicitly threatening military invasion "as in Annam" if the demand was not met (p. 48).
- A *Ming shi-lu* entry of 1427 references "10,000 crack troops who had formerly been sent to the Western Ocean," and Mills' analysis of a typical mission indicates that 26,800 of 27,400 personnel were rank-and-file military, sailors, and clerks—confirming the predominantly martial character of the fleets (pp. 45–46).
- A 1445 reference in the Ming reign annals records the capture of three Javanese traders sailing to Siam, suggesting that Ming policing of regional maritime trade continued for at least half a century beyond the formal end of the Zheng He voyages (p. 52).

### Conclusion

Wade's definitive takeaway is that the Zheng He voyages must be understood not as benign diplomatic missions but as a form of maritime proto-colonialism—a precursor to the later Portuguese and Dutch trade empires in which a dominant maritime power used military coercion to control port-polities, shipping lanes, and the economic networks between them. The failure of this proto-colonialism to mature into full territorial colonialism was attributable to the death of its patron, the enormous cost of the expeditions, and sustained opposition from the civil bureaucracy, but its logic and methods were fundamentally colonial in character.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the *Ming shi-lu* (Veritable Records of the Ming dynasty), the *Wu-bei-zhi* (a seventeenth-century military compendium containing fifteenth-century charts), Ma Huan's *Ying-yai sheng-lan*, and the *Dong-xi-yang kao* of 1618, supplemented by Portuguese sources including De Barros and the *Novus Orbis* of Simon Grynaeus (1555).
- Historiographically, the article challenges the dominant Chinese "popular" narrative of Zheng He as a peaceful envoy and contributes to a broader revisionist turn in Zheng He studies, while also extending the temporal boundaries of Southeast Asian colonial history beyond the European period to include Chinese imperial expansion.
