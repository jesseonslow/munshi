# The Malay keris: its origin and development

G.C. Woolley, writing in 1947, presents a comprehensive study of the Malay keris that argues for its origin as an indigenous Javanese invention rather than a borrowing from foreign sword traditions or adaptation of pre-existing weapons such as spears or fish stings. Drawing on temple sculpture, colonial-era ethnographic accounts, and museum specimens, Woolley traces the weapon's evolution from a talismanic object forged from meteoric iron in the Majapahit period through its diversification into seven regional types across the Archipelago, a process he links closely to the spread of Islam.

## Summary

Woolley's central task is to resolve competing theories about the keris's origin. He systematically dismantles the two most prominent alternatives: Williams' derivation from a primitive spear (which he finds impractical, since a "double-purpose" blade that could be quickly transferred between spear shaft and dagger hilt in combat is mechanically implausible) and Gardner's derivation from the sting of the *ikan pari* (sting ray), which he rejects on morphological grounds—the sting lacks the characteristic *ganja* widening and has serrations pointing in the wrong direction (pp. 63–65). Having excluded external introduction and adaptation, Woolley concludes the keris was an original local invention, most probably in Java, and connects its earliest form to the use of meteoric iron (*besi pamur*), whose nickel content produces the distinctive *pamur* markings on the blade (p. 66).

The article then follows the keris's development and dispersal. The earliest type, the *Keris Majapahit*, was a small talisman with blade and hilt cast as a single piece, the hilt representing a Hindu deity (probably Vishnu). Woolley dates this to the 14th century, supported by a sculptured scene at the Sukuh temple (inscribed 1361–1362) showing a workman forging a keris (p. 65). As iron became more available and the Majapahit Empire expanded and then collapsed (c. 1513–1522), the weapon spread across the Archipelago and diversified. The arrival of Islam was the critical transformative force: the deity figure on the hilt was progressively abstracted—first into a *Garuda* (bird-headed form), then into a cobra, and finally into a plain, almost abstract shape that nonetheless retained an effective grip (pp. 69–71). Woolley classifies the resulting regional types as: Bali/Lombok/Madura (retaining Hindu figural hilts), Javanese (simpler, post-Islamic), Peninsula/Northern (the *Jawa demam* type), Bugis, Sumatran (the long *keris bahari* or *panjang*), Patani (the *pekakak* or kingfisher-hilted type), and Sulu (the *keris suluk* or *sundang*, a cutting weapon) (pp. 68–75).

The article also covers the metallurgical process of *pamur* production (laminated strips of different metals forged together, then etched with acid), the system of blade measurements used to determine a keris's auspiciousness for a particular owner, the magical and talismanic beliefs surrounding the weapon, its role in royal regalia, the rules governing its wearing, and its use in execution (pp. 76–79).

### Key Findings

- The Sukuh temple in Java, dated by inscription to 1361–1362, contains a sculptured scene of a workman forging a keris with several blades hanging in his shop, providing the earliest firm visual evidence for the weapon (p. 65).
- Raffles records over 100 varieties of Javanese keris; Crawfurd gives a figure of 54 varieties, divided into 21 straight and 33 wavy (p. 69).
- The *Keris Majapahit*—the earliest type—has blade and hilt in one piece, the hilt in the form of a seated deity (Vishnu or Siva); it was too small and weak for practical use as a weapon and functioned as a talisman (pp. 66–67).
- The *Keris Suluk* (Sundang), developed in Sulu, is a cutting rather than thrusting weapon, with a heavier blade, a rounded point, and a hilt set at a slight angle with an upward crest and downward prong to prevent hand slippage; a metal loop and strip connect the blade to the hilt to prevent twisting during a blow (p. 75).
- Execution by keris involved the condemned man sitting while the executioner stood behind, placed the point on a small pad of cotton-wool behind or over the collar bone (usually on the left side), and drove the blade down to the heart; the cotton-wool was held in place to prevent a flow of blood as the blade was withdrawn (p. 79).
- The *Keris Bahari* (or *panjang*) of Sumatra is a long, narrow, rapier-like blade, sometimes with a raised central rib, developed to give extra reach against sword-armed opponents; it was also the type generally used for executions (*Keris Penyalang*) (pp. 73–74).

### Conclusion

Woolley's definitive takeaway is that the keris was not borrowed, adapted, or imitated but was an original invention of the Malay Archipelago, most likely Java, arising from the working of meteoric iron into a talismanic object that was subsequently transformed into a practical weapon and then diversified into distinct regional forms as it spread with trade, conquest, and the religious shift from Hinduism to Islam.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on specimens in the Raffles Museum, Singapore, and the Ashmolean Museum, Oxford, supplemented by illustrations from Raffles' *History of Java* and the Dutch publication *Nederlandsch Indie, Oud en Nieuw* (1936).
- Woolley's article is a synthesis that builds on and corrects the earlier work of Williams, Gardner, Winstedt, Skeat, and Crawfurd, and it represents a significant consolidation of keris scholarship at a time when the weapon was still in active use across the Malay world.
