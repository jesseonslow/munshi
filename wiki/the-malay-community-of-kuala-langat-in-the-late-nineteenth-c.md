# The Malay community of Kuala Langat in the late nineteenth century

Mahani Musa (2004) examines the economic agency of Malay women in Kedah between 1881 and 1940, arguing that they were active participants in land acquisition, wealth accumulation, and property defense rather than passive dependents confined to domestic roles. Drawing extensively on Land Office, Stamp Office, Estate Duty Office, and State Secretariat records, the article reconstructs the material lives of women across social strata—from the sultan's wives to commoner paddy planters—across the transition from Siamese suzerainty to British colonial administration.

## Summary

Musa's central argument is that Malay women in Kedah exercised sustained economic agency that predated and outlasted British colonial rule. In the pre-colonial period, the *Undang-Undang Kedah* (Ku Din Ku Meh version, 1893) contained Article 16 on *harta sepencarian*—jointly acquired marital property—drafted under the influence of Chief Kadhi Sheikh Abu Bakar, who insisted that kadhis protect women's property rights in divorce and inheritance proceedings. This legal provision, Musa notes, represented a departure from other versions of the *Adat Temenggong* and even the *Undang-Undang Melaka*, as it explicitly acknowledged women's labour contributions (p. 3). The article then traces how the British takeover in 1909, while bureaucratizing land administration and abolishing the *orang berhutang* (debt-bondage) system, inadvertently created new opportunities for women to apply for state land, register property, and litigate disputes through formal legal channels.

The bulk of the article is built on granular archival evidence. Land Office indexes from 1908 to 1940 reveal that Malay women applied for rubber and paddy land both individually and jointly, with applications ranging from one to twenty *relong* for commoners and 200 to 800 *relong* for members of the ruling house. Stamp Office and Estate Duty Office records document the estates left by women of varying wealth, from Che Manjalara's $59,649 to the modest holdings of commoners like Balkis binte Hassan ($3,950) and Embon binte Chain ($1,302). Court files and State Council petitions show women taking husbands, stepsons, and even the sultan's favourite wife to court over property claims, demonstrating a willingness to challenge authority to protect their economic interests (pp. 9–16).

The article also addresses the problem of indebtedness, showing that both commoner and royal women fell prey to chettiar moneylenders, with the State Council repeatedly intervening to manage the fallout. Census data from 1911, 1921, and 1931 reveal a declining official count of "working" Malay women, which Musa attributes to surveyors' failure to capture unpaid domestic and agricultural labour rather than an actual withdrawal from economic activity (pp. 16–17).

### Key Findings

- Che Manjalara, the sultan's favourite wife, left an estate valued at $59,649.00 in landed and moveable property, but debts of $36,595.07 reduced her heirs' inheritance to $23,053.93 (p. 11).
- Government land revenue in Kedah rose from $10,000 prior to 1905 to $36,344.00 within six months of the establishment of nine Land District Offices in 1905 (p. 4).
- The Baling Land Office received approximately 84 applications from Malay women for state land in 1928–29, 97 in 1930, and about 360 for paddy and rubber land from 1932 to 1934 (p. 7).
- In 1911, 99 per cent of 89,934 Malay women in Kedah were engaged in some form of paid work; by 1931, only 31 per cent of 139,137 surveyed women were classified as working, a decline Musa attributes to survey methodology rather than actual economic withdrawal (pp. 16–17).
- The State Council agreed in July 1912 to pay the balance of all *orang berhutang* debts in the state, found to total $5,000, effectively freeing debt-bonded individuals (p. 5).
- Tunku Aishah's debts to Karpen Chettiyar totalled $13,100.00, secured against four plots of land (73, 78, 54, and 76 *relong*), which the regent Tunku Ibrahim was compelled to settle to prevent the land falling into non-Malay hands (p. 20).

### Conclusion

Musa concludes that the archival record of Kedah demonstrates Malay women were never marginalized from economic life; they were income earners, wealth accumulators, and determined defenders of their property who operated across the full spectrum of colonial and pre-colonial institutions, from village paddy fields to the State Council chamber, even at the risk of becoming entangled with moneylenders.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on Kedah State Secretariat files (SUK Kedah), Land Office district indexes, Stamp Office and Estate Duty Office estate files, High Court records, and the 1911, 1921, and 1931 censuses of Kedah.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malay women's economic history by demonstrating that the Kedah Stamp Office records—previously identified as a neglected database by Khoo Kay Jin (1992)—provide a rich quantitative and qualitative source for tracking women's property holdings across social classes.
