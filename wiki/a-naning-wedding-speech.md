# A Naning wedding-speech

J.L. Humphreys, a Straits Settlements civil servant, published this article in 1916, presenting a verbatim transcription and English translation of a set Malay wedding speech recited in Naning (Alor Gajah, Malacca) by Ungkai Lisut, a local headman of the Mungkar tribe. The speech, first heard by Humphreys in 1908 and verified by re-recitation in 1914, serves as a compact codification of the domestic Menangkabau marriage custom as it survived in that community, preserving a ritual formula that Humphreys argues carried semi-religious significance from pre-Muhammadan times (p. 25).

## Summary

The article is fundamentally an act of ethnographic preservation. Humphreys records a fixed oral formula—131 lines of rhymed Malay prose—that a headman would recite before the bride's clan headman at the moment the bridegroom is led onto the verandah of the bride's house. The speech is structured in three movements: an opening invocation of the fourfold hierarchy of authority (the law of God, the law of the Prophet, the law of tradition, and the custom of the land); a middle section tracing the life-cycle obligations of parents toward their children, framed as a fivefold "debt" (severing the navel, paying the midwife, washing and cleansing, circumcision, and giving in wedlock); and a closing section narrating the specific marriage being consummated, from the informal advances of go-betweens through the exchange of a pledge-ring to the final fulfilment of the bond (pp. 25–26).

Humphreys situates the speech within the broader Menangkabau exogamic system, directing readers to the fuller treatment in *Rembau* (Parr and Mackray, JSBRAS vol. 56) for the Negri Sembilan variant. His own analytical contribution is modest but pointed: he notes the speech's homely precepts recall passages in Virgil's Second Georgic, drawing a parallel between the Naning peasantry and the Virgilian farmer bound to sacred custom (p. 25). The three footnotes he appends clarify specific ritual details—the naming of a child before the cord is cut, the role of go-betweens in informal courtship, and the meaning of "no string across the path" as the absence of religious or exogamic obstacle to the union (p. 33).

The evidential basis is singular: the speech itself, preserved in the memory of one speaker and confirmed by his ability to reproduce it verbatim six years later. Humphreys adds no independent archival or documentary corroboration; the value of the piece lies in capturing a performative text before it was lost to the rapid social changes of the colonial period.

### Key Findings

- The speech was first recorded in 1908 at a wedding in Naning and re-verified in 1914 in Singapore, with "hardly the alteration of a word" between the two recitations (p. 25).
- The fourfold hierarchy of authority invoked is: (1) the law of God, (2) the law of the Prophet, (3) the law of tradition, (4) the custom of the land (lines 10–15, p. 26).
- The parental "debt" to a child comprises five obligations: severing the navel, paying the midwife, washing and cleansing, circumcision (akir baligh), and giving in wedlock (lines 74–79, p. 28).
- The marriage process described involves a pledge-ring (chinchin) sent as a token: if received, the bride-price is paid and the couple wedded; if rejected, it returns (lines 98–108, pp. 30–31).
- The ritual context included "fencing, fireworks, and show of resistance below the house," with Hailam kulis performing Chinese boxing before the headman led the bridegroom up the steps (p. 25).
- The speaker, Ungkai Lisut, was local headman of the Mungkar tribe at Kelemak, Alor Gajah, and had no written record of the speech beyond his own memory (p. 25).

### Conclusion

Humphreys presents the Naning wedding-speech as a living fossil of Menangkabau domestic custom, its formulaic structure and solemn delivery marking it as a text of considerable antiquity whose semi-religious origins predate Islamisation. The article's historical value is primarily documentary: it fixes in writing a performative oral text that, by the 1910s, was already at risk of disappearing from living practice.

## Context

- Humphreys was a member of the Straits Settlements Civil Service; his access to the speech came through his administrative presence in the Alor Gajah district, and his collaboration with W.H. Mackray (F.M.S. Civil Service) reflects the standard colonial practice of civil servants producing ethnographic material for the Journal (p. 25).
- The article is a product of the early colonial ethnographic impulse to record "vanishing" indigenous customs, and its framing—emphasising the speaker's inability to reproduce the text without the author's prompting—positions the colonial observer as the necessary agent of preservation.
