# Muhammad ibni al-Marhum Yam Tuan Antah

## The Person and the Office

Muhammad ibni al-Marhum Yam Tuan Antah (1853–1933) was the Yang di-Pertuan Besar of Negri Sembilan, the hereditary paramount ruler of the confederation of Malay states on the west coast of the Malay Peninsula situated between Malacca and Selangor. Installed in 1898 after a generation of inter-state estrangement, he reigned for forty-five years until his death on 1 August 1933, making his tenure the longest in the modern history of the office [[1, pp. 9–10]](#ref-1), [[2, p. 272]](#ref-2). His full ceremonial title, as recorded in the 1933 succession documents, was Tuanku Muhammad, G.C.M.G., K.C.V.O., ibni al-Marhum Yam Tuan Antah [[2, p. 272]](#ref-2). The office he held was not merely a personal dignity but the apex of a constitutional structure that combined Malay customary law, Islamic principles, and, from 1895 onward, the framework of British protection.

## Origins of the Dynasty and the Constitution of the Nine States

The constitutional framework within which Muhammad exercised his authority derived from the ancient polity of the Negri Sembilan, or "Nine States." According to the account recorded by E.W. Birch, the British Resident to Negri Sembilan, the original settlement of the region was effected by a migration of Sakai peoples from the hills, led by four chiefs known as the *Undang yang Ampat*, or Four Lawgivers [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1). These four—representing Johol, Klang, Sungei Ujong, and Jelebu—established the foundational political units. Over time, free immigration from Menangkabau (Sumatra) introduced additional tribal groups, each governed by an elected head called a *Lembaga*, or manager of the tribal system [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1).

The need for a suzerain to adjudicate inter-state disputes led, through the mediation of the Ruler of Johor, to the invitation of a Menangkabau prince. In 1773, or thereabout, Raja Melewar was installed by the Four Lawgivers as the first Yang di-Pertuan Besar and took the title Yam Tuan Besar Sri Menanti, named for the valley in which the royal *Astana* (palace) was built [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1). The dynasty thus represented a fusion of Menangkabau royal lineage with Sakai constitutional practice.

By the late nineteenth century, the original nine states had been partially replaced: five of the original territories had been lost or alienated, and their gaps filled by Ulu Muar, Terachi, Jempol, Gunong Pasir, and Inas, with Tampin and Gemencheh subsequently added as settlements from Rembau and Johol [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1). The state of Rembau had succeeded to the position formerly held by Klang among the Four Lawgivers [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1).

## British Protection and the Restoration of 1898

The path to Muhammad's installation was shaped by the progressive extension of British protection over the component states: Sungei Ujong in April 1874, Rembau in March 1884, Jelebu in September 1886, and Sri Menanti in June 1887 [[1, pp. 8–9]](#ref-1). The agreement of August 1895 arranged the Confederation of all the states under a single British Resident as adviser to the chiefs [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1). Birch assumed the duties of Resident early in 1897 and found that, despite the formal arrangement, the constitutional machinery was in disrepair: the Yam Tuan of Sri Menanti had never been formally installed; of the Four Lawgivers, only Johol acknowledged allegiance to the paramount ruler, while the other three declared themselves independent [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1).

Birch's account, published in 1906, presents the restoration as an exercise in administrative mediation. He argued that the Malay political and social order was built upon *adat* (custom), and that the chiefs' resistance to the Yam Tuan's authority rested on a strained reading of the 1895 agreement rather than on genuine constitutional principle [[1, pp. 9–10]](#ref-1). After a series of consultations, the three recalcitrant Lawgivers agreed to draft a written instrument setting forth their allegiance while preserving their freedom from interference in internal affairs [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1).

The resulting Agreement, executed on 29 April 1898 (8 Zil-haijah 1315), was signed in seven copies by the Yang di-Pertuan, the Four Lawgivers, and the British Resident, each bearing the State seal [[1, pp. 11–12]](#ref-1). Its principal provisions were: that the Four Lawgivers returned to elect the Yang di-Pertuan in accordance with the ancient constitution; that the Yang di-Pertuan could not interfere in the customs of the country or in Muhammadan Law; that inter-state boundary disputes would be adjudicated by the Yang di-Pertuan; that the Lawgivers would celebrate the festivals of Hari Raya and Hari Raya Haji in their own states rather than at Sri Menanti; and that upon the death of the Yang di-Pertuan, the Four Lawgivers, in conjunction with their hereditary officers, would elect and install a successor from among the royal princes [[1, pp. 11–12]](#ref-1).

## The Installation Ceremony of 7 May 1898

The formal installation took place on Saturday, 7 May 1898, at the Astana in Sri Menanti. Birch recorded the event in considerable detail, noting that the concourse numbered between three and four thousand persons [[1, p. 22]](#ref-1). The ceremony lasted two and a half hours and concluded at 3 p.m. [[1, p. 22]](#ref-1).

The procedural sequence followed ancient custom as closely as time permitted. The four chief *Lembaga* of Ulu Muar were dispatched as heralds to summon the Four Lawgivers to Sri Menanti, each pair proceeding to a designated resting place en route—Terachi for the Sungei Ujong chiefs, Cheriau for Johol, Gunong Pasir for Rembau, and Kampong Bukit for Jelebu [[1, pp. 12–13]](#ref-1). The Dato' of Johol, being ill, was represented by the Dato' Baginda Tan Amas, his representative in the Council of State [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1).

On the day of the ceremony, the Lawgivers arrived at the Astana in succession, each received by a guard of honour of the Sikh Police and conducted to the royal hall by a designated British officer [[1, pp. 18–19]](#ref-1). The agreement was signed and sealed, and the Lawgivers retired to their reception halls. The Yang di-Pertuan then entered the *balai penghadapan* (royal hall) preceded by the *orang ampat Astana* and his *orang anam* carrying the weapons of State, followed by his brother the young Tungku Besar and his brother-in-law the Tungku Laksamana, with a score of Rajas completing the procession [[1, p. 19]](#ref-1).

The proclamation was made by the *Bentara Kanan* (Right Herald), who stood on one leg with the sole of his right foot resting on his left knee, holding his right ear open with his right hand and shading his eyes with his left, and declared: "Oh, gentlemen all, illustrious and humble, great and small, old and young, by order of the four Lawgivers, Tungku Muhammad, the son of the late Yam Tuan Antah, is proclaimed as Yang di-Pertuan of the Negri Sembilan" [[1, p. 20]](#ref-1). The people signified their homage by shouting three times, "Dolat Tuanku" [[1, p. 20]](#ref-1).

Each Lawgiver then made obeisance: emerging from the enclosure, squatting on the ground, proceeding along the floor seven times to the dais, where the Yang di-Pertuan gave his right hand, which the chief received in both of his and kissed thrice, before retiring with five further obeisances [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1). The Dato' Jelebu departed after his homage; the Dato' Inas, Dato' Gemencheh, and Dato' Muda Linggi made their obeisance together, followed by all the chief *Lembaga* and *Waris* of the various districts [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1). The ceremony was concluded by the voluntary obeisance of approximately twenty-five Rajas of royal blood, dressed in yellow, who moved up the hall in regular time—a scene Birch described as more impressive than anything he had previously witnessed at a Malay gathering [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1). Prayers were offered by the *Kathi* (Islamic priest) of Tampin and the assistant *Kathi* of Kuala Pilah [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1).

In his subsequent address to the *Undang* on Sunday, the Yang di-Pertuan affirmed that the constitution was now complete, comprising three tiers: the Yang di-Pertuan; the Four *Undang* (from Sungei Ujong, Jelebu, Johol, and Rembau); and the Four *Serambi Undang* (from Ulu Muar, Jempol, Terachi, and Gunong Pasir), who would represent the *Undang* in their absence and attend annually at the festival of Hari Raya [[1, p. 22]](#ref-1).

## Death, Funeral, and Succession

Tuanku Muhammad died at the Astana Besar, Sri Menanti, on 1 August 1933, after a brief illness [[2, p. 272]](#ref-2). A twenty-two-gun salute marked the death [[2, p. 272]](#ref-2). The notification protocol followed the constitutional chain: the Tunku Besar Burhanuddin summoned the four *Penghulu* of the Tanah Mengandong, who in turn dispatched *Lembaga* to inform the Four *Undang* of the death [[2, pp. 272–273]](#ref-2).

The funeral took place on 3 August 1933. A sixty-eight-gun salute was fired, corresponding to the late ruler's age in years [[2, p. 274]](#ref-2). The coffin was pentagonal in shape, constructed without iron and morticed and fastened with brass and copper screws, and contained approximately nine inches of earth from the grave along with rice and herbs [[2, p. 273]](#ref-2). The traditional ceremonial carriage, the *Maharaja di-raja*, was deemed inadequate for the weight of the coffin, which was instead transported on a specially adapted lorry [[2, p. 273]](#ref-2). The *mas manah* (funeral offering) from the Four *Undang* totalled $384, comprising four *bahara* of $24 each from each *Undang* [[2, pp. 275]](#ref-2).

The election of the successor was conducted as a conclave of the Four *Undang* in Seremban on 2 August 1933. The British Resident read Section 6 of the 1898 Agreement to confirm that the election was unanimous before the decision was communicated to the High Commissioner [[2, p. 276]](#ref-2). Tunku Abdulrahman, the late ruler's son, was proclaimed as the new Yang di-Pertuan Besar. His accession address, preserved in the archival record, explicitly affirmed the continuity of the friendship and protection of Great Britain over Negri Sembilan [[2, pp. 278–279]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The two principal archival records concerning Muhammad ibni al-Marhum Yam Tuan Antah represent distinct genres of official documentation. Birch's 1906 account, published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, is a first-person narrative by the sitting British Resident, presenting the 1898 restoration as both a procedural record and a statement of administrative philosophy [[1]](#ref-1). Birch explicitly framed the event as evidence that British protection had not undermined Malay constitutional traditions but had enabled their repair, and his discussion of *adat* and Malay conservatism reveals the intellectual assumptions of the colonial administrative class at the turn of the century [[1, pp. 9–10]](#ref-1).

The 1936 record by I.W. Blelloch and H.P. Bryson, published in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, is a contemporaneous administrative compilation by two members of the Malayan Civil Service, documenting the death, funeral, and succession of 1933 [[2]](#ref-2). Unlike Birch's reflective narrative, the Blelloch and Bryson text is a procedural record, preserving the physical arrangements, the order of the cortege, the seating plan, and the specific acts of obeisance, together with the original Malay texts of the proclamation speech and the accession address [[2, pp. 272–279]](#ref-2). The authors noted several points of procedural uncertainty, including the anomalous high position of the Dato' Shah Bandar in the order of precedence and the omission of the *anak putera* (royal children) from the ceremonial obeisance, likely due to inability to resolve their relative ranking [[2, pp. 275–276]](#ref-2).

Together, these two documents bracket the entire reign of Muhammad ibni al-Marhum Yam Tuan Antah: the first records his installation and the constitutional settlement that defined his authority; the second records his death and the orderly transfer of that authority to his son. The interval between them—forty-five years of continuous rule—remains largely undocumented in the Society's published literature, a gap that reflects the administrative rather than scholarly character of the records and the relative stability of the period they encompass.
