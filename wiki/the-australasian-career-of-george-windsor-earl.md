# The Australasian career of George Windsor Earl

Dr. R. Hanitsch's 1913 article in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (No. 65, pp. 39–48) presents four letters by Nathaniel Wallich, the Danish-born Superintendent of the Calcutta Botanic Gardens, documenting his brief but consequential visit to Singapore in 1822 and his direct role in proposing the establishment of the colony's first Botanic and Experimental Gardens. The article, prepared at the suggestion of H. N. Ridley, former Director of the Singapore Botanical Gardens, draws on copies made from originals held in the Royal Botanical Gardens, Calcutta.

## Summary

The article is structured around four primary documents that trace the arc from Wallich's arrival in Singapore to the formal proposal for a botanical institution. Wallich, who had known Stamford Raffles for several years through their shared interest in natural history, arrived in Singapore in late August 1822 under the pretext of a six-month health leave intended for China. He never went to China; instead he spent his entire leave in the settlement, which was then only in its fourth year of existence.

The first letter (19 July 1822) is Wallich's formal application for leave, citing repeated fever attacks following a severe bout of jungle fever in Nepal. The second document is a committee report dated 23 October 1822, signed by Wallich alongside Dr. James Lumsdaine and Captain Francis Salmond, assessing the southern bank of the Singapore River for the construction of European merchant warehouses. The report endorses the site as salubrious and practicable, noting the healthy condition of the Chinese settlers already occupying the low ground and the hydraulic advantages of the concave bank.

The third and most significant letter is Wallich's recommendation to Raffles for the establishment of a Botanic and Experimental Garden. Wallich argues that Singapore's climate, soil fertility, and position within the richest archipelago in the world make it ideally suited to such an institution. He pays tribute to the recently deceased Dr. William Jack, whose *Malayan Miscellanies* had documented the island's flora, and enumerates the commercial potential of indigenous timber species, spice cultivation (clove, nutmeg, pepper, gambier), and the successful trial of cotton, coffee, and tea. He proposes that the garden be sited near the European town, staffed with apprentices and gardeners from Calcutta, and self-sustaining through spice tree production.

The fourth letter, a reply to Raffles, provides specific details of the proposed site: an oblong tract of approximately 48 acres (144 Bengal biggahs) on the eastern and northern slopes of Fort Canning Hill, bounded by Selegie Road, a small rivulet (the present Stamford Road Canal), and Bukit Salegy (now Mount Emily). Wallich proposes that the broader government hill be laid out as a park under the superintendent's charge, and reports that Assistant Surgeon Montgomerie will hold temporary charge, with a monthly budget of $60 allocated for ten labourers and one overseer.

### Key Findings

- Wallich arrived in Singapore towards the end of August 1822 and departed on the *John Adam* for Bengal shortly after 21 November 1822 (pp. 39–40, 48).
- The proposed garden site comprised approximately 48 acres (144 Bengal biggahs) on the slopes of Fort Canning Hill, bounded by Selegie Road, the Stamford Road Canal, and Bukit Salegy (p. 47).
- The monthly operating budget was set at $60, to support ten labourers and one overseer (p. 48).
- The committee report on the southern river bank was signed by James Lumsdaine, N. Wallich, and Francis Salmond, dated 23 October 1822 (p. 43).
- The gardens were discontinued after 30 June 1829, leaving Singapore without botanical gardens for thirty years until the Tanglin Gardens were established in 1860 (p. 48).
- Wallich identified five distinct wild species of nutmeg in the immediate vicinity of the Cantonments alone, and noted that clove and nutmeg experiments by Lieutenant Colonel Farquhar had already proven successful (p. 45).

### Conclusion

Hanitsch's article establishes that Singapore's first botanical gardens were a direct product of Wallich's personal initiative during a brief health-related visit, and that their premature discontinuation in 1829—likely due to budget overruns beyond the modest $60 monthly allocation—created a thirty-year gap in the colony's scientific infrastructure. The article serves as a primary-source foundation for the institutional history of Singapore's botanical heritage, linking the Wallich-era gardens to the later, world-famed Tanglin establishment.

## Context

- The letters were copied from the Library of the Royal Botanical Gardens, Calcutta, by permission of Superintendent Major A. T. Gage, I.M.S., at the request of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society (p. 39).
- The article was prompted by H. N. Ridley, C.M.G., F.R.S., former Director of the Singapore Botanical Gardens, who identified the letters during a visit to Calcutta after his retirement in early 1912 (p. 39).
