# The Australasian career of George Windsor Earl

R.H.W. Reece's 1992 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* provides a comprehensive biographical survey of George Samuel Windsor Earl (1813–1865), a British trader, publicist, and colonial official whose career spanned Western Australia, the Indian Archipelago, northern Australia, and the Straits Settlements. Reece argues that Earl's life epitomises the mercantile and strategic logic by which early nineteenth-century British actors perceived northern Australia as a geographical extension of island Southeast Asia, and that his subsequent historical neglect reflects the social distance later created between White Australia and its Asian neighbours.

## Summary

Earl was born in Hampstead, London, in 1813 to a shipowner of questionable reputation. After a brief stint as a midshipman and a failed attempt at settlement in the Swan River Colony, he departed for Batavia in 1832 and immersed himself in the trading world of the archipelago, acquiring proficiency in Malay, Bugis, Bajau, and Macassarese. His 1834 voyage to the Chinese gold-mining settlement of Montrado in north-western Borneo made him the first Englishman to visit that community, and his published account of its *kongsis* system directly inspired James Brooke's historic 1838 expedition to Sarawak (pp. 44–45).

Back in London, Earl became an energetic publicist for a British settlement on the north Australian coast, publishing *The Eastern Seas* (1837) and a series of memoranda to the Colonial Office. His lobbying, combined with strategic anxieties about French interest in the region, secured his appointment as Linguist and Draughtsman on the North Australia Expedition under Captain Sir Gordon Bremer, which established the garrison settlement at Port Essington in October 1838 (pp. 45–47). Over the next six years he served as the settlement's interpreter, collector of ethnographic and linguistic data, and supplier of provisions from the eastern islands, while also advocating for Macassan and Chinese settlement and the importation of Timorese labour (pp. 48–52).

After Port Essington's abandonment in 1849, Earl settled in the Straits Settlements as a law agent and later as a colonial official, serving as magistrate and Assistant Resident Councillor in Penang and Province Wellesley. In 1864, while convalescing in Adelaide, he exercised decisive influence on the South Australian government's choice of Adam Bay rather than the Victoria River as the initial site for its new Northern Territory, an argument grounded in his own experience of malaria and his long-standing conviction that the settlement should function as an entrepot port linked to Singapore (pp. 57–59). He died at sea in August 1865, two days out of Penang, having left an estate of only 10,000 Straits dollars (p. 59).

### Key Findings

- Earl was the first Englishman to visit the Chinese gold-mining settlement of Montrado in north-western Borneo (1834), and his detailed account of its *kongsis* governance and mining methods provided the first authentic description of that system, directly prompting James Brooke's 1838 voyage to Borneo (pp. 44–45).
- His 1836 pamphlet and 1837 memoranda to the Colonial Office were instrumental in securing the British government's decision to establish Port Essington, though the Admiralty's strategic motives (countering French interest) were cloaked in the commercial language he supplied (pp. 45–47).
- In February 1864, Earl's memorandum to South Australian Chief Secretary Henry Ayers successfully argued for Adam Bay over the Victoria River as the Northern Territory's initial settlement site, and his recommendations were reflected in the official instructions to Government Resident Boyle Travers Finniss (pp. 57–58).
- Earl coined the ethnolinguistic term "Indunesia" to describe the cultural-linguistic unity of the archipelago's brown peoples, a concept that anticipated the modern term "Indonesia" and reflected his belief in Australia's future economic integration with its Southeast Asian neighbours (p. 60).
- He served as Australian agent for the Berdan Pan, a gold ore-crushing machine invented by Hiram Berdan, selling dozens of units at Bendigo and Ballarat in Victoria and attempting to market a modified version for coconut oil extraction in the East Indies (pp. 54–56).
- In a letter of 6 June 1865, written while gravely ill in Province Wellesley, Earl offered to lead a locally-recruited rescue expedition to relieve Finniss's stranded party at Escape Cliffs and proposed his own appointment as the first judge of the Northern Territory; no reply was recorded, his death having probably reached Adelaide first (p. 59).

### Conclusion

Reece's definitive assessment is that Earl was a natural enthusiast and gifted publicist whose qualities obscured his practical judgement—he overestimated populations and trade volumes, and his advocacy of Adam Bay rested more on personal prejudice against malarial climates than on first-hand knowledge. Nevertheless, Reece concludes that Earl's vision of a multi-racial, tropical-agricultural northern Australia linked commercially to "Indunesia" was genuinely prophetic: the White Australia policy discredited that vision for over a century, but by 1992 Darwin had become precisely the kind of multi-ethnic, Southeast Asia-oriented community Earl had envisaged, and "can rightly claim him as its own Raffles" (p. 60).

## Context

- The article draws on family papers deposited in the Mitchell Library, Sydney (MS A7129 and FM/1553), letters in the Royal Geographical Society archives, and South Australian Archives records, building on earlier work by Dora Howard (1933), C. Gibson-Hill (1959), F. J. Allen, P. G. Spillett, Russell Jones, and Donald Chaput (p. 41).
- Earl's omission from both the *Dictionary of National Biography* and the *Australian Dictionary of Biography* is cited as indicative of his historical neglect, though he was included in the *Northern Territory Dictionary of Biography* (1990) (pp. 41–42).
