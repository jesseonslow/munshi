# Studying Srivijaya

J.W. Cushman and A.C. Milner published "Eighteenth and Nineteenth-Century Chinese Accounts of the Malay Peninsula" in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, Vol. 52, No. 1 (235), in 1979 (pp. 1–56). The article presents English translations of three Chinese geographical texts covering the Malay peninsula—Ch'en Lun-ch'iung's *Hai-kuo wen-chien lu* (1730), Hsieh Ch'ing-kao's *Hai-lu* (c. 1842), and Wei Yuan's *Hai-kuo t'u-chih* (1842)—and argues that these neglected sources offer valuable complementary perspectives on the region's commerce, society, and political relationships during the century before British colonial rule.

## Summary

The article addresses a gap in Malayan historiography: while Chinese works have long been recognized as important sources for early Malayan history, those written after European entry into Southeast Asia have been largely overlooked by historians. Cushman and Milner translate and annotate excerpts from three distinct Chinese texts to demonstrate their value for understanding eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century conditions on the peninsula. The three sources differ markedly in character and purpose. Ch'en Lun-ch'iung, a military official from Fukien, compiled his *Hai-kuo wen-chien lu* primarily to aid coastal defence officials, drawing on his father's military experience and his own service in Taiwan. Hsieh Ch'ing-kao, a merchant from Kwangtung who travelled overseas for fourteen years (1782–1795), provides the only first-hand eyewitness account among the three, recorded by Yang Ping-nan in Macao in 1820. Wei Yuan, the eminent historian who never left China, compiled his *Hai-kuo t'u-chih* as a strategic handbook for Ch'ing officials confronting British expansion, drawing on a far wider range of sources including the other two texts and Western writings (pp. 1–4).

The translations reveal several themes of historical significance. The Chinese authors' primary interest was commercial: they catalogue local products in considerable detail, describe port charges and trading mechanics, and note the occupations of Chinese settlers. The *Hai-lu* in particular provides rare information on the internal organization of Chinese communities in Malaya, including the division of labour between Fukien and Kwangtung migrants, the role of the *chia-p'i-tan* (Kapitan) as Chinese headman, and the tax systems imposed by local rulers. The texts also illuminate the complex and shifting political relationships between the peninsular states and Siam, with the three authors offering somewhat contradictory accounts of which states were dependencies and which enjoyed greater autonomy. Wei Yuan's commentary, uniquely among the three, addresses the developing threat of British power, particularly the founding of Singapore in 1819 and its strategic implications for China's maritime borders (pp. 5–9).

The authors also use the texts to explore broader questions of Malay identity and the practice of Islam in the pre-colonial period. The broad application of the term "Melayu" across the peninsula in these eighteenth- and nineteenth-century sources contributes to the picture of a developing ethnic identity during this era. The Chinese descriptions of Malay legal customs—including ordeals by water, by hot oil, and by spear—and the absence of any reference to Shari'a law or the authority of Islamic religious leaders suggest a Malay world that differed substantially from the more formalized Islamic polity described in later nineteenth-century sources (pp. 8–9).

### Key Findings

- The *Hai-lu* records that several hundred Chinese came to Kelantan every year, with Fukien people settling in the port to trade and cultivate pepper, while Kwangtung people lived on the hilltops to mine gold; more than ten thousand Fukien and Kwangtung people had settled in Pinang to plant pepper (pp. 21–22, 31).
- Port charges in Kelantan were specified: large vessels carrying heavy goods were taxed five to six hundred foreign silver dollars, smaller vessels two to three hundred (*teng-t'ou chin*); a per-person annual tax of one silver dollar (*ya-hsieh*) was levied on residents, and a duty on goods (*ma-tzu*) was also collected (p. 21).
- Kelantan and Patani each paid yearly tribute of thirty *chin* (catties) of gold to Siam; Trengganu paid tribute to Siam, Annam, and Holland (pp. 15, 22, 24).
- The *Hai-kuo wen-chien lu* (1730) records that Johor's trade could support only three or four ocean-going junks annually, and that "not many" went to Trengganu—written just three years after the Chinese government lifted prohibitions on trade with Southeast Asia in 1729 (pp. 11, 6).
- Wei Yuan's *Hai-kuo t'u-chih* (1842) notes that after the British occupied Singapore, "several tens of thousands of Chinese and [other] foreign people" had already gathered there, and that the British established a bureau of cannon casting, shipyards, and an Anglo-Chinese college (*Ying hua shu yuan*) (pp. 41, 43).
- The *Hai-lu* describes the Pinang garrison as comprising two to three hundred Englishmen with an additional one thousand or more *hsu-po* (free coloureds), and records that annual taxes on spirit brewing, opium trading, and gambling houses in Pinang amounted to more than one hundred thousand *liang* (p. 31).

### Conclusion

Cushman and Milner demonstrate that these three Chinese texts, though static in their descriptive approach and lacking the narrative detail of European accounts, provide a fresh and complementary picture of eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century Malaya. They confirm European observations on the prominence of certain states in the China trade, document the scale and organization of Chinese settlement, and—through their very silences on Islamic legal institutions—help explain why Muslim writers like Munshi Abdullah were so critical of the state of Islam in the Malay territories in the 1830s.

## Context

- The primary sources are the *Hai-kuo wen-chien lu* (Taipei, 1958 ed.), the *Hai-lu chu* annotated edition (Shanghai, 1955), and the *Hai-kuo t'u-chih* (Yangchou, 1852; 1875 reprint), with the *Hai-lu* text consulted in an unpublished romanized version of MS. Cod. Or. 7304 at Leiden University Library prepared by Dr R.R. Roolvink.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in bringing a neglected category of Chinese sources—those composed after European arrival in Southeast Asia—into the study of Malayan history, and in demonstrating their value for understanding Chinese commercial activity, the development of Malay ethnic identity, and the pre-colonial practice of Islam.
