# State museums and their representation of the past in Malaysia

Abu Talib Ahmad (2008) evaluates how Malaysian state museums construct and project official narratives of the past, arguing that these institutions function as instruments of nation-building that selectively foreground Malay achievement while marginalizing other ethnic groups, political movements, and uncomfortable historical episodes. Drawing on site visits to museums in Kelantan, Terengganu, Kedah, Penang, Selangor, Kuala Lumpur, and Sabah, the article assesses whether these institutions have taken cognizance of advances in historical research or remain locked in a post-independence consensus narrative.

## Summary

The article's central argument is that Malaysian museums are intrinsically associated with nation-building and project what Ahmad calls "the history of the victors in the nationalist struggle," with losers marginalized or subjected to oblivion (p. 45). A structural problem underlies this selectivity: the same group of writers and historians serve both the Ministry of Education's school history texts and the Ministry of Unity, Culture, Arts and Heritage's museum narratives, creating a collusion that reinforces a single official version of the past (p. 46). This is most visible in the treatment of the Japanese Occupation, where the Kedah State Museum offers a mild, politically convenient account emphasizing Malay "awakening" while the National History Museum's narrative is designed to placate Japanese visitors, and only the Kota Bharu War Museum presents a more critical local perspective (pp. 60–66).

A recurring theme across all museums examined is the systematic exclusion of non-Malay contributions and politically inconvenient episodes. The Kedah State Museum omits the Kulim War of 1888, the role of Chinese secret societies, the chettiar moneylenders, the Sam-sam mixed-ethnic group, and the Kesatuan Melayu Muda's pre-war nationalist activities (pp. 51–52). The Malayan People's Anti-Japanese Army (MPAJA) is consistently banished from anti-Japanese resistance sections and instead placed in the Emergency narrative as villains, a treatment Ahmad contrasts unfavourably with Singapore's museums, which do not hide the negative aspects of the Occupation (pp. 62–63, 67). The Kelantan museums, by contrast, project an idealized vision of "Malayness" through folk culture, royal ceremony, and handicrafts, with the silver jubilee exhibition of Sultan Ismail Petra explicitly designed to instil "love and loyalty" toward the royal institution (pp. 56–57).

The Lembah Bujang Archaeology Museum emerges as a relative positive example, preserving the Hindu-Buddhist legacy of ancient Kedah and corroborating local chronicles through archaeological evidence. Ahmad notes that out of seven administrative capitals mentioned in the *Hikayat Merong Mahawangsa* and six in the *Al-Tarikh Salasilah Negeri Kedah*, archaeological evidence has confirmed five, lending historical credibility to indigenous sources (p. 53). Yet even here, the museum's emphasis on temple reconstruction for tourism over stratigraphic research reflects a heritage-driven rather than research-driven priority (p. 55).

### Key Findings

- The Kedah State Museum claims Kedah as the oldest sultanate in Malaysia and the first Islamic state in South-East Asia, based on an unbroken line of 28 rulers since 1136, a date drawn from the semi-official *Al-Tarikh Salasilah Negeri Kedah* (first published 1928) (p. 48).
- The Bujang Valley covers 400 sq km with 41 historical sites identified over 160 years of archaeological research; the Archaeology Museum was established in 1977 and eight ancient temples have been rebuilt as heritage sites (p. 52).
- The Kota Bharu War Museum's Death Railway section claims the project killed 16,000 people—equating to one life for every 28 yards of track—though the Malay word *maut* (death) was erroneously translated as "lethal" in the museum's English signage (p. 66).
- The Kedah State Museum's Japanese Occupation section contains only three original exhibits, with the rest consisting of framed (non-original) photographs; the entire wartime narrative comprises four paragraphs (p. 61).
- A Langkawi flag measuring 180 cm × 90 cm, bearing the first three verses of Surah Al-Fat'h (48) on a red background, is displayed as evidence that Kedah Malays regarded the 1821–41 war against Siam as *jihad*, though the museum does not explicitly state this (p. 49).
- The Kedah State Museum's list of eight individuals who achieved "a first" in the country includes all Malays: the first prime minister, the first medical practitioner to become prime minister, the first managing director of the Islamic Bank, and others (p. 51).

### Conclusion

Ahmad concludes that after fifty years of independence, a paradigm shift in the national narrative is necessary to reflect the current needs of the nation-building process, which differ fundamentally from those of the 1970s or the immediate post-Merdeka period (p. 67). Without such a shift, national history will remain frozen as museum exhibits, and the institutions themselves will fail to serve a pluralistic citizenry. The selective foregrounding of exhibits—determined by museum bureaucrats and consultants—means that neither all ethnic groups nor more recent scholarship find representation on public display.

## Context

- The study is based on direct site visits to museums in seven states and examination of their publications and brochures, supplemented by engagement with museum consultants and local historians; it does not draw on a single archival collection but rather treats the museums themselves as primary sources.
- Historiographically, the article extends the critical museum studies initiated by Virginia Matheson Hooker (2003) on the National History Museum and Nigel Worden (1987) on Melaka, applying their framework to state-level institutions for the first time in a systematic comparative manner.
