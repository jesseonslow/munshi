# Some ethnographic notes on Semelai in northern Pahang

Rodney Needham published these ethnographic notes in 1974, drawing on a single day's field contact made in May 1955 with a small group of Semelai in the upper Sat and Wau river valleys of northern Pahang. The article's central argument is that the relationship terminology recorded from these northern Semelai is Mon-Khmer in character and therefore likely archaic, in contrast to the Malay-derived kinship terms of the better-studied southern Semelai around Tasek Bera.

## Summary

Needham's notes arise from an indirect encounter: he had been sent to search for fully nomadic Negritos in the Kenyam area of the upper Tembeling river and was directed first to a group on the Sat river who would supposedly guide him. Instead he found a small Semelai camp, and the article records what he could observe and elicit in that single visit. The broader historical problem he addresses is the persistent opacity of aboriginal populations in Pahang, a difficulty already flagged by Williams-Hunt in the early 1950s, who estimated that the true number of aborigines in the state might be four times the official census figure because of the state's inaccessibility.

The core analytical contribution is linguistic. Needham presents a fourteen-term relationship vocabulary and a short dozen-word general vocabulary, and he compares the former against both the Siwang (a Senoi language) and the Malay-derived terms recorded by Hood bin Mohamad Salleh for southern Semelai. The northern terms are Mon-Khmer in structure and show a sibling/cousin distinction absent from Hood's list, leading Needham to surmise that the southern Semelai have largely adopted Malay kinship terminology while the more isolated northern group retains older forms. He is careful to flag the uncertainties: the vocabulary was elicited at random without checking denotations, and he could not work out full genealogies to verify the kinship terms.

On subsistence, Needham records that the group he met were hunters and gatherers who supplemented their diet with rice and salt obtained through trade in forest products such as cane and bezoar stones. He notes the possibility that they may simply have been at a forest-foraging phase of an annual economic cycle (as Hood describes for southern Semelai), but their physical circumstances and the testimony of local Malays suggest they were characteristically forest-dwellers.

### Key Findings

- The group Needham met comprised eleven persons (three men, two boys, two women, four girls) camped in two bamboo-and-leaf shelters near Kg. Tumpat; they belonged to a larger Semelai group of about ten families under headman Patoh in the Sepia valley, with a separate group of about ten persons under headman Nyang who had come from Ulu Kelantan (pp. 123–124).
- The fourteen-term relationship table includes *aki* (FF), *nenek* (FM), *ibe'* (F, FB, MB), *moi* (M, FZ, MZ, WM, HM), *i'e'* (eB), *adi'* (yB, yZ), *ga'u* (Z), *badak* (FBC, FZC, MBC, MZC), *nyiwak* (C), *anag* (BC, ZC), *cenu'* (CC), *kelog* (H), *uja* (W), *kanéda* (WF, HF) (p. 126).
- The only direct correspondence with Siwang terminology is *moi/moi* for "mother"; the phonetically similar Siwang *keleg* (parent-in-law) contrasts with Semelai *kelog* (husband), though Hood's southern list gives *kenlek* for "husband," complicating the comparison (p. 127).
- Structurally, the Semelai terminology distinguishes siblings from cousins (a feature absent from Hood's southern list), and equates mother-in-law with mother while keeping father-in-law as a distinct term (p. 128).
- The general vocabulary includes *us* (fire), *tare* (day), *suag* (path), *kelign* (sky), *cerehwo* (sun), *semak* (man), *tampogn* (tree), *sia* (shelter), *lawak* (stream), *telop* (mat), alongside Malay borrowings *bintang* (star) and *bulan* (moon) (p. 126).
- Burial practice: the dead were interred in shallow graves roofed with palm leaves; the body of a man described as "slightly superior" (*lebeh sedikit*) was placed in the branches of a tree (p. 124).

### Conclusion

Needham's definitive takeaway is that the northern Pahang Semelai represent a linguistically and economically more archaic variant of the Semelai population: their Mon-Khmer kinship terms, their apparent reliance on a hunting-and-gathering economy, and their greater isolation from Malay settlement collectively suggest that the southern Semelai's Malay-derived terminology and more settled rice-growing pattern represent later cultural change rather than the original condition. He publishes the notes despite their fragmentary character on three grounds: they document Semelai presence in a region where they were not previously known, they preserve a potentially archaic linguistic stratum, and they may record a subsistence mode that was once typical of Semelai life.

## Context

- The article draws on a single unpublished field report (Needham 1955) and a brief personal encounter; no archival collections were consulted. Its historiographical contribution lies in extending the known geographic range of the Semelai into northern Pahang and in providing a comparative data point for the Mon-Khmer versus Malay question in Semelai kinship terminology.
- The notes were prompted by collaboration with Hood bin Mohadis Salleh's B.Litt. thesis (Oxford, 1974), which documented the southern Semelai and whose relationship terms (Malay-derived) made the contrast with Needham's northern data visible.
