# Trengganu Malay

D. F. A. Hervey published this annotated translation of Valentyn's seventeenth-century Dutch account of Malacca in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1884, presenting a comprehensive portrait of the settlement under early VOC administration alongside a reconstructed Malay royal chronology extending from the legendary founding at Singapura to the Portuguese conquest of 1511. The work serves as both a colonial administrative record and a vehicle for Malay historiographical tradition, drawing on rare Arabic-script Malay manuscripts to trace the genealogy of the Malacca sultans.

## Summary

The text opens with a physical and economic description of Malacca as Valentyn observed it in the late 1660s: a crescent-shaped town built around a hilltop fortress of Portuguese origin, flanked by two islets (Ilha das Naos and Ilha das Pedras) where carracks anchored in four or five fathoms of water, and drained by the Chrysorant river on the north-west. Valentyn records the town's dramatic demographic decline from a former population of 12,000 to merely 200–300 families of mixed Dutch, Portuguese, and Malay origin, while noting that the surrounding country was barren and dangerous, abounding in tigers, elephants, and alligators. The administrative apparatus of the VOC government is laid out in detail, with a Governor holding supreme authority, assisted by a Supercargo, Attorney-General, Paymaster, and a Shahbandar collecting customs duties, all operating through three distinct councils for police, law, and ecclesiastical affairs.

The ethnographic section offers Valentyn's characterisation of the Malays as the most cunning, ingenious, and polite people in the East, and provides a comparative linguistic analysis of Bahasa Malayu as a lingua franca stretching from Persia to the Philippines. He distinguishes between the pure common language and the "adulterated" court register interlarded with Arabic, noting that the latter was the preserve of princes, courtiers, and priests. Valentyn's account of Malay dress, temperament, and the relative status of women is presented with the frankness typical of Dutch colonial observers of the period.

The historical narrative, drawn from three rare Malay manuscripts in Arabic script—*Tadjoo Esslatina* (Makota Segalla Radja), *Misa Gomitar*, and *Kitab Hantoowa* (Soolalet Issalathina)—traces the Malay people from their origin on the river Malayoo in Sumatra, through the founding of Singapura by Siri Toori Bowana (c. 1160), the migration to the site of Malacca in 1253 under Siri Iskander Shah, the conversion to Islam under Sultan Mohammed Shah (r. 1276–1333), and the apogee of the sultanate under Mansor Shah (r. 1447–1477), who married into the Madjapahit and Chinese imperial families. The account culminates in the Portuguese arrival: Jacob Sequeira's diplomatic mission of 1509, the failed assassination plot, and Afonso de Albuquerque's conquest of 1511, after which Sultan Mahmud Shah fled to found Johor, completing 252 years of Malay rule at Malacca.

### Key Findings

- The gross revenue of Malacca in 1669, consisting of a 10 per cent import duty, 3 per cent export duty, and minor taxes, amounted to 74,958.18 guilders; 116 Javanese vessels arrived that year in addition to Danish, Portuguese, and Moorish shipping (p. 4).
- The fortress on the central hill, of Portuguese construction, was described as approximately the size of Delfshaven, with two gates, one side on the hill and the other washed by the sea; it housed the Governor, company officers, and garrison (p. 3).
- Valentyn's Malay royal chronology places the founding of Singapura at c. 1160 under Siri Toori Bowana (reign of 48 years, d. 1208), the founding of Malacca in 1253 by Siri Iskander Shah, and the first Muslim ruler, Sultan Mohammed Shah, ascending in 1276 after 115 years and 6 months of heathen kingship (pp. 63–66).
- Sultan Mansor Shah's reign of 73 years (1447–1477) saw Malacca ranked first among the great cities of the region (ahead of Pasi and Haru), a marriage alliance with the Emperor of China, and the acquisition of Indragiri through marriage to a Madjapahit princess (pp. 67–68).
- Albuquerque's fleet arrived at Malacca on 1 August 1511; the town possessed approximately 9,000 brass guns at the time of the conquest, and the royal fifth of the booty seized amounted to 200,000 ducats (pp. 73–74).
- The Perak factory was broken up after the murder of all its personnel in 1651; the Kedah factory was quietly abandoned in December 1656, and the Oudjong Salang factory was broken up in 1660 (pp. 55, 57).

### Conclusion

Hervey's translation preserves Valentyn's account as a rare composite document that fuses Dutch colonial administrative observation with Malay royal historiography, offering a window into how seventeenth-century European and indigenous knowledge systems were brought into contact. The work's enduring value lies in its transmission of the *Kitab Hantoowa* genealogical tradition and its detailed record of VOC governance at Malacca during a period when the settlement was in pronounced economic and demographic decline.

## Context

- The translation was executed by Mr. Muller, Government Translator, and edited by D. F. A. Hervey, who noted that a partial translation had previously appeared in Logan's *Journal* (Vol. IV) but was incomplete and unreliable (p. 2).
- The primary archival sources underpinning the historical narrative are three Malay manuscripts in Arabic script (*Tadjoo Esslatina*, *Misa Gomitar*, *Kitab Hantoowa*), which Valentyn claimed to have obtained personally and which he described as available in "very few libraries" (p. 62).
