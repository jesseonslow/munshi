# An abortive plan for an Anglo-Chinese College in Singapore

H.E. Wilson's 1972 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the abortive plan by the Malayan Mission of the Methodist Episcopal Church to establish an Anglo-Chinese College in Singapore in the years immediately following the First World War. Set against the backdrop of the Straits Settlements colonial administration's chronic neglect of post-secondary education, the article argues that the project's failure was driven less by legitimate educational policy considerations than by the prejudices of senior colonial officials—particularly Governor Sir Laurence Guillemard and Acting Director of Education R. O. Winstedt—who perceived an American-based missionary institution as a threat to British prestige and colonial authority.

## Summary

Wilson traces the origins of the proposed college to 1914, when Reverend J.S. Nagle, principal of the Anglo-Chinese School, was commissioned by Bishop William F. Oldham to plan a post-secondary institution. The project gained momentum through the formation of an Anglo-Chinese College Council that included prominent Chinese community leaders, and by 1920 had attracted substantial financial contributions. The colonial government, however, had its own competing scheme: the Raffles College, conceived as a centenary memorial for Singapore's founding in 1919 and designed to serve as a feeder toward a future university. Wilson's central argument is that the government's opposition to the Mission's college was not grounded in rational educational planning but in the personal and political anxieties of its senior officials.

The article draws heavily on confidential despatches between the Governor and the Colonial Office, the *Malayan Bulletin of Political Intelligence*, and the published *Prospectus* of the Anglo-Chinese College to reconstruct the decision-making process. Wilson demonstrates that Winstedt's report to Guillemard—recommending that "no further encouragement be extended to the project"—rested on unsubstantiated claims about American bias in the Mission's teaching and a speculative fear that an American college would become the "highest educational institution in a British Colony in the Far East." The *Bulletin of Political Intelligence* compounded these suspicions with vague, hearsay-based assertions about subversive American missionary activity, including a reference to the content of silent films shown in local cinemas. Wilson shows that the Colonial Office in London, while somewhat less alarmist than its local counterpart, ultimately concurred with the Governor's advice, citing the threat of "throat-cutting competition" between the two proposed colleges.

The article also situates the episode within the broader pattern of colonial educational policy, which was dominated by parsimony and a reluctance to commit public funds to higher education. The Queen's Scholarships had been discontinued in 1912, and the government's own Raffles College was delayed by bureaucratic inertia, an unnecessary architectural competition, and Guillemard's decision to divert the donated "Mount Rosie" site for use as a military residence. Wilson uses these parallel failures to underscore that the Anglo-Chinese College's demise was not an isolated incident but a symptom of a system in which educational policy served the interests of the colonial administration rather than the population it governed.

### Key Findings

- By 1920, the Anglo-Chinese College had raised $400,000 in contributions from the local community, demonstrating significant public demand for post-secondary education (p. 100).
- The government's formal position, communicated by Colonial Secretary F.S. James on 18 October 1917, was that it would offer "no objection" to the Mission opening a college but could not support the project or sanction the granting of degrees (pp. 98–99).
- Guillemard diverted the 40-acre "Mount Rosie" site originally donated for Raffles College, securing a Legislative Council vote of $29,500 for repairs to the existing building and $2,500 for furniture, to convert it into a residence for the General Officer Commanding (pp. 101–102).
- The *Malayan Bulletin of Political Intelligence* (October 1922) recorded that American missionaries' teaching was "not thought to be doing them much good from a British point of view," and that their activities were "coming to be regarded with some suspicion" (p. 107).
- The Queen's Scholarships, discontinued in 1912, were reintroduced in 1923 as a "sop to public pressure" after Raffles College still had no immediate prospect of opening (p. 108).
- Winstedt was appointed Principal of Raffles College in 1921, the same year he authored the report recommending against the Mission's college, raising the possibility of a personal stake in eliminating a rival institution (p. 108).

### Conclusion

Wilson's definitive takeaway is that in the colonial context of the Straits Settlements, educational policy was determined not by public need or professional educational judgment but by "sectional pressures, prejudice or personal preference" of senior officials, with the Colonial Office in London generally deferring to the "man on the spot" and the powerless protests of Unofficial Legislative Council members offering no effective check (p. 109).

## Context

- The article draws primarily on confidential despatches held in the Colonial Office records (CO 273 series), the *Malayan Bulletin of Political Intelligence*, Legislative Council proceedings, and Methodist Mission conference minutes, providing a rare glimpse into the internal reasoning of colonial administrators.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the growing body of work on colonial education policy in Malaya by demonstrating how geopolitical anxieties about American influence and fears of student politicization (informed by events in China, India, and Burma) shaped decisions that had direct consequences for the educational opportunities available to the colony's Chinese and Indian communities.
