# A note on ‘the Rawa’

[A.C. Milner](./ac-milner.md) (1978) examines the Rawa, a group of Sumatran migrants who played a significant but under-studied role in mid-nineteenth-century Peninsular [Malay](./malay.md) politics. The article argues that the Rawa were identifiable as Orang Rau from the region behind Tapanuli in West [Sumatra](./sumatra.md), displaced by the Padri wars and Dutch conquest, and that their presence in the [Malay](./malay.md) States was shaped by an association with Islamic reformist movements that made them both valuable mercenaries and deeply distrusted outsiders.

## Summary

The article addresses a gap in the historiography of nineteenth-century [Malaya](./malaya.md) by identifying a group that appears frequently in colonial and Malay records yet has received little scholarly attention. Milner traces the Rawa's appearances across multiple Malay states from the late 1820s through the 1870s, showing their involvement in trade disputes, civil wars, and territorial conflicts. The core problem is one of identification: the term "Rawa" was used loosely in [European](./european.md) sources, and Milner works to narrow it to a specific ethnic and geographic origin.

Milner's central argument is that the Rawa were Orang Rau from the district behind Tapanuli in West [Sumatra](./sumatra.md), displaced eastward by the Padri wars and the Dutch conquest of the Rau region in 1835. He supports this identification through multiple converging lines of evidence: the linguistic equivalence of "Rao" and "Rawa" in Minangkabau, the association of the Rawa with gold mining (a known Rau occupation), and the appearance of the term "Rinchi"—referring to Tuanku Nan Rintjih, a radical Padri leader—in East Sumatran records describing the same people. The Padri connection is crucial to understanding why the Rawa were perceived as a threat in Malay territories: they carried with them a reformist Islamic ethos fundamentally at odds with traditional Malay political structures.

The article draws on Malay-language chronicles, British colonial correspondence from the [Straits Settlements](./straits-settlements.md) and [Malacca](./melaka.md), Dutch archival records from East Sumatra, and [European](./european.md) reference works. Milner also incorporates a postscript reporting on a 1978 field visit to the Rau district in West Sumatra, where local inhabitants confirmed the emigration of Raus to the Malay Peninsula and their alternative designation as "orang Rintjeh."

### Key Findings

- The Rawa first appear in Peninsular records in the late 1820s, accompanying the chief of Linggi in an invasion of [Rembau](./rembau.md) (p. 143).
- By 1859, approximately one hundred Rawa arrived in [Malacca](./melaka.md) from [Pahang](./pahang.md), claiming to be on their way to Mecca, with rumours they intended to gather at Langat in [Selangor](./selangor.md) (p. 144).
- During the Pahang civil war (1857–63), four hundred Rawa gold miners in the Raub district joined the people of Jelai in revolt against Bendahara Mutahir (p. 144).
- In late 1873, five hundred Rawa under Sutan Bangka Ulu surrendered to Pahang forces; despite a promise of safety, many were slaughtered and their women and children taken captive (p. 145).
- The Orang Rau numbered more than 25,000 in the mid-nineteenth century and were engaged in gold mining, consistent with the Rawa's "principal occupation" in [Pahang](./pahang.md) (pp. 145–146).
- The Dutch conquered the Rau region in 1835, after which numerous inhabitants fled to East Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula (p. 147).

### Conclusion

Milner concludes that the Rawa's reputation as "evil mercenaries" with "no reverence" for Malay authority was well-founded in the context of their Padri origins. Their reformist Islamic commitments made them inherently unreliable as subjects of traditional Malay sultanates, and the massacre of 1873 represented a logical—if extreme—response by a victorious ruler seeking to eliminate a permanently destabilising element. Milner speculates that the Rawa's devout Muslim religiosity, still respected in [Malaya](./malaya.md) today, may have contributed to the development of the Kaum Muda movement among Malay Muslims.

## Context

- Primary sources include British colonial correspondence (Straits Settlements Factory Records, CPD files), the *Hikayat Pahang*, Dutch Kabinets-archief records from Riau, and the *Encyclopaedie van Nederlandsch-Indie*.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in identifying a previously diffuse term with a specific ethnic group and geographic origin, and in linking Peninsular Malay political [history](./history.md) to the broader dynamics of the Padri wars and Dutch colonial expansion in West Sumatra.
