# The Malayan communist struggle for survival

Laurent Metzger's 1996 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* reconstructs the brief but heavily publicised episode of Joseph Ducroux, a French Comintern agent arrested in Singapore in June 1931. Drawing on British colonial records, French police files, Ducroux's own retrospective diary, and two Malay-language novels of the period, Metzger argues that Ducroux was a minor and poorly prepared operative whose significance was deliberately inflated by the British authorities to serve their own institutional and inter-colonial rivalries.

## Summary

Metzger's central intervention is corrective: earlier accounts, particularly those by Rene Onraet (Director of Criminal Intelligence in Singapore) and Gene Hanrahan, had portrayed Ducroux as a mastermind who, upon arrest, confessed the entire structure of the Malayan Communist Party and thereby caused its temporary destruction. Metzger demonstrates that this narrative was a fabrication. Ducroux, aged 26, had no prior experience in Southeast Asia, did not speak Chinese or Malay, and had been in Singapore for barely five weeks. The task of reorganising the MCP had in fact been assigned to Ho Chi Minh during the latter's visit to Singapore in April 1930. The arrests of Chinese communists such as Fu Tai-keng and Wong Muk-han were the product of a broader, pre-existing intelligence operation rather than a consequence of Ducroux's capture.

The article's second major contribution is its documentation of the extraordinary degree of intelligence collaboration among colonial powers in the region. British, French, and Dutch authorities shared information on communist agents across their respective territories. The British had warned Singapore months before Ducroux's arrival that "Lefranc" might be Ducroux; the French Sureté in Saigon provided (initially erroneous) identification details; and the Dutch in Batavia received reports on the Singapore arrests. This network of cooperation was part of a wider tightening of the net in early 1931, catalysed by the arrest of Gu Shunzhang, head of the Chinese Secret Services, in April 1931, which led to the capture of over 800 communists and the near-capture of Zhou Enlai.

Metzger also explores the literary afterlife of the Ducroux affair in Malay-language fiction. Shamsuddin Saleh's novels *Siasat yang Dasyat* (1936) and *Pemimpin Sulit* (1937) feature a character "Lipan" (a light disguise of "Lefranc") described as "Director of the Communist Organization for all Malaya and Indonesia." These texts provide tantalising but inconclusive evidence of Ducroux's contacts with Malay and Indonesian communists, including a possible liaison agent named Marie Arnaud (or "Roslina" in the novel) and a connection to the Comintern agent Hilaire Noulens in Shanghai.

### Key Findings

- Ducroux arrived in Singapore on 27 April 1931 and was arrested on 1 June 1931, after only approximately five weeks in the territory; he was sentenced to 18 months' rigorous imprisonment on 22 June 1931 (pp. 4, 8).
- Fifteen persons were arrested simultaneously with Ducroux; Wong Muk-han, arguably more significant, received a sentence of life imprisonment that passed unreported in the local press, while Ducroux's 18-month sentence was headlined in the *Straits Times* (pp. 8, 11).
- The MCP reorganisation mandate had been assigned to Ho Chi Minh at a secret meeting in Singapore in April 1930, not to Ducroux; Metzger refutes Onraet's and Hanrahan's claims to the contrary (pp. 6, 12).
- The arrest of Gu Shunzhang on 8 April 1931 led to the capture of more than 800 communists; the French police subsequently arrested all members of the Indochinese Communist Party Praesidium in Saigon on 5 May 1931 (p. 6).
- Ducroux was banished for life from Singapore and deported to Saigon aboard the *Paul Doumer* in late 1932; he was immediately re-arrested by French authorities, tried for using a false identity, sentenced to one year, and finally released on 3 November 1933 (pp. 12–14).
- A figure of $50,000 in gold was cited at trial as funds Ducroux was allegedly carrying for the MCP; Ducroux consistently denied ever handling such a sum (p. 10).

### Conclusion

Metzger concludes that Ducroux was neither a mastermind nor a particularly important Comintern agent, and that the extensive publicity surrounding his case was driven by the British authorities' desire to showcase a European communist caught on their territory and to assert their intelligence superiority over rival colonial powers. The Singapore episode represented, in Ducroux's own words, "a real turnabout" in his political life, ending his international career and confining him to a diminished role in France.

## Context

- The study draws on British colonial records (CO 273 series), French police files (Archives d'Outre-Mer, Aix-en-Provence), Ducroux's unpublished diary, and Malay-language primary texts, representing a genuinely multi-archival approach that corrects the one-sided British narrative dominant in earlier literature.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Comintern operations in Southeast Asia by demonstrating the extent of inter-colonial intelligence cooperation in the early 1930s and by using Malay literary sources as supplementary historical evidence for communist networks in Singapore.
