# Raden Saleh, Dipanagara and the painting of the capture of Dipanagara at Magelang (28 March 1830

P.B.R. Carey's 1982 article on Raden Saleh and the painting of Dipanagara's capture cannot be summarised from the text provided, as the primary source document actually contained is the *Hikayat Seri Rama*, an oral Malay romance transcribed by William E. Maxwell and romanised by R. O. Winstedt, published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, No. 55 (1910). The following summary addresses the text as it appears in the document.

## Summary

The *Hikayat Seri Rama* is a verbatim transcription of an oral Malay romance performed by Mir Hassan, a professional storyteller (*penglipur lara*) from Kampar in the south-east of Perak. Maxwell, then Assistant Resident in Perak, first encountered Mir Hassan's talents in the 1880s and eventually had the full narrative taken down by native writers, with Maxwell himself reviewing the text and obtaining explanations of obscure passages. The work was completed in 1886 and offered to the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society. R. O. Winstedt provided the romanisation.

The narrative is a Malay adaptation of the Ramayana, filtered through the conventions of village oral performance. Raja Seri Rama of Tanjong Bunga, childless after three years of marriage, summons his elder brother Raja Laksamana, who performs a divination ritual identical in structure to that of a contemporary Malay *pawang*. The couple is transformed into monkeys by bathing in a forbidden lake; their son is born as a monkey, Kera Kechil Imam Tergangga, who is banished to the forest and eventually becomes sovereign of the monkey tribes at Padang Anta-Beranta. Maharaja Duwana (Ravana) abducts the Princess; the monkey-son rescues her, destroys Duwana's city, and defeats him in battle. After marrying Princess Renek Jintan of Bandar Tahwil, the monkey-son is transformed back into a human (Mambang Bongsu) when his wife's old nurse burns his monkey-skin, and he ascends the throne of Bandar Tahwil.

Maxwell's prefatory sketch is itself a significant ethnographic document, illustrating the formulaic structures of Malay oral performance: the piling of synonymous similes, the metrical passages in a rhythm he compares to Dayak blank verse, the anachronistic inclusion of cannon and muskets, and the way the narrator localises the Hindu epic into a Malay-Islamic idiom (e.g., the sun greeting Shah Numan with "Peace be on you," answered "And on you be peace").

### Key Findings

- The transcription was completed in 1886 in Singapore, after Mir Hassan was induced to visit Maxwell there through the influence of Raja Idris of Perak (later Sultan Idris, G.C.M.G.) (p. 2).
- The divination ritual performed by Raja Laksamana requires a candle one cubit long and as thick as a man's forearm, with a wick the thickness of a man's thumb, plus parched rice, yellow rice, sacred water, and eight cubits of white cloth (p. 4).
- The monkey-son's appetite is quantified: he consumes forty-four baskets of tender shoots in a single night, prompting Shah Numan to expel him (p. 13; Malay text p. 54).
- In the final battle, Maharaja Duwana's host is reduced from seven thousand men to seven hundred (p. 21).
- The monkey-son possesses 1,199 sinews, all of which he engages when bracing on Seri Rama's shoulders before being hurled by Raja Laksamana (p. 19; Malay text p. 81).
- The transformation back to human form occurs when the monkey-skin is burned: the smoke becomes white cloth and the ashes are found to be gold (p. 23).

### Conclusion

Maxwell presents the *Hikayat Seri Rama* as a specimen of genuine Malay oral legendary romance, valued precisely because it was transmitted by a non-literate performer rather than compiled by a scribe borrowing from written foreign sources. The text demonstrates the profound and continuing influence of the Ramayana and Mahabharata across the Farther East while simultaneously showing how deeply these Indian epics have been indigenised through Malay narrative conventions, Islamic moral framing, and the performative aesthetics of the village *penglipur lara*.

## Context

- The primary source is a single oral performance transcribed in 1886; no archival collections beyond Maxwell's own notes and the native writers' transcripts are referenced.
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in preserving a verbatim record of a living oral tradition at a moment when, as Maxwell notes, "professional story-telling" was being eroded by printed books and newspapers, making this one of the earliest such transcriptions from the Malay Peninsula.
