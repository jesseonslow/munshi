# More on Bencoolen

R.J. Wilkinson (C.M.G.), writing in 1941, uses William Marsden's autobiographical memoir of his Bencoolen service (1771–1779) as a lens to trace the broader trajectory of the British East India Company's West Sumatran settlement from its mid-eighteenth-century relative prosperity through its cession to the Dutch in 1825. The overarching argument is that Bencoolen's failure was structural—rooted in the Directors' reductive treatment of the settlement as a mere pepper-buying operation rather than a genuine administrative or strategic enterprise.

## Summary

The article's primary source is Marsden's memoir, which Wilkinson uses to reconstruct the social and administrative life of Bencoolen in the 1770s. Marsden's rapid promotion through the Secretariat, his Malay language studies, and his daily interaction with the native *pangeran* council illustrate a settlement that was more socially integrated and institutionally developed than the earlier Collet-era account had suggested. Wilkinson contrasts this with the brutal punitive practices current elsewhere in the archipelago—Dutch impalement and quartering, Javanese tiger-throwing—to argue that Bencoolen's record with local populations was comparatively benign, a point corroborated by the protest of the native chiefs at the 1825 handover.

The article then broadens to trace the settlement's economic and political decline. Wilkinson argues that the East India Company's fundamental error was treating governance as an instrument of trade rather than the reverse: artificially low pepper prices depressed cultivation, compulsion drove growers to abandon plantations, and the resulting losses made the settlement a chronic financial burden. Raffles' tenure (1818–1824) represented a brief attempt to reform this model through free ports, treaties with interior chiefs, and the opening of new trade routes at Samangka Bay, but his vision of a British Sumatran empire was never supported by London, and his departure for Singapore in 1819 effectively ended any serious developmental programme at Bencoolen.

The article closes with a comparative assessment of Dutch and British colonial policy in Indonesia. Wilkinson contends that the Dutch, after the abolition of their Company, developed a more systematic approach to civil service training, agricultural development, and peasant welfare, while the British left the Straits Settlements to private enterprise in a way that benefited foreign capital more than local populations. The "men on the spot" at Bencoolen, he concludes, were less to blame than the Directors in London, who treated them as mere pepper-buying agents and provided no administrative or developmental framework.

### Key Findings

- The loss on Bencoolen between 1778 and 1783 amounted to £37,589, a figure Wilkinson cites as evidence of the Directors' mismanagement and a probable factor in the Company's willingness to shift resources to Penang (p. 104, 107).
- The annual cost of Bencoolen and its many dependencies was estimated by Campbell at £100,000, while the total pepper export found by the Dutch in 1825 was only 2,539 cwt. (2,131 pikuls), representing an export-duty revenue of merely $1,500 per year (p. 113).
- Following the breaking of the Banda–Amboyna spice monopoly, 24,820 nutmeg cuttings and 15,958 clove cuttings were taken from the Moluccas to Penang in 1800–1801, establishing a spice industry that flourished there for some considerable time (p. 109).
- Bencoolen was formally ceded to the Dutch by the convention of 17 March 1824 (in exchange for Malacca) and surrendered on 6 April 1825 by British Resident J. Prince to Dutch Commissioners Col. de Stuers and B.C. Verploegh (pp. 113–114).
- The Rejang Lebong gold mine, within easy reach of Bencoolen, yielded a 100 per cent dividend in 1908 and an output of £353,750 in 1911—a resource the British never exploited (p. 118).
- In 1807, Bencoolen Resident Thomas Parr and writer Charles Murray were murdered by "a band of Malay assassins," an event Wilkinson attributes to political resistance against attempts to make the settlement pay its way through taxation (p. 107).

### Conclusion

Wilkinson's definitive historical takeaway is that Bencoolen's inglorious end was not the product of local incompetence but of a structural failure at the centre: the East India Company's Directors reduced a strategically positioned and resource-rich territory to a pepper-trading post, provided no coherent administrative vision, and ultimately abandoned the settlement when it ceased to be marginally profitable. The Dutch, by contrast, after absorbing Bencoolen, eventually built a more sustainable colonial system grounded in trained civil servants and directed agricultural development—though Wilkinson insists that much of the intellectual groundwork for that system was laid by British officers like Marsden and Raffles, whose contributions were overlooked by the very Company that employed them.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on William Marsden's autobiographical memoir, Raffles' memoir (compiled by his widow), Maclaine Campbell's *Java*, and E.B. Kielstra's *Opstellen over Sumatra's West Kust*, supplemented by Dr. de Kat Angelino's *Colonial Policy* for the Dutch administrative reforms.
- Historiographically, the piece sits within the MBRAS tradition of recovering the administrative history of the Straits Settlements and their precursors, and it engages the broader comparative-colonial debate by juxtaposing British "free trade" governance with the Dutch post-Company model of directed development.
