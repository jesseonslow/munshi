# List of the Odonata taken on an expedition to Mt. Batu Lawi together with descriptions of supposed new species

F. F. Laidlaw's contribution to the 1912 *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 63, pp. 92–99) is a taxonomic list of Odonata collected during the 1911 Moulton expedition to Mount Batu Lawi in the upper Limbang–Madihit region of Sarawak, accompanied by descriptions of species considered new to science. The paper forms one of several specialist appendices to the main expedition narrative by J. C. Moulton and colleagues, which documents the first successful European ascent of the mountain's lower slopes.

## Summary

The expedition, led by Sarawak Government official J. C. Moulton, departed Kuching on 2 May 1911 and reached the base of Batu Lawi on 29 May, after a 40-day journey from the Limbang Government station to Claudetown on the Baram River. The route followed the Limbang River upstream to the Kuala Madihit, then the Madihit to its headwaters, and finally an overland traverse across the Turan–Derian range to the mountain itself. The return journey took a different course, descending via the Seridan, Mago, Tutau, and Baram rivers—a circuit that had never before been completed by a European (pp. 1–57).

The narrative is as much an ethnographic and geographical account as a travelogue. Moulton records detailed observations on the Tabuns, Kalabits, Muruts, Sea-Dayaks, Land-Dayaks, Penans, Kayans, and other groups encountered along the route, including their burial customs (jar burial in limestone caves such as Libong Seriou), omen systems, house architecture, linguistic relationships, and the slave practices still observed among up-river Kalabits. The historical section situates the expedition within a sparse tradition of European penetration into this region: Sir Spenser St. John's 1858 journey up the Limbang, Ricketts' visits in 1888 and 1895, Douglas' 1908 expedition via the Baram, and Skrine's 1911 trip, none of which had reached Batu Lawi itself (pp. 1–4).

The mountain proved as formidable as its reputation suggested. The base of Batu Lawi sits at approximately 3,500 ft above sea level, and the mountain rises a further 2,700 ft, with the higher peak presenting a sheer limestone face of some 600 ft that the party was unable to climb. Persistent cloud and rain obscured the summit for most of the time the party spent on the mountain, and the dense humidity at altitude produced a remarkable paucity of animal and insect life (pp. 39–41). The collections, though small in volume given the hurried nature of the expedition, yielded a surprising number of new and rare species across multiple taxonomic groups, underscoring the previously unexplored character of the region's fauna.

### Key Findings

- The base of Batu Lawi stands at approximately 3,500 ft above sea level; the mountain rises a further ~2,700 ft, with the higher peak presenting a sheer limestone column of roughly 600 ft that resisted all climbing attempts (pp. 42–43).
- The total journey from Limbang to Claudetown occupied 40 days, of which 31 were spent in actual travelling; the party slept two consecutive nights in the same place on only five occasions (p. 56).
- Between Penribut's house (2,100 ft) on the Madihit and Batu Lawi, the party was 13 days without encountering any native habitation; a similar 5-day gap occurred on the return between the Mago and Apoh rivers (p. 56).
- Tama Kuling's feast at the Kuala Madihit involved 35 great jars of *tuach* (fermented rice liquor), each holding approximately 23 gallons, totalling some 800 gallons (p. 22).
- The expedition's highest recorded temperature reading at altitude was 62° F (at 2 p.m. on Mt. Derian, 25 May), while the lowest was 62° F at 6:30 a.m. on 2 June at the Rapaw camp (pp. 35, 42).
- Two old skulls measured at Penribut's house yielded cephalic indices of 68.9 and 77.9, both classified as ovoid in shape (p. 31).

### Conclusion

Moulton's definitive takeaway is that the Batu Lawi region represents one of the last truly unexplored tracts of Borneo, both geographically and biologically. The expedition confirmed that the mountain's legendary inaccessibility was well founded—its sheer upper peak remains unclimbed—but demonstrated that the surrounding highlands, though sparsely populated and logistically challenging, are rich in previously undocumented species and cultural practices. The author explicitly frames the publication as a stimulus for more experienced observers to publish their own detailed records of native life, arguing that the cumulative value of such "trifling observations" far exceeds the accounts of hurried globe-trotters (p. 56).

## Context

- The primary archival basis is Moulton's own field diary, supplemented by St. John's 1858 map and narrative, entries in the *Sarawak Gazette*, and Douglas' 1909 and 1912 publications; no major institutional archive collection is cited.
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in providing the first reliable topographical and ethnographic account of the upper Madihit and the immediate vicinity of Batu Lawi, filling a gap left by St. John's 1858 journey, which passed within two days' walk of the mountain without reaching it.
