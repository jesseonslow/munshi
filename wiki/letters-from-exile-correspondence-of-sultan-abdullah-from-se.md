# Letters from exile: correspondence of Sultan Abdullah from Seychelles and Mauritius

Cheah Boon Kheng (1991) examines the correspondence of Sultan Abdullah of Perak, the most prominent Malay political detainee of early British rule in Malaya, written from exile in the Seychelles and Mauritius between 1877 and 1891. The article argues that this long-forgotten body of letters—preserved in a parliamentary volume and the Colonial Office series C.O. 273—provides the basis for a fundamental revision of the established narrative of Sultan Abdullah's complicity in the murder of British Resident J.W.W. Birch in 1875.

## Summary

The article presents and analyses the correspondence of Sultan Abdullah of Perak, who was deposed and exiled to the Seychelles in 1877 on charges of complicity in the murder of J.W.W. Birch, the first British Resident in Perak. Cheah draws on the parliamentary volume *Straits Settlements (Ex-Sultan Abdullah of Perak)* and the Colonial Office series C.O. 273 to trace the Sultan's sustained legal and political campaign for justice over fourteen years of detention. The correspondence reveals three distinct phases of his struggle: first, an attempt to obtain a fresh investigation and court trial; second, a bid for restoration to the throne; and third, a plea for freedom through return to the Malay States as "Ex-Sultan Abdullah."

A central argument is that the guilt of Sultan Abdullah has never been satisfactorily proven. The Straits Settlements Executive Council, chaired by Governor Jervois, concluded that as a sovereign ruler he could not be tried under existing law, and therefore recommended deportation on a political rather than judicial basis. The Sultan was never afforded the opportunity to confront his accusers or to test the evidence in a court of law. Cheah notes that both British colonial historians and post-independence Malaysian nationalist historians have uncritically accepted the British government's version of events—the former portraying him as a conspirator, the latter as an anti-colonial patriot—without either engaging with his own written protests of innocence.

The letters, written in English by lawyers retained on the Sultan's behalf, reveal a man who maintained his innocence throughout, who cultivated European cultural values during his detention, and who won the sympathy of British officials in the Seychelles and Mauritius as well as several Members of Parliament. The article also situates the case within the broader context of British colonial governance, noting that indefinite detention without trial established in this case set a precedent that persisted through the Malayan Emergency and into the Internal Security Act. Cheah suggests that a full reconstruction of the events surrounding Birch's murder, using the parliamentary papers and the Sultan's correspondence, would likely produce a version that fits neither the British colonial nor the Malaysian nationalist narrative, and that Sultan Abdullah's role was more complex—possibly that of a man caught between rival factions of Perak chiefs rather than a straightforward conspirator or collaborator.

### Key Findings

- The Straits Settlements Executive Council's decision to deport Sultan Abdullah was carried by a majority of only one vote; the Auditor General, Treasurer, and Attorney General had argued he could safely be allowed to live in Singapore on a monthly allowance (p. 42, n. 31).
- Sultan Abdullah's first appeal to Queen Victoria, dated 13 August 1880, was rejected by the Earl of Kimberley on 18 January 1881; his second letter of 26 October 1881, pressing for a public trial and restoration to the throne, was also turned down (pp. 43–46).
- A petition signed by 475 persons of all classes of the Seychelles community was sent to Queen Victoria in May 1887 on the occasion of her Golden Jubilee, testifying to the Sultan's exemplary conduct; it was ignored (p. 50).
- In 1889, Lord Knutsford proposed transferring the detained chiefs to Sarawak under Rajah Charles Brooke; Sultan Abdullah and the Laksamana and Shahbandar declined the offer, preferring to remain in the Seychelles, while the Mantri of Larut accepted (subject to a relaxation of the condition prohibiting correspondence with the Malay Peninsula) and sailed for Sarawak in June 1890 (pp. 50–52).
- Sultan Abdullah was finally permitted to leave the Seychelles in 1893, visited London in June 1894 accompanied by M.P. J. Henniker-Heaton, and was granted the title "Ex-Sultan of Perak"; he was subsequently allowed to reside in Singapore and, in 1903, to return to Perak, where he died in Kuala Kangsar in 1922 (pp. 55–56).
- The national anthem of Malaysia, "Negaraku," originated during Sultan Abdullah's exile in the Seychelles, having been heard there played by a French band and later adopted as the Perak state anthem (p. 35, n. 8).

### Conclusion

Cheah's definitive takeaway is that Sultan Abdullah's case represents the earliest instance of indefinite political detention without trial under British rule in Malaya, a practice that set a precedent continuing through the Emergency Regulations of 1948–1960 and the Internal Security Act. The correspondence demonstrates that the Sultan's detention was a political decision rather than the outcome of a judicial process, and that his repeated, well-argued protests of innocence—supported by sympathetic British officials and Members of Parliament—were systematically ignored by the Colonial Office. The article calls for a revision of the historical record, arguing that neither the British colonial account of a guilty conspirator nor the Malaysian nationalist account of an anti-colonial hero adequately captures the complexity of a man who was, in Cheah's assessment, primarily fighting for his own position within the Perak chiefly conflict.

## Context

- Primary archival sources: the parliamentary volume *Straits Settlements (Ex-Sultan Abdullah of Perak)* (H.M.S.O., London, 29 July 1891), the Colonial Office series C.O. 273 at The National Archives, London, and the Perak Inquiry Papers at the National Archives, Malaysia.
- Historiographical contribution: The article challenges the silence in both British colonial and post-independence Malaysian historiography regarding Sultan Abdullah's exile, and identifies the parliamentary papers as a previously underutilized source that enables a revision of the established narrative of the 1874–1877 events in Perak.
