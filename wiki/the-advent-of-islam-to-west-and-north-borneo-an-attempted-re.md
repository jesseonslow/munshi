# The advent of Islam to west and north Borneo: an attempted reconstruction of some possible sequences

Tom Harrisson (1972) attempts to reconstruct the timing of Islam's arrival in West and North Borneo (Sarawak, Brunei, Sabah), arguing that the conventional dating of c. 1500 A.D. for the Brunei Sultanate is too late and that three independent indigenous sources converge on a significantly earlier process of Islamisation beginning in the 14th century.

## Summary

The article addresses a historiographical gap: the absence of detailed documentation for West and North Borneo comparable to that available for the Malay peninsula or much of Indonesia. Harrisson's core argument is that the widely accepted c. 1500 A.D. date for Brunei's conversion rests on an over-reliance on formal historical sources, and that indigenous evidence — however imperfect — points to a process that was well under way a century or more earlier. He examines three source types: a Jawi-language manuscript from the Idahan people at Madai on Sabah's north-east coast, which records a genealogy anchored to a first Muslim convert named Abdullah; the Brunei royal chronicles, which he cross-references with Pigafetta's 1521 account of Sultan Bolkiah and applies a 25-year generational model to work backward; and Iban *tusut* genealogies, which he tests against the same framework to trace a figure named Abang Musa — described as having "traded to Brunei" — into the late 14th century.

The methodological approach is deliberately tentative. Harrisson acknowledges the limitations of each source: the Idahan text is a later copy with possible transcription errors; the Brunei chronicles mix folklore with history; and the Iban genealogies carry increasing myth-content the further back they reach. Yet he argues that the convergence of all three on a similar timeframe is too significant to dismiss, and that the established date reflects a conservative bias in the absence of inscriptions or other hard documentary evidence in Borneo.

He also situates Brunei within the broader maritime context: by 1490, Muslim states existed in Sulu and Maguindanao to the north and east, and in Sumatra well before 1300 A.D., meaning Borneo was effectively encircled by Islamic polities before the conventional Brunei date. The Chinese trade presence, including the possibility of a link between Admiral Cheng Ho's fleet and the figure of Ong Sum Ping in Brunei chronicles, further complicates any single-direction model of Islamic transmission from the west.

### Key Findings

- The Idahan manuscript from Madai, Sabah, records a first Muslim convert "Abdullah" dated to 1408 A.D., with 10 generations of pre-Islamic ancestors preceding him (p. 12).
- Applying a 25-year generational model to the Brunei chronicles and Pigafetta's 1521 account of Sultan Bolkiah (the fifth Muslim sultan), Harrisson calculates the first Muslim sultan's accession at c. 1400–1430 A.D. (p. 14).
- The Iban *tusut* genealogy tracing 24 generations from a living schoolboy (1968) back to Abang Musa yields a date of approximately 1380 A.D. for his "trading to Brunei" (pp. 17–18).
- Of fifteen radio-carbon dates established for the Kota Batu site in Brunei, twelve fall before 1400 A.D., indicating the ancient capital was fully established well before the conventional Islamisation date (p. 15).
- Harrisson's provisional summary: c. 1325–1350 possible first strong Islamic impacts in West Borneo; c. 1380 first significant major Muslim impacts on Brunei; c. 1400–1430 sultanate and court converted extensively; 1500 large-scale establishment but with pagan elements remaining (p. 15).
- A postscript notes the 1303 A.D. date for Islam's official adoption in West Malaysia (Trengganu inscription, re-interpreted by Al-Attas), reinforcing the argument that rigid attention to formal sources has led to delayed dating (p. 20).

### Conclusion

Harrisson's definitive takeaway is that the advent of Islam in West and North Borneo was a gradual, multi-directional process beginning in the 14th century rather than a single event around 1500 A.D., and that the conventional dating reflects an over-reliance on formal historical sources in the absence of Bornean inscriptions. He calls for a more "boldly commonsensical" approach to indigenous evidence, arguing that the convergence of three independent source types on a similar timeframe constitutes a meaningful signal even if none can be taken as final.

## Context

- **Primary sources explored:** Idahan Jawi manuscript (Madai, Sabah); Brunei royal *silasilah* chronicles; Iban *tusut* genealogies; radio-carbon dates from Kota Batu; Pigafetta's 1521 narrative.
- **Historiographical contribution:** Challenges the D.G.E. Hall (1964) consensus of c. 1500 A.D. for Brunei's Islamisation by triangulating three independent indigenous source types, and argues for the methodological legitimacy of genealogical folklore in Bornean historiography where formal documentation is absent.
