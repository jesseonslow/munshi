# Penang’s historic city centre before the repeal of the Rent (Control) Act

Mai Lin Tjoa-Bonatz's 2000 article examines the socio-spatial fabric of Penang's historic city centre in the immediate aftermath of the Rent Control Act's repeal, arguing that the dense, multi-ethnic shophouse neighbourhoods of Georgetown represent a sustainable urban model threatened by market-driven redevelopment. Drawing on a 1996 household survey of 50 shophouse dwellings in Penang Street and King Street alongside colonial census data and building regulations, the study traces how immigrant settlement patterns, housing typologies, and collective memory have produced a distinctive urban identity that persists into the late twentieth century.

## Summary

The article is structured around two interlocking concerns: the historical formation of Penang's inner-city neighbourhoods and the contemporary threat posed by the 2000 repeal of rent control. Tjoa-Bonatz begins by situating Penang within the typology of European colonial harbour cities, where spatial segregation along racial lines was both a planning principle and an organic outcome of immigrant labour flows. Chinese and Indian migrants, drawn by the rubber and tin industries in the second half of the nineteenth century, established ethnically bounded neighbourhoods whose internal logic was reinforced by dialect-group networking, clan institutions, and occupation-specific street specialisation. The informal Chinese street names—'Chinamen's Street,' 'Macao Street,' 'wood workmen street'—encode a mental map that reveals how residents organised their urban microspace along lines of collective identity rather than administrative boundaries.

The housing stock analysis demonstrates a clear morphological transition from the courtyard-type shophouse to the rowhouse-type terrace house, driven by public health legislation (Straits Settlements Ordinance No. VIII of 1896) and later building codes (1916) that imposed minimum standards for ventilation, drainage, and street width. This architectural shift was not merely regulatory but reflected broader economic processes: the densification of the centre by low-income workers, the outward migration of the middle classes, and the increasing separation of commercial and residential functions within the shophouse form. By 1901, the western area around Campbell Street had reached 97 per cent occupation density, and the oldest grid was at its carrying limit.

The household survey findings reveal a community that, despite being predominantly tenant-based, exhibits strong attachment to its neighbourhood. Residents identified business opportunity as the primary reason for living in the area but also valued intangible qualities—long-term familiarity, ethnic and religious institutions, and the vibrancy of street life centred on clan houses and hawker stalls. The demographic profile is ageing: the average household head was 56 years old, and the proportion of residents over 65 was more than double the urban average. A separate survey of young people confirmed that while they appreciated the heritage value of the historic centre, they preferred modern housing on the urban fringes, raising questions about the neighbourhood's long-term sustainability.

The final section addresses the political economy of the Rent Control Act's repeal. Introduced in 1948 to address housing shortages, the Act had frozen rents for over five decades, resulting in widespread building deterioration but also preserving the social composition of the inner city. The 2000 repeal triggered immediate rent increases of 50 to 1,000 per cent and eviction notices, with only 2,830 of 60,000 affected tenants registering for alternative housing. Tjoa-Bonatz critiques the proposed conservation guidelines as promoting 'facadeism'—allowing infill buildings up to five storeys or unlimited height in some areas—rather than genuine adaptive reuse of the shophouse morphology.

### Key Findings

- The 1901 census recorded 74 per cent Chinese in the commercial district rising to approximately 90 per cent in the harbour area; the 1991 census showed 66.6 per cent Chinese in urban Penang, closely matched by the 66 per cent Chinese in the survey sample (p. 55, 61).
- Between 1901 and 1910, housing space in the city centre reached its limits: the western area around Campbell Street recorded 97 per cent occupation density versus 94 per cent in the older city centre (p. 60).
- The Rent Control Act, introduced in 1948, covered 12,543 units in Penang according to a 1992/93 Universitas Sains Malaysia study; 66 per cent of rent-controlled colonial buildings in Peninsular Malaysia were located in Georgetown (pp. 65–66).
- Following the 2000 repeal, rents were announced to increase by 50 to 1,000 per cent; only 2,830 of 60,000 affected tenants registered for alternative housing in 1997 (p. 66).
- The average household size in the survey sample was 4.8, with extended families of 15–18 people common; the average age of household heads was 56, compared to 47.8 for the urban census (p. 64).
- Only 15 per cent of Penang's urban area was covered by the conservation zone gazetted in 1987, and a 1991 newspaper headline reported that six historic buildings were destroyed every week (p. 66).

### Conclusion

Tjoa-Bonatz's definitive argument is that Penang's historic centre possesses the structural conditions for sustainable urban development—mixed land use, flexible shophouse morphology, dense social networks, and a shared heritage identity that transcends ethnic boundaries—but that these conditions will be destroyed if conservation policy is reduced to superficial facade control and market forces are allowed to operate unchecked. The repeal of rent control, without adequate tenure protection or a coherent adaptive-reuse strategy, represents a critical juncture at which the neighbourhood's social fabric and built heritage face simultaneous erosion.

## Context

- The household survey was conducted in 1996 by the University of Darmstadt and Universitas Sains Malaysia, Penang, covering 50 households in mixed-use shophouses along Penang Street and King Street (p. 53, n. 1).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malaysian urban history by providing one of the first micro-level socio-demographic analyses of a historic inner-city neighbourhood, filling a gap Tjoa-Bonatz identifies as the absence of a comprehensive urban history of Penang (p. 53).
