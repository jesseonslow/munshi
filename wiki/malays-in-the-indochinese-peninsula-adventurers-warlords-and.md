# Malays in the Indochinese Peninsula: Adventurers, Warlords and Ministers

## Abstract

This paper features the military and political fortunes of a man of Malay origin and called Tuen Phaow/Toan Phu/Tuan Pha. Vietnamese, Cam and Khmer historical sources record his activity in the Indochinese Peninsula from 1786 to 1820. After crowning himself king in the region south of today’s Central Vietnam highlands, he fought the Vietnamese (1796–97) and then fled, only to reappear a decade later at the Khmer royal court. Promoted to the highest position, he was then used by the Vietnamese imperial court to further their influence over Cambodian politics. His spectacular ascension abruptly ended in 1820 when he was put to death. Celebrated by some as a hero, reviled as a traitor or loathed as a ruthless brute by others, this complex and unique character exemplifies patterns of mobility and political opportunism in mainland Southeast Asian precolonial politics.

## Summary

This study examines the political and military trajectory of Tuen Phaow, a man of Malay origin active in the Indochinese Peninsula between 1786 and 1820. Attested in Vietnamese, Cam, and Khmer sources, Tuen Phaow (also known as Toan Phu, Đong Phu, or Tuan Pha) exemplifies the complex mobility and political opportunism characteristic of precolonial Southeast Asia (p. 1). His career began in the highlands of Southern Vietnam, where he seized a power vacuum to proclaim himself king, utilizing Malay kingship traditions and Islamic legitimacy to rally diverse ethnic groups including Cam, Churu, and Raglai (p. 3–4). Following a failed uprising against the Nguyen dynasty forces in 1796–97, he vanished from the highlands only to reappear a decade later at the court of King Ang Chan in Cambodia (p. 13–14).

In Cambodia, Tuen Phaow leveraged his connections to serve Vietnamese imperial interests, rising to the position of first minister or regent by 1819 (p. 16). He facilitated Vietnamese expansion, including the construction of the Canal of Vinh Te, and acted as a proxy to suppress anti-Vietnamese factions within the Khmer court (p. 17). However, his tenure ended abruptly in 1820 following the suppression of a revolt led by the monk Ke, where he was accused of excessive brutality and immorality (p. 18). Despite his execution, historical accounts remain fragmented, portraying him variously as a hero, a traitor, or a ruthless brute depending on the source (p. 19).

### Key Findings

*   **Origins and Identity:** Tuen Phaow is identified as Malay through the use of the title 'Tuan' and references to 'Makah' (potentially Kelantan or Aceh), though his specific country of origin remains unconfirmed in primary sources (p. 2).
*   **Highland Kingship (1796–97):** He established military outposts in the Bien Hoa region, crowned himself with the title *bhoradhik* ('Greater Lord'), and constructed a palace to assert royal legitimacy among highland populations (p. 4).
*   **Religious Legitimacy:** Tuen Phaow rooted his authority in Islam, referencing 'Ali (Po Athur Ali) and enforcing practices such as Ramadan fasting to distinguish his orthodox faith from local unorthodox traditions (p. 9–10).
*   **Cambodian Ascension:** Supported by the Vietnamese imperial court and pro-Vietnamese Khmer elites, he was appointed Minister of Judicial Affairs and later the first minister (Chavea Ta-la-ha) in 1819 (p. 16).
*   **Instrument of Vietnamese Policy:** He actively promoted Vietnamese strategic interests, including the supervision of the Canal of Vinh Te project which linked the Mekong Delta to the Gulf of Siam (p. 17).
*   **Downfall and Execution:** Following the Ke revolt of 1820, he was arrested and executed by order of King Ang Chan, accused of using troops against the people and corrupting officials (p. 18).

### Conclusion

The divergent historical narratives surrounding Tuen Phaow illustrate the multifaceted nature of precolonial political identity, where a single individual could be simultaneously revered as a hero by Cam poets, reviled as a traitor by Vietnamese officials, and condemned as a brute by Khmer chroniclers (p. 19). Ultimately, his life serves as a definitive case study of the fluid alliances and dependency networks that defined mainland Southeast Asian politics during the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, highlighting how non-royal figures could ascend to supreme power through strategic opportunism and foreign patronage (p. 1).

## Context

*   **Archival Diversity:** The reconstruction of Tuen Phaow’s life relies on the triangulation of official Vietnamese records (*Veritable Records of Đai Nam*) against vernacular Cam narratives (*ariya*) and Khmer Buddhist chronicles (*Sastra Voat Kroch*).
*   **Colonial Role:** His career coincides with the establishment of the Vietnamese Protectorate over Cambodia (1812), where he functioned as a key intermediary in the imperial court's efforts to bypass traditional Khmer royal lines (p. 16).
