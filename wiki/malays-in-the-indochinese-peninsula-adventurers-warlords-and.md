# Malays in the Indochinese Peninsula: Adventurers, Warlords and Ministers

## Abstract

There has been extensive trade between Borneo and the outside world for at least 1000 years but there are considerable problems in establishing the precise locations of the major polities that were involved in this trade. One of them, Tanjungpura (with spelling variations), appears in Chinese, Javanese, Malay, and Portuguese records until the end of the 16th century CE and then disappears. The popular view is that it lay on the Pawan river and that Sukadana is its successor, but some maps show Tanjungpura in northerly locations in Borneo or in the south. This issue now deserves attention, particularly because a recently published oral history mentions more than one Tanjungpura in Borneo. This article reviews references to Tanjungpura in early records, to address the question in the title, and wider occurrences of the name in the Indonesian archipelago.

## Summary

The article tackles one of the most persistent locational puzzles in Bornean historiography: the identity and position of Tanjungpura, a polity that appears in Chinese, Javanese, Malay, and Portuguese records spanning roughly the 13th to 16th centuries before vanishing from the documentary record. The conventional view, inherited from older Dutch scholarship, places Tanjungpura on the Pawan river in present-day West Kalimantan with Sukadana as its successor. Smith challenges this by systematically reviewing early Chinese texts, Majapahit dynastic histories, Portuguese navigational accounts, and 16th-century European maps, arguing that the weight of 16th-century evidence points instead to a powerful polity centred in the Barito basin of southern Borneo, which later gave way to Banjarmasin as the dominant trading port.

A complicating factor is the appearance of a name—Taiapura or similar—north of the equator on several 16th-century maps, alongside the familiar southern Tanjungpura. Smith examines whether this represents a genuine second polity or merely cartographic confusion arising from incomplete information. He also engages with a recently published oral history from the Santubong region of Sarawak, which references multiple polities called Tanjong Pura in different locations and time periods, including one near Ketapang and another in the vicinity of Sambas. The oral history raises the possibility of an early northern Tanjungpura that was destroyed by the early 16th century, but Smith is cautious about its reliability, noting that it appears to borrow and modify material from other dynastic sources to fill chronological gaps.

The article ultimately resists a single definitive answer. Smith proposes that the name Tanjungpura may have been applied to more than one polity in Borneo at different times, and that later adoption of the name by unrelated polities—such as the displaced Sukadana sultans who assumed the title "Matan and Tanjung Pura" in the 19th century—has compounded the confusion. The plurality of place names across the archipelago (multiple Sukadanas, multiple Tanjungpuras in Java and Sumatra) makes this scenario plausible.

### Key Findings

- Tome Pires (c. 1515) states that Tanjompura was approximately 70 Portuguese leagues (about 350 km) from Java and that Lawei was a further four days' sailing (about 900 km) beyond it, placing Tanjungpura firmly in southern Borneo and ruling out proximity to the Pawan river (pp. 7–8).
- The anonymous Wolfenbüttel map (c. 1540) shows "Tajopura" on the northern coast of Borneo south of Tanjung Datu, while maps by Mercator (1569) and Ortelius (1570) show both a southern "Tamiampura" near the Barito delta and a northern "Taiapura" in the northwest corner of the island (pp. 8–11).
- By the 1580s, Portuguese commercial summaries note that Borneo was no longer being visited because of warfare; Eredia (1597–1600) and Pedro Teixeira (1600) mention Banjarmasin and Sukadana but not Tanjungpura, confirming the name had disappeared from active use by the end of the 16th century (pp. 12–13).
- The Santubong oral history references Tanjong Pura in at least three distinct locations: north of the Sambas river mouth (6th–9th centuries), near Ketapang (10th century), and near Sukadana (late 15th–early 16th century), with the polity renamed "Bakulapura" in the mid-14th century (pp. 14–16).
- Archaeological evidence at Benua Lama (Negeri Baru) on the Pawan river includes 12th–13th century Chinese ceramics and 15th-century tombstones with Arabic and Javanese inscriptions, while Kuta Bataguh in the Kapuas Murung tidal area shows a fortified Ngaju settlement dated between the 8th and 18th centuries (pp. 13–14).
- The displaced Sukadana sultans assumed the title "Matan and Tanjung Pura" only after moving to the village of Tanjung Pura on the Pawan river around 1870, not in any earlier documented period (p. 13).

### Conclusion

Smith concludes that the question posed in the title cannot be answered with certainty. The evidence supports at least three areas in Borneo—southern, western, and northwestern—associated with a past Tanjungpura, which may have been separate polities or successive centres of a single entity. The name's later adoption by unrelated rulers and the parallel uncertainties surrounding "Boni" suggest a pattern of name-transfer and rivalry that has obscured the historical record. Further investigation of the Santubong traditions and comparative analysis of dynastic accounts from Sukadana, Sambas, and Mempawah would be needed to resolve the remaining ambiguities.

## Context

- The article draws on Portuguese cartographic sources held at the Herzog Augustus Bibliothek (Wolfenbüttel), the Folger Shakespeare Library, and published editions of Linschoten's *Itinerario*, alongside Chinese texts in pinyin and Wade-Giles transliteration and the Santubong oral history as published by McLaughlin and colleagues.
- Historiographically, the article challenges the long-standing West Kalimantan claim that Sukadana was the direct successor to a Pawan-based Tanjungpura, and contributes to the broader debate on the location of "Boni" in 14th-century Chinese sources by engaging with the recent work of Johannes Kurz.
