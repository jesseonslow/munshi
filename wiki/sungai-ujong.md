# Sungai Ujong

R.J. Wilkinson, a senior British colonial administrator (C.M.G.), published this study in 1921 in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (No. 83, pp. 123–141). It is a constitutional and genealogical history of Sungai Ujong, the premier state of Negri Sembilan, tracing its political institutions from aboriginal origins through the Johor and Minangkabau periods to the British protectorate. Wilkinson's overarching thesis is that Sungai Ujong's peculiar constitutional anomalies—its dual rulership, its late adoption of matriarchal *adat*, and its hybrid of Bugis, Malay, and Minangkabau elements—stem from a prolonged period of patriarchal *adat temenggong* that was only partially overwritten by Sumatran custom, producing a political system unlike any other in the Negri Sembilan.

## Summary

Wilkinson approaches Sungai Ujong as a problem of political anthropology: how did a state that was originally governed under patriarchal law come to incorporate matriarchal *adat perpateh* without fully submitting to it, and what institutional compromises resulted? He begins by dismissing the popular claim that Negri Sembilan chiefs govern by descent from aboriginal Batins through the female line, arguing instead that in the early days of Sungai Ujong descent was traced through the male line and that the *adat perpateh* was not introduced until the days of Engku Sabun, "hardly a century ago" (p. 126). The genealogies he reconstructs—drawn from tradition, seal inscriptions, and the *Malay Annals*—reveal marriages that would be triply incestuous under Minangkabau custom, confirming that the later matriarchal framework was a retroactive imposition on an older patriarchal reality (p. 126).

The article's central analytical move is to identify the title of Dato' Klana as a Bugis dignity rather than a Malay one. Wilkinson notes that the first Bugis Yamtuan Muda of Riau bore the title Klana Jaya Putra, and that local assertion equates the rank of the Dato' Klana with that of a Raja Muda, making it probable that the old *Penghulu Manteri* received his elevated title from the Bugis chiefs of Riau during or after their invasion of the territory (p. 128). This reclassification reframes the entire political history: the Klana was not a native Malay chief who happened to acquire a fancy title, but a vassal of the Bugis state of Riau who inherited its hierarchical vocabulary.

The second major thread is the rise of the Dato' Bandar to a position of co-equality with the Klana. Wilkinson traces this from the office of Shahbandar (conferred by Sultan Abdul-Jalil III in 1715) through its transformation into a joint rulership, arguing that it was the commercial wealth of the port that elevated the Bandar above the rank of a simple *lembaga* (pp. 133–134). The resulting dual sovereignty—expressed in the local legal maxim that one cannot have two swords in one yard—created chronic instability that only British intervention resolved (p. 131). Wilkinson's treatment of the subordinate titles (the *lembaga tiang balai*, the *waris* families, the court officers) is similarly driven by the question of how a state that never fully adopted Minangkabau custom managed to produce a coherent, if anomalous, constitutional order.

### Key Findings

- The title of Dato' Klana is identified as a Bugis dignity (Klana Jaya Putra, Yamtuan Muda of Riau), not a Malay one; the local assertion that the Klana's rank equals that of a Raja Muda supports the hypothesis that it was conferred by the Bugis chiefs of Riau (p. 128).
- The Bugis invasion of Sungai Ujong is dated to the period between 1725 and 1770 A.D.; the Linggi settlement is dated to between 1775 and 1790, with the specific year 1783 given for the arrival of the Riau colonists who would not accept Rembau's matriarchal *adat* (pp. 127, 137).
- The office of Dato' Bandar dates from 1715 A.D., conferred by Sultan Abdul-Jalil III of Johor; the Dato' of Jelebu received his seal from Abdul-Jalil V c. 1759 (pp. 127, 133).
- Dato' Klana Kawal's reign is bracketed by the death of Yamtuan Lenggang in 1824 (first war) and Raja Radin's arbitration in 1849 (last war); his death is unrecorded (p. 130).
- The British Residential system was introduced into Sungai Ujong in 1874 under Dato' Klana Saiyid Aman (Abdu'r-Rahman), an Arab who supported British intervention against the lawless Dato' Bandar Tunggal (p. 130).
- The *adat perpateh* (Minangkabau matriarchal law) was not introduced into Sungai Ujong until the days of Engku Sabun, "hardly a century ago" from 1921, and even then did not obtain the full power it possessed in Rembau (pp. 126, 136).

### Conclusion

Wilkinson's definitive takeaway is that Sungai Ujong constitutes a unique constitutional hybrid within the Negri Sembilan: a state that was originally patriarchal, that acquired its premier title from Bugis rather than Malay sources, that only partially and belatedly adopted matriarchal custom, and that consequently developed a dual-rulership system (Klana and Bandar) with no parallel elsewhere in the group. The British protectorate, by fixing equal allowances and dividing revenues equally, froze this anomalous arrangement in place rather than resolving it, preserving for the modern era a political structure that is neither fully Minangkabau nor fully Malay nor fully Bugis, but a product of all three in unequal measure.

## Context

- Wilkinson held the rank of C.M.G. and was a senior British official in the Straits Settlements or Federated Malay States; the article reflects the administrative ethnography typical of colonial residencies, written to inform British governance of a state whose internal constitution was poorly understood by the Residency.
- The study draws on seal inscriptions, the *Malay Annals* (1612), local genealogical tradition, and the author's own administrative experience; its historiographical contribution lies in identifying the Bugis origin of the Klana title and in demonstrating that the *adat perpateh* was a late and incomplete imposition on a fundamentally patriarchal political culture.
