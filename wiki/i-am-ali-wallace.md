# ‘I am Ali Wallace‘: The Malay Assistant of Alfred Russel Wallace

## Abstract

The early literature on Malay animism and magic includes a passing reference to a concept called *maya*. This reference is hardly noticeable in the literature, and when Kirk Endicott wrote his *Malay Magic*, he omitted the word altogether. In this article the author uses ethnographic material from the Malayan-speaking Orang Sakai of Riau to examine the concept of *maya* (image) as it relates to a ‘lifeless soul’ inherent in material objects, giving physical objects vitality of form, appearance and use.

## Summary

This article by Nathan Porath restores to the literature on Malayan animist thought a concept—*maya* (rendered *mayo* in Sakai pronunciation)—that appeared only once in the early colonial ethnographic record before being quietly dropped by later scholars. Drawing on eighteen months of participant-observation fieldwork with the Orang Sakai of mainland Riau (Sumatra), Porath argues that *mayo* is not a peripheral or redundant term but a concept of central importance to the Sakai pan-soul-substance ontology. It designates the refined *Semanget*-substance that gives inanimate physical objects their form, appearance, and use without conferring life-full consciousness, thereby distinguishing the inanimate world from the animate world of embodied *semanget* (pp. 3–10).

The article traces the term's disappearance from the scholarly record: it appears in a single bracketed reference in Annandale and Robinson's 1903 account of Patani Malay boat and house construction, is quoted once by Winstedt, and is then omitted entirely by Kirk Endicott in his 1970 *An Analysis of Malay Magic*, who subsumed it under *semangat* (pp. 6–7). Porath contends that this omission obscured a critical analytical distinction. Using Sakai ethnographic data—shamanic healing performances (*dikei*), celebratory all-night rituals (*kelonkap*), and the practice of *ilmu batin* (inner-power-knowledge)—he demonstrates how *mayo* operates across multiple domains: as the image inherent in physical objects, as the de-physicalized goods of the spirit dimension, as the filmy images seen in dreams and altered states of awareness, and as the medium through which medicinal and magical efficacy is transferred (pp. 10–19).

The article also engages the broader historiography of Malayan animism, situating the *semanget*/*mayo* distinction within the Tylorian animism framework and its late-Victorian pan-psychist underpinnings. Porath argues that the Sakai system is best understood not as a static pan-soul-substance model but as an interactive, relational pan-psychism in which conscious first-person experience of altered awareness generates the ontological categories (pp. 19–21).

### Key Findings

- The term *maya/mayo* appears only once in Annandale and Robinson (1903: 81, 100) and once in Winstedt (1993: 18); Endicott (1970: 51) omits it entirely, replacing it with *semangat* (pp. 6–7).
- The Sakai distinguish *bahan kasa'* (crude materiality) from *bahan 'alus* (refined materiality); *mayo* is of refined *Semanget* substance but is not life-full *semanget*—it provides form (*upo*) and use (*guno*) without consciousness (pp. 10–13).
- In *kelonkap* ritual, palm-leaf model offerings (boats, yachts, cars, palaces) are made for their *mayo*, not their physical form; the model shimmers yellow at the ritual's start and wilts over three days as the *mayo* departs, after which the shaman hands the *mayo* to his spirit-familiar in a brief private trance (pp. 16–18).
- Photographs are understood as capturing a splinter of a person's embodied *semanget* materialized as *mayo*; medicine men use photographs of unseen individuals to locate and approach their *semanget* in dreams, a practice that explains why Sakai individuals resist having their photograph taken (p. 16).
- In the 'healing the house' (*bu'ubet 'uma') scenario during *kelonkap*, the shaman slams his staff into the four cross-beams and mimes hammering in *paku bulano* (Dutchman nails) to strengthen the *mayo* of the house, symbolically welding the community and anchoring it in the cosmic order (pp. 18–19).
- The Sakai *kemantat* (shaman) does not experience bodily possession by spirits (unlike Malay spirit-mediums); rather, the shaman's *semanget* travels (*jalat*) into the spirit dimension and sees *mayo* images through the *mato batin* (inner eye) (pp. 20–21).

### Conclusion

Porath's definitive takeaway is that *mayo* is a concept of central analytical importance that, once restored, reveals the Malayan animic world-view to be an interactive (relational) pan-psychism rather than a mere pan-soul-substance model. The distinction between life-full embodied *semanget* and the form-giving but non-conscious *mayo* resolves ambiguities in the earlier literature and provides a more precise account of how physical objects, spirit goods, dreams, and ritual efficacy are understood within the Sakai ontological framework (pp. 19–21).

## Context

- Primary ethnographic data derive from Porath's 18 months of participant-observation fieldwork with Orang Sakai communities in Riau, Indonesia (1995–1999), during which he lived with Sakai shamans and recorded numerous healing sessions (p. 9, n. 26).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in correcting the analytical record of Malayan animism by recovering a term that Endicott's influential 1970 synthesis inadvertently erased, and in demonstrating its operational significance through contemporary ethnographic evidence.
