# Frugivorous habits of the Tupaia

C. A. Gibson-Hill's 1950 article provides a systematic typological account of the fishing boats licensed and operated from Singapore Island, drawing on Fisheries Department licence returns for 1947–1949. The study is grounded in the physical characteristics of the Singapore coastline—sheltered, silt-laden waters with extensive mangroves and few sandy beaches—which constrain the design and operational scope of local craft. Gibson-Hill's overarching aim is to classify and describe each boat type in terms of hull form, construction technique, rigging, and the ethnic communities associated with its use, serving as a preliminary study for a forthcoming official survey of the Singapore fisheries.

## Summary

The article is organised around the licence categories employed by the Fisheries Department, which Gibson-Hill restructures into three principal groups: powered boats, keeled small boats (the Kolek family), and Chinese keelless boats, with a brief additional note on the Perahu Payang. The keeled small boats—Johore Kolek, Kolek Chiau, and Kolek Selat—comprise roughly three-quarters of all licensed craft and form the natural core of the study. Gibson-Hill distinguishes them by stem and stern post morphology, construction method (wood-pegged Malay technique versus nailed Chinese technique), and the ethnic communities that build and operate them. The Johore Kolek is presented as the representative Malay boat of the region, with a discussion of its survival of the dug-out base construction method into the early 1920s, later than elsewhere in Malaya. The Kolek Chiau and Kolek Selat are Chinese-built variants, the former being finer-lined and associated with kelong operations, the latter heavier, beamier, and used as a general-purpose craft.

The Chinese keelless boats form a less homogeneous group, united primarily by their flat or near-flat bottoms, shallow draughts, and Chinese origin. Gibson-Hill treats the Sampan Kotak (in its various sizes from 18 to 30 feet), the Lepap, the Kwa-Tow, the Pomehai, and the Chinese Seine-boat, noting that the latter was probably introduced from the Rhio-Lingga Archipelago by Hylam or Teochew Chinese. The powered boats, while numerous, are described as largely fish carriers rather than fishing vessels in the strict sense, with most licensed only for carrying fish to market. The article also addresses nomenclatural difficulties throughout, particularly the imprecision of terms like "Sampan," "Kolek," and "Jongkong," and attempts to establish more precise usage.

The appendix provides a comprehensive table of fishing gear licensed in 1948 and 1949, identifying the kelong as the single most productive unit, followed by drift nets, portable fish traps, and beach seines. The article thus functions as both a nautical typology and a snapshot of the fishing industry's structure in the immediate post-war period.

### Key Findings

- A total of 1,722 boats were licensed for fishing in 1949, up from 1,530 in 1947; the three Kolek types alone accounted for approximately 1,377 of these (pp. 148).
- As of 31 December 1949, 213 power-driven vessels held valid fishing-boat licences, of which 186 were licensed solely for carrying fish to market; only 43 were registered for work inside the 30-mile limit (pp. 148–149).
- In 1949, 247 kelongs were licensed (139 in depths greater than 3 fathoms, 108 in shallower water), making the kelong the most important and productive fishing unit in the area (p. 170).
- The registered fishing population of Singapore in 1949 numbered 4,387, comprising 3,073 Chinese, 1,301 Malay, 10 Indian, and 3 of other nationalities (p. 170).
- Construction costs in the late 1940s: a Kolek Chiau at approximately $6.50 per foot (16 feet and above); an 18-foot Kolek Selat at roughly $100; a 20-foot Kolek Selat at about $120; and a small Kotak of about 20 feet at roughly $320 (pp. 154, 157, 165).
- Drift nets (Jaring Hanyut) numbered 281 in 1949 and portable fish traps (Bubu) 255, representing the next most important gear categories after the kelong; approximately 250 pelontang and 800 hand-lines were also in operation but were exempt from licensing (p. 170).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive takeaway is that the fishing craft of Singapore are shaped by the specific physical conditions of the island's sheltered, silt-heavy waters, which favour small, lightly built, shallow-draught vessels over larger sailing craft. The kelong stands as the dominant productive unit, while the Kolek family constitutes the backbone of the inshore fleet. The article also documents a clear ethnic division of labour in boat-building and operation—Malays associated with the Johore Kolek and the Perahu Payang, Chinese with the Kolek Chiau, Kolek Selat, and the keelless boats—and notes the gradual adoption of Chinese construction and propulsion techniques by local Malays, a process that had not yet reached the east coast of the peninsula.

## Context

- The study is based primarily on Fisheries Department, Singapore licence returns for 1947–1949, supplemented by direct observation of boats in builder's yards (notably on Beach Road) and in operation. Gibson-Hill acknowledges T. W. Burdon, Fisheries Officer, Singapore, for information and advice (p. 169).
- The article is explicitly a preliminary study for a later official survey of the Singapore Fisheries and Fishing Industry, and it situates itself within a broader comparative literature on Southeast Asian and Chinese watercraft, citing Hornell (1946), Waters (1946), and Worcester (1947, 1948) (pp. 169–170).
