# Mineral production on the east coast of Malaya in the nineteenth century

## Abstract

The title of the paper I think broadly explains the contents. In it I argue that whilst the economic and political importance of tin production on the West coast of Malaya from the mid-nineteenth century onwards has understandably pre-occupied historians of politics, economics and demography, in fact it has resulted in a distortion of the historical record in that up to about this date it was gold rather than tin that was the main mineral product from the peninsula and that, unlike tin production, gold production was centred on the East Coast and largely produced by the Chinese. As a result assessments about the extent of East coast isolation from world trade and estimates about the size and distribution of the Chinese population before 1850 have to be reviewed. I hope that in justifying these claims I have also filled in certain lacunae about mining, demography, Malay politics and imperialism in the 19th century.

## Summary

Dodge challenges the dominant historiographical framing of nineteenth-century Malayan mining, which has been written almost exclusively in terms of West Coast tin production. He argues that this focus has distorted the historical record: up to approximately 1850, gold rather than tin was the peninsula's principal mineral export, and the East Coast states—particularly Kelantan, the Patani states, and Pahang—were the centre of mining activity and Chinese industrial settlement, not the West Coast. The article reconstructs this overlooked history from a patchwork of primary sources including Newbold's trade statistics, Hsieh's late-eighteenth-century travel account, Crawfurd's descriptive dictionary, and various geological surveys, to demonstrate that the East Coast was far from the isolated, mineral-poor periphery that later European observers assumed.

The narrative traces the geography of gold extraction along a narrow belt from Patani through Kelantan and into Pahang, identifying the major mining localities and the Chinese communities that operated them. Dodge shows that the Chinese mining populations in Kelantan and Pahang alone were large enough to invalidate the low estimates (15,000–20,000) that have been accepted for the peninsula's total Chinese population in the 1830s, supporting instead Crawfurd's higher figure of 40,000. The article then follows the post-1850 decline as Chinese miners migrated westward to exploit the richer deep tin deposits, leaving the East Coast to revert to small-scale Malay panning and to become the object of European speculation that repeatedly proved groundless.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to explaining why the East Coast's mineral potential was so consistently overestimated by Europeans. Dodge shows that the rulers of Kelantan and Trengganu actively discouraged both Chinese mining and European access, partly to prevent the very annexation that their resistance ultimately failed to avert. The Duff Syndicate's acquisition of a vast concession in Kelantan in 1900–1902, and its international repercussions, precipitated the final British extension of power over the northeastern states in 1909—a move motivated by the same "Invincible Error" that had driven earlier interventions: the assumption that mineral wealth must exist because officials had said so.

### Key Findings

- In 1835–6, gold accounted for approximately 47 per cent of the total value of the peninsula's exports to Singapore, with 99.9 per cent of that gold originating from the East Coast; the East Coast's combined gold and tin exports were valued at over $500,000 p.a. compared to the West Coast's approximately $290,000 (pp. 93–94, 101).
- Newbold's 1835–6 tin production figures show the East Coast states (Trengganu 417 tons, Kelantan 179 tons, Pahang 60 tons, Patani 60 tons) produced 714 long tons, or 34.9 per cent of the peninsula's total of 2,059 tons, with Trengganu alone exceeding all West Coast states except Perak, Selangor, and Negri-Sembilan (pp. 96–99).
- Newbold estimated the Chinese population of Pahang at 12,000 in 1835 (approximately one-third of the state's total population); combined with Kelantan's "several thousands" of Chinese miners, this alone accounts for 15,000–20,000 Chinese in two states, supporting Crawfurd's peninsula-wide estimate of 40,000 rather than Low's 15,000–20,000 (pp. 103–104).
- By 1890, the Chinese mining community in Kelantan had declined to approximately 1,000 (Foster's estimate), and Pahang's had fallen from 12,000 in 1835 to "a few hundred" by 1888; Kelantan's tin production in 1906–7 was less than 1,000 lbs and its gold production of 6,461 ounces was nearly all produced by European firms (pp. 105–106).
- The Duff Syndicate's 1900 concession in Kelantan covered approximately 3,000 square miles—over half the total area of the state—and its international repercussions directly precipitated the British annexation of Kelantan, Trengganu, and Kedah in 1909 (pp. 107–108).

### Conclusion

Dodge's definitive takeaway is that the East Coast's post-1850 economic marginality was not a natural or inevitable condition but the product of a specific historical shift: the discovery of rich deep tin deposits on the West Coast drew Chinese labour and capital away from the East Coast's goldfields, which were either exhausted or beyond the technological reach of the miners who remained. The subsequent European belief in the East Coast's vast mineral wealth was a self-reinforcing error that served as the political justification for British annexation, yet the promised riches never materialised—meaning the East Coast's retention of its "essentially Malay and peasant character" was, in Dodge's framing, almost a fortunate accident of geological poverty.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on Newbold's *British Settlements in the Straits of Malacca* (1839), Hsieh's eighteenth-century travel account (as edited by Tweedie, 1953), Crawfurd's *Descriptive Dictionary* (1820/1856), and the geological surveys of Bozzolo (1888), Savage (1925), and Lock (1907), alongside Wong Lin Ken's *The Malayan Tin Industry to 1914* (1964) and Purcell's *The Chinese in Malaya* (1948).
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in correcting the West Coast–centric bias in the mining and demographic literature, demonstrating that pre-1850 assessments of Chinese population size and East Coast economic isolation require fundamental revision.
