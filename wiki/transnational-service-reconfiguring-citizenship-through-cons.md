# (Trans)national service: reconfiguring citizenship through conscription in Singapore

Theophilus Kwek's 2019 article examines how Singapore's National Service (NS) conscription regime reconfigures citizenship for non-citizens, particularly permanent residents (PRs) who are legally required to complete military service to retain their residency status. Published in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, the piece argues that NS functions not simply as an assimilationist tool but as a mechanism through which neoliberal citizenship is negotiated along transnational lines, with migrants simultaneously embracing and instrumentalising their conscription obligations.

## Summary

Kwek situates his analysis within the broader literature on neoliberal citizenship and migration, drawing heavily on Aihwa Ong's framework of "neoliberalism as exception" to interrogate how Singapore's conscription policy operates at the intersection of national security, population management, and global mobility. The article's central problem is the contradiction between the state's stated intent—using NS to foster a shared sense of national belonging among PRs—and the lived experience of conscripted non-citizens, many of whom approach their service with a distinctly transactional or transnational orientation. The death of Pte Liu Kai, a PR killed during a field training exercise in November 2018, serves as a focal point through which Kwek explores the asymmetries of obligation and recognition between citizens and non-citizens in uniform (pp. 67–90).

The article traces the policy trajectory from the 2012 decision to require NS-liable PRs to complete conscription as a condition of retaining their status, through to the broader implications of Singapore's "most powerful passport" strategy. Kwek demonstrates that rather than uniformly deterring onward migration, NS participation can be reframed by migrants as an investment in long-term transnational mobility—converting the sacrifice of conscription into a credential that enhances global positioning. This finding complicates the straightforward assimilationist narrative that underpins official MINDEF rhetoric about NS "bonding our people."

The evidential base combines policy documents (the Singapore Armed Forces Act, the Official Secrets Act, the Constitution), government speeches and statements (including Singapore's 2017 Human Rights Council submission), survey data from the Institute of Policy Studies, and media discourse surrounding high-profile NS incidents. Kwek also engages with the Official Secrets Act's particular implications for PRs in service, noting how legal frameworks governing military information create additional vulnerabilities for non-citizens that are rarely acknowledged in public debate.

### Key Findings

- Approximately 2,600 NS-liable PRs renounced their permanent resident status over a five-year period, indicating significant friction between the conscription requirement and migrants' long-term plans (Sin, 2014, cited in article).
- The 2012 policy change mandated that PRs must complete National Service to retain their residency status, a shift that formalised the link between military obligation and legal belonging (Dass, 2012).
- The death of Pte Liu Kai in November 2018—a PR killed when a Bionix vehicle reversed into the Land Rover he was operating during a field training exercise—became a site of public contestation over the fairness and recognition afforded to non-citizen conscripts (Mahmud, 2018; Facebook/Straits Times, 2018).
- Singapore's passport was ranked the "most powerful" in the world in 2017, a status that Kwek links to the state's broader strategy of cultivating globally mobile citizens whose NS service is reframed as a credential for transnational advantage (Seow, 2017).
- The Official Secrets Act (1935, as amended) imposes restrictions on PRs in NS that create a distinct legal vulnerability not shared by citizen conscripts, a dimension largely absent from public policy discourse (Singapore Statutes Online, 1935/2012).
- The 2013 IPS survey on Singaporeans' attitudes to NS revealed persistent gaps in how citizens and PRs perceive the meaning and fairness of conscription, underscoring the limits of NS as an assimilationist instrument (IPS, 2014).

### Conclusion

Kwek's definitive takeaway is that NS in Singapore operates as a site where neoliberal citizenship is actively reconfigured rather than simply reproduced: for some migrants, conscription deepens identification with the state, but for a significant cohort it functions as a calculative step in a transnational life strategy. The article calls for policymakers to move beyond the binary of assimilation versus exclusion, suggesting that creating more pathways to citizenship and strengthening legal guarantees for PRs would better align official rhetoric with the complex, often transnational, orientations that non-citizen conscripts actually bring to their service.

## Context

- The study draws on Singapore government archives (National Archives of Singapore), MINDEF publications, MFA statements, and the Singapore Statutes Online database, alongside media reporting and social media discourse.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the emerging field of migration and citizenship studies in Singapore by foregrounding the voices and perspectives of non-citizen conscripts, a group that Kwek notes remains "marginal in public and policy conversations."
