# New Malayan plants

H. N. Ridley, then Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, published this sweeping taxonomic paper in 1904 describing over forty new plant species from the Malay Peninsula across twelve families. Drawing on collections gathered by field collectors across Perak, Selangor, Johore, Penang, Malacca, Pahang, and Singapore, the paper represents a single concentrated burst of neotypification that significantly expanded the known flora of the region during the height of colonial botanical surveying.

## Summary

Ridley's paper is organized by family and proceeds from the Scitamineae (Zingiberaceae) through to the Cyperaceae, with the bulk of new species falling in the Palmae, Aroideae (Araceae), and Pandanaceae. The descriptions are concise but diagnostic, each accompanied by locality data, collector attributions, and brief comparative notes linking the new taxon to its nearest known relatives. Ridley frequently notes the ecological context—elevation, substrate, and habitat type—and occasionally records Malay vernacular names or ethnobotanical uses, as with *Forrestia gracilis* (known as "Setawa betina," whose roots the Sakai people used in a decoction for rheumatism) and *Iguanura spectabilis* (the "Teruno" palm).

The Thaiping Hills in Perak emerge as a recurring centre of endemism, yielding new species of *Curculigo*, *Iguanura*, *Pandanus*, *Amorphophallus*, and *Dracaena*. The Lankawi Islands and the limestone caves near Kuala Lumpur also feature prominently, the latter providing *Livistona rupicola*, a dwarf palm growing on cliff faces, and *Typhonium fultum*, an aroid with an unusual above-ground stem propped on roots. Ridley also undertakes taxonomic revisions within genera, most notably consolidating the *Dracaena* species of the Peninsula (bringing the total to sixteen) and reinterpreting the *Iguanura* group, where he argues that several previously described species are merely forms of *I. geonomaeformis* and *I. polymorpha*.

The paper closes with a single new species of *Mapania* (Cyperaceae), a genus Ridley had previously treated in the same journal. The overall effect is that of a working botanist processing a backlog of specimens accumulated over several years of fieldwork and collector correspondence, producing a reference document that would anchor subsequent floristic work on the Peninsula for decades.

### Key Findings

- Over forty new species are described across twelve families, with the Palmae (eight species), Aroideae (ten species), and Pandanaceae (six species) contributing the largest numbers (pp. 38–50).
- *Dracaena* diversity in the Peninsula is assessed at no fewer than sixteen species, with Ridley noting that several additional unnamed plants in his collections "appear quite distinct from any described" but lacked flowers for determination (p. 37).
- *Iguanura spectabilis*, the "Teruno" palm of Perak, had previously been misidentified by Dr. Masters as *Geonoma Pynærtiana* in the *Gardener's Chronicle* (1898); Ridley corrects this based on Curtis's living specimens (p. 40).
- *Calamus aquatilis*, a rattan of tidal river swamps known locally as "Rotan Bakau," is confirmed as distinct by Professor Beccari, who was then preparing a monograph on Malayan rattans for the Calcutta Botanic Gardens (p. 44).
- *Cryptocoryne purpurea* from Johore had been figured in the *Botanical Magazine* (pl. 7719) under the incorrect name *C. Griffithii*; Ridley separates it on the basis of its longer spathe tube and more numerous female flowers (p. 44).
- *Forrestia gracilis*, described as "our commonest species" of the genus, had escaped formal description until this paper, having been previously lumped with the rarer *F. mollis* (p. 38).

### Conclusion

Ridley's definitive takeaway is that the Malayan Peninsula's flora remained substantially underdocumented even by 1904, with common plants (such as *Forrestia gracilis* and *Calamus aquatilis*) going undescribed for want of flowers or fruit, and with mountainous localities like the Thaiping Hills and the Lankawi Islands harbouring a disproportionate share of endemics. The paper implicitly argues for continued systematic collection in these under-surveyed areas, a message reinforced by Ridley's repeated appeals to future visitors to "recollect" and "look for again" species known from only a single specimen.

## Context

- The paper was produced from the Singapore Botanic Gardens, which under Ridley's directorship (1888–1913) served as the principal taxonomic hub for the Straits Settlements and the Federated Malay States, processing specimens from collectors including Wray, Napier, Curtis, King, Cantley, and Scortechini.
- Several of the new species were subsequently validated or reinterpreted in later floras; the paper remains a foundational reference for the pre-1900 botanical literature of the Peninsula held in the MBRAS archives.
