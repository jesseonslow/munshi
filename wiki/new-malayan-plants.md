# New Malayan plants

H. N. Ridley, then Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, published this second series of new and little-known Malayan plant descriptions in 1905, presenting formal accounts of species collected across the Malay Peninsula, Borneo, and Siam by himself and several field collectors. The article's most significant contribution is the description of *Borassus machadonis*, an indigenous Palmyra palm from the forests of Kamuning in Perak, which Ridley argues is a genuinely native species rather than a cultivated introduction of the African-Indian *B. flabelliformis* (pp. 203–205).

## Summary

Ridley describes a broad taxonomic range spanning Orchidaceae, Scitamineae, Burmanniaceae, Aroideae, Flagellariaceae, Palmae, Cyperaceae, and Gramineae, with the majority of new species being orchids and palms. The specimens were gathered by a network of collectors—Dr. Gimlette in Kelantan, Mr. Rostado in Tringanu, Mr. Machado in Perak, and Mr. Burn-Murdoch at Semangkok Pass in Selangor—reflecting the collaborative model of botanical exploration operating under the Singapore Botanic Gardens at the turn of the century. Ridley's descriptions follow the standard Linnaean format of the period, providing morphological detail sufficient for identification and, where possible, notes on habitat, distribution, and affinity with known species.

Several descriptions carry broader biogeographical significance beyond mere species addition. The discovery of *Joinvillea malayana* in the Flagellariaceae is notable because the genus was previously known from only three species in the Hawaiian Islands and New Caledonia, making the Malayan representative a striking case of disjunct distribution that Ridley compares to the pattern seen in *Cyrtandra* (pp. 199–200). Similarly, the new *Borassus* challenges the long-held assumption that the genus was monotypic and that all Palmyra palms in Southeast Asia were introductions from India or Africa; Ridley marshals evidence from Rumphius, Loureiro, and Wendland to argue that the Kamuning plant is distinct in its thornless petiole, three-lobed calyx, and narrow petals (pp. 203–205). The bamboo descriptions, including *Dendrocalamus pendulus* with its 60-foot arching culms, extend the known diversity of a group of considerable economic and ecological importance in the region.

### Key Findings

- *Borassus machadonis* was discovered in dense forest at Kamuning, Perak, by A. D. Machado; it is distinguished from *B. flabelliformis* by its entirely thornless petiole, three-lobed calyx, narrow petals, and long linear anthers, and is known natively as "Chang-hai" (pp. 203–204).
- *Joinvillea malayana* was found at the Thaiping Hills in Perak and Semangkok Pass in Selangor; it is the fourth known species of the genus, the others being from the Sandwich Islands and New Caledonia, and is more closely allied to a Hawaiian species than to the New Caledonian one (pp. 199–200).
- *Dendrocalamus pendulus* reaches approximately 60 feet in height with culms 3 inches in diameter and long pendulous branches; it was collected at 2,000 feet altitude on the Selangor–Pahang Track and had previously been cultivated in the Singapore Botanic Gardens without flowering (pp. 210–211).
- *Pinanga acanlis*, a dwarf palm with a stem barely 4 inches long, was found in abundance at Kamuning, Perak, and is unique in its genus for its extremely short stem, double spathe, and crimson rachis bearing pink female flowers (p. 202).
- *Mnesithea pubescens* from the rocky cliffs at Minyak Buku, Johor, is the third known species of the genus and is distinguished by pubescent leaves and spikes and by curious perforations in the outer glume each bearing a cilium (pp. 207–208).
- *Bambusa magica*, known to the Malays as "Bulu Perindu," was collected in flowering condition by Wray on Gunong Berumbun, Perak, and is credited with mystic properties by local inhabitants who use it in love rituals (p. 209).

### Conclusion

Ridley's central historical takeaway is that the Malay Peninsula's flora, particularly its palms and grasses, remains far from fully known and that indigenous species previously assumed to be introductions or variants of known taxa are in fact distinct. The *Borassus* discovery is presented as the most remarkable finding of the series, overturning the assumption that the genus was monotypic in the region and demonstrating that forest-dwelling palms of considerable size can persist in areas where they have not been recorded.

## Context

- The article draws on collections held at the Singapore Botanic Gardens and the British Museum (Ridley acknowledges A. B. Rendle's assistance with the Flagellariaceae identification), and on field specimens from collectors operating in Perak, Selangor, Kelantan, Johor, and Sarawak (pp. 189, 199).
- As a second series following an earlier contribution, the article represents the ongoing output of Ridley's programme to document the flora of the Straits Settlements and the Federated Malay States during the period when the Singapore Botanic Gardens functioned as the regional centre for tropical botanical research.
