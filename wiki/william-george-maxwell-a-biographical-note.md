# William George Maxwell: a biographical note

J.M. Gullick's biographical note on William George Maxwell (1871–1959), published posthumously in JMBRAS in 2017, situates one of the most consequential colonial administrators in Malaya within the three-generation Maxwell dynasty that dominated Straits Settlements and Malay States governance from the 1850s through the 1920s. The article, originally prepared as a foreword to a new edition of Maxwell's *In Malay Forests* that never materialised, traces Maxwell's career from junior district officer to Chief Secretary of the Federated Malay States and examines his central role in the inter-war decentralisation controversy and his opposition to the Malayan Union scheme.

## Summary

Gullick frames Maxwell's career against the evolving administrative architecture of British Malaya, from the Straits Settlements crown colony through the protected native states, the FMS (formed 1896), and the unfederated Malay States (acquired from Siam in 1909, with Johor added in 1910). The narrative's central tension is the power struggle between Maxwell, as Chief Secretary FMS from 1920, and Governor and High Commissioner Sir Lawrence Guillemard (1919–27), which Gullick characterises as a "poisoned chalice" that "blighted the careers of both men" (p. 121). Maxwell's advocacy for restoring powers to the state governments was entangled with the interests of the local business community, which viewed a strong federal centre as a bulwark against Singapore-driven policy, while the rulers and Residents welcomed Guillemard's proposals for a looser structure. The dispute was further complicated by the question of whether restored powers should vest in the Residents or in the rulers acting through state council, and by thorny financial questions of allocation and control (p. 121).

The article also addresses Maxwell's post-retirement engagement with Malayan affairs, particularly his opposition to the Malayan Union announced in late 1945. Gullick notes that Maxwell initially welcomed the scheme as "full of promise and of hope" but reversed his position upon studying its implications for the rulers and the Malay community, particularly the automatic grant of citizenship to non-Malays based on birth or residence (p. 122). Maxwell's prescient suggestion of an enlarged federation of all peninsular states anticipated the 1948 solution, though Gullick is careful to note that the old guard's public objections, while causing the Colonial Office embarrassment, were not the decisive factor in the scheme's withdrawal (p. 123).

The final section turns to Maxwell's literary output, placing *In Malay Forests* (1907) within the tradition of Malayan administrative travel writing that included Swettenham, Clifford, Robson, Skeat, and Harrison. Gullick emphasises the book's value as a record of a Malayan natural environment and village life that has since been largely transformed by development, while acknowledging the anachronism of its hunting narratives by contemporary ethical standards (pp. 123–124).

### Key Findings

- Maxwell's Malayan career spanned 35 years (1891–1926), progressing from junior district officer in Perak to Solicitor General of the Straits Settlements (1906), British Adviser to Kedah (1909–14), and Chief Secretary FMS (from 1920), before forced retirement at age 55 (pp. 119–121).
- He was awarded the CMG in 1915 and the KBE in 1924; his father, Sir William Edward Maxwell, had introduced the Torrens system of registered land title to Malaya, which became the basis of the National Land Code (p. 118).
- The FMS decentralisation controversy of the 1920s pitted Maxwell (supported by the business community) against Guillemard (supported by the rulers and Residents); the office of Chief Secretary survived another decade after Maxwell's departure before being downgraded to Federal Secretary in the 1930s (pp. 121–122).
- Maxwell served as vice-chairman of the League of Nations Committee on Slavery from 1932 to 1939 and chaired the committee that produced *The Civil Defence of Malaya* (1942) in response to criticism of the civil government's collapse in 1941–42 (pp. 122–123).
- *In Malay Forests* was published by William Blackwood and Sons in 1907; Maxwell also contributed 15 articles to the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society and served as its president in the post-war period (pp. 123–124).
- Maxwell's grandfather, Peter Benson Maxwell, served as Chief Justice of the Straits Settlements (1856–71) and later published *Our Malay Conquests* (1878), expressing disapproval of British intervention in the Malay states (p. 118).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive assessment is that Maxwell was a man of considerable ability and integrity whose career was ultimately derailed not by incompetence but by the structural contradictions of the colonial system he served—specifically the irreconcilable tension between federal centralisation and state autonomy that no amount of administrative skill could resolve. His post-retirement opposition to the Malayan Union, while prescient in its alternative proposal, ultimately proved ineffective, and Gullick closes on a note of quiet marginalisation: an octogenarian "out of the game for many years" who "no longer counted" (p. 123).

## Context

- The article draws on Maxwell's personal papers held at the Rhodes House Library (Bodleian, Oxford) and on microfilm in the National University of Singapore library, alongside standard secondary sources including Yeo Kim Wah's *The Politics of Decentralisation* (1982) and Stockwell's *British Policy and Malay Politics during the Malayan Union Experiment* (1979) (p. 124).
- As a posthumous publication from Gullick's unpublished papers, the note represents a rare insider's perspective from a fellow JMBRAS contributor of the same generation, offering biographical colour and administrative nuance that formal secondary literature tends to omit.
