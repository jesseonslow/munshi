# Keramat see Karamat Kingship and enthronement in Malaya

R.O. Winstedt, a British colonial administrator and leading Malay scholar, published this comparative study of Malay kingship in 1947 (reprinted from the *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society*, October 1945). Writing from direct observation of Perak and Negri Sembilan enthronement ceremonies and extensive knowledge of Siamese, Burmese, and Javanese ritual, Winstedt argues that Malay royal authority is a palimpsest of three successive civilisational layers—shamanic, Hindu-Buddhist, and Islamic—each retaining structural traces of the one that preceded it.

## Summary

Winstedt's central thesis is that the Malay king's divine right did not emerge fully formed with Islam but was grafted onto an older substrate of shamanic magic and Hindu-Buddhist cosmology. He begins by tracing the legendary genealogy of the Perak and Negri Sembilan dynasties back to Bichitram, a kinsman of the Sri Vijaya maharajas, and then demonstrates how the *Sejarah Melayu*'s account of Chōla and Pāṇḍya ancestors is a folkloric confusion of Tamil dynastic names. The real analytical work begins with the shamanic layer: Winstedt shows that the Malay king and the Malay *bomoh* (shaman) shared the same conception of mastery over the "mannikin soul of things," a microcosmic power he traces to a prehistoric diffusion from the Middle East through Yunnan to the archipelago. The hereditary office of the Perak Sultan Muda as State Shaman, who annually revived the regalia with food and drink and sacrificed to the guardian spirits of the country, is presented as a living fossil of this layer, still observable as late as 1874 when Sultan 'Abdu'llah sat at a seance on the shaman's mat.

The second layer, the Hindu-Buddhist, is explored through the concept of the king as incarnation or receptacle of a deity, specifically as lord of a miniature Mount Meru. Winstedt identifies the palace, temple, or sacred hill as the evolved form of the ancient earth-mound described by Confucius, and shows how the circumambulation of the palace at enthronement (seven times in Perak, once in Negri Sembilan) enacts the sovereign's taking possession of his kingdom in miniature. The four colours of Meru's faces correspond precisely to the colours assigned to the Perak Sultan, Heir Apparent, Prime Minister, and Minister of War. The Siamese parallel—where the King, after lustration, faces each compass point in turn and is addressed as guardian of that quarter—is shown to be the same ritual logic made explicit.

The third and most recent layer is the Islamic caliphate, which Winstedt treats as a veneer rather than a replacement. The Negri Sembilan enthronement invocation to the angel of the rising sun, the four archangels, and notably Karnain the horned angel of the moon (a syncretic figure linked to Alexander the Great as Dhul'l-Karnain) illustrates how Muslim missionaries fabricated a pedigree for royal converts to compensate for their loss of Hindu godhead. The article closes with a survey of the numerical symbolism (4, 8, 16, 32) pervading Malay, Siamese, Burmese, and Javanese state organisation, which Winstedt, following Heine-Geldern, interprets as the empire conceived as an image of the heavenly world of stars and gods.

### Key Findings

- The name Bichitram is whispered into the ear of every Perak Sultan at his enthronement as the ancestor of the Perak and old Malacca dynasty; the Negri Sembilan Yang di-pertuan claims descent from the same line via Minangkabau (p. 127).
- In 1874, Perak folk observed Sultan 'Abdu'llah sitting at a seance on the shaman's mat, possessed by the genies of the State, who prophesied the death of the British Resident (p. 129).
- Aditiavarman's knife, bearing Bhairava and his *sakti* inlaid in gold wire on both faces of the blade, was discovered in 1930 in the house of an old lady descendant of the former Minangkabau royal family; it formed part of the Minangkabau regalia (p. 133).
- The Negri Sembilan regalia comprise 8 tufted spears, 8 swords, 8 creeses, 8 large candles, 8 small tapers, 8 betel-boxes, 8 handfuls of ashes, 8 water-vessels, 16 pennons, and 16 umbrellas; the ruler may have only 4 wives, and there are 4 princes of the blood, 4 territorial chiefs, and 4 major court officers (p. 137).
- The Perak Sri Nara-diraja, the hereditary herald of Sivaite origin who alone outside the royal family may handle the regalia, belongs to a family for whom beef is tabu—a residual Brahminical prohibition (pp. 135, 138 n.3).
- In the Negri Sembilan enthronement, the four Palace Officers circumambulate the nine-tiered bathing pavilion seven times carrying rice-paste in a silver bowl, which the royal couple dip into four times with their right hands; the invocation to Karnain the horned angel of the moon is a syncretic addition linking the royal line to Alexander the Great (Dhul'l-Karnain) (pp. 135, 138 n.3).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that Malay kingship is not a single tradition but a stratified accumulation in which each civilisational contact—shamanic, Hindu-Buddhist, Islamic—added a new ritual grammar without erasing the old. The enthronement ceremonies of Perak and Negri Sembilan, still performed in the 1930s and 1940s, are therefore living documents of this accretion, and their abandonment would mean the loss of "old-world details" that encode a cosmological logic shared across the entire Hindu-Buddhist and shamanic world of Southeast Asia.

## Context

- Winstedt served as British Resident in Perak (1917–1924) and was a founding figure of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society; his direct access to Perak court ceremonies and his earlier publications on the *Sejarah Melayu* (1938), the history of Perak (1934), and the *Misa Melayu* (1919) gave him an evidential base unavailable to most scholars of his era.
- The article draws on contemporary observations by J.J. Sheehan of the 1936 Negri Sembilan installation and the 1939 Selangor enthronement (Pl. XV), as well as on Heine-Geldern's comparative work on Farther Indian state symbolism, positioning itself at the intersection of colonial ethnography and the emerging field of Southeast Asian political anthropology.
