# Tunku Kudin of Kedah

J.M. Gullick's 1987 article, the fourth in a multi-part series on Tunku Kudin (Dhiauddin), covers the final phase of this remarkable Malay prince's career: his failed bid for power in Kedah following the death of his brother Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin II in 1879, and his subsequent 27-year exile in Penang and Selangor until his death in 1909. The article argues that Kudin's political defeat was the product of a complex interplay between Kedah's internal dynastic rivalries, Siamese suzerain intervention, and the emerging British presence in the Straits Settlements, and that his exile years reveal a man of considerable charm and adaptability who nonetheless could not reconcile his Europeanised lifestyle with the Malay political world that had rejected him.

## Summary

The article opens with the succession crisis that followed the death of Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin II in June 1879. The structural weakness of the Kedah dynasty—exposed by the Sultan's untimely death at 47, leaving only youthful sons—pitted Kudin against his brother Tunku Yakob (Raja Muda) and the chief minister Wan Mat Saman, who were aligned with the sons of the Sultan's more influential consort, Wan Hajar. Kudin aligned with the sons of the other consort, Wan Jah, and married his daughters to two of them. The Siamese, as suzerain, had to arbitrate. The first resolution produced a joint regency of the royal uncles with the young Tunku Petra as Sultan, but this collapsed when Petra died in 1881, reopening the conflict.

The second and decisive struggle saw the Siamese take six months to decide, during which they contemplated—though ultimately abandoned—outright annexation of Kedah and the imposition of a Siamese Commissioner modelled on the British Resident system. The Siamese ultimately backed Tunku Abdul Hamid (Wan Hajar's son) as Sultan with Tunku Yakob as regent, and excluded Kudin entirely. A brief Siamese military presence in Kedah confirmed the new regime's dependence on external support. Kudin, whose dignity would not permit him to live as a private citizen in Alor Star, settled in Penang for the rest of his life.

The second half of the article traces Kudin's exile years with considerable sympathy. He maintained social and family connections with the Kedah and Selangor royal courts, pursued commercial ventures in Selangor (mangrove cutting, padi and sago planting, coffee cultivation), and remained a prominent figure in Penang's mixed Malay-European social world. His relationship with his wife Raja Arfah (daughter of Sultan Abdul Samad of Selangor) was strained by their incompatible temperaments and cultural orientations. The article closes with Kudin's death in May 1909 and his elaborate funeral procession back to the Kedah royal burial ground at Kota Langgar.

### Key Findings

- Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin II died on 22 June 1879 at approximately age 47; his will nominated Tunku Petra (Zainal Rashid) as immediate successor with Tunku Abdul Hamid second in order (p. 75).
- The Siamese contemplated pensions of $20,000 per annum to Kudin, $10,500 to Yakob, and $10,000 to Yusof as part of a plan to retire the royal uncles from public office (p. 77).
- Governor Weld reported in early June 1882 that "Kedah is now being brought more and more under the immediate dominion of the Siamese, a military Siamese guard is now there and much discontent prevails among the Malays" (p. 78).
- Kudin's coffee estate at Klang comprised 2,000 acres of leasehold land, of which approximately 300 acres were planted with coffee by 1894 before the price slump and disease ended the venture (pp. 84–85).
- The Selangor Padi and Sago Planting Company, in which Kudin held a half interest, received a government grant of 6,000 acres free of rent and a loan of $1,700, but the venture ended in failure (p. 84).
- Kudin died on 24 May 1909 at approximately age 74; his coffin was borne by 20 men, carried by royal barge to the burial ground at Kota Langgar, accompanied by hundreds of prahus and the beating of nobat drums (p. 91).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that Tunku Kudin was a man of genuine political talent and personal charm who was ultimately defeated not by any single adversary but by the structural logic of Kedah's succession system operating under Siamese suzerainty. His Europeanised lifestyle and devious political methods, while effective in the fluid environment of Selangor during the civil war era, proved incompatible with the more rigid and tradition-bound court culture of Kedah. The article positions Kudin between two models of Malay rulership—closer to Sultan Idris of Perak in his ability to engage Europeans on their own terms, but lacking Idris's deep roots in Malay tradition, and further from the complete cultural assimilation of Abu Bakar of Johor. His 27-year exile and his dignified refusal to return to Alor Star as a private citizen represent, in Gullick's telling, the final tragedy of a man whose political world had closed around him.

## Context

- Primary sources include the Kedah Malay chronicle (Hassan, 1928/1968), British official despatches from the Straits Settlements (CO 273 series), the Pinang Gazette, Swettenham's journals, and the unpublished journal of the Collector, Kuala Langat. The article is the fourth in Gullick's multi-part series on Kudin and Kedah, building on his earlier volumes on Kedah 1821–1855, the reign of Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin II, and Kudin in Selangor 1868–1878.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the understanding of Siamese suzerain intervention in Malay succession disputes during the 1880s, a period for which primary evidence is notably thin. Gullick is candid about the limitations of his sources—particularly the biased Kedah chronicle and the absence of first-hand accounts of the Bangkok negotiations—and frames his reconstruction as necessarily tentative where the evidence is fragmentary.
