# Malay women: involvement in land ownership, the accumulation of wealth and indebtedness in Kedah (1881–1940

Mahani Musa's 2004 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the economic agency of Malay women in Kedah between 1881 and 1940, arguing that they were active participants in land acquisition, wealth accumulation, and property defence rather than passive dependents confined to domestic roles. Drawing extensively on Land Office, Stamp Office, Estate Duty Office, and State Secretariat records, Musa demonstrates that women across social strata—from the sultan's wives to commoner paddy planters—engaged in sustained economic activity that the colonial administrative apparatus both facilitated and, at times, constrained.

## Summary

Musa's central argument is that Malay women in Kedah never marginalized themselves from economic life, and that the transition from pre-colonial to British colonial rule did not fundamentally alter their participation in the economy. The article traces this continuity from the pre-colonial period, when women's labour in paddy fields was formally recognized in the *Undang-Undang Kedah* (the Ku Din Ku Meh version of 1893), through the British introduction of the Land Office system after 1909, which bureaucratized land administration but did not impede women's applications for state land. Musa shows that women applied for land individually and jointly, petitioned Land Offices when applications were mishandled, and treated land as both income source and capital for future use. The abolition of the *orang berhutang* (debt-bondage) system in 1910–12, which had previously bound women and children to creditors through inherited obligations, is presented as a significant improvement in women's socio-economic position (pp. 4–6).

The article then turns to inheritance and property disputes, using Stamp Office and court records to show that women actively defended their claims to *harta sepencarian* (jointly acquired marital property) and inherited estates. Musa documents cases in which women took their husbands to court, challenged the decisions of close relatives of the ruler, and filed repeated petitions with the State Council when unsatisfied with outcomes. The occupational data from the 1911, 1921, and 1931 censuses reveals that while official statistics undercounted women's labour (categorizing much of it as "household chores"), women constituted a substantial proportion of the agricultural workforce and were present in commercial and industrial sectors, including rubber tapping after 1924 (pp. 16–17).

The final section addresses indebtedness, showing that both commoner women and members of the ruling house fell prey to moneylenders—primarily Indian chettiars—whose loans were secured against land or jewellery. Musa argues that the State Council's repeated interventions to manage royal women's debts, and the eventual establishment of the Kampung Baharu Girls' School in 1924 as a response to the problem, reveal how indebtedness shaped both policy and social reform in the state (pp. 18–21).

### Key Findings

- The Baling Land Office received approximately 84 applications from Malay women for state land in 1928–29, 97 in 1930, and about 360 for paddy and rubber land from 1932 to 1934, indicating a sharp increase in female land applications during the Depression (p. 7).
- Che Manjalara, the sultan's favourite wife, left behind landed and moveable property valued at $59,649.00 at her death in April 1941, though debts totalling $36,595.07 reduced the net inheritance to $23,053.93 (pp. 2, 11).
- In the 1911 census, 89,908 of 89,934 Malay women in Kedah (99 per cent) were recorded as engaged in paid work, representing 49.8 per cent of the total Malay population; by 1931 this figure had fallen to 31 per cent, though Musa attributes the decline to survey methodology rather than actual disengagement (pp. 16–17).
- The State Council agreed in July 1912 to pay the balance of all outstanding *orang berhutang* debts in the state, found to total $5,000, effectively freeing all debt-bonded persons (p. 5).
- Tunku Aishah, wife of the Regent Tunku Ibrahim, owed $13,100.00 to Karpen Cettiyar, secured against four plots of land totalling 281 relong; the Regent personally settled the debt to prevent the land from passing to a non-Malay creditor (p. 20).
- The *Land Revenue Exemptions Enactment* allowed descendants of the sultan to own up to 500 relong of land each with tax exemption, a privilege that was frequently abused through speculative sales to Chinese buyers (p. 8).

### Conclusion

Musa concludes that the historical record of Kedah provides a "clearer picture" of Malay women's economic involvement than previously recognized, demonstrating that they were income earners, wealth accumulators, and active defenders of their property rights across both the pre-colonial and colonial periods. Their economic participation was not incidental or derivative of male activity but constituted a crucial contribution to household and state-level economic development, even as it exposed them to the risks of indebtedness and the constraints of a legal and administrative system that was not designed with their agency in mind.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on Kedah State Secretariat files (SUK Kedah), Land Office district indexes (Baling, Kulim, Kubang Pasu, Padang Terap), Stamp Office and Estate Duty Office estate records, High Court and Lower Court files, and the 1911, 1921, and 1931 census reports—collections that had been underutilized in prior scholarship on Malay women.
- Historiographically, the article extends the work of Khoo Kay Jin, who first identified the Kedah Stamp Office records as a neglected data base (1992), and contributes to the small body of historical (as opposed to sociological or anthropological) studies of Malay women's economic roles in the colonial period.
