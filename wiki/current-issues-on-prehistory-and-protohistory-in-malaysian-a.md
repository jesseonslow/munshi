# Current issues on prehistory and protohistory in Malaysian archaeology,

Raimy Ché-Ross (2007) presents a preliminary codicological and provenance study of the *Hikayat Hikamat*, a rare nineteenth-century Malay autobiographical manuscript long presumed lost since the Japanese Occupation. The article reconstructs the work's transmission from its composition in 1861 by Hikamat—a Sumatran youth educated and converted to Christianity at Reverend Keasberry's Mission School in Singapore—through its passage into the author's own family library and ultimately into Za'ba's private papers at the Arkib Negara Malaysia. Ché-Ross's central argument is that the manuscript's rediscovery and the identification of its owner "Marjan" with the author himself constitute a radical addition to Malay literary studies, challenging the assumption that Munshi Abdullah's autobiographical innovations had no successors.

## Summary

The article addresses a significant gap in Malay literary studies: the survival and identification of a manuscript first referenced by Za'ba in 1940 and subsequently believed destroyed during the Japanese Occupation. Ché-Ross traces the *Hikayat Hikamat* from its disappearance from the Sultan Idris Training College library to its rediscovery among Za'ba's private papers at the Arkib Negara Malaysia, where it had been deposited in 1970. The paper's central intellectual achievement is the identification of the manuscript's owner "Marjan" with the author Hikamat himself, a revelation drawn from a passage in the memoirs in which Hikamat's slave-master renames him "Marjan" using a book of divination (p. 66). This identification transforms the manuscript from an anonymous curiosity into a documented family heirloom, with ownership seals and inscriptions linking it to Hikamat's wife Fatimah binti Tambi Iman, his daughter Rokiah, and his son Encik Paya.

The provenance reconstruction draws on a comparative codicological analysis of six Malay Jawi manuscripts sharing common ownership links: three from Major Chamberlayne's collection at Cambridge University Library and three from Za'ba's papers at the Arkib Negara Malaysia. Shared frontispiece inscriptions ("M. Bujang" with dates ranging from 1866 to 1872), colophon statements, and posthumous bequest seals collectively establish that these volumes constitute the residual holdings of Marjan's family library and the output of a late nineteenth-century Singaporean scriptorium (pp. 62–65). The manuscript's route from Singapore to the SITC at Tanjung Malim is reconstructed through the institutional history of Malay teacher-training colleges and the known collecting activities of R. J. Wilkinson and O. T. Dussek, who actively acquired Malay manuscripts for the SITC library in the 1890s and 1920s (pp. 67–69).

The article also provides a structural overview of the work's contents, which span approximately three decades (c. 1845–1867) and cover Hikamat's childhood in Sumatra, his enslavement, his arrival in Singapore, and his education at the Mission School at Bukit Zion. Ché-Ross positions the *Hikayat Hikamat* as a work that "negates the general belief" that Munshi Abdullah had never influenced succeeding generations of authors, and argues for the urgent need for a full critical edition and broader engagement with hitherto ignored manuscript collections (pp. 74–75).

### Key Findings

- The *Hikayat Hikamat* manuscript (ANM SP 18/5c/3) comprises 190 leaves (380 pages), with a *terminus a quo* of 1861 (prologue) and a *terminus ante quem* of 1880 (colophon of the appended *Syair Penghiburan*, copied by Muhammad Hassan bin Haji Muhammad Jan on 20 November 1880) (pp. 65, 83–85).
- The appended *Syair Penghiburan* consists of 372 stanzas (368 complete, two incomplete, two single-line interjections) totalling 1,472 verse lines (p. 73).
- The manuscript was rebound by the Arkib Negara Malaysia on 21 April 1992 (job-lot No. 408/92J); its original bindings were discarded during conservation (p. 83).
- Za'ba's private papers, including the *Hikayat Hikamat*, were donated to the Arkib Negara Malaysia by his daughter Zaharah and her husband Adnan Mohd. Isa on 15 September 1970 (p. 71).
- The manuscript's paper stocks include multiple watermarks: "Horn & Shield" over "E W & Co / S S / SUPERFINE" (ff. 7–84), "LONDON / SUPERFINE" (ff. 85–100 and 179–90), and Britannia in an oval shield (ff. 3–6 and 150–67) (p. 84).
- Five other manuscripts share ownership links with the *Hikayat Hikamat*: CUL Or. 850, Or. 855, and Or. 857 (from Chamberlayne's collection, acquired in Singapore, Labuan, and Ceylon between 1868 and 1873), and ANM SP 18/5c/13 and SP 18/5c/14 (from Za'ba's papers) (pp. 62–64).

### Conclusion

Ché-Ross concludes that the *Hikayat Hikamat* is "one of the most enigmatic and important examples of Malay literature ever studied," whose discovery forces a rethinking of the field's boundaries. The work's egocentric voice, unconventional subject matter, and candid theological reflections make it a "refreshing and radical addition" to Malay literary studies, and it addresses what Bastin lamented in 1964 as the "almost total absence of clearly defined historical personalities" in the Malay literary record (p. 75). The author calls for a full critical edition and broader engagement with hitherto ignored manuscript collections to recover similar works.

## Context

- Primary archival collections examined: Arkib Negara Malaysia (Za'ba's private papers, SP 18 series, 108 boxes covering over 12 metres of shelving) and Cambridge University Library (Chamberlayne's Malay Manuscript Collection, Or. series).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malay manuscript studies by demonstrating the value of detailed codicological description and comparative ownership analysis in reconstructing the social and institutional contexts of manuscript production and transmission in late nineteenth-century Singapore.
