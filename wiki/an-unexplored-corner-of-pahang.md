# An unexplored corner of Pahang

C. W. S. K. composed this 1899 memorial for Sir William E. Maxwell, K.C.M.G., the Straits Settlements' most prolific contributor to the BRAS Journal and a central figure in the colony's administrative and scholarly life. The piece, published in JSBRAS Vol. 32, traces Maxwell's career from the Supreme Court through the Perak War, the Land Question, and his final posting as Governor of the Gold Coast, where he died in the prime of life. Its overarching thesis is that Maxwell was the indispensable engine behind the Society's early decades of work, and that his loss represents a gap the membership must now fill through increased collective effort.

## Summary

The obituary frames Maxwell's career as a continuous thread of administrative innovation and scholarly production. From his early years in the Supreme Court (1855–1869), where his father Sir P. Benson Maxwell was Chief Justice, through his service in the Perak War of 1875, his work as Commissioner of Land Titles, and his development of the District Administration system later replicated in the Federated Malay States, Maxwell is presented as a man whose official duties and intellectual pursuits were inseparable. The author emphasises that Maxwell's legal mastery of the Land Question was achieved "in spite of determined opposition" and that its "good effects must now be admitted by his former critics" (p. 2).

The memorial then turns to Maxwell's contributions to the BRAS itself. He served as Honorary Secretary from 1883 to 1887, a period during which, the author notes, the Society "owed its existence in a great measure to the energy of its Secretary, as it received but scanty support in the way of contributions from members" (p. 3). His published output spanned Malay proverbs, Perak manuscripts, the Sakai and other aboriginal tribes, a 67-page account of a journey on foot to the Patani frontier, the history of Perak from native sources, shamanism, land tenure law, and three edited Malay fairy tales. He also produced a *Manual of the Malay Language* and bequeathed a fine library of Malay manuscripts to the Royal Asiatic Society (pp. 3–4).

The final paragraphs assess Maxwell's wider scholarly influence, noting that he was "constantly referred to by Dutch writers, and (except on one point) always with authority" (p. 4). The author closes with a pointed warning: the Society's future depends on whether members will collectively sustain the standard Maxwell set, rather than leaving the work to a single individual, as Logan's *Journal* had been (p. 5).

### Key Findings

- Maxwell served in the Supreme Court from 1855 to 1869, qualified as an advocate in 1867, and was called to the Inner Temple bar in 1881 (p. 2).
- He served as District Commissioner with the Larut field force during the Perak War of 1875, was specially mentioned in despatches, and received the Perak Medal (p. 2).
- He was appointed Assistant Government Agent, Province Wellesley, in 1874; Assistant Resident, Perak, in 1878; Resident Councillor, Penang, 1884–1889; British Resident, Selangor, in 1889; Colonial Secretary, Straits Settlements, in 1892; and Governor of the Gold Coast thereafter (p. 2).
- He was created C.M.G. for services in connection with the Nisero affair and K.C.M.G. for distinguished services in the Ashanti Expedition (p. 2).
- He served as Honorary Secretary of the BRAS from 1883 to 1887 and was elected Vice President in 1893 (pp. 3–4).
- His bequest of a "fine library of Malay MSS." went to the Royal Asiatic Society (p. 4).

### Conclusion

The author's definitive takeaway is that Maxwell was not merely a contributor to the BRAS but its structural backbone during its most formative years, and that the Society now faces a crisis of sustainability: without a significant increase in member participation, the standard of scholarship he established cannot be maintained (p. 5).

## Context

- The author is identified only by the initials "C. W. S. K." and writes from the position of a senior Society member addressing the membership directly; the tone is that of an internal memorial rather than a detached biographical study.
- The piece draws on the Society's own publication history (Journal numbers 2, 3, and 9) and on Maxwell's known official career to construct its argument, without consulting external archival collections.
