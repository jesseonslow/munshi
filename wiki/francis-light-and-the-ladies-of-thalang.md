# Francis Light and the ladies of Thalang

E.H.S. Simmonds, writing in 1965 as a tribute to Carl Gibson-Hill, draws on a collection of fifty-nine Siamese-language letters from Thalang (modern Phuket) to reconstruct Francis Light's personal and commercial entanglements with the island's women between the 1770s and 1794. The article argues that Light's relationship with Thalang was far more complex and intimate than the standard Penang-founding narrative suggests, revealing a web of patronage, trade credit, and romantic attachment that ultimately constrained his ability to return to the island after 1785.

## Summary

Simmonds uses the Thalang letters—held in the Marsden Collection at SOAS—to trace Light's presence on the island from his first visit around 1771 through his abandonment of his establishment by 1780, and his continued correspondence with local women after he became Superintendent of Penang in 1786. The central historical problem is one of chronology and causation: why did Light, who had once claimed part of Thalang as his property and who maintained active trade there, never return after founding Penang? Simmonds suggests the answer lies not only in the political reassertion of Bangkok authority after 1786–87 and the East India Company's refusal to support a Thalang settlement, but also in a personal dimension—specifically Light's intimate connection with a woman called Chao Rat, whose former husband and associates may have made his return unwelcome.

The article examines six women who wrote to Light: Martina Rozells, his long-term partner; Lady Chan, widow of the governor Phya Phimon, who treated Light as a political patron and trade partner; her daughter Mae Prang; Chao Rat, with whom Light appears to have had a romantic relationship; and two others, Bun Kaeo and Chao Bun Chan, who wrote in terms of gratitude and commercial dependence. Simmonds also documents the practice of bond service—wives and children pledged against debts to European traders—which Light himself participated in and which is reflected in the Thai-named bonded persons he freed in his 1794 will. The letters reveal a society in which women of Thalang exercised considerable economic agency, managing trade credit, negotiating with European captains, and navigating the political tensions between local ruling families and Bangkok.

A secondary thread concerns the identity of the "Nonya" who mediated the earliest Kedah negotiations for Penang. Simmonds, following A. Francis Steuart, argues that this was almost certainly Martina Rozells herself—a woman of Portuguese-Malay origin with family connections to both Kedah and Thalang—rather than a princess, and that the romanticized "dowry" story popularized by Elisha Trapaud in 1788 was a later embellishment.

### Key Findings

- Light's house on Thalang was plundered by the Chao Phya (Superintendent-General) following a dispute over 1,700 bags of rice sold to islanders payable in tin; Light's agent died in July 1780 with 700 peculs of tin still outstanding (p. 215).
- Lady Chan's four letters to Light span December 1785 to December 1787; in her July 1787 letter she requests fifty Sutan muskets, sandalwood oil, rose-water, and goods worth 30–40 bahar, promising "all the Thalang tin shall be at your disposal" in return (p. 220).
- A bond contract of 21 June 1779 (letter no. 38) records Nai Ru'ang pledging his wife Amdaeng Bun to the ship's captain Limao for six phaen phukan of silver, with a clause that if she ran away or died the captain would have a claim on the husband himself (p. 224).
- Light's will of 20 October 1794 frees at least nine bonded persons, all bearing Thai names with the slave prefixes Ai (male) and I (female), including I Mai, Ai Bun, Ai Bun Lek, I Chan, Ai Thong, Thong Dam, Ton Chan, I San, and I Loi (p. 225).
- Chao Rat's letter of 20 April 1787 describes her as "like a nestling or a tiny fawn bereft of its mother" during Light's absence, and records that a robber stole all her remaining clothes on 8 February 1787; a note by James Scott on the back of another letter confirms Light paid 2½ bahar toward her debt and Scott paid a further 4 bahar on Light's authority (pp. 222–223).
- The Burmese siege of Thalang in February–March 1786 was called off due to failure of the besiegers' commissariat; Lady Chan and her sister Muk were later rewarded by Rama I with the titles Thao Thepkasatri and Thao Sisunthon (p. 219).

### Conclusion

Simmonds concludes that Light's failure to return to Thalang after 1785 was the product of converging political, commercial, and personal factors: the death of his ally Phya Phimon, the reassertion of Bangkok control, the Company's disinterest, and possibly a personal feud arising from his relationship with Chao Rat. The article's broader implication is that the standard Penang-founding narrative, with its romanticized Nonya and its focus on Light as a solitary pioneer, obscures the dense network of local relationships—commercial, political, and intimate—through which a European trader actually operated in the southern Siamese periphery.

## Context

- Primary archival source: the Thalang Letters, 59 manuscripts in Siamese, Marsden Collection 12157A, School of Oriental and African Studies, University of London; supplemented by Light's will (British Museum, Add. MS. 29210) and India Office Factory Records.
- The article extends Simmonds's 1963 BSOAS study of the political and arms-trade dimensions of the same letter collection, shifting focus from state-level diplomacy to the personal and domestic sphere.
