# On a collection of reptiles from Sarawak

Tan Liok Ee's 1995 article in JMBRAS Vol. 68 reconstructs the life trajectories of three prominent ethnic Chinese figures in Malaya — Tan Cheng Lock (1883–1960), Tan Kah Kee (1874–1961), and Lim Lian Geok (1901–1985) — to demonstrate how descent, though biologically given, produces radically different identity constructions depending on historical conditions, class background, and personal agency. The overarching thesis, drawing on Ien Ang's framework that "Chineseness" is constantly renegotiated rather than fixed, is that even lives set in the same locale and spanning a similar period can diverge into fundamentally different political identities: one rooted in Malayan nation-building, one in Chinese nationalism, and one in the painful negotiation between the two.

## Summary

The article uses the three men's death ceremonies as its framing device — Tan Cheng Lock received a Malayan state funeral, Tan Kah Kee a Chinese state funeral presided over by Premier Chou En-Lai, and Lim Lian Geok died stateless after the Malaysian government revoked his citizenship — to dramatize how differently the same descent was politically legible at the moment of death (pp. 1–2). From this starting point, the author traces each man's trajectory through the major historical phases of the period: the rubber boom, the rise of huaqiao nationalism in the 1920s and 1930s, the Japanese occupation, the Malayan Union crisis, the Emergency, and the road to Merdeka. The reconstruction is not a chronological digest but a thematic juxtaposition, showing how the same external pressures — colonial rule, Japanese aggression, the push towards independence — were metabolized differently by each man according to his origins, education, and the organizational contexts he inhabited.

The article's most analytically rich section brings the three lives into direct contact at the point where their paths converged on the Chinese education question. Tan Cheng Lock, an English-speaking Baba who could not speak Chinese, and Lim Lian Geok, a Chinese-educated teacher who could not speak English, met as political allies in November 1952 and again at the decisive Malacca meeting of January 1955, where a historic bargain was struck that secured Chinese schools' place within the national education system (pp. 19–20). This juxtaposition — two men with no shared language becoming co-architects of a multi-ethnic education policy — is used to illustrate the article's central argument about the contingency and constructiveness of identity. Lim Lian Geok's subsequent isolation, as UMNO leaders grew intransigent on language policy and his citizenship was ultimately revoked in 1964, stands in stark contrast to Tan Cheng Lock's state funeral and Tan Kah Kee's honoured retirement in China, underscoring that the "renegotiation" of identity carried very different risks and costs depending on one's position within the political structure.

### Key Findings

- Tan Cheng Lock was appointed Unofficial Member of the Malacca Municipal Council in 1912 at age 29; by 1935 he had been nominated to the Straits Settlements Legislative Council and was one of the few Asians appointed to its Executive Council before relinquishing all posts in frustration with the colonial system (pp. 3, 9).
- Tan Kah Kee's business empire peaked at $7.8 million in 1925 before collapsing under the Great Depression; he faced winding up in 1934, a decline the author characterizes as a "blessing in disguise" that freed him for political leadership (p. 11).
- Tan Kah Kee's 1940 Comfort Mission carried delegates across the Burma Road to visit both Chungking and Yenan; after the trip he publicly abandoned his non-partisan stance, criticizing KMT corruption and expressing admiration for CPC austerity, which made him "Public enemy No.1" to the KMT (pp. 13–14).
- Lim Lian Geok obtained Malayan citizenship on 25 September 1951; the government subsequently asked him to show cause why his citizenship should not be revoked, and he lost his appeal to the Privy Council in 1964 (pp. 18, 22).
- The January 1955 Malacca meeting produced a bargain in which Chinese school teachers agreed to temporarily refrain from demanding Chinese as an official language, in return for the Alliance's election manifesto commitment that "it would not be the Alliance's policy to destroy the schools, language and culture of any race" (p. 20).
- Tan Kah Kee was naturalized as a British subject in 1916; his father Tan Kee Peck had been granted naturalization in 1904, a marker of social standing in the colonial context (pp. 5, 14).

### Conclusion

The author's definitive historical takeaway is that the three men represent three distinct responses to the problem of Chinese identity outside China, each shaped by the specific historical conditions of its moment: Tan Kah Kee embodied the huaqiao model in which China remained the permanent centre of political and cultural life until his 1950 return; Tan Cheng Lock pioneered the model of the Malayan-born Chinese who constructs a new national identity while retaining cultural roots; and Lim Lian Geok represents the most costly transition — a migrant who chose Malaya as his permanent home and paid the ultimate price of statelessness for insisting that cultural pluralism must be part of the national compact. The article concludes that these were not merely individual choices but were structured by the political opportunities and constraints of each phase, and that the identity "renegotiations" Ien Ang describes become visible in the concrete decisions each man made at critical junctures.

## Context

- The article draws on the Tan Cheng Lock Papers held at the Institute of Southeast Asian Studies (Singapore) and at Arkib Negara (Kuala Lumpur), Lim Lian Geok's published memoirs and speeches (*Fengyu shiba nian*, 1990; *Huawen jiaoyu huyu lu*), and C.F. Yong's biography of Tan Kah Kee (1987).
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the study of Chinese political history in Malaya by placing three well-known figures in direct structural juxtaposition, demonstrating how the same historical forces produced divergent identity outcomes depending on class background, educational formation, and organizational context — a method that moves beyond single-subject biography to reveal the systemic logic of identity formation in colonial and post-colonial Southeast Asia.
