# Transmissions through practical example: women and Islam in 1920s Malay fiction

Virginia Matheson Hooker's 1994 article examines two early Malay novels—*Faridah Hanom* (1925–26) by Sayyid Shaikh al-Hadi and *Hikayat Percintaan Kasih Kemudaan* (1927) by Ahmad Kotot—to demonstrate how Egyptian reformist Islamic thought, particularly the teachings of Muhammad 'Abduh and the journal *al-Manar*, was transmitted into Malay literary culture through the narrative device of the love story. Hooker argues that both novels function as vehicles for a specifically *social* (rather than doctrinal) reformism, using the moral authority of their female protagonists to articulate a system of values—loyalty (*setia*), duty (*wajib*), and humanism (*kemanusiaan*)—that positioned Islam as the foundation for modernisation without Western secularism.

## Summary

The article situates the two novels within the broader context of late 19th- and early 20th-century Muslim communities grappling with modernity under colonial rule. Hooker draws a parallel between Egypt and Malaya as traditional societies dominated by colonial powers, lacking developed secular education systems, and experiencing rapid social change. In both, a reformist response emerged that sought to return to the Qur'an and Sunna while engaging with modernity. The Malay reformists, known as the *Kaum Muda*, translated and promoted Egyptian reformist writings, and Hooker identifies the two novels as the first works to move these ideas from the theoretical into the narrative, exploring their practical implications for individual lives.

The two novels are presented as complementary texts addressing different audiences. *Faridah Hanom*, set in Cairo with Egyptian characters, is a novel "for leaders"—its heroine Faridah is an aristocratic, wealthy, educated woman who functions as a moral guide and *murshidah*, teaching her lover Shafik (a representative of Western-educated youth) that Islam, not the West, provides the framework for modernisation. The novel's moral architecture is built on three key values that Hooker traces directly to 'Abduh's writings: *setia* (loyalty, described by 'Abduh as "the mother of all virtues"), *wajib* (duty, extended from ritual obligation to social responsibility), and *kemanusiaan* (humanism, a term Hooker suggests was given new connotations in this period). The novel's resolution of a marriage impediment through a fatwa based on *maslaha* (welfare/utility) directly mirrors 'Abduh's own rulings as Grand Mufti of Egypt. *Hikayat Percintaan Kasih Kemudaan*, by contrast, is a novel "for followers"—set in a Malay village, narrated from the male protagonist's perspective, and focused on the transformation of an inexperienced youth into a responsible adult through the wisdom of his fiancée Zamrud and the counsel of his elders. Its emphasis is on discrimination (*akal*), hard work, and the correct upbringing of children as the key to national renewal.

Hooker's analysis draws heavily on the secondary literature of Egyptian reformism—particularly Charles Adams's *Islam and Modernism in Egypt* and Albert Hourani's *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age*—to demonstrate the specific textual resonances between the novels and the writings of 'Abduh, Rashid Rida, Qasim Amin, and the journal *al-Manar*. She shows that the Malay authors were not merely reproducing translated articles but had absorbed and adapted reformist ideas into a coherent moral framework appropriate to their respective social contexts. The urban, cosmopolitan setting of *FH* presents Islamic values as absolute and non-negotiable, while the village setting of *HP* acknowledges the need for *adat dunia* (secular knowledge) alongside *ilmu agama* (religious knowledge).

### Key Findings

- *Faridah Hanom* was published in two volumes by al-Aminiyah Press, Penang, in 1925 and 1926; its first romanised edition appeared in 1964 (Pustaka Antara, Kuala Lumpur) and runs 330 pages with 20 named chapters. *Hikayat Percintaan Kasih Kemudaan* was first published in Jawi script in 1927 by Matba'ah al-Rawdzah wa Syarikah; its romanised edition (Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka, 1975) is 235 pages with 14 chapters (pp. 95, 110).
- The novel *FH* is set in Cairo in 1894 and references real historical figures and events including Muhammad 'Abduh, the journal *al-Manar*, the Mahdi uprising in the Sudan (1884–85), and Egyptian feminists such as Huda Sha'arawi, president of L'Union Feministe Egyptienne (founded March 1923) (pp. 96–97).
- The fatwa that resolves Faridah's marriage impediment is based on the principle of *maslaha* (welfare/utility), which Kerr (1966) identifies as "the most liberal principle of legal interpretation in traditional currency" and the one in which "human value judgements were allowed their widest role" (p. 102).
- Sayyid Shaikh al-Hadi (1867–1934) attended 'Abduh's lectures at al-Azhar, served as a lawyer in the Johor religious courts, and founded and taught in Arab schools in Singapore and Penang; he was in his late 50s when he wrote *FH* (p. 96). Ahmad Kotot (born 1894) was a young Malay school teacher from a traditional rural background who had not travelled outside the Peninsula (p. 96).
- Hooker identifies the three key values of *FH*—*setia*, *wajib*, and *kemanusiaan*—as corresponding to the *mu'amalat* (social dealings) domain of Islamic law, as distinct from *ibadat* (ritual worship), and notes that Rashid Rida explicitly stated that *mu'amalat* principles "are of only a general character, allowing for considerable adaptation by successive generations of Muslims in the light of the demands of their worldly welfare" (pp. 115–116).
- The 1920s–30s reformist debates in Malaya centred on outward ritual forms (*ibadat*): whether to hold *khenduri* (ritual feasts) after death, visit graves for blessings, pray without sarong or traditional head dress, and use the mosque drum to signal prayer times—yet *FH* deliberately focused on *mu'amalat* instead (pp. 115–116).

### Conclusion

Hooker's definitive takeaway is that the two 1920s Malay novels represent a selective but deeply absorbed transmission of Egyptian reformist thought, one that deliberately foregrounded the *social* and *moral* dimensions of Islam (*mu'amalat*) over the doctrinal and ritual (*ibadat*) concerns that dominated public debate in the Malay world at the time. By choosing the novel as their medium, Sayyid Shaikh al-Hadi and Ahmad Kotot were pioneers in a genre that had only recently appeared in the Malay world, and *FH* in particular established a model of Islam as a moral discourse for narrative that was not repeated in Malay fiction until the 1960s.
