# The Tampin succession

Tom Harrisson published this article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1976, arguing that the ethnonyms "Ngaju" (the upriver people of Kalimantan) and "Bajau" (the sea nomads of the Sulu Sea and northern Borneo) are not distinct racial categories but refer to segments of a single, historically fluid population that combined agricultural, jungle-foraging, and maritime roles. Drawing on 17th- and 18th-century European voyage accounts, early 20th-century ethnological fieldwork, and Bajau oral tradition, Harrisson contends that the modern separation of these groups into fixed "races" is an artifact of colonial administration and the disruption of older, more elastic patterns of life across the archipelago.

## Summary

Harrisson's central problem is one of classification: how to make sense of a people who appear in the historical record simultaneously as inland agriculturalists and as sea-going mariners, sometimes described as "pirates." He begins with Daniel Beeckman's 1718 account of the southwest coast of Borneo, where the "Byajo" are described as fierce, pagan inlanders who go naked, wear gold ear-plugs, and pull out their front teeth — yet who also appear at sea in large prows, launching attacks on European trading vessels. The same people who brought gold dust, diamonds, and rattans downriver in "very ill-shaped" boats were the same ones who "skulked" offshore as pirates. Harrisson reads this not as a contradiction but as evidence of a single population with multiple economic roles, whose coastal intermediaries (the "Banjareens") deliberately blocked European access to the inlanders to maintain a price monopoly (pp. 1–4).

He extends the argument through several parallel cases. The Orang Laut "Kappir" of Trang, southwest Thailand, recorded by Annandale and Robinson in 1901–2, had recently settled ashore from a purely aquatic existence, maintained cemeteries in limestone caves, and claimed descent from pre-Islamic populations displaced by Malay conquest of Langkawi (pp. 5–6). In Sabah, the modern Bajau of Kota Belud — now expert rice-farmers and horsemen who played polo with the Duke of Edinburgh — are the descendants of the same sea-nomads who, a century earlier, combed offshore islands for trepang and shellfish (p. 7). The wheel, Harrisson writes, has turned full circle.

The article's most significant methodological move is its turn to oral tradition as the only viable evidence for pre-literate proto-history. Harrisson seizes on Clifford Sather's 1975 publication of a Bajau Laut narrative from Semporna — the tale of Pitun Depah, a culture hero who eats a barrel of rice daily as a baby, survives his father's attempts to kill him at sea and in the forest, then demonstrates complete mastery of rice cultivation before journeying inland through seven valleys. Sather himself flagged the story as "curious" because the Bajau Laut are "not a rice-cultivating people" and "not an agricultural people," yet they celebrate a new-rice-harvest festival despite having no harvest of their own (pp. 10–11). Harrisson reads this not as an anomaly to be dismissed but as a symbolic expression of an ancient ambivalence between sea and land: in the deep past, the Bajau could plant as well as fish with equal competence, and the narrative preserves that memory (p. 11).

### Key Findings

- Beeckman's 1718 account records the first known encounter between Europeans and a "Byajo" boat at sea off the southwest Bornean coast; the crew fired "poison'd arrows" before the Europeans realised by dress and language they were not Banjareens (p. 2).
- The Banjareen coastal intermediaries explicitly blocked European trade with the inland Byajo, purchasing their goods (gold dust, diamonds, rattans, bezoar) and reselling at markup, and invented "frightful Stories" of Byajo barbarity to deter direct contact (p. 2).
- A tabulated appendix of term usages shows the progression from Byajo/Biajo/Beaju/Beadje/Viadj (17th–18th c., Kalimantan inland) through Badjoo (18th c., Sabah coast), Baju (mid-19th c.), Bajou (late 19th c.), to Bajau (20th c., both inland and sea) (p. 8).
- The Orang Laut Kappir of Trang were still living entirely in boats until within the last decade of the early 1900s; they had recently settled on Pulau Menti, then moved to Pulau Lontar where they maintained rice-fields and palm trees, and were organised into clans under chiefs called "Sinin" (p. 6).
- The Bajau Laut of Semporna celebrate an annual new-rice-harvest festival despite not cultivating rice, obtaining their vegetable food primarily as cassava by barter — a ritual residue Harrisson interprets as evidence of former agricultural competence (p. 11).
- The largest single group of people proudly using the name "Bajau" as of 1975 are the rice-farmers and horsemen of the Tempassuk River valley around Kota Belud, Sabah, who spread inland up the foothills of Mount Kinabalu (p. 7).

### Conclusion

Harrisson's definitive takeaway is that the modern taxonomic separation of "Ngaju" and "Bajau" into distinct ethnic or racial categories is a historical distortion produced by the layered imposition of Islam, European colonial administration, and the steam-and-diesel revolution in maritime trade, all of which froze formerly fluid populations into fixed, narrow specialisations. He warns that educated classifiers are "too liable to classify everything, until in so doing they over-simplify so far that they classify human reality out of recognition" (p. 9). The only remaining methodological path to testing his hypothesis is through properly recorded oral tradition, and he calls for comparative fieldwork across multiple Bajau-related groups to recover the temporal dimension that space-only classification has erased.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on early European voyage literature (Beeckman 1718; Forrest 1780), the *Fasciculi Malayensis* (Annandale & Robinson 1903), Sopher's 1965 monograph *The Sea Nomads*, and Sather's 1975 *Brunei Museum Journal* publication of a Bajau Laut narrative; no new archival collection is introduced.
- Harrisson's historiographical contribution is to reframe the "sea nomad" literature — which had been dominated by social anthropologists focused on immediate organisational structure — as a problem in historical population dynamics, arguing that the Bajau must be understood as a section of a larger land-based "nation" rather than as a specialised maritime people from the outset.
