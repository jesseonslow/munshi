# The Tampin succession

John Gullick's "The Tampin Succession" (1976) reconstructs the constitutional crisis in Negri Sembilan between 1876 and 1894, centred on the competing claims of Haji Sahil and Syed Hamid to authority over Rembau and Tampin. The article argues that Governor Weld's arbitration at Malacca in March 1883 — which deposed Haji Sahil and confirmed Syed Hamid as ruler of Tampin alone — represented a pragmatic but constitutionally novel solution that dismantled both the earlier Johore-mediated arrangement and the hereditary pretensions of the Rembau royal house.

## Summary

The article begins by tracing British policy in Negri Sembilan from 1876, when Governor Jervois, unable to obtain Colonial Office approval for direct protection, instead introduced the Maharaja of Johore as a supervisory intermediary. Jervois signed a treaty with Haji Sahil in March 1877 that secured British recognition for the Undang of Rembau but was constitutionally defective because it lacked the signatures of the eight Suku Datuk whose consent was essential to the validity of any engagement dealing with Rembau's rights (p. 2). The arrangement gave the Maharaja a supervisory role over disputes, which in practice meant he could be relied upon to support Haji Sahil against Syed Hamid. Governor Weld, who succeeded Jervois, set out to reverse this policy as soon as circumstances permitted. His first move came in April 1881 when he met Yam Tuan Antah at the Bukit Putus pass and declared that the Governor's duty obliged him to act directly, not through the Maharaja, effectively nullifying the 1876 treaty's supervisory clause (p. 4).

The crisis came to a head in early 1883 when the murder of Laksamana Budin, for which Haji Sahil was widely believed responsible, drove his opponents to threaten military action. Weld summoned the principal parties to Malacca at the end of March 1883. The proceedings combined a trial of Haji Sahil on fourteen charges of misgovernment and homicide with an election of a new Undang by the Rembau electorate. Weld rejected the request for a British Resident in Rembau, instead offering informal guidance. After three days of enquiry, the voters — 23 out of a possible 24 — acquiesced in Sahil's deposition and elected Serun bin Saidin as the new Datuk Penghulu (pp. 10–12). Syed Hamid was not installed as Yam Tuan Muda; Weld explicitly declined to appoint a Raja Muda, stating that the desire for one was not universal and that the Governor himself could serve as the final arbiter (p. 13).

Gullick then devotes a substantial appendix to the genealogy of the Rembau royal house, demonstrating that the office of Yam Tuan Muda had no consistent principle of patrilineal inheritance. Raja Asil, the first holder, was succeeded by the usurper Raja Ali (related only through his mother), who in turn was succeeded by Syed Shaaban, an Arab of Malacca origin who had no blood connection to Rembau except through marriage to two of Raja Ali's daughters (p. 6). By the time of the 1883 arbitration, the patrilineal descendants of both Raja Asil and Raja Ali had effectively vanished from the political scene, leaving Syed Hamid as the only claimant in the field — a fact that both Hervey and Weld took for granted without examining the constitutional implications (p. 7).

### Key Findings

- The 1877 treaty between Jervois and Haji Sahil was invalid under Rembau constitutional law because it lacked the signatures of the eight Suku Datuk, a requirement established as early as 1832 (p. 2).
- Weld's 1881 declaration to Yam Tuan Antah that the Governor would communicate directly with Malay rulers, bypassing the Maharaja of Johore, effectively terminated the 1876 supervisory arrangement without formal abrogation (p. 4).
- At the Malacca meeting of 31 March 1883, 23 of 24 eligible Rembau voters participated in the deposition of Haji Sahil and election of Serun bin Saidin; Weld justified this as representative of Negri Sembilan *kebulatan* principle, though the standard actually requires 100% participation (p. 10).
- The treaty of 31 March 1883 was signed by representatives of Sri Menanti, Perak, Selangor, and Sungei Ujong in addition to the Rembau chiefs, binding them in a confederation to uphold the arrangement — an unprecedented assembly for the affairs of a single state (p. 13).
- Syed Hamid's claim to the office of Yam Tuan Muda rested on no principle of patrilineal inheritance; the succession from Raja Asil through Raja Ali to Syed Shaaban was achieved by usurpation and marriage, not descent, and the patrilineal lines of both Raja Asil and Raja Ali had died out or migrated (pp. 6–7).
- The 1889 treaty confederated the Negri Sembilan states under a British Resident at Kuala Pilah, with an explicit clause that no ruler should exercise authority beyond the state he already possessed — thereby fixing Syed Hamid's position as ruler of Tampin only (p. 16).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that Weld's 1883 settlement, while "rather rough justice," was the only workable outcome given the facts on the ground: Haji Sahil was untenable after the murder charge, Syed Hamid's claim to the broader office of Yam Tuan Muda lacked constitutional foundation, and the Rembau electorate itself did not universally desire a Raja Muda (p. 7). The British had effectively created a new constitutional category — an independent ruler of Raja status isolated from both the Sri Menanti dynasty and the Rembau electoral college — for which there was no precedent in Negri Sembilan theory (p. 14). Syed Hamid preserved this position through the 1889 confederation but died in 1894 impoverished and under a cloud, having achieved recognition by "sheer persistence" despite a "dubious title and an inheritance of family controversy" (p. 17).

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the CO 273 series at the Public Records Office (Weld's despatches to the Colonial Office) and on the Maxwell and Gibson collection of treaties (1924), with supplementary reference to the oral Malay tradition as recorded by Hervey, Begbie, Newbold, and Wilkinson (p. 35).
- The paper was prompted by an approach from the patrilineal descendants of Raja Ali, who still considered their claim to the office of Tengku Besar of Tampin valid, and who contacted the Honorary Editor of the Journal (p. 7).
