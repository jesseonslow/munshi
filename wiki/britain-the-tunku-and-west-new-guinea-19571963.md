# Britain, the Tunku and West New Guinea 1957–1963

Nicholas Tarling (2010) examines how the West New Guinea dispute between the Netherlands and Indonesia shaped British and Malayan foreign policy between 1957 and 1963, arguing that the crisis was a decisive but underappreciated catalyst for Tunku Abdul Rahman's proposal to create Greater Malaysia. Drawing primarily on British archival records, the article reconstructs the Tunku's failed mediation efforts and shows how Indonesian military build-up, facilitated by Soviet arms supplies, transformed the security calculus for both London and Kuala Lumpur.

## Summary

The article addresses a gap in two intersecting historiographies: the history of the West New Guinea dispute, which had been told predominantly from Dutch, Australian, and American perspectives, and the history of Malaysia's creation, which had not adequately accounted for the Indonesian threat as a motivating factor. Tarling's central argument is that the Tunku's proposal to incorporate Singapore, North Borneo, Sarawak, and Brunei into an enlarged Federation was driven significantly by anxiety over Indonesia's growing military power and its potential designs on the Borneo territories, not merely by demographic or economic considerations.

The narrative traces how the Tunku, as a newly independent leader, attempted to mediate between the Netherlands and Indonesia through a UN trusteeship formula that would ultimately transfer West New Guinea to Indonesia. British officials, particularly Fred Warner at the Foreign Office, opposed this scheme on the grounds that it denied the Papuans a voice, was legally inconsistent with Article 76 of the UN Charter, and would set a dangerous precedent for British dependencies. The Tunku's mediation ultimately failed when Indonesian Foreign Minister Subandrio publicly rejected any settlement short of direct transfer, and the crisis culminated in the New York Agreement of August 1962, brokered by the United States.

Throughout, Tarling uses British diplomatic correspondence to illuminate the strategic logic behind both British and Malayan decisions. The British position was constrained by the need to avoid a war that would collapse their remaining Southeast Asian commitments while managing the expectations of a Commonwealth partner whose security was directly at stake. The Malayan position was constrained by domestic politics: UMNO's pro-Indonesian sympathies made it impossible for the Tunku to take an openly anti-Indonesia stance, yet the security implications of Indonesian militarisation demanded a structural response.

### Key Findings

- The Tunku first raised the idea of enlarging the Federation to include Singapore, North Borneo, Sarawak, and Brunei on 10 June 1960, in a conversation with Lord Perth at the Colonial Office, prompted by the dispatch of the Dutch aircraft carrier *Karel Doorman* to New Guinea waters in May 1960 (p. 80).
- The Tunku's initial mediation formula proposed a UN trusteeship with ultimate transfer to Indonesia; Prime Minister Djuanda accepted only on the condition that the trust period not exceed one year, and that Indonesia's concurrence was limited to the peace-and-security provisions of Article 76 of the UN Charter (pp. 82–83).
- Fred Warner at the Foreign Office opposed the trusteeship proposal, warning that "the next stage would almost certainly be Indonesian agitation for Australian New Guinea and the British Borneo territories" and that the scheme was "quite unacceptable to the Dutch" (p. 83).
- The British High Commissioner in Kuala Lumpur, Geofroy Tory, assessed in December 1958 that the "ultimate Indonesian aim" was to absorb Malaya, and that if the government failed to create a Malay consciousness, Malay leaders might look to Indonesia as a way of preserving Malay dominance (p. 79).
- The New York Agreement was concluded in August 1962 with Ellsworth Bunker as US interlocutor, transferring West New Guinea to UN administration and thence to Indonesia; the British embassy in Jakarta speculated that the settlement would be "too much of an anticlimax" for Sukarno, who would be left with "the tiresome and unromantic problem of setting up a civil administration in Hollandia" (p. 88).
- The Tunku declared in the Malayan parliament that Malaya would be neutral if hostilities broke out, and when Acting UN Secretary-General Thant asked him to influence Indonesia against aggression, he replied that he was "not prepared to stick his neck out a second time" (p. 88).

### Conclusion

Tarling's definitive takeaway is that the West New Guinea crisis was a crucial structural factor in the creation of Malaysia, one that has been systematically underplayed in existing accounts. The future of Singapore and the Borneo territories was "crucial for both" the British and Malayan governments, and "it is hard to argue that they were not seen in the context provided by the West New Guinea crisis" (p. 89). The crisis made Malaysia both more necessary and more likely to provoke Indonesian confrontation, a dynamic that the article links directly to the second confrontation that followed Malaysia's formation in September 1963.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on National Archives, Kew records from the Foreign Office, Commonwealth Relations Office, and Colonial Office, with no equivalent Malayan archival material available; Tarling acknowledges this asymmetry and its implications for understanding the centre of Malayan decision-making (p. 89).
- The study contributes to the historiography of the West New Guinea dispute by supplying the British and Malayan perspectives, which had been absent from earlier accounts that focused on Dutch, Australian, and American viewpoints (p. 89).
