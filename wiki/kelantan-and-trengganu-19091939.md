# Kelantan and Trengganu, 1909–1939

Chan Su-ming's 1965 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* provides a comprehensive administrative history of the Unfederated Malay States of Kelantan and Trengganu during the first three decades of British protection (1909–1939), following the transfer of Siamese suzerainty under the Anglo-Siamese Treaty of March 1909. The overarching thesis is that the British advisory system, though constrained by treaty limitations and a deliberately cautious pace, succeeded in transforming two deeply backward states into functioning modern polities while preserving their indigenous Malay character.

## Summary

The article is structured around a comparative treatment of the two states, which entered British protection under markedly different conditions. Kelantan, having already experienced a decade of Siamese administrative reform under W. A. Graham's Resident Commissionership (1902–1909), possessed a rudimentary but functional framework of government—courts, a police force, organised departments, and a revenue system that had grown from practically nil to $320,000 by 1909. Trengganu, by contrast, was in a state of near-anarchy: the Sultan was a figurehead reduced to the demands of territorial chiefs who controlled autonomous river districts, debt slavery persisted, secular education was absent, and the Farm System of revenue collection bred widespread corruption. Chan draws on the 1918 Commission of Enquiry into Trengganu, which found conditions so deplorable that it recommended "fundamental alterations" and actual British control, to underscore the gulf between the two states at the point of transfer (pp. 159–190).

The central narrative problem the article addresses is how the advisory system—designed to preserve the sovereignty of the Sultans while enabling modernisation—functioned in practice. In Kelantan, the first two decades were a "period of construction" during which the British Adviser and a small European staff rebuilt the administrative machinery from scratch, integrating Malay chiefs into the new structure while introducing institutions such as the Conservancy Board (1912), the Majlis Ugama dan Istiadat Melayu (1916), and a codified legal system. The Duff Development Company Concession, which had granted a private company quasi-sovereign powers over vast tracts of interior land in 1900, dominated Kelantan's financial history and consumed the state in debt until a final settlement in 1930. In Trengganu, the initial arrangement of a British Agent with no advisory powers proved wholly inadequate; it was only after the 1919 Agreement replaced the Agent with a full Adviser that meaningful reform became possible, and even then progress was hampered by the Sultan's initial resistance and the entrenched power of the Ruling House (pp. 183–198).

Chan's argument is that the advisory system in these two states operated in its "purest form" precisely because the absence of significant non-Malay economic interests and the deliberate policy of restricting immigration allowed the British to direct development almost exclusively toward Malay welfare. The result was a transformation that was "just short of revolutionary" yet carefully calibrated to avoid the appearance of direct rule. The Sultans and their Councils were maintained as the formal sovereign authority, but in practice the British Adviser and European departmental heads controlled policy, while Malay officers were progressively trained to assume executive responsibilities. By 1938, both states had achieved a degree of administrative coherence, financial stability, and social development that, while still lagging behind the Federated Malay States, represented a decisive break from the medieval conditions of 1909 (pp. 198–201).

### Key Findings

- Kelantan's revenue rose from $400,000 in 1910 to $3,209,722 in 1937, but the state's external debt stood at $5,119,069 in 1938, largely a legacy of the Duff Development Company dispute, in which the 1925 Arbitration Award of Sir E. A. Speed adjudged £378,000 in damages to the Company and the final 1930 settlement cost the state £140,000 (pp. 166–167, 175).
- Trengganu's 1918 Commission of Enquiry found that the Ruling House absorbed 31.5% of total state expenditure and 24% of total revenue, while costs of administration consumed 67% of total expenditure; the Sultan's personal emolument alone was $6,000 per month (pp. 185–186).
- The 1919 Agreement replacing the British Agent with a British Adviser in Trengganu immediately reduced the Sultan's monthly allowance from $9,500 (as raised by Sultan Muhammad II) to $3,500, prompting his abdication within eighteen months (p. 187).
- By 1938, Kelantan had 291 miles of road and was served by the East Coast Line of the F.M.S. Railway (opened 1931); Trengganu had 145 miles of road by 1927 compared with only 10 in 1915, and the new road linking Trengganu to Kelantan opened in 1932 (pp. 173, 194).
- The 1938 Census recorded Kelantan's population as 399,299 (350,526 Malay) and Trengganu's as 179,789 (164,564 Malay), confirming the near-total preservation of Malay demographic character in both states (p. 201).
- Kelantan's mortality rate was reduced from 40 per mille to under 20 per mille over the service of two medical officers (Dr. J. D. Gimlette and Dr. L. W. Evans), and cholera and smallpox, which had claimed one in two children, were practically unknown by the late 1930s (p. 174).

### Conclusion

Chan Su-ming concludes that thirty years of British influence in Kelantan and Trengganu produced changes "just short of revolutionary," achieved not through innovation imposed from without but through the correction and development of indigenous administrative structures. The advisory system, in these two states, functioned as an experiment in "raising a people by development of its own forces from within," with the British providing guidance and clearing obstacles while Malay officers progressively assumed the responsibilities of governance. The preservation of Malay demographic and cultural character, the deliberate restriction of alien immigration, and the pro-Malay orientation of policy distinguished these states from the Federated Malay States and made them, in Chan's assessment, the purest expression of the advisory system's theoretical principles.

## Context

- The article draws extensively on the Annual Reports of Kelantan and Trengganu (A.R.K. and A.R.T.), the 1918 Commission of Enquiry into Trengganu, W. A. Graham's *Kelantan: A Handbook of Information* (1908), and R. Emerson's *Malaysia: A Study in Direct and Indirect Rule* (1937), representing a study grounded in official colonial administrative records.
- Historiographically, the article represents an early systematic treatment of the Unfederated Malay States' administrative development under British protection, filling a gap in the literature that had previously focused predominantly on the Federated States. It predates the more critical post-independence historiography and reflects the administrative perspective of the colonial period, though Chan's analysis of the advisory system's limitations and the Sultans' reduced political significance is notably candid.
