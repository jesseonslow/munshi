# Malay proverbs

W.E. Maxwell, a British official stationed in Perak, published this collection of Malay proverbs in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1879, presenting it as a continuation of a prior volume (the numbering resumes at 198). The work argues that Malay proverbs are not merely folkloric curiosities but a reliable window into the social values, legal customs, and historical memory of the Malay people, possessing an originality of thought that merits comparison with Western and other Asian traditions.

## Summary

The article presents approximately 104 numbered proverbs (198–301), each given in Malay with an English rendering followed by Maxwell's commentary. The commentary ranges from brief linguistic glosses (identifying obscure or dialectal words such as *amris*, *biang*, *sior*, and *grenying*) to extended cultural and historical digressions. Several entries draw on Maxwell's direct observation of Perak society: the ordeal by diving (*ta'bertepat janji, ta'bertiban taroh, ta'bertangkap mangmang*) is described in full procedural detail from the testimony of a Malay chief who witnessed it at Tanjong Sanendang during the reign of Sultan Abdullah Mohamed Shah (pp. 29–30); the murder of a former Shahbandar by a Panglima Prang is recounted as the origin of a specific poetic challenge (p. 37); and the social dynamics of polygamous households are analysed through a cluster of related sayings (pp. 43–44).

The concluding analytical section (pp. 42–49) constitutes the article's principal argumentative contribution. Maxwell contends that Malay proverbs are "pointed and idiomatic" and sometimes embody ideas "well worthy of Western races," citing maxims on honour, the condemnation of the *nouveau riche*, and the aristocratic faith in good blood. He traces a small number of proverbs to foreign sources—Hindustani (*Juhari juhar pachane*; *Lathi-se pani juda nahin nota*), Arabic or Biblical (Proverbs XXVI, 27, "Whoso diggeth a pit shall fall therein"), and Buddhist (a parallel in the Singhalese *Prataya Sataka*)—while insisting that the great majority are of indigenous origin. He also identifies a class of "historical proverbs" commemorating specific events, including the Dutch siege of Palembang and the Dutch factory on the Perak river, and a class of "local proverbs" that extol or satirise particular villages and districts within Perak.

Throughout, Maxwell positions his collection as complementary to the earlier work of Klinkert (Dutch) and Favre (French), noting that specimens in those works have not yet been rendered into English and inviting future translators to supplement the corpus.

### Key Findings

- The Perak ordeal by diving required the sanction of the Sultan and the presence of the *Orang Besar Ampat*; the loser paid a fine in addition to forfeiting his stakes (half of which went to the Raja), plus fixed fees of $6.25 for the use of the *balei*, $12.50 to the *krani*, and $5 to each of the two boys who carried the sealed bamboo tubes (pp. 29–30).
- A Dutch factory operated on the Perak river during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries; the proverb *Ai ka-lagi-lagi bagei blanda minta tanah* ("More, more! like the Dutchmen asking for land") is attributed to a forgotten land transaction between the early Dutch traders and the Raja (p. 20).
- The poetic challenge in No. 282 (*Kalau ta'bermariam baiklah diam…*) was sent by a Perak Shahbandar in reply to a complaint by Panglima Prang Smahon; a few days later the Panglima Prang, with three companions, ambushed and killed the Shahbandar on the riverbank as he returned home after ablution before the *maghrib* prayers (p. 37).
- Maxwell identifies a parallel to the Perak ordeal by diving in Hamilton's *New Accounts of the East Indies* (1727), which describes a similar water ordeal in Pegu in which the accuser and accused grip a stake driven into the riverbed and the one who remains submerged longest is credited (p. 30).
- The Menangkabau Code is cited as the source for at least two proverbs: the injunction that a ryot must "go when told and come when bidden" (*Disuroh pergi dipanggil datang*, No. 274) and the figurative description of a woman as *ibu Iblis saudara segala Sheitan* ("the mother of Satan and the sister of all the devils") (pp. 35, 44).
- Maxwell notes that the Malay proverb *Barang siapa menggali lobang iya juga terprosok kadalamnia* ("Whosoever digs a pit shall fall into it himself") appears in the *Hikayat Abdullah* (p. 165) and is likely a translation from either Proverbs XXVI, 27 or a Mohamedan legend recorded by Burton in his *Pilgrimage to El-Medinah and Meccah* (II, 265) (p. 49).

### Conclusion

Maxwell's definitive historical takeaway is that Malay proverbs constitute an original and substantial body of popular wisdom that reflects a society intensely aristocratic in its principles, conservative in its belief in good blood, and governed by a code of personal honour in which dishonour is considered worse than death. He explicitly rejects the contemporary European characterisation of Malays as "treacherous and faithless," arguing that the proverbs themselves condemn bad faith and that the diplomatic cunning for which Malays were credited is better understood as a rational response to inbred suspicion born of political experience.

## Context

- Maxwell was a British administrative officer in Perak during the period of the Pangkor Treaty (1874) and the subsequent installation of a British Resident; his access to Malay chiefs, court proceedings, and local oral tradition was a direct product of this colonial administrative position.
- The article is framed as a contribution to English-language Malay studies, explicitly positioned alongside and in deference to the earlier Dutch (Klinkert) and French (Favre) collections, and it reflects the late-Victorian scholarly convention of treating non-European folk wisdom as a legitimate object of philological and anthropological inquiry.
