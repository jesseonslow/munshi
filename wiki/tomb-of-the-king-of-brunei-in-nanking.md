# Tomb of “The King of Brunei” in Nanking

The 1920 Annual Report of the Straits Branch, Royal Asiatic Society, compiled by Hon. Secretary J. C. Moulton and V. Knight, provides a comprehensive administrative and financial account of the Society's activities during a period of post-war adjustment. The report documents membership fluctuations, journal output, and financial positions while raising concerns about the declining proportion of active contributors relative to the Society's total membership.

## Summary

The report opens with a review of membership changes during 1920, noting the deaths of five members—including the eminent Singapore lawyer Sir Evelyn Ellis and W. H. Mackray, co-author of the Society's notable "History of Rembau"—and the resignation of 29 members, 19 of whom were removed under Rule 6 for non-payment of subscriptions. The Council acknowledged that the membership roll had been "fictitiously large" in recent years, with some members retained despite seven years of arrears, and stated that the revised total of 329 now represented the true active membership (p. viii). Sir Laurence Guillemard, K.C.B., was installed as Patron in succession to Sir Arthur Young, who had left the country in 1919.

The report then turns to the Society's scholarly output, recording that two Journal issues (Nos. 81 and 82) were published in March and September, totalling 226 pages—down from 306 pages per annum in the Society's first forty years. The subject distribution was dominated by fourteen papers on Malayan folk-lore, literature, and local history, with smaller contributions in philology, botany, and zoology. The Council expressed concern that the burden of authorship fell on too few: only twelve members contributed papers, representing roughly 3–4 per cent of the membership, despite the fact that 83 per cent resided in Malaya and were therefore well placed to contribute (pp. viii–ix).

Financially, the Society was in a sound position, with total balances of $8,309.27 carried forward, an increase over the previous year's $7,142.89. Subscription income of $1,670 represented a satisfactory rise over the five-year average of $1,127, partly aided by $435 in arrears collected for 1915–1919. The Council noted that rising paper and printing costs would make future Journals approximately 120 per cent more expensive than the last pre-war issue, and flagged that Dr. E. D. Merrill's forthcoming paper on the Flora of Borneo—some 600 pages in length—would absorb a large proportion of the Society's balance (pp. ix–x). A notable policy change was the decision to place portions of the Society's library on indefinite loan to the Botanic Gardens, the F.M.S. Geological Department, and the Raffles Museum, reflecting the practical difficulty of serving a dispersed membership from a single Singapore location.

### Key Findings

- Active membership stood at 329 after the removal of 29 members (19 under Rule 6 for non-payment), a correction of what the Council described as a "fictitiously large" roll (p. viii).
- Journal output for 1920 was 226 pages across two issues, well below the 306-page annual average of the Society's first forty years (p. viii).
- Only 12 of 329 members (approximately 3–4 per cent) contributed papers during the year, a figure the Council identified as the Society's principal weakness (p. ix).
- Total financial balances carried forward were $8,309.27, including $2,500 in Victory Loan, $2,200 in S.S. War Loan, and $2,000 in fixed deposits (pp. ix–x).
- Dr. E. D. Merrill's paper on the Flora of Borneo, approximately 600 pages, was expected to be completed in early 1921 and would absorb a large proportion of the Society's balance (p. x).
- The Society's library was restructured by placing botanical, geological, and museum journals on indefinite loan to the Botanic Gardens, the F.M.S. Geological Department, and the Raffles Museum respectively (p. x).

### Conclusion

The report presents a Society in stable but cautious condition: financially solvent and maintaining a respectable scholarly output, yet hampered by a small pool of active contributors and the escalating costs of post-war printing. The Council's tone is one of measured optimism—acknowledging structural weaknesses while expressing confidence that the "supply has by no means run dry" and that the Society could sustain its output without raising subscriptions, provided membership remained above 300.

## Context

- This is an official administrative dispatch of a colonial-era learned society, written in the formal register of British imperial institutional reporting. The Council's framing of membership as a resource to be "tapped" and its concern with "active assistance of members" reflect the top-down governance norms of the period.
- The report is a primary source for the institutional history of the Straits Branch and for understanding the social composition of the colonial expatriate community in Singapore and the Straits Settlements in the immediate post-war period.
