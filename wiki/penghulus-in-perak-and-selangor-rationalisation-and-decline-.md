# Penghulus in Perak and Selangor: rationalisation and decline of a traditional Malay office

Paul H. Kratoska published this comparative administrative history in 1984 in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, tracing the Penghulu (subdistrict headman) in Perak and Selangor from the establishment of British residential rule in 1874 through the post-independence period. The article's central argument is that the colonial administration created an unresolvable tension between two incompatible models of the office—the traditional local leader whose authority derived from personal influence and community standing, and the bureaucratized government servant selected for education and administrative efficiency—and that this tension, compounded by the arrival of electoral politics after 1957, led to the progressive decline of the Penghulu as a meaningful point of articulation between state and rural community.

## Summary

The article examines how the British administration in the Federated Malay States incorporated the Penghulu into a new bureaucratic order while simultaneously altering the character of the office. The fundamental dilemma was that the characteristics which made a Penghulu effective at the local level—family ties, personal reputation, long residence in the mukim—were precisely those that conflicted with the requirements of a rationalized civil service: willingness to accept transfer, literacy in English, and subordination to departmental directives. Perak and Selangor followed divergent paths in response to this dilemma. Perak, where the population was predominantly indigenous and the challenge was to legitimize a new government in the eyes of established communities, moved steadily toward bureaucratization: formal salary scales, systematic transfers to higher-class mukims, and merit-based selection. Selangor, which contained large populations of recent Sumatran and other immigrant Malays requiring strong local authority to manage, retained greater reliance on aristocratic appointees and local selection, at least through the 1930s.

As the government's rural agenda expanded from basic land registration and smallpox vaccination to welfare programmes and then to a major push for commercial rice production after 1930, the demands placed on Penghulus proliferated to an extraordinary degree. The Surat Kuasa, originally a brief document of appointment, became an unwieldy legal instrument; a separate handbook (the *Buku Pemimpin Penghulu*) was compiled to codify duties drawn from dozens of separate enactments. Simultaneously, the Penghulu's role as community leader—hosting visiting officials, hosting feasts at the end of Ramadan, burying the indigent, maintaining personal dignity through conspicuous consumption—imposed financial and social obligations that a modest salary could not cover. The development of the *Ketua Kampong* (village headman) as a subordinate tier added further complexity to the administrative hierarchy at the local level.

After independence, the arrival of electoral politics proved decisive in settling the question of what the Penghulu would be. As civil servants, Penghulus were constrained in their political participation, and the bureaucratization process had already severed the personal ties to their communities that would have made them valuable to political parties. The *Ketua Kampong*, not being a government servant, was free to join parties and, knowing residents personally, became the more effective political intermediary. The Penghulu thus completed its transformation from a figure of local authority into a routine administrative clerk, a process the article documents through the shifting language of official correspondence, salary schemes, and selection criteria across six decades.

### Key Findings

- The 1907 Perak salary scheme classified mukims into four grades (Special, First, Second, Third) with annual salary ranges from $180–$240 (Assistant) to $900–$1,200 (Special Class); promotion required transfer to a mukim of the next higher class, a practice that drew sustained criticism from the 1920s onward (pp. 35–36, 56).
- In 1904, 18 of 43 Penghulus in Selangor were Rajas of royal descent; by 1924 the figure was 19 of 40. In Perak, only 8 of 64 Penghulus held that status in 1915, though many non-royal appointees had connections to the royal establishment (pp. 36–37).
- The *Buku Pemimpin Penghulu*, issued in the 1930s to clarify Penghulus' statutory powers and duties, comprised 230 pages drawn from all or part of 83 separate enactments (p. 41).
- Between September and December 1932, 2,251 Notices were served under the Irrigation Enactment in the Krian area; only 58 instances of non-compliance resulted, 27 were prosecuted, 12 convicted, with fines averaging less than $7 (p. 45).
- A 1975 memorandum by the Malaysian Penghulus' Association listed 99 duties and responsibilities under 20 different headings, illustrating the continued proliferation of the role after independence (p. 48).
- In 1937, approximately 88% of Perak Penghulus were over forty years of age, reflecting a long-standing tendency to appoint older men; by 1978 the service had partially rejuvenated, with 29 of 65 Penghulus aged 30–39 (p. 51, Table 1).

### Conclusion

Kratoska's definitive takeaway is that the Penghulu's decline was not simply a story of colonial imposition destroying a traditional institution, but of structural incompatibility within the colonial order itself. Both the "traditional" and the "bureaucratic" variants were creations of British rule: the former was stripped of the mechanisms of power—command over forced labour, independent revenue collection, and patronage—that had sustained pre-colonial authority, leaving a position dependent on personal character that offered few inducements to attract men of strong character; the latter lacked the local roots that gave the office its original purpose. Independence delivered the final blow by rendering the Penghulu's interpretive function unnecessary (the government now shared a language with the governed) and by introducing electoral politics that the bureaucratized, transferred, and politically constrained Penghulu could not serve, pushing the office irreversibly into the general civil service.

## Context

- Primary sources are drawn from the Malaysian National Archives (Selangor and Perak State Secretariat files, District Office records, State Council minutes, Perak Government Gazette) and the Service Section of the Perak State Secretariat, supplemented by oral interviews conducted in 1974 (p. 53).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its comparative approach, demonstrating how divergent local demographic conditions—indigenous population in Perak versus large immigrant communities in Selangor—produced different trajectories of bureaucratization within the same colonial administrative system, and in situating the Penghulu within broader debates on the transformation of indigenous institutions under indirect rule.
