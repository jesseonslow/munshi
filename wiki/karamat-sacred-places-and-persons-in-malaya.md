# Karamat: sacred places and persons in Malaya

R.O. Winstedt, a senior colonial administrator and scholar in the Straits Settlements, published this article in 1924 in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 2, No. 3), presenting a systematic catalogue of fifty-one sacred places and persons (*karamat*) across the Malay Peninsula. The article argues that the study of *karamat* illuminates both the indigenous nature-worship and ancestor-worship of the primitive Malays and the "facile canonization" of Muslim saints inherited from the Indian subcontinent, with syncretism rendering the original kernel of many traditions difficult to isolate (p. 264).

## Summary

Winstedt organizes the fifty-one recorded *karamat* into six typological categories: natural objects (rocks, hilltops, capes, whirlpools); sacred tigers and crocodiles; graves of magicians; graves of settlement founders; graves of Muslim saints; and living Muslim saints (p. 264). This classification reveals a layered religious landscape in which pre-Islamic animist practices have been absorbed into, or overlaid by, Islamic saint-veneration. The traditions frequently involve a Muslim *jin* or a *Sayid* being invoked to sanctify a pre-existing nature-worship site, as in the case of Pulau Aur, where a dead Sayid was "dragged in of recent years to hallow the nature-worship centring round" the island (p. 264). The article draws on oral tradition collected from local informants, *pawangs*, and community memory rather than written sources, making it a record of living popular religion as it existed in the 1910s and 1920s.

A recurring analytical thread is the phenomenon of syncretism, which Winstedt identifies as the principal obstacle to understanding the "kernel" of each tradition. For instance, the *karamat* at a site comprising hills, a founder, a saint, and a shadowy grave tradition is explicitly described as a composite product of syncretism (p. 272). The veneration of these sites is notably cross-ethnic: Malays, Tamils, Indians, Chinese, and Eurasians are all recorded as frequenting the same sacred spots, and in several cases Chinese patrons have built physical shrines over Muslim saints' graves, as at *Karamat Tangga China* near Ampang (p. 274). Winstedt also notes diagnostic physical marks associated with sanctity—both in the living (hairy uvula, dwarfism, a partially black tongue, short-sightedness) and in the dead (saffron-like fairness of the corpse, the mysterious separation of head and foot stones of a grave)—which function as empirical proof of a person's saintly status in popular belief (pp. 264, 277–279).

The article closes with an explicit invitation for other enquirers to add to the record, and a cross-reference to W.W. Skeat's *Malay Magic* (pp. 61–71, 153, 163–165, 283) for complementary material on the magical and ritual dimensions of these sites.

### Key Findings

- Fifty-one sacred places and persons are catalogued across the Peninsula, classified into six typological groups: natural objects, sacred animals, graves of magicians, graves of settlement founders, graves of Muslim saints, and living saints (p. 264).
- The grave of Dato' Janggut at Kampong Jalan Baharu, Penang, belongs to a religious teacher born in 1824 and died in 1884; after burial, a voice reciting the Quran was heard from the grave, and a figure resembling the deceased was seen reading a religious book on a Friday night (p. 274).
- At Ketapang, Pekan, Pahang, the head and foot stones of the grave of the pious unmarried Malay Jaafar (*To' Panjang*) separated to a length of 15 feet after his death, confirming his saintly status (p. 275). A parallel case at Kampong Marhum, Kuala Pahang, records a grave that parted to 30 feet after an angelic figure confirmed its sanctity (p. 275).
- The *karamat* of Che Nah, a girl whose body turned to stone after becoming trapped in a cave crevice, is venerated by Malays, Tamils, Indians, and Eurasians; a hut has been built over the sacred stone (p. 268).
- At *Karamat Tangga China* (Ampang, three miles from Kuala Lumpur), the animals of a pious Sumatran immigrant—his pony, cow, and buffalo—refused food and died at his grave in succession, prompting the community to recognize him as a saint; rich Chinese subsequently built a shrine over the grave (p. 274).
- A living saint near Simpang Lima, Klang, possessed a hairy uvula which he clipped weekly, could read thoughts, and cured the sick by the words "Be well"; a test for whether a dead saint would fulfil a vow involved declaring that a stick should grow longer if help was to come (p. 276).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that the *karamat* tradition in Malaya represents a palimpsest of religious belief in which primitive nature-worship and ancestor-worship have been continuously reinterpreted through the lens of Islamic saint-veneration, producing a syncretic popular religion that transcends ethnic and doctrinal boundaries. The article serves as both a typological framework and an empirical baseline, documenting the physical, ritual, and narrative features of sacred sites before they were further altered by modernization.

## Context

- Winstedt held senior administrative posts in the Straits Settlements and the Federated Malay States and was a founding figure of the Royal Asiatic Society's Malayan Branch; this article reflects the colonial-era scholarly interest in cataloguing indigenous belief systems as objects of ethnographic study.
- The primary evidential basis is oral tradition gathered from local communities and *pawangs*, supplemented by cross-reference to W.W. Skeat's *Malay Magic*; the study's historiographical contribution lies in establishing the first systematic typology of *karamat* in the Malay Peninsula.
