# About Kinta

H.W.C. Leech, a legal professional and government official in the Straits Settlements, published "About Kinta" in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1879, presenting a geographical, geological, and ethnographical survey of the Kinta River valley and its eastern tributaries in Perak. The article's overarching purpose is to document an almost entirely unexplored interior for the benefit of prospective European settlers, miners, and the colonial administration, arguing that the region's tin deposits, limestone formations, and agricultural potential warranted immediate investment and further exploration.

## Summary

Leech's account is structured as a progressive geographical survey moving eastward from the Kinta River through its tributaries—the Sungei Raya, Kampar, Batang Padang, Bidor, Songkei, and Slim—drawing on his own two expeditions into the interior (one in August 1878 with Ceylon coffee planters Christie and Handyside, and a second in March 1879 commissioned by the Perak Government) as well as correspondence from Mr. Deane, a Ceylonese surveyor in treaty with the Perak Government. The narrative interweaves physical description with practical assessment: the limestone cliffs rising 500 to 2,000 feet from the plains are noted for their crystalline purity and absence of fossils; the tin-bearing alluvial deposits along the Blanja range are described in terms of both Malay surface-washing and Chinese deep-shaft methods; and the soil quality of the Plus valley is quoted at length from Deane's field observations as "simply magnificent."

A substantial portion of the article is devoted to the Sakei, the aboriginal hill peoples inhabiting the valleys to the east and north of the Kinta. Leech provides a detailed ethnographical sketch covering their settlement patterns, subsistence strategies, weaponry (notably the blow-pipe and spring traps), marriage customs, burial practices, and physical characteristics, while also recording their near-universal suffering from a skin disease he terms *Kurap*. His treatment is characteristically colonial in its framing—simultaneously admiring and condescending—but he explicitly argues against the assumption that the Sakeis are "incapable of improvement," pointing to groups near the Bidor who had already adopted Malay customs as evidence of their assimilability.

The article functions as much as a prospectus as a report. Leech repeatedly signals openings for European enterprise: the limestone could be worked into marble paving by water-powered saws; the Plus valley's soil was superior to anything else in Perak; the tin deposits, even at depreciated prices, yielded profits to miners using "rude appliances and wasteful methods," implying that scientific methods would multiply returns; and gutta-percha cultivation was proposed as a viable alternative to the declining wild supply. The piece concludes at the Sungei Trola, a tributary of the Slim, with Leech promising a continuation if members of the Society found the account sufficiently interesting.

### Key Findings

- Gunong Robinson, named after the Governor, was measured at nearly 8,000 feet; the Plus valley, visible from Gunong Aslet, extends 40 to 70 miles and rises to 6,000–8,000 feet (pp. 22, 26).
- Tin ore in the Kinta district is found at depths varying from one to eight or nine fathoms below the surface soil; Gopeng, the most important mining centre on the Kampar side, employed 700–800 Chinese miners (pp. 23, 28).
- The limestone cliffs between the Kinta and Sungei Raya rise vertically 500 to 2,000 feet, are highly crystalline, contain no fossils or stratification marks, and are associated with some of the richest tin deposits in the district (pp. 24–25).
- The Sakei are confined to an elevation zone of 500 to 1,500 (possibly 2,000) feet, live in groups of 8–30 individuals, and approximately 90 percent of those observed were suffering from *Kurap*, a skin disease (pp. 28, 30).
- The Sakei blow-pipe, 7–8 feet long, was effective at 30–40 paces; a single poisoned dart could kill a bird or monkey within minutes (p. 29).
- The coasting steamer *Pyah Pekhet* called weekly at Durian Sebatang (40 miles up the Perak River); from there, Kota Bahru was reachable in two to three days by native boat, and the Kinta was navigable a further two days to Musjid Lama (p. 25).

### Conclusion

Leech's definitive takeaway is that the Kinta interior represents a frontier of extraordinary economic and scientific potential that remains virtually untouched by European enterprise. The combination of rich alluvial tin, superior agricultural soil, workable limestone, and navigable waterways—set against the backdrop of a sparse and assimilable indigenous population—makes the district, in his assessment, a near-certain prospect for profitable settlement and mining, provided that practical knowledge and moderate capital are brought to bear.

## Context

- Leech held a legal qualification (L.L.B., F.C.D.) and had previously served in the Selangor Government; his second expedition was explicitly commissioned by the Perak Government, and the article was published under the auspices of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, reflecting the administrative-scientific culture of the colonial service (pp. 24, 27).
- The article draws on Leech's own field diaries (one of which was "subsequently published by the Straits Government"), correspondence from Mr. Deane, and direct observation during two expeditions in 1878–79; it represents one of the earliest European geographical accounts of the Kinta interior and the Sakei peoples of Perak (pp. 22, 26).
