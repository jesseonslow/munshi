# Sir Hugh Clifford in Malaya, 1927–9. “ Pinang pulang ka-tampok ”, with appendix

Nathan Porath's 2011 article re-examines the *Hikayat Patani*—the principal literary source for the early history of the Patani sultanate—through the theoretical lens of René Girard's mimetic theory and Stephen Harrison's concept of "fracturing resemblances." Set against the political world of pre-modern Southeast Asian kingdom hierarchies, the article argues that the text's narrative structure reveals an unstated truth: Patani derived its prestige and political confidence in the Malay world from its mimetic relationship with Ayutthaya as a fellow Indic kingdom, while simultaneously working to differentiate itself from the emerging power of Johor.

## Summary

Porath approaches the *Hikayat Patani* not merely as a historical chronicle but as a "mimetic text" that encodes power relations through tropes of similarity and difference. The first book of the *hikayat*, which Bradley has identified as originally oral in composition, is structured around Patani's relationships with two neighbouring kingdoms: Ayutthaya to the north and Johor to the south. Porath's central observation is that the text performs a paradoxical operation: it affirms Patani's mimetic similarity with Ayutthaya even in the very passages narrating Patani's rebellions against it, while it fractures every possible resemblance between Patani and Johor. This dual strategy, he argues, reveals that Patani's sense of elite political standing among Malay-speaking states was contingent upon its position within the Ayutthaya mandala system.

The article traces this argument through several narrative episodes. The founding legend and the conversion story are read as "social contract" metaphors in which the kingdom's health depends on its political and religious alignment. The first rebellion under Sultan Mudhaffar Syah (c. 1564) is reinterpreted not as an act of secession but as a failed usurpation within the Indic frame of mimetic kingdom-hood, prompted by the weakness of Ayutthaya's king during the Burmese wars. A key piece of textual evidence is the sultan's statement that "the king is not different from us" and that "rather than one country, two is better"—which Porath connects to the Ayutthaya chronicles' image of two kingdoms becoming "one golden country" under Burmese suzerainty. The second rebellion under Queen Raja Ungu (c. 1630s) is analysed through the queen's rejection of her Thai royal title in protest against King Prasat Thong's usurpation, marking the first time Patani severed its ties with Ayutthaya wholesale.

Throughout, Porath draws on the 1839 *Hikayat Patani* (as translated by Teeuw and Wyatt), van Vliet's seventeenth-century Dutch account of Siam, the Ayutthaya royal chronicles, and the *Sejarah Melayu* to cross-reference and contextualize the *hikayat*'s claims. He also engages with Bradley's recent revision of the Kelantan dynasty's dates and Bougas's work on Patani's position between two mandala systems.

### Key Findings

- The *Hikayat Patani* manuscript was composed in six books between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries, following the end of Patani's founding dynasty and during the kingdom's decline under Kelantanese rulers (p. 47).
- Sultan Mudhaffar Syah's attack on the Ayutthaya palace (c. 1564) is confirmed by both the Ayutthaya chronicles and van Vliet; the *Luang Prasoert* chronicle records the sultan riding one of King Cakkraphat's white elephants in the palace plaza, symbolically appropriating Ayutthaya's cosmic power (p. 56).
- Queen Raja Ungu rejected her Thai royal title *Phra Nang Chaou Ying* and substituted the Islamic title *Paduka Syah Alam* in protest against King Prasat Thong's usurpation of the Ayutthaya throne in 1629, severing Patani's ties with Ayutthaya for the first time (p. 60).
- The Ayutthaya army sent against Patani in the second rebellion (resolved 1636) numbered 60,000 men under the Governor of Ligor, Okya Kalahom, and included a Malay faction; the Dutch East India Company agreed to send armed ships but arrived too late (p. 61).
- Van Vliet's 1644 dispatch to Batavia reported that war was imminent between Patani and Johor and that the Johor raja's mother and relatives in Patani were massacred; a subsequent dispatch noted a Patani envoy had arrived to settle differences (p. 54).
- Bradley's revision places the deposal of the last founding-dynasty queen (Raja Kuning) at 1649 and the installation of a Kelantanese prince in 1651 with Ayutthaya's support, rather than the c. 1688 date given by Teeuw and Wyatt (p. 52).

### Conclusion

Porath's definitive takeaway is that the *Hikayat Patani* should not be read through a modernist ethnicist lens that would interpret Patani's rebellions as proto-secessionist acts against "Thai rule." Instead, the text reveals that Patani's kingdomhood was constituted through its mimetic relationship with Ayutthaya—a shared Indic cosmos of hierarchical political order that was not shared with Johor. The *hikayat*'s ultimate political message, composed during the kingdom's decline, is that the maintenance of peace and hierarchic order with Ayutthaya was a precondition for the viability of the Patani *kerajaan*. The kingdom was ultimately "shattered and swallowed up by its original patron, Siam" (p. 64).

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Teeuw and Wyatt (1970) translation of the 1839 *Hikayat Patani*, van Vliet's *Siam* (Baker et al. 2005 edition), the Ayutthaya royal chronicles (Cushman 2006), and Bradley's (2009) contextualization of the text within Patani's political history.
- Historiographically, the article extends Bougas's (1994) framework of Patani as caught between two mandala systems by introducing Girardian mimesis and Harrison's "fracturing resemblances" as analytical tools, offering a structural reading of the *hikayat*'s narrative that previous scholars (Teeuw and Wyatt, Bradley, Syukri) had not attempted.
