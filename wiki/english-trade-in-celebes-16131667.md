# English trade in Celebes, 1613–1667

W.W. Skeat, a British colonial administrator and Malay scholar, published this first-hand ethnographic account in 1898, recording Malay magic ceremonies he witnessed in the Negri Sembilan region of the Malay Peninsula. The article's overarching thesis is that these rituals, far from being "mere childish folly," are intelligible expressions of sympathetic magic as theorized by Frazer, and that rigorous eyewitness documentation was urgently needed before such practices disappeared.

## Summary

Skeat's article is structured around several distinct ceremonial sequences he either witnessed directly or had performed for his benefit by local practitioners. The most extensively documented is the padi (rice) ceremony, in which a female pawang removes the "rice child" spirit from the first sheaf of a paddy field, carries it home in a basket-cradle, and subjects the household to three days of taboo. The ceremony involves a sugar-cane stem planted in the sheaf as a fertility symbol, an iron nail as protection against evil spirits, the application of *tepong tawar* (magic powder), and a series of prayers blending Islamic formulae with indigenous spirit invocations. Skeat records the full text of the "Ten Prayers," the omens read from bird behaviour during the cutting of the first seven ears, and the specific taboos observed (no cutting hair, no letting shadows fall on the rice, the hearth fire must not go out).

A second major sequence is a healing ceremony in which a male pawang, accompanied by his wife playing a tambourine, performs the *Lagu Pemanggil*—a long summoning song addressed to a sea-spirit aboard a metaphorical golden ship. The pawang enters a trance, is possessed by a tiger spirit, and licks the patient's body from head to foot before letting his own blood over the sick man. Skeat provides a vocabulary table of *Bhasa Hantu* (spirit language) and the specific charms used to consecrate the three water jars central to the rite.

The article also covers the *Lanchang* (spirit-boat) ceremony for expelling evil, the elaborate offering trays prepared for water-spirits at fishing stations (*kelong*), and the taboos and charms associated with planting fishing stakes. Throughout, Skeat intersperses his observations with philological notes, alternative local versions of charms, and explicit interpretive commentary drawing on Frazer's *Golden Bough* to explain the logic of sympathetic magic underlying each gesture.

### Key Findings

- The padi ceremony's "cradle" contained seven specific items: a strip of white cloth, coloured thread (*benang pancharona*), an egg, a jungle-nut (*buah kras*), a *krang* shell, a long iron nail, and five *hasta* of red cloth for slinging (p. 3).
- The *Lagu Pemanggil* summoning song comprises 61 lines, describing a golden ship with a mast named "Raja Mendelu," a rudder named "The Hanging Bees' Nest," and twice seven oarsmen (pp. 16–19).
- The spirit language (*Bhasa Hantu*) vocabulary table records 16 terms, including *simbangan* (bird), *merak b'layang* (betel leaf), *gandum* (rice/corn), and *bayu* (wind) (pp. 23–24).
- The *kelong* (fishing stake) offering tray contained 28 small rice receptacles (14 *katupat* and 14 *lepat*), five portions each of cooked and uncooked food, seven kinds of coloured rice, sugar-cane juice, fowl blood, five tapers, five betel chews, five cigarettes, and five silver coins (pp. 30–31).
- The 1891 census showed that in Rembau, not one per cent of native customary land holdings were registered as the property of men, reflecting the matrilineal inheritance customs traced to intermarriage between the Waris tribe and the Sakai (p. 42).
- The *tepong tawar* brush for the padi ceremony required leaves from seven specific plants: *sapenoh*, *sapanggil*, *lenjuang merah*, *gandarusa*, *pulut-pulut*, *selaguri*, and *sambau dara*, bound with *terap* bark and *ribu-ribu* cord (p. 3).

### Conclusion

Skeat's definitive takeaway is that Malay padi and healing ceremonies are not irrational superstition but coherent systems of sympathetic magic in which every gesture—from the pawang turning up her eyes to whiten the rice, to the tiger-spirit licking the patient, to the cat's kittens substituting for a human birth—operates on the principle that "any effect can be produced by imitating it." He positions his eyewitness records as a corrective to the conjectural approach of earlier scholars, arguing that only direct observation can capture the precise sequence, material culture, and verbal formulae that give these rituals their operative force.

## Context

- Skeat was a senior colonial administrator in the Straits Settlements (later Chief Secretary of the Federated Malay States) and a prolific contributor to the Journal; this article reflects his dual role as both official and ethnographer, with the administrative framing evident in his references to census data and state boundaries.
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in being one of the earliest systematic first-hand records of Malay *pawang* ceremonies in English, predating most anthropological fieldwork in the region by several decades, and in its explicit (if brief) engagement with Frazerian comparative methodology.
