# New harbour, Singapore and the cruise of H.M.S. Maeander, 1848–49

C.D. Cowan's 1965 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* reconstructs the East Indian cruise of HMS *Maeander* (1848–49) from the ship's log held at the Public Record Office, London, to correct persistent misconceptions about Captain Henry Keppel's relationship with New Harbour (now Keppel Harbour), Singapore. Building on C.A. Gibson-Hill's 1954 foundational study of the Old Strait, Cowan demonstrates that while Keppel was not the harbour's discoverer, *Maeander* was the first vessel to make sustained use of it as an anchorage, and that the popular story of Keppel grounding the ship on the shoal that bears her name is unsupported by the documentary record.

## Summary

The article traces the full career of HMS *Maeander*, a 44-gun sailing frigate commissioned at Sheerness on 2 November 1847 to convey James Brooke, Raja of Sarawak, to his new post as Governor of the recently acquired colony of Labuan. Keppel was selected for command largely because of his prior friendship with Brooke, formed during their joint anti-piracy operations in Borneo (1842–45). The voyage out was marked by severe weather, the death of Napier's infant son at sea, a man jumping overboard in delirium, and another lost overboard in the Southern Ocean, bringing casualties to eight before the ship reached Singapore on 20 May 1848 after a passage of three months and five days.

The central argument concerns the precise sequence of *Maeander*'s use of New Harbour. Keppel's memoirs and his 1853 published account both suggest that the ship entered the harbour immediately upon arrival, but the log makes clear that *Maeander* remained in Singapore Roads from 22 May until 16 June, refitting and painting, before entering New Harbour by the Western entrance on 19 June. She stayed there until 18 August, during which time she was apparently alone for the first month, conducting gunnery practice that would have been impractical with other ships nearby. A violent squall on 29 July forced her to shift berth and moor with two anchors to a swivel. On subsequent visits to Singapore in November 1848 and February 1849, the ship did not enter New Harbour at all, and on her final visit in August 1849 she spent a month there refitting.

The remainder of the cruise is narrated with equal attention to the ship's log: the establishment of Labuan, the outbreak of fever that sickened the entire colony, a convalescent cruise through the Sulu Sea, a grounding on an uncharted coral bank in the Balabac Strait (17 July 1849), and the eventual homeward passage via Australia, Port Essington, and Valparaíso. The ship paid off at Sheerness on 30 July 1851, with only 150 of her original 360 men remaining.

### Key Findings

- *Maeander* was commissioned at Sheerness on 2 November 1847 and paid off there on 30 July 1851, a service of three years and nine months; of the original 360 men, only 150 remained at the end (p. 240).
- The ship's log (Adm. 53/3601–3603, Public Record Office, London) shows *Maeander* entered New Harbour on 19 June 1848 and remained until 18 August, a stay of nearly two months, making her the first vessel to use the anchorage on a protracted basis (pp. 233–234).
- HMS *Rainbow* had passed through New Harbour in 1826, predating Keppel's use by twenty-two years, confirming Gibson-Hill's earlier correction that Keppel was not the first European to navigate the passage (p. 239).
- The ship suffered 15 deaths from disease or drowning by the time she reached Sarawak in September 1848; total casualties over the full cruise were such that only 150 of 360 original crew survived to pay off (pp. 235, 240).
- On 17 July 1849, *Maeander* ran aground on an uncharted coral bank in the Balabac Strait, heeling at nearly forty degrees; she was refloated within twenty-four hours by jettisoning guns and shot (pp. 238–239).
- The log records no incident of *Maeander* striking a shoal in New Harbour on any of her four visits to Singapore, undermining the traditional account that Keppel grounded the ship there in 1848 (p. 240).

### Conclusion

Cowan's definitive takeaway is that Keppel's own accounts, written with a flair for self-dramatisation, have distorted the historical record of *Maeander*'s use of New Harbour. The ship's log, written contemporaneously and without literary ambition, reveals a more prosaic sequence of events: the harbour was entered not in a moment of serendipitous discovery but as a deliberate refitting berth, and the famous "Maeander shoal" was almost certainly struck by one of the ship's boats rather than by the frigate herself. Keppel's genuine contribution was demonstrating the harbour's practical value as an anchorage, not discovering it.

## Context

- The primary source is the ship's log of HMS *Maeander* (Adm. 53/3601–3603), Public Record Office, London, supplemented by Keppel's 1853 published account and his posthumous memoirs (1899).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its methodological insistence on the primacy of the contemporaneous log over retrospective memoir, a corrective to the reliance on Buckley (1902) and Keppel's own writings that had characterised earlier Singapore maritime history.
