# The Putri of Mount Ophir

Ivor H. N. Evans, a British administrator and ethnographer based in North Borneo, published this short comparative study in 1951, arguing that the "house-horns" found on Dusun gable ends in the Tempasuk District are not merely ornamental but are survivals of a protective talismanic practice with deep Malayo-Polynesian roots, evidenced by striking parallels as far afield as the Hovas of Madagascar.

## Summary

Evans begins from his own fieldwork among the Dusun of Kota Belud (Tempasuk) District, where he observed that houses commonly feature upward projections of the rafters at either gable end, left untrimmed above the roof-beam. When he asked Dusun informants about the significance of these projections, he was uniformly told they were purely decorative. However, the local term *sinasangau*—derived from *sangau*, "horn"—led him to suspect an older meaning. He proposes that the word may be a euphonic alteration of *kinasangau*, meaning "there were in past time horns," which would imply that actual animal horns once occupied the position now filled by carved wooden or bamboo projections. A wealthy Dusun at Mangkahak, for instance, would commission a pair of carved wooden horns for his house, a pattern called *kinagai-kagai* (from *kagai*, "branch").

The article's central move is comparative. Evans establishes that the Malay term used in the district, *tandok rumah*, is etymologically identical to the Hova Malagasy *tandro trano*—both meaning "house-horns." He then marshals descriptions from Madagascar (Ellis, Sibree, Retzel), the Angami and Sema Nagas of the Himalayan foothills (via Professor Hutton), the Menangkabau and Bataks of Sumatra, and a Flores model in the Colonial Institute at Amsterdam, to demonstrate that gable-end projections are a widespread feature across Malayo-Polynesian and related cultures. The Hova horns, in particular, closely mirror the Dusun form: crossed rafters projecting above the ridge, sometimes topped with carved birds representing clan crests, and more elaborate on the houses of the wealthy.

On function, Evans engages with Professor Hutton's suggestion that the Angami Naga horns carry phallic and fertility significance. While accepting that religious symbols often serve multiple purposes, Evans argues for a primarily protective interpretation: just as a buffalo's horns are its weapons against enemies, so house-horns served as talismans against evil spirits. He supports this by noting that Dusun ceremonial life explicitly identifies the roof-beam (*tiningusan*) as an assembling place for spirits called *Koolungung*, making the gable ends a logical locus for apotropaic devices. He closes with a personal recollection of similar spikes on Zulu beehive huts in Natal, which he was told served to prevent evil spirits from alighting.

### Key Findings

- The Dusun term *sinasangau* derives from *sangau* ("horn"); Evans hypothesizes an older form *kinasangau* ("there were in past time horns"), suggesting original use of actual animal horns (p. 165).
- The Malay *tandok rumah* and Hova Malagasy *tandro trano* are etymologically identical, both meaning "house-horns," providing a direct linguistic link between Borneo and Madagascar (p. 166).
- Among the Angami Nagas the horns are called *kika*; among the Sema Nagas, *tenbaku-ki* ("snail-horns"); the Sema, like the Hovas, mount small birds on them (p. 166).
- A model "maison collective" from Flores, held at the Colonial Institute in Amsterdam, shows gable horns mounted to resemble sixteenth-century arquebus rests (p. 167).
- Dusun religious ceremonies reference the roof-beam (*tiningusan*) as a spirit-assembling place, with the spirits named *Koolungung* (or *Ungkauulung*), supporting the protective-talisman interpretation (p. 167).

### Conclusion

Evans concludes that house-horns across the Malayo-Polynesian world were originally apotropaic devices—protective talismans against evil spirits—rather than purely ornamental or phallic symbols, and that the Dusun practice in North Borneo represents a living, if semantically faded, continuation of this widespread tradition.

## Context

- The article is grounded in Evans's direct fieldwork in the Tempasuk District and in correspondence with Professor J. H. Hutton (Cambridge), Dr. W. Linehan (Director of Museums, Malayan Federation), and Mr. H. D. Collings (Raffles Museum, Singapore), who supplied comparative material on the Nagas, Bataks, and Hovas.
- The study contributes to the comparative ethnography of Malayo-Polynesian architectural symbolism and provides one of the earliest explicit linguistic links (*tandok/tandro*) between Bornean and Malagasy domestic architecture.
