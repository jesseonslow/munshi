# Outline history of the British connection with Malaya

A.M. Skinner published this outline history in 1882, writing from within the Straits Settlements colonial administration to provide a connected narrative of British involvement in Malaya spanning 280 years. The article's overarching thesis is that British presence in the region evolved through three distinct phases — private trade, Company-mediated commerce, and direct political-military intervention — culminating in the unified Colony as it stood in the early 1880s.

## Summary

Skinner structures the British connection with Malaya into three periods: individual trading (1602–1684), East India Company commerce (1684–1762), and political-military intervention (1762 onward). The first two periods are treated as preface, establishing that early English ventures were exclusively commercial and politically subordinate to the Portuguese, Spanish, and Dutch. The third period, which constitutes the bulk of the article, traces the transition from commercial outposts to sovereign territory, identifying three pivotal administrative dates — 1805 (Penang elevated to a separate Presidency), 1827 (Lord Bentinck's personal settlement of the united Colony), and 1867 (transfer from Indian to Colonial Office rule) — as the structural joints of colonial development.

The article then turns to a local survey of each settlement and the newly protected Native States. Skinner draws on official sources, treaty texts, and administrative correspondence to explain how each territory came under British control and how its commercial significance shifted over time. A recurring theme is the displacement of one entrepôt by another: Malacca's decline after Penang's founding, and Penang's subsequent eclipse by Singapore. The final section addresses the 1874 Pangkor Treaty and the establishment of Residents in Perak, Selangor, and Sungei Ujong, including the Birch murder and the brief military occupation of 1875–76, which Skinner presents as a successful vindication of Sir Andrew Clarke's protective experiment.

The revenue and population tables Skinner appends serve as quantitative proof of the Colony's prosperity under the new administrative arrangements, framing the entire narrative as a story of orderly, progressive development.

### Key Findings

- Penang was ceded by the Raja of Kedah in 1785 for an annual payment of 10,000 dollars; Province Wellesley was purchased in 1800 for 2,000 dollars (p. 277).
- Singapore's population exceeded 5,000 within four months of its founding in February 1819, principally Chinese immigrants, according to Raffles's letter of 11 June 1819 (p. 278).
- Colony revenues grew from $1,301,839 in 1868 to $4,756,130 in 1883, with Singapore alone rising from $364,918 to $1,697,920 over the same period (p. 274).
- The Pangkor Treaty of 20 January 1874 established British Residents in Perak, Selangor, and Sungei Ujong; the murder of Resident Birch on 2 November 1875 led to a full military occupation of Perak in January 1876 (pp. 278–279).
- Malacca passed through Portuguese (1511–1641), Dutch (1641–1795), and two periods of British occupation before its final cession under the Treaty of March 1824, which also excluded the Dutch from the Malay Peninsula permanently (p. 275).
- Colony population increased from 273,000 (1868) to 423,384 (1881) (p. 274).

### Conclusion

Skinner's definitive takeaway is that the British connection with Malaya, though originating in commerce, has been progressively and successfully transformed into a political and administrative enterprise, and that the protective system introduced in 1874 represents the latest and most promising stage of that evolution. The article is written to reassure both colonial officials and metropolitan readers that the Colony's trajectory is one of orderly expansion and growing prosperity.

## Context

- Skinner writes as a colonial administrator using the possessive "our" throughout ("our countrymen," "our Company's merchants," "our Settlement"), situating the narrative firmly within the Straits Settlements bureaucratic perspective of the early 1880s.
- The article was compiled explicitly "in the hope that it may be of assistance to those, both in and out of the Colony, who are anxious to know something of its antecedents" (p. 269), indicating its function as an internal reference document rather than a work of critical historiography.
