# A journey on foot to the Patani frontier in 1876; being a journal kept an expedition undertaken to capture Datoh Maharaja Lela of Perak

W.E. Maxwell, a political officer attached to the Larut Field Force and former Stipendiary Magistrate of Province Wellesley, published in 1882 a detailed journal of his March–April 1876 overland expedition through the uncharted interior of Ulu Perak and the Patani frontier, undertaken to capture Datoh Maharaja Lela, the chief suspect in the murder of British Resident J.W. Birch. Though the expedition failed in its primary objective—Lela escaped across the Perak river—it produced the first European account of the route from the Krian river to the Patani border and contributed directly to the eventual identification, trial, and execution of the Birch murderers (pp. 1–67).

## Summary

Maxwell departed Butterworth on 24 March 1876 with forty Malay followers, travelling up the Krian river to Salama, where Che Abdul Karim's Sumatran mining community provided provisions and local intelligence. From Salama the party crossed the "thirty-three hills" pass (Bukit Tiga Puloh Tiga) on foot, reaching the Perak river at Kota Tampan on 29 March, a strategic hilltop position never before visited by a European (pp. 4–19). At Tampan, Maxwell established contact with Patani settlers who had long suffered under Perak chiefdoms, and learned from a local freebooter named Etam that Lela had shifted his hiding-place from Kwala Piah to Banai and had probably already crossed into Patani territory (pp. 20–21).

The party pushed north through Lunggong and Sumpitan, crossing Bukit Naksa—the popular boundary between Perak and Patani—on 5 April, and reached Kampong Padang in Patani on the 6th. There, Maxwell's arrival coincided disastrously with a village feast commemorating a death; Lela's followers, present at the celebration, fled to warn their chief, and when Maxwell's men surrounded Lela's house at Kwala Kendrong they found it already evacuated (pp. 39–42). Negotiations with Tuan Prang, the local Patani chief, and his envoy Wan Mohsin yielded no active cooperation, both men insisting they could not act without orders from the Raja of Reman at Kota Bahru (pp. 44–51). Maxwell returned to Penang via the Intan tin mines and the Kedah frontier, reaching British territory at Pangkalan Bongoh on 15 April (pp. 55–66).

The journal is as much an ethnographic and geographical document as a military narrative. Maxwell records in detail the primitive tin-smelting technology at Salama and Intan, the truck-system economics binding Chinese miners to Malay landlords, the customs and physical characteristics of the Sakai aborigines, the legendary traditions of Jambai (including a solar myth of Che Puteh Jambai), and the complex boundary history between Perak and Patani centred on the Intan and Endah mines (pp. 8–11, 23–26, 36–38, 55–59).

### Key Findings

- The expedition covered approximately 200 miles of previously unexplored terrain from the Krian river to the Patani frontier and back via Kedah, providing the first European geographical account of the route that later informed a 1877 government survey and Stanford's map of the Peninsula (pp. 66–67).
- Maharaja Lela's escape was caused by the accidental coincidence of Maxwell's arrival at Kampong Padang with a village mourning feast; a guest named Sirat, son of the Penghulu of Grik, carried the warning to Lela, who waded the Perak river and abandoned most of his property (pp. 42–43).
- The identification of Si Tuah (one of the five proscribed co-conspirators) as a captive slave at Salama, confirmed by Penghulu Dolah upon Maxwell's return to Province Wellesley, led to his trial, condemnation to death, and eventual reprieve on grounds of "weakness of intellect" (pp. 31, 66).
- The Intan tin mines, claimed by Perak as ancient territory, were operated under exploitative terms by the Raja of Reman: Chinese miners paid $24 per bhara to the Patani chief, received opium at the same price, and sold their tin at Baling for $40 per bhara against a Penang price of $62, yielding the Patani government a $16 per bhara profit (pp. 58–59).
- The ancient Perak–Patani boundary, asserted by Perak chiefs, ran from Lobang Gandang to Padang Limau Nipis to Gunong Jambul Mrak, encompassing the Intan and Endah mines; Patani had held the territory de facto since the Kedah war of 1817–18, and Siamese officials surveyed the Bukit Naksa line in 1877 (pp. 36–38).
- The Patani Malays settled in Ulu Perak (at Tampan, Lunggong, and surrounding villages) were a distinct community—honest, quiet, and industrious by Maxwell's account—who had been systematically oppressed by Perak chiefs and actively welcomed British protection (pp. 19–20, 28).

### Conclusion

Maxwell's definitive historical takeaway is that the expedition, while failing tactically, achieved strategic success: the presence of his detachment at Kwala Kendrong prevented Lela's return to the Perak side of the river, and the intimidation of Patani chiefs who now feared harbouring him compelled Lela to retrace his steps to Kota Lama, where he surrendered and was subsequently tried, convicted, and executed for the murder of Birch (pp. 66–67). The journal thus documents the closing phase of the Perak crisis of 1875–76 and the transition from military to judicial resolution of the Birch assassination.

## Context

- Maxwell's account forms part of the JSBRAS (Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society) archive, Vol. 9 (1882), and complements other contemporary accounts of the Perak campaign including Major McNair's *Sarong and Kris* (cited p. 4) and the official despatches of the Larut Field Force.
- The expedition's geographical findings directly fed into the 1877 government survey of Ulu Perak and the subsequent Stanford & Co. map of the Peninsula, making this journal a foundational source for the cartography of northern Perak's interior (p. 67).
