# Kuala Lumpur

Kuala Lumpur, the capital of Malaysia, is a city whose name is commonly translated as 'muddy confluence,' referring to the junction of the Klang and Gombak Rivers on the west coast of the Malay Peninsula [[1]](#ref-1). However, recent toponymic research has demonstrated that the city's name derives not from this confluence but from a now-lost tributary stream called *Sungai Lumpur* (Mud River), which once ran through the heart of the modern city before being renamed, channelised, and largely covered over [[1]](#ref-1). The settlement that became Kuala Lumpur originated in the late 1850s as a river transhipment point serving the tin mines of the upper Klang valley, and within four decades it had grown from a thatched-roofed mining village into a multi-ethnic colonial capital with functioning municipal institutions, a railway network, and a population estimated at 25,000 by 1895 [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3). The city's development was shaped by the interplay of tin-mining economics, colonial administrative decisions, and the personal agency of key figures—above all the Hakka mining magnate Yap Ah Loy, whose leadership during the Selangor Civil War (1867–74) and the subsequent reconstruction period made him the principal architect of the town's early growth [[4]](#ref-4), [[3]](#ref-3).

## Origins and Toponymy

The earliest evidence of settlement at the site of Kuala Lumpur dates to the first quarter of the nineteenth century, when Sumatran Malays had established small mining communities along the upper reaches of the Klang River [[3]](#ref-3). In 1824, the British surveyor John Anderson listed 'Sungei Lumpoor' as the twenty-third and final tributary village on the Klang, noting that 'most' of the local tin was obtained there [[1]](#ref-1), [[3]](#ref-3). The trading post at the confluence of the Gombak and Klang Rivers may date from a couple of years after 1857, when Raja Abdullah bin Raja Ja'afar, then district chief of Klang, despatched 87 Chinese miners from Lukut to open mines at Ampang on the outskirts of the modern city, investing approximately $70,000, much of it borrowed from Melaka *towkays* (wealthy Chinese merchants) [[3]](#ref-3). The first trading post at the confluence itself was established by two Hakka men, Hiu Siew and Ah Sze, in 1859 [[3]](#ref-3).

The name 'Kuala Lumpur' has long been a subject of scholarly debate. The conventional explanation—that it means 'muddy confluence'—runs contrary to normal Malay usage, since the Gombak River runs into the Klang at this point and the place should logically have been called 'Kuala Gombak' [[3]](#ref-3). Lim and Leong (2025) have provided the first systematic topographical analysis of the question, scoring five candidate streams against eight criteria including naming status, size, swamp presence, bank position, settlement history, relative location, distance from town, and proximity to Tangga China [[1]](#ref-1). Their conclusion is that the Treacher Valley Stream, which is 5 km long with a 600-hectare catchment and whose *kuala* (river mouth) lies approximately 1.1 miles upstream from Pangkalan Lumpur on the Klang's east bank, is the physical remnant of the *Sungai Lumpur* [[1]](#ref-1). The stream was renamed by the British after Resident W.H. Treacher (1892–96) and appears on the 1929 town map under that designation [[1]](#ref-1). The metonymic transfer by which the stream's name passed to the *mukim* (administrative district, Mukim Lumpur, later renamed Mukim Kuala Lumpur), then to the landing stage (Pangkalan Lumpur), and finally to the growing town itself, has been reconstructed in detail by the authors [[1]](#ref-1).

The disappearance of the *Sungai Lumpur* from the map was facilitated by the British renaming of the stream, the resettlement of the Temuan community from Bukit Nanas in 1897, and the channelisation of the watercourse into a monsoon drain [[1]](#ref-1). A 10-metre storage tunnel uncovered by Kuala Lumpur City Hall in 2014 beneath Bukit Nanas is interpreted as part of an underground labyrinth dug during the Selangor Civil War, its mouth only about 150 metres from the *Sungai Lumpur*'s *kuala* [[1]](#ref-1). The first recorded report that the stream had become unidentifiable on local maps was a 1911 letter to the *Malay Mail* by a correspondent signing 'C.W.H.', who urged readers to locate it 'before all trace of its existence is lost' [[1]](#ref-1).

## The Mining Village and the Civil War

By the 1860s, Kuala Lumpur had become a significant settlement, though its precise character is difficult to reconstruct from the limited contemporary evidence. In 1872, when the British official F.A. Swettenham first visited the town, he found 'a purely Chinese village, consisting of two rows of adobe-built dwellings, thatched with palm leaves,' with the exception of the Capitan China's own house, which was 'more pretentious and solidly built' [[3]](#ref-3). The village was completely destroyed in the last stage of the Selangor Civil War in August 1872, when Sayid Mashhor and Raja Asal captured the town, killing Yap Ah Loy's close associates and forcing him to retreat to Klang [[4]](#ref-4). The war reached its turning point in March 1873 when Ah Loy, reinforced by Pahang troops, retook the town after a two-day battle [[4]](#ref-4).

The rebuilt town of 1875 impressed Swettenham as 'by far the best mining village I have seen, the streets wide and excellently arranged, the shops most substantial' [[3]](#ref-3). By March 1875 the town had a population of approximately 1,000 Chinese and 700 Malays, with a working mining population of 7,000 in the surrounding area [[3]](#ref-3). The American zoologist Hornaday, visiting in July 1878, described the market produce in precise detail—sweet potatoes, yams, beans, melons, cucumbers, radishes, Chinese cabbage, onions, egg-plant, and lady's fingers among the vegetables; durian, mangosteen, pineapple, banana, and plantain among the fruits—and noted that the principal streets were 'lined with Chinese shops, and are uniformly clean and tidily kept' [[3]](#ref-3).

The economic fortunes of the town were intimately bound to the world tin price. In 1874 the average London price of Straits tin was £98 per ton, but a steady decline brought it down to £61 by early 1879, bringing Yap Ah Loy to the verge of bankruptcy [[3]](#ref-3). The price then rocketed from £73 to £95 in a single month in October 1879, and stabilised at about £90 per ton for some years [[3]](#ref-3). This recovery was decisive for the town's future, as it enabled the rapid expansion that followed the transfer of the Selangor capital to Kuala Lumpur in 1880 [[3]](#ref-3), [[2]](#ref-2).

## Colonial Administration and Urban Transformation

The transfer of the state capital from Klang to Kuala Lumpur in 1880 marked a decisive moment in the town's history [[3]](#ref-3). The first Selangor Government building—a combined courthouse and resthouse—had been erected in 1879, with Sultan Abdul Samad placing the first pole on 9 May 1879 in a ceremony involving the smearing of *minyak atal* (sacred oil) and a short prayer [[3]](#ref-3). The building was sited at the north end of what later became Klyne Street [[3]](#ref-3). When the capital was formally moved, the more substantial government buildings at Klang were dismantled and their component timbers shipped up the river for re-erection in Kuala Lumpur [[3]](#ref-3). The Residency, originally at Klang, stood in Kuala Lumpur until rebuilt in 1888 and had 'much of the appearance of an armed post amidst a hostile population'; Douglas himself called it a 'redoubt' and conducted gunnery practice from its terrace [[3]](#ref-3).

The first two years of British rule under Resident Bloomfield Douglas and his son-in-law D.D. Daly were, in Gullick's assessment, a disaster: no effective land administration, no public health measures, and a Residency that operated more like a military redoubt than a seat of government [[2]](#ref-2). When Frank Swettenham took over in September 1882, he and his deputy J.P. Rodger set about rebuilding the administrative machine, recruiting competent departmental heads and launching a programme of physical improvement that included street cleaning, the replacement of thatch roofs with brick and tile, the construction of bridges, and the creation of a fire brigade [[2]](#ref-2).

The most consequential decision of the Swettenham era was the construction of a railway between Klang and Kuala Lumpur, opened in October 1886 at a cost of $750,000—twice the State's annual revenue at the time [[2]](#ref-2). The line was a financial success, yielding a 28 per cent return on capital by 1889, and its profits funded extensions north to Rawang (1892) and Kuala Kubu (1894) and south to Pudu and Sungai Besi (1895), creating a 70-mile network by the end of the period [[2]](#ref-2). The railway simultaneously stimulated tin mining (output rose from 42,293 *piculs* in 1878 to 208,164 in 1891), attracted European planters who established coffee estates on the hills around the town, and drew a growing population that reached an estimated 25,000 by 1895 [[2]](#ref-2).

The building campaign of the mid-1880s was catalysed by a series of destructive fires. The 4 January 1881 fire left 500 people homeless and caused an estimated $100,000 in damage [[5]](#ref-5). A second fire that August destroyed twelve houses, prompting the Selangor State Council to ban wooden shophouses in the business area and require non-flammable materials [[6]](#ref-6). The 1884 building regulations imposed a maximum of twelve houses per block with at least 20 feet separation, required ceilings over 15 cm thick of non-flammable material, and carried a penalty of up to $100 for non-compliance [[6]](#ref-6). The result was a rapid transformation of the town's physical fabric: from only 4 tiled-roof houses at the end of 1884 to 518 brick houses by 1887 [[2]](#ref-2). By 1886, 15 brick factories and 6 limestone factories operated in and around Kuala Lumpur [[6]](#ref-6).

The brickmaking industry that supplied this building boom was itself a significant economic activity. Mechanised production, introduced via machines from H. Clayton, Son & Howlett's, scaled output from 10,000 to 16,000 to 25,000 bricks per day while reducing labour requirements—a Single Chamber Machine needed only one operator to produce 5,000 bricks daily [[6]](#ref-6). The Selangor State Railway consumed 1,050,000 bricks for drain construction, and the Sanitary Board's 1897 annual report records brick usage across multiple streets for sewerage works [[6]](#ref-6). The Bangunan Sultan Abdul Samad, completed in 1897 in the Neo-Saracenic style, required 4 million bricks, 2,500 barrels of cement, 18,000 *pikuls* of lime, 5,000 pounds of copper, 50 tons of steel, and approximately 30,000 cubic feet of timber [[6]](#ref-6).

The later years of the period saw the consolidation of civic institutions. The Sanitary Board was established in 1890 with A.R. Venning as chairman; the Chartered Bank opened a branch in 1888; the Victoria Institution opened in 1894 with 201 pupils; and the Lake Gardens, begun by Venning in 1888, became a focus of community life [[2]](#ref-2). Gullick emphasises that these institutions represented a new kind of Asian leadership: figures like Yap Kwan Seng, the last Capitan China, no longer exercised the all-encompassing personal authority of Ah Loy but instead served as interpreters between the British administrative apparatus and the Chinese community, sitting on innumerable boards and committees [[2]](#ref-2).

The human cost of this rapid industrialisation was considerable. Beriberi cases in Selangor hospitals rose from 191 in 1883 to 2,817 in 1894, with hospital death rates between 12 per cent and 51 per cent [[2]](#ref-2). The European mining boom of 1882–84, which leased 3,800 acres to foreign companies, collapsed within two years; the Chinese miners then absorbed the abandoned steam engines, increasing their number from 2 in 1884 to 99 in 1888 [[2]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Kuala Lumpur spans nearly a century and reflects shifting scholarly priorities. The earliest contribution is a brief etymological note by E. Macfadyen (1916) on the name 'Kuala Lumpur,' published in the *JSBRAS* [[7]](#ref-7). This was followed by S.M. Middlebrook's comprehensive biography of Yap Ah Loy (1951), which established the narrative framework for understanding the town's origins in the context of the Selangor Civil War and the personal agency of its Chinese leader [[4]](#ref-4). Middlebrook's work drew on seven primary sources, the most important being a Chinese-language 'Record' dictated for Ah Loy, a 1924 family petition claiming $1,109,778 in war expenditure, and a 'Short History' dictated by his surviving *panglima* (military commander) Hiu Fatt, all deposited in the Raffles Museum archives [[4]](#ref-4).

J.M. Gullick's monograph *Kuala Lumpur, 1880–1895* (1955) provided the definitive account of the town's transformation from mining village to colonial capital, drawing heavily on Selangor Government records, the *Selangor Gazette*, and the *Selangor Journal* [[2]](#ref-2). Gullick was himself a senior civil servant in the Straits Settlements and Federated Malay States, and the article reflects insider knowledge of the colonial administrative system it describes [[2]](#ref-2). His 1990 article, 'The growth of Kuala Lumpur and of the Malay community of Selangor before 1880,' extended the narrative backwards to the pre-colonial period and was explicitly framed as a contribution to the preservation of the city's remaining nineteenth-century buildings [[3]](#ref-3). A short identification note (1959) demonstrated the value of photographic evidence for reconstructing the town's physical geography, dating an undated Braddell photograph to 1883/84 on the basis of the presence of the timber Market Street bridge and the absence of the 1884 river-widening works [[8]](#ref-8).

More recent scholarship has shifted attention from the dominant mining narrative to the material and industrial infrastructure that underpinned the city's growth. Gullick's 'The Builders' (2012) reconstructed the emergence of a building industry in the western Malay states between 1875 and 1914, arguing that no such industry existed before colonial intervention [[5]](#ref-5). Arba'iyah and Sharif (2017) traced the brickmaking industry's development from the fires of 1881 through to the large-scale government infrastructure projects of the 1890s, drawing on Selangor Secretariat Files including building regulations, land grant applications, government tender documents, and machinery advertisements [[6]](#ref-6). Lim and Leong (2025) have intervened in the long-running toponymic debate by providing the first systematic topographical elimination of candidate streams and a detailed reconstruction of the metonymic transfer chain from stream to *mukim* to town, situating this recovery within a broader critique of how colonial cartography and developmental urban planning erased indigenous and ecological place names [[1]](#ref-1). Their study draws on archival maps held at the National Archives of Singapore, Arkib Negara Malaysia, and the Nationaal Archief in The Hague, supplemented by Temuan oral history interviews conducted in 2024–25 [[1]](#ref-1).
