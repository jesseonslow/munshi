# Singapore in the remote past. Il

W. George Maxwell's "Mantra Gajah," published in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society (No. 45, 1905), presents a translated Malay manuscript of elephant-related incantations from Ulu Perak and argues that the core of Malay elephant lore derives from a Siamese source rather than from India, as previously assumed by Crawfurd and others. The article combines philological analysis, ethnographic fieldwork among Perak elephant drivers, and a full literal translation of the manuscript to reconstruct the layered religious and linguistic heritage embedded in the *elmu gajah* (science of elephants).

## Summary

Maxwell's central problem is the origin of Malay elephant-catching and training knowledge. Crawfurd had attributed it to Indian (Sanskrit) influence on the basis of shared vocabulary — *gajah*, *gambala*, *kosa*, *sengkela* — but Maxwell demonstrates that the operative words of command used by Perak and Kedah drivers are not Malay and differ between the two states, pointing instead to a Siamese source. He supports this with the testimony of every elephant driver he interviewed in Perak, who unanimously described their knowledge as *katurunan daripada Siam* ("descended from Siam") without being able to say when or how the Siamese connection originated. Maxwell draws an analogy with the *lumbong siam* (Siamese mines) — old shafts throughout the Peninsula that Malays attribute to Siamese ancestors but which scientists identify as the work of earlier Mon-Khmer or Indonesian populations — suggesting that "Siamese" functions as a catch-all term for a pre-Malay substrate.

The manuscript itself, twenty-six years old at the time of writing and held by Che Pandak Abdullah, was committed to writing by Toh Sarif Aman from the oral tradition of Tunku Mantri Ibrahim bin Jaffar, the Mantri of Larut banished to the Seychelles after the Perak War. Maxwell identifies three purely Malay mantras (sections 4–5) that invoke Islamic formulae alongside Hindu deities (Betara Guru, Betara Kala) and the Azazil of Leviticus, while the remaining sections are in a non-Malay language he identifies as corrupt Siamese. He provides a Siamese reading for key terms: *rengab* (antidote), *chang* (elephant), *chengrai* (ill fortune), and *genaling* (evil influence), and notes that a Siamese informant he consulted recognized the sounds but could not always parse them, raising the question of whether a non-Siamese element is superimposed.

The bulk of the article is the literal translation of all 84 sections of the manuscript, which covers site selection for elephant enclosures, augury by candle-wick, driving away forest spirits, noosing techniques, the *chelong* (training stocks), veterinary remedies for a dozen named elephant diseases, and physiognomic rules for judging an elephant's fortune. Maxwell flags the extraordinary remedies — rhinoceros navel, fish and prawns for a herbivore, water from a pig's wallow, fragments of an abandoned house's threshold — and the symbolic logic behind them (three or five limes on a single stem mixed with clinging love-grass to bind a wild male to a herd of females).

### Key Findings

- The manuscript was committed to writing in 1296 AH (1879 CE) by Toh Sarif Aman, drawing on the tradition of Tunku Mantri Ibrahim bin Jaffar, Mantri of Larut, who was banished to the Seychelles after the Perak War (p. 1).
- The *chelong* (training stocks) confines a wild elephant for forty to one hundred days; the hundred-day limit is described not as the boundary of the elephant's resistance but of "its tenacity of life," and the common epitaph for forest-caught elephants is *mati dalam chelong* — "it died in the stocks" (p. 45).
- A full year (*pulang pohun*) must elapse between an elephant's release from the stocks and its first being saddled with a *rengka* (pannier); during this period the animal is called *Gajah Barak* (p. 46).
- The words of command in Perak and Kedah differ from each other and are not Malay; for example, "go on" is *hee-hee* in Perak but *hee* in Kedah, "stop" is *hoh-hoh* in Perak but *hoh* in Kedah, and "kneel down" is *têrum* in both but "get up" is *tah* in Perak versus *puan* in Kedah (pp. 42–44).
- The manuscript prescribes remedies including rhinoceros navel skin soaked in honey and Siamese salt (section 78), water from a pig's wallow mixed with merbau resin for a person afflicted by *chemahang* (section 83), and the cooking-place, ladder, and threshold beam of an abandoned house broken up and fed to an elephant to make it return home of its own accord (section 80) (pp. 36, 38).
- Maxwell identifies the Siamese etymology of *rengab* (antidote), *chang* (elephant), *chengrai* (ill fortune), and *genaling* (evil influence), and notes that *genaling* has no "g" in Siamese, indicating corruption in Malay transmission (p. 8).

### Conclusion

Maxwell concludes that the Malay knowledge of elephant management is a composite: an Indian-derived substrate (evidenced by Sanskrit vocabulary for the animal and its gear) overlaid with a Siamese system of capture, training, and magical protection that the Malays inherited from a pre-Malay population and have preserved, in corrupted form, for generations beyond their ability to trace. The mantras, he argues, are the fossilized residue of that Siamese layer, now unintelligible to both Malay and Siamese speakers alike, and their full decipherment remains an open question.

## Context

- Primary source: a single Malay-language manuscript in Maxwell's possession, translated in full; supplemented by oral testimony from Perak elephant drivers and a Siamese informant consulted on the non-Malay words.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malay-Siamese cultural transmission and challenges Crawfurd's Indian-origin thesis for Malay elephant lore, a question that remained unresolved at the time of publication.
