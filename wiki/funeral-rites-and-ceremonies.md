# Funeral rites and ceremonies

Funeral rites and ceremonies across the Malay Peninsula and Borneo encompass a diverse range of practices, from the elaborate state funerals of Malay rulers to the intimate mortuary rituals of Chinese communities, and from prehistoric gold-foil burial amulets to contemporary national mourning periods. These ceremonies serve not merely as expressions of grief but as powerful mechanisms for the construction of social, political, and spiritual narratives—consecrating the dead, legitimising the living, and binding communities to shared understandings of obligation, hierarchy, and continuity. The evidence preserved in the Society's journals spans more than a century of observation, from the detailed administrative record of a 1933 royal funeral in Negri Sembilan to the ethnographic documentation of Cantonese mortuary practice in 1950s Singapore and the archaeological analysis of burial amulets dating to the first century B.C. [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3), [[4]](#ref-4)

## Royal and State Funerary Protocol

The most formally documented funerary ceremony in the archival record is the death, funeral, and succession of Tuanku Muhammad, Yang Di-Pertuan Besar of Negri Sembilan, which took place on 1 and 3 August 1933. I. W. Blelloch and H. P. Bryson, both serving members of the Malayan Civil Service, compiled a contemporaneous administrative account of the proceedings, recording the sequence from the ruler's death at the Astana Besar, Sri Menanti, through the funeral to the formal proclamation of his son Tunku Abdulrahman as successor [[2]](#ref-2). Tuanku Muhammad had reigned for forty-five years since 1898, and his death was marked by a twenty-two-gun salute; a sixty-eight-gun salute was fired at the funeral, corresponding to the late ruler's age in years [[2, pp. 272]](#ref-2).

The notification protocol was meticulously observed. The Tunku Besar Burhanuddin summoned the four *Penghulus* of the Tanah Mengandong, who in turn dispatched *Lembagas* (messengers) to inform the four *Undang* of the death. Blelloch and Bryson offer an interpretive note on the routing of these messengers, suggesting that the assignment of *Lembagas* from Terachi, Gunong Pasir, and Jempul reflected historical travel patterns rather than strict constitutional correspondence between *Penghulu* and *Undang* [[2, pp. 272–273]](#ref-2). The coffin itself was pentagonal in shape, constructed without iron and morticed and fastened with brass and copper screws, and contained approximately nine inches of earth from the grave along with rice and herbs [[2, p. 273]](#ref-2). The traditional ceremonial carriage, the *Maharaja di-raja*, was deemed inadequate for the weight of the coffin, which was instead transported on a specially adapted lorry—a practical concession that nonetheless preserved the ceremonial sequence [[2, p. 273]](#ref-2).

The election of the successor was conducted as a conclave of the four *Undang* in Seremban on 2 August 1933. The British Resident read Section 6 of the 1898 Agreement to confirm unanimity before the decision was communicated to the High Commissioner [[2, p. 276]](#ref-2). The *mas manah* offering from the four *Undang* totalled $384, comprising four *bahara* of $24 each from each *Undang* [[2, pp. 275]](#ref-2). The appendices to the record preserve the original Malay texts of the Dato' Klana's proclamation speech and the new ruler's accession address, the latter explicitly affirming the continuity of the friendship and protection of Great Britain over Negri Sembilan [[2, pp. 278–279]](#ref-2). Blelloch and Bryson note several points of procedural uncertainty, including the anomalous high position of the Dato' Shah Bandar in the order of precedence and the decision to omit the *anak putera* from the ceremonial obeisance, likely due to inability to resolve their relative ranking [[2, pp. 275–276]](#ref-2).

## Chinese Mortuary Practice in Singapore

Marjorie Topley's 1952 ethnographic study provides the most detailed account of Chinese funerary ritual in the archival record, documenting the Cantonese mortuary rites performed in Singapore with particular focus on the *ta chai* ("Paying of Respects") ceremony as carried out in the "Death Houses" of Sago Lane [[4]](#ref-4). Topley's central observation is that, to the Chinese, the adaptation of the soul to its new and complex environment in hell is a matter of the primest importance. This hell, in its administrative aspects, is rather like another China "ploughed under," with a similarly complicated system of rewards, punishments, and financial obligations on the part of the soul. Ransom payments must be made to the ruler of Hades to procure rebirth under favourable circumstances; "squeeze" money must be given to judges, and certificates owned (burnt for one by one's relatives) to enable one to pass any barrier encountered on one's wanderings there [[4, p. 147]](#ref-4).

The full Cantonese mortuary sequence comprises six rites: *K'ai t'an* (Opening the Ceremony), *Po ti-yu* (Breaking Hell), *Kwoh hsin ch'iao* (Crossing the Bridge), *Pu t'u ku yuan* (Universal Helping over of the Wandering Spirits), *San hua* (Distributing Flowers), and the burning of paper things [[4, p. 150]](#ref-4). The "Breaking Hell" rite, based on the Buddhist legend of Mu Lien (Loh Pok) saving his mother from the miseries of hell, involves a structure of nine unfired tiles corresponding to the nine courts of the underworld, each painted with a tiger's head, arranged in a circle with a fire-clay bridge and saucers of oil at the corners. The priest, wielding a flaming sword, dances around the structure and breaks the tiles one by one, the dance becoming quicker and wilder as the deeper strata of the underworld are reached, until the structure is shattered in a sheet of flame [[4, pp. 153–155]](#ref-4).

The *Nam-ma-lo* ("chanting fellows"), lay Taoist priests who do not shave their heads, eat meat, and marry, performed these rites in Singapore and Malaya. Unlike in Kwang-Tung, where a separate monastic type performed post-funeral rites, the *Nam-ma-lo* in the Straits served at all mortuary rites and at others performed in cases of sickness and misfortune [[4, p. 150]](#ref-4). Topley notes that the tradition was already in decline at the time of her fieldwork, referring to "the only remaining Taoist priest in Malacca" [[4, p. 147]](#ref-4). The economic dimensions of these rites were considerable: a first-quality paper house for funeral burning cost approximately $300 before the war and approximately $1,000 in the early 1950s, while a typical *ta chai* ceremony (priest's fee plus paper things) cost about $200 [[4, pp. 148]](#ref-4). The "Death Houses" in Sago Lane functioned as combined nursing homes, mortuaries, and ritual venues, housing temporary soul tablets (*Lung p'ai*) for up to one hundred days after death [[4, pp. 149–150]](#ref-4).

## Prehistoric and Comparative Mortuary Traditions

Stanley J. O'Connor and Tom Harrisson's 1971 article presents photographic and analytical evidence for a shared mortuary convention across maritime Southeast Asia: the placement of thin, leaf-shaped gold foil over the facial orifices of the dead. Drawing on excavated material from Bali, the Philippines, and Sarawak, with comparative parallels from China and South India, the authors argue that the practice represents a persistent ritual tradition spanning at least 1,700 years [[3]](#ref-3). The Gilimanuk eye-cover in Bali, excavated by R. P. Soejono in November 1964, dates to approximately 100 B.C.–100 A.D. and is made of silver with a thin gold facing; it was found attached to skeleton No. LX in sector No. XVIII of a bronze-age settlement containing about 80 skeletons [[3, p. 73]](#ref-3). At Santubong, Sarawak, eleven leaf-shaped gold foil pieces with median breach-cuts were recovered from the Jaong site (late T'ang–early Sung period), ranging from 52 mm to 20 mm in length [[3, p. 73]](#ref-3). The Oton, Iloilo eye covering, dated to the 14th or early 15th century, is decorated with *repousse* dots and is more elaborate and literal in its representation of the eye than the Sarawak pieces [[3, p. 72]](#ref-3).

Spanish colonial records, including an eighteenth-century account by Gaspar de San Agustin and a seventeenth-century text by Francisco Colin, describe the placement of gold sheets over the mouth and eyes of the rich and powerful in Philippine burials [[3, p. 72]](#ref-3). The authors weigh two external candidates for the origin of the practice against the possibility of indigenous development. The Chinese hypothesis rests on the well-documented practice of closing bodily apertures with jade amulets, supported by alchemical texts such as Ko Hung's *Pao P'u Tzu*; however, no gold equivalents have been identified in Chinese tombs, and the earliest Southeast Asian evidence predates any plausible channel of transmission from "nuclear" Chinese civilisation [[3, p. 74]](#ref-3). The Indian parallel from the iron-age urn burials at Adichanallur in Tinnevelly is more compelling in terms of material culture—megalithic thinking, iron working, carnelian beads, and similar *repousse* dot decoration—but the Adichanallur pieces differ in scale and were often found crushed, suggesting a different handling tradition [[3, p. 75]](#ref-3). O'Connor and Harrisson ultimately decline to resolve the question of origin, suggesting instead a broadly shared monsoonal Asian context [[3]](#ref-3).

## State Funerals and National Narrative

The state funeral of Lee Kuan Yew in March 2015 provides the most extensive contemporary documentation of funerary ritual as a vehicle for national narrative construction. Martha Kaplan and John Kelly's introduction to a special section of five ethnographic papers situates the collection within the context of 2015 in Singapore, a year marked by both the SG50 independence anniversary and Lee's death in March [[5]](#ref-5). The project originated when anthropologist Bernard Bate of Yale-NUS College interrupted his ritual theory class to study the public mourning events, and tragically he died in March 2016 before completing his own commentary [[5, p. 82]](#ref-5).

The scale of the state funeral was considerable. Approximately 450,000 people attended the lying-in-state at Parliament House, and over 1.2 million visited 18 condolence centres across Singapore during the mourning week [[6, pp. 125–126]](#ref-6). The State Funeral Organising Committee deployed approximately 40,000 public officers and 1,000 volunteers; 500 military personnel from the 3rd Battalion of the Singapore Guards erected 300 tents overnight to shelter mourners at the Padang [[6, pp. 126–128]](#ref-6). One hundred and seventy foreign dignitaries representing 27 countries, regions, and organisations attended the state funeral on 29 March 2015 [[6, p. 126]](#ref-6). The state funeral procession route was 15.4 km, carefully curated to pass locations commemorating milestones in Lee's political career, including Padang, Marina Bay, the Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau, and the public housing estates of Bukit Merah and Queenstown [[1, p. 118]](#ref-1).

R. Hong Cheng Yee's analysis argues that two distinct narratives were generated during the mourning period: one of Lee as ancestor, generated primarily by individuals, and another of Lee as founding father, generated primarily by the state [[1]](#ref-1). The ancestor narrative emerged organically from individual mourners connecting Lee to Chinese folk traditions of ancestor veneration—evident in both textual comments and the physical practices of bowing before tribute boards—while the founding father narrative was deliberately inscribed onto physical landscapes through the procession route [[1, pp. 116–119]](#ref-1). The earliest Facebook comment representing Lee as ancestor was posted at 4:30 a.m. on 23 March, invoking the Chinese folk saying *liu san can gei hou ren* ("leaving three meals behind to the descendants"), which interprets a morning death as a sign that the material needs of descendants would be met [[1, p. 117]](#ref-1). A rainstorm on the day of the funeral became a shared symbol that simultaneously served both narratives: for individuals, it represented ancestor beneficence; for the state, it evoked the 1968 National Day Parade and a narrative of national endurance into a post-Lee era [[1, pp. 118–119]](#ref-1).

John Kelly's broader analysis situates the funeral within Singapore's longer history of converting death into sacrifice to motivate and justify national existence. Because Singapore explicitly abjures claims of nationhood by blood, kinship, or ethnic heritage, its stories of blood sacrificed carry particular weight in consecrating the nation and its future [[7]](#ref-7). Kelly traces the layered history of Singapore's war memorials, from the Cenotaph (1922), which honoured only European dead, to the Civilian War Memorial (1967), designed with a four-fold parallel structure representing Chinese, Eurasian, Indian, and Malay civilian suffering under Japanese occupation [[7, pp. 86–98]](#ref-7). The public's response to Lee's death—expressed as personal debt and obligation—demonstrated what Kelly calls "civic sincerity," a mode of political engagement that overtopped state orchestration and revealed the genuine motivational force of sacrifice narratives in a prosperous, technocratic state [[7, pp. 84–85]](#ref-7).

The mourning period also generated friction. Ronald Chen's article examines the intersection of state ritual and dissent, documenting how the sacralization of Lee's death as that of a "founding father" and "ancestor" gave rise to a degree of tension between the dominant response and counter-voices, most notably when 16-year-old Amos Yee posted a YouTube video insulting Lee and Christianity, triggering his arrest and a sharply polarised public response [[6]](#ref-6). Chen argues that Yee functions as a "floating signifier"—his case carries different meanings for different audiences—and that the juxtaposition of dominant mourning rituals with Yee's counter-voice signals an evolving political culture in which minority and dissenting voices are increasingly asserting themselves in the public sphere [[6]](#ref-6).

Alisha Cherian's article extends the analysis beyond Singapore's borders, examining how Singaporean mass media and social media users reacted to reports of grassroots mourning rituals for Lee in the Tamil Nadu villages of Ullikkottai and Mannargudi. In Ullikkottai (approximately 3,000 households) and Mannargudi, residents planned a large-scale brass statue, a memorial hall, and a museum dedicated to Lee; a local man named his newborn son after Lee [[8, pp. 137]](#ref-8). Cherian frames the analysis through Arnold van Gennep's rites of passage and Victor Turner's concept of liminality, arguing that the week of national mourning, the SG50 celebrations, and the 2015 general election together created an extended liminal period in which Singapore as a nation-state had to reconstruct its identity without its founding father [[8, pp. 138–139]](#ref-8). The Tamil Nadu mourning rituals functioned as "sacra" within this liminal space—symbolically charged elements that catalysed reflection on race, immigration, statehood, and national identity [[8, pp. 138–139]](#ref-8).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on funeral rites and ceremonies reveals a clear evolution in methodological approach and analytical framework over the period covered by the archival records. The earliest document, Blelloch and Bryson's 1936 account of the Negri Sembilan royal funeral, is an administrative record compiled by serving civil servants as a contemporaneous documentation of proceedings rather than a scholarly analysis [[2]](#ref-2). Its value lies in the preservation of procedural detail, the Malay-language appendices, and the candid notation of points of uncertainty in ceremonial protocol.

Topley's 1952 study represents a shift toward ethnographic fieldwork, with direct observation, participant accounts, and photographic documentation of Cantonese mortuary rites in Singapore. Her account is notable for its attention to the material culture of death—the paper effigies, the "Death Houses," the economic costs of ritual—and for its recording of a performative tradition that was already in decline at the time of writing [[4]](#ref-4). The study contributes to the ethnographic record of Chinese religious practice in Malaya at a moment when the *Nam-ma-lo* tradition was waning, making it a valuable documentary source for a practice that has since largely disappeared [[4]](#ref-4).

O'Connor and Harrisson's 1971 article marks a turn toward archaeological and comparative analysis, addressing what the authors identify as a conspicuous gap in the literature: the near-total absence of systematic discussion of gold-foil burial amulets in Southeast Asia [[3]](#ref-3). Their contribution lies in providing the photographic documentation and wider comparative context that their earlier 1970 data paper could not, and in introducing the Adichanallur material as a previously underutilised comparative datum for early iron-age burial practice in the region [[3]](#ref-3).

The cluster of five papers on the Lee Kuan Yew funeral, published in 2016, represents the most recent and methodologically diverse body of work in the archival record. Kaplan and Kelly's introduction situates the collection within the context of 2015 and acknowledges the tragic loss of Bernard Bate, whose death before completing his own commentary underscores the urgency of documenting such events [[5]](#ref-5). The individual papers apply a range of theoretical frameworks—Kaplan's ritual theory, van Gennep's rites of passage, Turner's liminality, Durkheim's collective effervescence, Williams's structures of feeling, and Spivak's postcolonial critique—to a single contemporary event, producing a multi-dimensional analysis of how funerary ritual functions as a site for the construction, contestation, and performance of national identity [[1]](#ref-1), [[8]](#ref-8), [[6]](#ref-6), [[7]](#ref-7). The collection extends the earlier work of Lily Kong and Brenda Yeoh on national spectacle to the domain of state funerals, bridging political geography and public memory studies [[1]](#ref-1), [[6]](#ref-6).

Across the full span of the records, a consistent theme emerges: funeral rites are never merely private or symbolic acts but are embedded in structures of power, economy, and narrative that extend well beyond the immediate occasion of death. From the constitutional protocols of a Malay succession to the political ramifications of a national mourning period, from the financial obligations of a Cantonese family to the archaeological evidence of a 1,700-year-old mortuary tradition, the archival record demonstrates that the treatment of the dead remains a fundamental arena in which societies negotiate their understanding of obligation, hierarchy, and continuity.
