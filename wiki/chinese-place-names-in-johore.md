# Chinese place-names in Johore

J.V. Cowgill published "Chinese Place-names in Johore" in December 1924 in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 2, No. 3, pp. 221–251). Writing from within the colonial administration, Cowgill compiled the first systematic reference list of Chinese toponyms for the state, arguing that the defunct *kangka* plantation system was the primary generator of the place-name landscape that persisted long after the system's abolition in 1911.

## Summary

The article is fundamentally a practical gazetteer, organized by administrative district (Batu Pahat, Kukub, Johore Baharu, Kota Tinggi, Muar, Segamat, Endau) and further subdivided by river systems. Each entry pairs a Malay name with its Chinese equivalent in characters and romanized form, with Teochiew chosen as the default dialect for romanization because of its numerical predominance and its function as a *lingua franca* among Chinese communities in southern Johore. Exceptions are made for Muar (Hokkien) and Mersing (Cantonese) districts, where other dialects held local sway (p. 222).

Cowgill devotes his introductory discussion to explaining the *kangka* system, which he identifies as the structural source of nearly all established Chinese place-names in the state. A *kangka* was a collection of families, usually of the same surname, living and working as serfs under a headman (*Kang Chu*) who was officially recognized by the colonial government and granted sweeping administrative and economic monopolies over his territory. The *Kang Chu* held the opium and gaming farms, exclusive rights of pawnbroking, liquor sales, and pork slaughter, and paid rent or taxes for the entire area, effectively removing the burden of Chinese peasant administration from the government (p. 221). Cowgill asserts that it is "hardly an exaggeration" to state that no area in South Johore could be described without reference to the nearest *kangka*, and that names derived from these settlements survived long after the original plantations disappeared and the physical orientation of the neighbourhood was altered by roads and railways (pp. 221–222).

A secondary concern is the "dog-Chinese" phenomenon, in which Malay sounds are rendered in Chinese characters for railway stations, post offices, and other official signage. Cowgill acknowledges that these forms are inconsistent—citing Segamat as an example where the railway station, post office, and Chinese school each used a different character form for the same name—and that no standardization is likely. He includes such forms only where no pure Chinese equivalent exists and the place is of sufficient local importance to warrant inclusion (p. 222). The list concludes with a comprehensive alphabetical index of all entries (pp. 248–251).

### Key Findings

- The *kangka* system, abolished in 1911, was the structural source of nearly all well-established Chinese place-names in Johore; the *Kang Chu* held monopolies over opium, gaming, pawnbroking, liquor, and pork slaughter, and paid taxes for his entire territory (p. 221).
- Teochiew was selected as the default romanization dialect based on the 1921 census, which showed Teochiews still numerically predominant in South Johore and their dialect functioning as a *lingua franca* across much of the state (p. 222).
- The Yi Hing (Gi Hin) Society, referenced in a Johore Baharu street-name entry, was a registered society in Johore until 1916 (p. 231).
- The list covers approximately 255 numbered entries across seven districts plus a supplement for islands and adjoining territories, with a full alphabetical index (pp. 224–251).
- In Segamat, four different character forms for the same place name were in simultaneous use across the railway station, post office, Chinese school, and a private letter, illustrating the absence of any standardization mechanism (p. 222).
- The *kangka* was described as "peculiar to Johore" within recent years, distinguishing it from similar systems elsewhere in the Straits Settlements or Federated States (p. 221).

### Conclusion

Cowgill's definitive takeaway is that the Chinese toponymic landscape of Johore is a fossil record of the *kangka* plantation system: the names persist as administrative and social markers long after the institutional framework that produced them was dismantled, and any practical engagement with Chinese-language place-names in the state requires understanding this now-defunct structure. The list is explicitly framed as a working tool for government servants and others in contact with the Chinese language, not as a scholarly etymological study.

## Context

- Cowgill was a colonial government official; the article is framed as a practical reference for "government servants" and others whose "business brings them into contact with the Chinese language" (p. 221). His acknowledgments to Assistant Advisers, the Chief Police Officer, and a D.K.-bearing Malay official confirm his position within the Johore administration (p. 223).
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in documenting the *kangka* system's toponymic legacy at a moment when the system had been formally abolished for over a decade but its social and linguistic imprint remained pervasive, providing a baseline record before further modernization eroded these names.
