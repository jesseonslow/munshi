# ‘An Hour Before Dawn’: Social and Political Awareness among English-Educated Students in Post-War Singapore

## Abstract

Histories of post-war student activism in Singapore have generally focused on the Chinese middle school students, as well as undergraduates at the University of Malaya, with comparatively little attention to the attitudes and actions of the English-educated students in Government schools. This article adds to a growing historiography of the period by pointing to the potential of student publications as primary sources, read in context of late colonial education policy and the broader trajectory of Anglophone writing from Singapore. A close reading of six prose texts published by students at Raffles Institution in 1954, a watershed year for student activism, suggests that student writing can provide significant clues about the level of social and political awareness among the English medium schools of the period.

## Summary

This article addresses a persistent lacuna in the historiography of post-war Singapore student activism: the near-exclusive focus on Chinese middle school students and University of Malaya undergraduates, at the expense of the English-educated pupils in government schools. Kwek argues that student publications—specifically a 1954 class magazine from Raffles Institution—offer a viable and largely untapped primary source for understanding the social and political awareness of this cohort. He situates these texts within two parallel frameworks: the institutional tensions of late colonial education policy (the simultaneous grooming of future citizens and the maintenance of imperial trappings such as the Cadet Corps and Empire Day rallies), and the literary evolution of Anglophone writing in Singapore from imitative colonial forms toward a distinctively Malayan voice.

Through close reading of six prose texts from the Form VIII P class magazine, Kwek identifies three thematic clusters: responses to social injustice, the question of national loyalty and belonging, and the role of citizens in an emerging polity. The writing displays what he terms, following Choo and Bhabha, a combination of "mimicry and subversion"—derivative of Western literary tradition in form, yet containing moments of genuine questioning that gesture toward political consciousness. Cheng Tim Pin's sympathetic portrait of an unlicensed hawker, Richard Lam's empathetic depiction of Communist insurgents, and Lim Kheng Hock's inclusive vision of the city at dawn all point to a cohort that was acutely aware of the injustices of colonial rule even as it benefited from its educational largesse.

Kwek is careful to note that this does not mean English-educated students were as politically mobilised as their Chinese-educated peers. Rather, the article opens new lines of inquiry into why political mobilisation did not occur as visibly among this population during their high school years, even though many later became active at university. The student writing provides a window into the "life-worlds" of these students at a moment when the shape of the nation was not yet a foregone conclusion.

### Key Findings

- The six prose texts examined were published in the class magazine of Form VIII P, Raffles Institution, in 1954—the same year that saw the Chinese middle school students' petitioning of Chief Secretary Goode on 13 May and the pre-dawn arrest of eight University of Malaya students for the *Fajar* journal (pp. 83–84).
- Cheng Tim Pin's "The Unfortunates" depicts an unlicensed street hawker's encounter with police, offering a rare statement of solidarity with a marginalised occupation in Anglophone student writing of the period; the Hawkers Inquiry Commission of 1950 had recommended decongestion and licensing, but many hawkers continued to trade illegally and risk heavy-handed police encounters (pp. 89–92).
- Richard Lam's "Somewhere in Malaya" depicts Communist insurgents in the jungle with evident sympathy, deploying the phrase "Union is Strength" (a song associated with the student left) and contrasting the malnourished, exhausted state of the insurgents with the unseen counter-insurgency forces who execute them; his final line—"Such is the sorrowful scene repeated monotonously in some parts of Malaya"—underscores his sympathetic position (pp. 95–97).
- The Raffles Institution Cadet Corps was re-started in 1948 with Education Department funding and quickly attained "pre-war strength"; annual Empire Day rallies continued with the school captain reading the Earl of Gowrie's address, and the Bishop of Singapore reminded students of their "loyalty towards the British Commonwealth" at a war-dead commemoration (pp. 87–88).
- The University of Malaya was inaugurated on 8 October 1949 through the merger of Raffles College and the King Edward VII College of Medicine; founding chancellor Malcolm MacDonald described it as "the crucible of the Malayan nation" (p. 87).
- *The Rafflesian* was published three times in 1954 (the first time after the war), and the Magazine Committee purchased an electrical Gestetner machine to encourage literary effort; the editors described being "swamped by a wave of literature" and spoke of laying "the foundation stone" for a Malayan literary tradition (pp. 89–90).

### Conclusion

Kwek's definitive historical takeaway is that the common assumption of English-educated political apathy is not supported by the evidence in students' own published writing. The texts reveal a cohort that was "acutely aware of the injustices of under British rule even as they benefited from its largesse," and the narrative of insurmountable ethnic and linguistic division in decolonisation fails to account for the ties of solidarity that bound some English-educated students to their Chinese-educated peers. The article is explicitly preliminary, inviting a more comprehensive study that could corroborate these findings with oral histories and reveal more specifically why political mobilisation did not occur as visibly among this population during their high school years.

## Context

- Primary archival source: the digitised back issues of *The Rafflesian* and other Raffles Institution publications (dating back to 1886), made available online by the Raffles Archives and Museum in preparation for the school's bicentennial (p. 86).
- Historiographical contribution: extends the growing body of work questioning the English/Chinese student dichotomy (Weiss, Huang, Loh) by introducing student publications as a new primary source category for the period, and by situating Anglophone student writing within the broader trajectory of Singapore literature in English as charted by Patke and Holden (pp. 84–85, 88–89).
