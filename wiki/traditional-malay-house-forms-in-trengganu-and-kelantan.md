# Traditional Malay house forms in Trengganu and Kelantan

Charles Hose, a British colonial administrator and ethnographer based in Borneo, published this article in 1905 in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*. The piece examines how Dayak, Kenyah, and Kayan communities determine the correct seasonal timing for their slash-and-burn agricultural cycle in the absence of a written calendar. Hose's overarching thesis is that these peoples have developed sophisticated empirical instruments for tracking the solar year, though such knowledge is invariably accompanied by ritual and superstition.

## Summary

The article addresses a specific problem of tropical agriculture: near the equator, temperature and moisture offer little reliable signal for the time of year, making it difficult for farmers to know when to clear, burn, and sow. Hose explains that the critical factor is not the rainy season but the dry season, because the success of the cycle depends on the completeness of the clearing and the dryness of the felled timber at the time of burning. Without a written almanac or a clock, and with day-length varying very little in the tropics, these communities must turn to celestial observation or solar measurement.

Hose surveys three distinct methods. The Dayaks use stellar observation—specifically the pre-dawn rising of the Pleiades and Orion's belt—as seasonal markers, supplemented by omens sought in the jungle by pairs of men sent ahead of the main work party. The Kenyahs employ a gnomon: a vertical pole whose midday shadow is measured against a notched stick, the notches representing regular intervals of shadow change accumulated over years of observation. The Kayans use a variant method, admitting a beam of sunlight through a hole in the roof and measuring its distance to the floor with a levelled plank, demonstrating knowledge that the noon sun is always due north or due south.

Hose notes that the Kenyahs have not progressed to a sun-dial, which he attributes to the tropical problem that near the equinoxes the shadow points due west or east and any directional time-piece would fail for much of the year. He also observes that these empirical measurements are accompanied by what he terms "meaningless mummery," and that the designated observer is excused from farming in return for his services to the community.

### Key Findings

- The Dayaks use the pre-dawn rising of the Pleiades (called "Apai andau," the "father of the day") as the signal to begin felling, and the Pleiades reaching the zenith before dawn as the signal to burn and sow (p. 2).
- The Kenyah gnomon is a pole "more than a fathom" in height, verified as vertical by a weighted string thrown over its top, and is guarded by a fence to keep away children and animals (p. 4).
- The Kenyah measuring stick ("asu do") is notched at intervals representing three days of shadow change, with notches approaching more closely as they move further from the pole (p. 4).
- The optimal planting time is reached when the midday shadow equals the length of the forearm from fingertip to inner elbow; if the shadow is less than the length of the hand, sowing is unlikely to be productive (p. 5).
- The Kayan method uses a beam of light admitted through a hole in the roof, measured against a levelled plank fixed by chocks, demonstrating knowledge that the noon sun is always due north or due south (p. 4).
- The designated shadow-observer is excused from farming and supplied with necessaries in return for his services; in good years he is "very well treated" (p. 5).

### Conclusion

Hose's definitive takeaway is that Bornean indigenous peoples have developed genuinely accurate empirical instruments for tracking the solar year—comparable in principle to Greek gnomonics—yet these are embedded in a matrix of ritual and superstition that a European observer finds baffling. The article thus functions as an early example of the colonial ethnographic impulse to document "primitive science" while simultaneously framing it through the lens of European intellectual categories.

## Context

- Charles Hose served as a senior British colonial administrator in Sarawak and British North Borneo; his ethnographic writings, while empirically careful, reflect the Victorian colonial framework that categorized indigenous knowledge as "rude" or "primitive" even when acknowledging its accuracy.
- The article appeared in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (No. 42, February 1905), a periodical that served as a primary venue for colonial administrative and ethnographic reporting in the Straits Settlements and surrounding territories.
