# A list of the reptiles of Borneo

R.S. Shelford, Curator of the Sarawak Museum, published this comprehensive checklist of Borneo's reptilian fauna in 1901, synthesising nearly two decades of collecting activity across the island into a single reference work. The article's central purpose is taxonomic and biogeographical: to establish, as of December 1900, the full complement of reptile species known from Borneo proper, to identify which of those are endemic, and to correct errors in earlier literature arising from descriptions based on faded spirit specimens.

## Summary

Shelford's list encompasses 87 genera and 212 species across three major groups—chelonians (including crocodilians and turtles), lizards, and snakes—drawn from the collections of the Sarawak Museum, the British Museum, and other institutions. He explicitly excludes the Natuna and Palawan island groups, treating them as separate zoogeographical entities with their own published lists. The work builds directly on the foundational collections assembled by A.H. Everett, John Whitehead, and C. Hose, whose fieldwork across Sarawak, North Borneo, and the Dutch territories provided the bulk of the locality data. Shelford acknowledges that the fauna was already "well-known" by 1900, citing the recent discovery of new species on Mount Kinabalu (1899) and Mount Penrissen (1899) as evidence that the island was approaching full herpetological survey, though he concedes that a few species still awaited discovery.

A distinctive feature of the checklist is Shelford's practice of recording colouration from living or freshly dead specimens where it differed markedly from published descriptions, which he attributes to the deterioration of spirit-preserved material. This is evident in his notes on *Gonatodes penangensis* (coal black with minute yellow spots, unlike Malay Peninsula specimens), *Cylindrophis lineatus* (iridescent black with red head and tail), and *Calamaria leucogaster* (iridescent brown with eight longitudinal black stripes and a red tail). He also provides a full morphological diagnosis for *Lygosoma whiteheadi*, whose original description he judged inadequate, giving scale counts, measurements in millimetres, and a detailed account of head and body proportions.

The article serves as both a taxonomic reference and a biogeographical statement. By identifying 64 species as endemic to Borneo and six genera as entirely peculiar to the island, Shelford quantifies the degree of faunal distinctiveness that would later become central to debates about Borneo's zoogeographical isolation. His locality data—ranging from the headwaters of the Baram to the Sebroeang valley in Dutch Borneo—provides a spatial framework that maps the distribution of endemics against the island's major river systems and mountain ranges.

### Key Findings

- The Bornean reptile fauna comprises 87 genera and 212 species as of December 1900, of which 64 species and 6 genera (*Brookeia*, *Lanthanotus*, *Hydrablabe*, *Lepturophis*, *Oreocalamus*, *Idiopholis*) are endemic to the island (pp. 44–45).
- The genus *Calamaria* is the most species-rich endemic group, with 19 of the 64 endemic species falling within it, indicating a remarkable radiation of blind snakes in Borneo (p. 45).
- Three species are recorded for the first time from Borneo in this list: *Gonatodes penangensis* (Mt. Penrissen, 3000 ft), *Cylindrophis lineatus* (Pankalan Ampat), and *Xenodermus javanicus* (Kuching) (pp. 49, 56).
- *Lanthanotus borneensis*, the sole representative of a genus endemic to Borneo, is known from only two specimens at the time: one in the Sarawak Museum (Plagus Rapids, Rejang River, collected by Bampfylde) and the type in the Vienna Museum (p. 52).
- The *Draco* genus (flying lizards) is represented by nine species in Borneo, five of which (*D. cornutus*, *D. obscurus*, *D. cristatellus*, *D. maximus*, *D. microlepis*) are endemic, making it the most species-rich endemic lizard genus on the island (pp. 45, 51–52).
- *Lachesis* (pit vipers) is represented by five species, four of which are endemic or near-endemic (*L. borneensis*, *L. puniceus*, *L. gramineus*, *L. sumatranus*), with *L. wagleri* being the only widely distributed form (p. 68).

### Conclusion

Shelford's definitive takeaway is that Borneo's herpetological fauna, while approaching completeness, is characterised by a high degree of endemism concentrated in the island's upland and riverine habitats. The work establishes a baseline against which future discoveries could be measured, and its emphasis on locality precision and colouration correction sets a methodological standard for subsequent checklists of the region.

## Context

- Shelford wrote as Curator of the Sarawak Museum, giving him direct access to the island's principal herpetological collection; the "S.M." abbreviation throughout the list denotes specimens in that collection, making the work partly an institutional catalogue as well as a faunal summary.
- The article draws primarily on the Sarawak Museum collection, the British Museum Catalogues (Blanford), and the published descriptions of Mocquard (1890), Gunther, and Boulenger; its historiographical contribution lies in consolidating scattered records into a single localised checklist that remained the standard reference for Bornean herpetology until superseded by later syntheses.
