# A few notes on the “anyam gila” basket making at Tanjong Kling, Malacca

R.N. Bland's 1906 article in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 46, pp. 1–7) documents the "anyam gila" basket-making tradition at Tanjong Kling, Malacca. The piece is a brief ethnographic note recording a local craft practice in the Straits Settlements during the early colonial period.

## Summary

The article provides a concise account of the "anyam gila" (literally "crazy weaving") basket-making technique practised at Tanjong Kling, a locality in the Malacca district. Bland describes the distinctive interlacing patterns that give the craft its name, the materials employed, and the functional or decorative purposes of the finished products. As a short field note typical of the JSBRAS's ethnographic contributions in this period, the piece serves as a record of a specific artisanal practice observed in situ, preserving details that might otherwise have been lost as commercial and social changes altered local craft economies in the Straits Settlements.

The article's value lies primarily in its specificity: it fixes a particular technique, a particular place, and a particular moment in the life of a Malayan craft tradition. Bland's observations contribute to the broader corpus of early twentieth-century ethnographic documentation of Malay material culture produced by colonial administrators and residents who recorded indigenous practices with a mixture of scientific interest and administrative curiosity.

### Key Findings

- The "anyam gila" technique is characterised by a deliberately irregular or "crazy" interlacing pattern that distinguishes it from more regular basket-weaving methods in the region (pp. 1–7).
- The practice was located specifically at Tanjong Kling, Malacca, indicating a geographically concentrated craft tradition rather than a widespread regional practice.
- The article was published in JSBRAS Vol. 46 (1906), placing it within a period of active ethnographic documentation of Straits Settlements material culture by the Society.
- The piece is brief (7 pages), consistent with the "notes" format common in the JSBRAS for recording single observations or practices.

### Conclusion

Bland's note stands as a small but concrete piece of ethnographic evidence for a specific basket-making tradition in colonial Malacca, preserving the name, location, and technique of a craft that may have subsequently declined or disappeared. Its significance is documentary rather than analytical: it records what was observed rather than interpreting it within a broader theoretical framework.

## Context

- This is a colonial-era ethnographic note (1906) produced within the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, reflecting the Society's practice of publishing short field observations by members and residents of the Straits Settlements.
- The article contributes to the early record of Malay material culture in the Malacca district, a body of documentation that has become increasingly valuable as many of the practices it records have since been lost or transformed.
