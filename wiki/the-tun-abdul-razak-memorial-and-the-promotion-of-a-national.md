# The Tun Abdul Razak Memorial and the promotion of a national memory in Malaysia

Abu Talib Ahmad's 2011 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines how the Tun Abdul Razak Memorial Museum, opened in 1982 at the former official residence Sri Taman in Kuala Lumpur, constructs a selective national memory of Malaysia's second Prime Minister. Drawing on Benedict Anderson's distinction between popular and state-engineered nationalism, Ahmad argues that the memorial's systematic foregrounding of Tun Abdul Razak as "father of development" is functionally continuous with colonial-era museum practices of using cultural institutions to serve state interests.

## Summary

The article undertakes a detailed walkthrough of the memorial's two exhibition halls, its library, and its ancillary facilities, comparing what is displayed against what is known from biographical and scholarly literature. Ahmad identifies a consistent pattern of "exclusions and silences": the memorial omits Tun Abdul Razak's membership in the British Labour Party and Fabian Society, his socialist reading list, the role of leftist intellectuals (James Puthucheary, A. Samad Ismail, Abdullah Majid) in formulating the New Economic Policy, the University and University College Act of 1971, and the political fate of many development projects after 1976. The narrative also sidelines Abdul Aziz Ishak, whose earlier rural development work as Minister of Agriculture and Cooperatives (1955–63) was effectively erased from the national story after his expulsion from UMNO and detention under the Internal Security Act in 1965.

The author situates the memorial within a broader ecosystem of state-managed commemoration in Malaysia, noting that all three of the country's first prime ministers have dedicated memorial museums in Kuala Lumpur, all under the custody of the Ministry of Information, Communication and Culture with input from the National Archives. The memorial's linear narrative—emphasizing the Razak Report, the establishment of UKM and ITM, FELDA, the NEP, the National Front, and non-aligned foreign policy—presents a sanitized version that avoids the tensions between Malay and Malaysian leadership, the crony capitalism that emerged with the NEP, and the internal UMNO power struggles that consumed Tun Abdul Razak's final years. Ahmad observes that the memorial's primary audience is schoolchildren on organized tours, for whom the experience largely replicates the school history textbook, raising questions about the efficacy of such visits as instruments of civic education.

The article also surveys the biographical literature on Tun Abdul Razak—Shaw's foundational 1976 study, Paridah Abdul Samad's 1998 political biography, Dharmala's 2005 account, and the 2011 UKM Press biography by Nik Anuar and colleagues—assessing how each work relates to or diverges from the memorial's narrative. Ahmad notes that none of these more nuanced or critical accounts have influenced the memorial's exhibits, which have remained essentially unchanged since 1982 despite the addition of new items.

### Key Findings

- The memorial opened on 6 May 1982, a date deliberately chosen to coincide with both the formation of the 1956 Education Committee chaired by Tun Abdul Razak and the 1960 launch of the Red Book; it housed 700 exhibits at opening and had grown to 1,650 by 2010 (pp. 5–6).
- FELDA, Tun Abdul Razak's signature rural development project, opened 145,000 acres of virgin jungle for agricultural settlement in its first ten years (from 1956), predominantly for Malay rural poor (p. 20).
- The New Economic Policy, implemented from 1971, raised the Malay economic stake from approximately 3% to 17–18% by the 1980s, while simultaneously fostering crony capitalism and a new business elite (p. 21).
- The National Front, inaugurated in 1974 with UMNO at its head, won 135 of 154 parliamentary seats in the 1974 general election, aided by the Kuala Lumpur–Beijing diplomatic rapprochement of May 1974 (p. 16).
- Only one scholar, the late Professor Dato' Nik Anuar Nik Mahmud, was ever granted access to Tun Abdul Razak's classified official papers held at the National Archives, which require written permission from the Prime Minister's Office to consult (p. 3).
- The memorial omits any reference to the University and University College Act (UUCA) of 1971, which provoked violent student protests at Universiti Malaya and Universiti Sains Malaysia, or to the group of leftist intellectuals who provided the intellectual architecture for the NEP (pp. 22, 24).

### Conclusion

Ahmad concludes that the Tun Abdul Razak Memorial, the national narrative, and the National Museum collectively reinforce one another in promoting a curated, idealized image of the second Prime Minister that is "no different from the way court chroniclers fashion their narrative." The systematic control of both the official history and the memorial makes it difficult to imagine commemoration in any other form, and the entrenched popular reverence for the late premier—compounded by religious sensibilities surrounding the deceased—further constrains critical engagement. Ahmad expresses hope that emerging visitor feedback mechanisms in Malaysian museums might eventually prompt meaningful changes in how exhibits are presented.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the memorial's own exhibits, its 1982 catalogue published by the National Archives, and the major biographical studies (Shaw 1976; Paridah 1998; Nik Anuar et al. 2011), supplemented by secondary literature on Malaysian political economy (Gomez and Jomo 1997) and foreign policy (Chandran 2008).
- Historiographically, the study contributes to the emerging field of memory and museum studies in Southeast Asia by applying Anderson's framework of state-engineered nationalism to a post-independence political memorial, and by systematically cataloguing the "exclusions and silences" that distinguish the official commemorative narrative from the more complex picture revealed by archival and biographical research.
