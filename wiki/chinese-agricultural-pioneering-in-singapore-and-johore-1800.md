# Chinese agricultural pioneering in Singapore and Johore, 1800–1917

James C. Jackson's 1965 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the history of Chinese gambier and pepper cultivation in Singapore and Johore between 1800 and 1917, arguing that this form of agricultural pioneering was the most extensive and successful export-oriented enterprise in southern Malaya prior to the rubber era. Jackson contends that the system's success lay not in the crops themselves but in the organisational framework—the "Kangchu System"—through which impecunious Chinese immigrants established self-sufficient agricultural colonies in virgin terrain.

## Summary

Jackson addresses a significant gap in the historiography of nineteenth-century Malaya, where political narratives of British administrators and Malay rulers have dominated at the expense of the social and economic realities of the frontier settlements they governed. The article reconstructs the processes by which Chinese immigrants, arriving largely under the Credit Ticket System, established gambier and pepper plantations in Singapore from the 1820s and subsequently expanded into Johore from the late 1820s onward. The central analytical contribution is Jackson's clarification of the spatial and administrative terminology—distinguishing between *kangkar* (the riverine village headquarters and its surrounding territory), *bangsal* (the individual cultivated clearing of 10–50 acres), and the loosely used term "plantation"—and his demonstration of how these units related to one another within the broader Kangchu System.

The article draws on a combination of contemporary administrative reports, Chinese-language sources (notably Tan Tek Soon's 1902 account and Siah U Chin's 1848 observations), cartographic evidence including the 1887 and 1904 maps of Johore, and place-name analysis to trace the expanding "frontier" of colonisation. Jackson shows that the system was fundamentally a truck system: urban-based merchants in Singapore provided credit to Kangchus, who in turn financed individual planters working bangsals, with produce sold back to the merchant at prices approximately 30 per cent below market value. The shifting cultivation method, driven by soil exhaustion (gambier depleted land in roughly 15 years) and the need for firewood to boil gambier leaves, produced a landscape of abandoned clearings reverting to lalang and belukar, perpetually pushing the frontier outward along river valleys.

### Key Findings

- By 1848, gambier and pepper occupied 24,220 acres and 2,614 acres respectively in Singapore, accounting for nearly three-fifths of the total value of agricultural produce on the island; gambier production rose from 22,000 pikuls in 1836 to 80,000 pikuls in 1848 (pp. 78–79).
- By the early 1860s, approximately 1,200 gambier and pepper plantations in Johore employed a labour force of some 15,000; in 1864, Chinese merchants in Singapore claimed to have advanced over one million dollars to develop these plantations (pp. 81, 83).
- The earliest recorded *surat sungei* (river document) granting Kangchu authority is dated 1833 for part of the Sungei Skudai; the system was formally codified by the *Law of the Kangchus* in 1873 and finally abolished by the *Kangchu Rights (Abolition) Enactment* of 1917 (pp. 82, 84).
- Johore's gambier exports to Singapore rose from 316,063 pikuls in 1880 to 400,544 pikuls in 1889, with pepper exports rising from 56,203 to 117,024 pikuls over the same period; by 1889 there were an estimated 4,000 gambier factories in the State (pp. 96–97).
- In Singapore, the total acreage under gambier and pepper fell from approximately 11,000 acres in 1890 to 600 acres in 1912 and 75 acres in 1913, as Chinese planters shifted to pineapple and rubber (pp. 94–95).
- Over 90 per cent of Chinese gambier and pepper planters in Singapore in 1848 were Teochew (Tiechiu) dialect speakers, and this dialect group dominated the expansion into Johore (pp. 80, 82).

### Conclusion

Jackson's definitive takeaway is that the Kangchu System was a specific manifestation of the broader *Kongsi* system that underpinned all successful Chinese enterprise in nineteenth-century Malaya. The system's longevity—surviving almost intact from the 1830s to 1917—was due to its capacity to provide security, credit, and labour independently of the existing colonial or Malay administration. Only when local governments themselves began supplying these foundations did European planters and miners seriously challenge Chinese dominance. The abolition of the Kangchu System in 1917 thus marked not merely the end of an administrative arrangement but the closing of an era in which Chinese immigrant society had been the primary engine of economic development in southern Malaya.

## Context

- Jackson's evidence base draws on Straits Settlements Blue Books, Annual Reports of the Straits Settlements, the *Singapore Free Press*, the *Singapore and Straits Directory* (1887), the 1887 and 1904 maps of Johore (Royal Geographical Society Map Collection), and Chinese-language sources including Tan Tek Soon's *Straits Chinese Magazine* article (1902) and Siah U Chin's *Journal of the Indian Archipelago and Eastern Asia* contribution (1848).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in shifting attention from the political history of Malaya to the economic and physical geography of Chinese agricultural colonisation, and in providing the first systematic clarification of the Kangchu System's internal structure and spatial logic.
