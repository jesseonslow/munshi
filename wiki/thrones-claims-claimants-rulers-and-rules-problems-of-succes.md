# Thrones, claims, claimants, rulers and rules: problems of succession in the Malay Sultanates

Kobkua Suwannathat-Pian's 1993 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the succession practices of the Malay sultanates from the Palembang-Melaka period through the colonial era, arguing that the widely assumed principles of primogeniture and maternal royal status were never the decisive factors in selecting a ruler, and that the real determinants were the dying ruler's expressed wish, the assent of the state council (*Majlis Negara*), and ultimately the relative power of competing claimants.

## Summary

Suwannathat-Pian opens by tracing the ideological foundation of Malay kingship as laid out in the *Sejarah Melayu*: the union of the sacred Iskandar Zul-karnain line with the indigenous people, sealed by a perpetual covenant between ruler and subjects. The claim to rule rested on impeccable genealogy and the unbroken transmission of *daulat* (sacred sovereign power). This system was disrupted by the regicide of Sultan Mahmud in 1699, after which the Bendahara rulers ascended the throne under the shadow of *derhaka* (treason), and royal legitimacy increasingly depended on personal military and moral ability rather than pedigree alone (pp. 1–2).

The article's central analytical move is to dismantle two principles long taken for granted: primogeniture and the precedence of the *anak gahara* (son of a royal consort). Drawing on the *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Tuhfat al-Nafis*, and state-specific genealogies, Suwannathat-Pian demonstrates that non-first-born sons and sons of minor wives ascended the throne with regularity throughout the Melaka and Bendahara periods. Sultan Muzaffir Shah of Melaka, the great king, was himself an *anak gundik* who deposed his fully royal elder brother. During the Melaka sultanate, only one *anak gahara*—Sultan Alauddin I, whose mother was a Batara of Majapahit—succeeded and "did well" in that office (pp. 3–6). The author attributes the later crystallisation of primogeniture as a "rule" to colonial administrators who needed a fixed, comprehensible principle to manage succession, and to Western-educated Malay elites of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries who adopted it to prevent dynastic conflict (pp. 6–8).

The "rules that were" are equally flexible: the dying ruler's expressed wish, the assent of the *Majlis Negara*, the candidate's membership in the ruling house, personal ability, the support of a powerful maternal clan, and the backing of an external military ally. Where these failed, "might is right" prevailed. The article then illustrates these dynamics through three case studies—Kedah, Perak, and Kelantan—showing how Siamese suzerainty and later British colonial rule progressively displaced traditional mechanisms. In Perak, the rotation system devised in the 1820s to accommodate multiple royal branches was rendered meaningless by British manipulation of the succession hierarchy. In Kedah, the 1879–1882 crisis was resolved only through the intervention of King Chulalongkorn as suzerain. In Kelantan, the Long Yunus dynasty's repeated armed struggles for the throne were eventually contained by Siamese and then British authority (pp. 14–27).

### Key Findings

- Primogeniture was never a governing principle in the Malay sultanates; the *Sejarah Melayu* records as many non-first-born successors as first-born ones, and the founder Seri Tri Buana was himself the youngest of three brothers (pp. 3–4).
- The *anak gahara* (fully royal son) held no automatic precedence over *anak gundik* (sons of minor wives); during the Melaka sultanate only one such son, Sultan Alauddin I, succeeded and ruled successfully, while his counterpart Raja Ibrahim was deposed after roughly eighteen months (pp. 5–6).
- The expressed wish of the dying ruler carried near-absolute weight in the traditional period, as demonstrated by Sultan Mansur Shah's designation of Raja Radin and Sultan Mahmud of Riau-Lingga's repeated naming of Tengku Abdul Rahman on his deathbed (pp. 9–10).
- The *Majlis Negara* (state council) served as the decisive arbiter when the ruler died without naming a successor, and its choice conferred full legitimacy on the new ruler (pp. 10–11).
- British colonial rule introduced the principle of "acceptability" to the colonial regime as the overriding criterion for succession, effectively displacing both the ruler's prerogative and the state council's role; the case of Tengku Musa'eddin of Selangor, stripped of the Raja Muda title by Resident T.S. Adams in 1934, exemplifies this (pp. 12–13).
- The Perak rotation system, established in the 1820s to pass the throne laterally among three royal branches, was systematically undermined by British intervention after the assassination of Birch in 1875, resulting in the near-exclusive succession of the Sultan Idris sub-branch (pp. 19–21).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian's definitive takeaway is that the Malay sultanate never possessed a hard and fast rule of succession comparable to Western primogeniture; its flexible, multi-factorial system was the root cause of the political instability that plagued each state during the traditional period. The modern sultanate, shaped by Western constitutional frameworks, has resolved this by vesting the succession decision in two institutions—the incumbent ruler and the state council or its constitutional successor—thereby replacing the old ambiguity with a definite, if externally imposed, procedure (p. 27).

## Context

- The article draws heavily on the *Sejarah Melayu* (both Raffles and Shellabear versions) and the *Tuhfat al-Nafis*, both held in MBRAS and national archives collections, alongside colonial administrative records (CO files, state annual reports) and Siamese sources.
- Suwannathat-Pian's broader research programme on Thai-Malay relations and the political culture of the Malay sultanates, published in her 1988 monograph *Thai-Malay Relations* and subsequent JMBRAS articles, provided the analytical framework for this study.
