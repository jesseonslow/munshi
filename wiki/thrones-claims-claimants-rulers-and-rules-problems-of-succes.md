# Thrones, claims, claimants, rulers and rules: problems of succession in the Malay Sultanates

R. O. Winstedt, a Straits Settlements administrative officer, published this concise survey in 1917 tracing the diffusion of Islam — which he consistently terms "Muhammadanism" — across the Malay Peninsula and Archipelago from the earliest Arab trading contacts in the ninth century through the eighteenth. The article argues that the religion spread not through a single missionary wave but through a succession of commercial and political centres (Kedah, Pasai, Malacca, Acheen, Java), each serving as a relay point, and that conversion was typically a court-level event preceded by trade contact and followed by military or dynastic consolidation.

## Summary

Winstedt structures his narrative around the shifting geography of maritime trade in the Indian Ocean world. He begins with Arab traders at Kedah around 915 and Marco Polo's 1292 observations at Perlak, then follows the relay from Pasai (whose ruler Merah Silu converted and took the title Sultan Maliku's-Salleh, dying in 1297) to Malacca, where Sultan Muhammad Shah (r. 1403–1414) is identified as the first Muslim prince. The *Sejarah Melayu* and Albuquerque's *Commentaries* supply the narrative backbone for the Malaccan phase, while Winstedt draws on folk-tales and material culture — tomb-stones, imported textiles, keris inscriptions — to illustrate the texture of daily Islamic presence in a still largely non-Muslim society.

The second half of the article shifts to the post-Portuguese period, when Acheen under Sultan Iskandar Muda (r. 1606–1636) became the dominant centre of both trade and religious scholarship. Winstedt highlights the role of Gujarati and Hadhrami Sayyid families, particularly Shaikh Nuru'd-din (al-Raniri), whose Malay-language works — the *Bustanu's-salatin* and the *Siratu'l-mustakim* (1634) — codified Islamic doctrine for a Malay readership. The spread to Kedah, Perak, the Sumatran interior, Java (following the fall of Majapahit in 1478), Borneo, the Celebes, and the Moluccas is then sketched in rapid succession, with Winstedt noting that genealogical study of ruling families in these regions would yield further clarification.

Throughout, Winstedt treats the conversion process as fundamentally a top-down, court-centred phenomenon: a ruler converts, the court follows, and the population gradually conforms. The *Sejarah Melayu* anecdotes about the Arab missionary Maulana Sadar Jahan — the drunk Sri Rama's retort about covetousness, Tun Mai's pronunciation challenge — serve to humanize the friction of cultural transmission without undermining the overall trajectory Winstedt charts.

### Key Findings

- Arab traders reached Kedah by 915 A.D.; Marco Polo found Muslims at Perlak in 1292 but still heathens at Samudra and Pasai (p. 2).
- Muhammadan tomb-stones at Pasai dated 1407 A.D. were identified by J. P. Magnette (1912) as originating from Cambay, India, and closely resemble stones at Grisek (Java) and Bruas (Perak) (p. 2).
- Sultan Mansur Shah's tomb-stone, bearing an Arabic inscription in non-Malay lettering, is dated 880 A.H.; the Pangkalan Kempas stone records the grave of Shaikh Ahmad Makhtar Ramali, erected A.H. 872 (1467 A.D.) "in the reign of Sultan Shah Mansur" (p. 4).
- The Achinese account gives 1474 A.D. as the date of Kedah's acceptance of Islam; the *Sejarah Melayu* independently records a Kedah raja receiving recognition from the Malaccan court, suggesting fifteenth-century court-level conversion (p. 5).
- In Java, Maulana Malik Ibrahim died at Grisek in 1419; the Muhammadan princes of Demak overthrew Majapahit in 1478, driving Hinduism to Blambangan and Bali (p. 5).
- Sultan Iskandar Muda of Acheen (r. 1606–1636) conquered Indrapura, Deli, Siak, Johor, Kedah, and Perak; during his reign the *Sejarah Melayu* and *Bustanu's-salatin* were composed or translated at Acheen (p. 5).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that Islam's spread across the Malay world was neither sudden nor uniform but followed the established circuit of maritime commerce, with each great port — Kedah, Pasai, Malacca, Acheen, Demak — serving as a successive node of conversion. The religion's consolidation depended on the convergence of foreign missionary presence (Arab, Gujarati, Hadhrami), royal adoption, and the production of vernacular religious literature, a process that was substantially complete across the archipelago's major polities by the late seventeenth century.

## Context

- Winstedt was a senior Straits Settlements official (later Director of Education); the article reflects the colonial administrative habit of cataloguing "natives" through the lens of religious and civilizational progress, using the somewhat dated term "Muhammadanism" and framing conversion as a civilizational milestone.
- The article draws on the *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai*, the *Kedah Annals* (*Hikayat Marong Mahawangsa*), Albuquerque's *Commentaries*, Marco Polo, Ibn Batuta, and the archaeological record of tomb-stones; it also references contemporary scholarship by Schrieke (1916), Snouck Hurgronje, Van der Tuuk, and Rinkes, positioning itself within the Dutch-Indies historiographical tradition of the period.
