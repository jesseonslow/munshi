# Teluk Anson, 1882–1941: port, agriculture and erosion

Khoo Kay Kim's 1995 article traces the development of Teluk Anson (now Teluk Intan) from its founding in 1882 through the eve of the Japanese Occupation, arguing that the town represents a distinctive case of a west coast port that transitioned from a trade-dependent settlement to one servicing a vast agricultural hinterland. Published in the Sheppard Memorial Issue of the JMBRAS, the study addresses what the author identifies as a significant gap in Malaysian historiography: the near absence of serious local and town histories.

## Summary

The article reconstructs the administrative and economic logic behind the founding of Teluk Anson as a planned replacement for the older settlement at Durian Sebatang. Hugh Low, the third British Resident of Perak, identified the need to relocate the administrative seat of Lower Perak to a site with better river access and more space for development. The new town was named in honour of Lieutenant-Colonel Archibald Anson, who had drawn the original plan, and was accompanied by the construction of a canal to shorten river navigation between Durian Sebatang and the new site. The canal, however, proved structurally defective within a few years of completion and was eventually filled in, rendering it a costly failure. The arrival of the Kinta–Lower Perak railway in 1895 proved far more consequential, linking the port to the rapidly expanding tin-mining district and transforming Teluk Anson into Perak's principal export gateway.

As the Kinta district's tin output surged and the north-south railway was completed by 1909, Teluk Anson's role as a tin port gradually diminished. The town's economic survival depended on its pivot to agriculture. European planters opened large rubber estates in Lower Perak from the early 1900s, and by 1908 the district hosted over two dozen plantations. Coconut cultivation, which had preceded rubber, also reached a significant scale. When the 1930s depression exposed the dangers of monoculture export dependence, the British administration launched the Sungai Manik padi scheme, recruiting primarily Banjarese settlers to cultivate a large irrigation area adjacent to the town. The article also examines the town's social fabric—its ethnic composition, religious institutions, schools, and the persistent threat of riverbank erosion that earned it the nickname "the sinking town."

The author situates Teluk Anson in comparative perspective with Klang, the other major west coast port that underwent a similar transition from trade to agriculture. Both towns shared structural similarities in their founding, ethnic composition, and economic trajectory, but Teluk Anson's greater distance from the federal capital and its state capital (Ipoh) meant it never achieved the commercial dynamism of Klang, which evolved into an international port and industrial centre.

### Key Findings

- Tin exports from Teluk Anson grew from 1,711 piculs in 1891 to 379,670 piculs in 1908, reflecting the Kinta district's rise as Perak's dominant mining area (p. 39).
- Port traffic increased from 514 steamers carrying 130,213 tons in 1902 to 640 steamers carrying 158,845 tons in 1903; after the new wharf opened in 1909, tonnage rose from 152,145 to 266,597 by 1920 (pp. 39–47).
- By 1908, Lower Perak contained 23 rubber estates totalling over 17,000 hectares, the largest being Sungai Wangi at 2,834 hectares; coconut cultivation covered approximately 30,000 acres with copra output exceeding 23,000 piculs (pp. 40–41).
- The 1931 census recorded a town population of 14,671 (Chinese 7,900; Indians 3,530; Malays 3,062), while the district's estate population numbered 26,584, of whom 24,596 were Indian (p. 44).
- The Sungai Manik padi scheme, planned to cover 9,600 hectares, employed 4,000 Malays in land clearing and allocated approximately 2.4 hectares of felled land plus half a hectare of homestead land to each settler (pp. 48–49).
- Erosion of the riverbank threatened property with an annual assessed value of $400,000; a protective wall was estimated at a cost of approximately $500,000, but the decision was made to relocate the wharf and railway terminus 4 kilometres inland, with the new port officially opened on 24 March 1909 (pp. 46–47).

### Conclusion

Khoo Kay Kim concludes that Teluk Anson's trajectory illustrates how a port town's fortunes were determined not by its own agency but by the broader economic geography of the colony: the shift of tin production to the Kinta, the completion of the north-south railway, and the federal government's preference for consolidating port facilities at Klang all conspired to limit Teluk Anson's growth. The town survived as a service centre for agriculture, but it never escaped the shadow of its more dynamic neighbours, remaining only the fourth largest town in Perak until the Japanese Occupation.

## Context

- The study draws on Colonial Office Records, Perak Annual Reports, FMS Council Reports, the 1931 census (Vlieland), and contemporary newspapers including *The Straits Times*, *The Malay Mail*, *The Perak Pioneer*, and *The Straits Maritime Journal*, supplemented by the author's own earlier monograph on the town (1982, 2nd ed. 1991).
- Historiographically, the article explicitly positions itself as a corrective to the neglect of local and town history in Malaysian scholarship, offering a model of how administrative, economic, and social histories can be integrated at the municipal level.
