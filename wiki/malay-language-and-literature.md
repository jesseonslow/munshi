# Malay language and literature

Reinhold Rost, the India Office librarian, published this survey of the Malay language and literature in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1885. Drawing on the philological scholarship of the late nineteenth century, the article presents a comprehensive overview of Malay phonology, morphology, syntax, dialectal variation, and literary production, situating the language within the Malayo-Polynesian family and tracing its foreign lexical influences from Sanskrit through Arabic to European contact languages.

## Summary

Rost's central concern is to establish the structural character of Malay as a language and to assess the state of its literary corpus. On the linguistic side, he argues that Malay is "singularly well-fitted" to serve as the archipelago's *lingua franca* owing to its simple phonetic inventory, regular grammatical structure, and copious nautical vocabulary. He devotes considerable attention to the derivational system—prefixes, infixes, affixes, and reduplication—demonstrating how a single root can generate upwards of a hundred distinct forms, and he notes the language's fundamental lack of inflection for number, gender, or case, with meaning carried instead by word order, particles, and context. The discussion of dialects centres on the Menangkabo variety of Central Sumatra, which Rost identifies as the most important and interesting, and he addresses the long-standing question of pre-Islamic Malay writing, siding with those who argue the Malays possessed an indigenous alphabet (variously identified as Rechang, Kawi, or Lampung) before the Perso-Arabic script was introduced with the spread of Islam.

On the literary side, Rost surveys the two great popular genres—proverbs in prose and *pantuns* in verse—before turning to the *hikayat* tradition, chronicles, and works of religious and ethical instruction. His assessment of Malay literary achievement is characteristically ambivalent: he acknowledges the richness of the *pantun* form and the descriptive power of certain *hikayats*, yet he judges that Malay histories are "a strange motley of truth and fiction" and that the poems and novels lack the coherence, imagination, and chivalric spirit found in the corresponding literatures of the leading Islamic nations. He attributes this partly to the absence of political cohesion among the scattered Malay peoples, which prevented the growth of a national epic comparable to those of Bugi and Macassar. The article closes with a survey of manuscript collections in European and colonial libraries, and with a brief tribute to Abdullah Munsyi of Singapore as the finest recent Malay writer.

### Key Findings

- No Malay manuscripts or inscribed monuments survive from before the spread of Islam in the archipelago, about the end of the thirteenth century; the Perso-Arabic alphabet was introduced with the Mohammedan conquest and remained in use for literary, religious, and business purposes (p. 93).
- The unvocalised Arabic script creates extreme ambiguity: the single string *bntng* may represent at least eight distinct words (*bantang, banting, bentang, buntang, bunting, bonteng, benteng*) depending on vowel pronunciation (p. 94).
- The only grammar of Malay composed by a native writer is the 70-page *Bustanu'lkatibin* by Raja Ali Haji of Rho, lithographed in Penang in 1857; the earliest surviving European Malay vocabulary is A. Pigafetta's list of 450 words from Magellan's voyage, followed by F. Houtman's trilingual *Vocabulary and Conversations* (Amsterdam, 1603) (p. 98).
- W. E. Maxwell's *Manual of the Malay Language* (London, 1882) records no fewer than sixteen distinct terms for different kinds of striking, a similar number for different kinds of speaking, and eighteen for various modes of carrying, illustrating the language's idiom-specific vocabulary (p. 96).
- The finest collection of Malay manuscripts, comprising upwards of 400 volumes, is held in the library of the Asiatic Society of Batavia; England's claim to the world's largest assemblage was undermined by the loss by fire of 300 Malay manuscripts belonging to the late Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles during their passage from India (p. 101).
- Abdullah ibn Abdelkadir Munsyi of Singapore, whom Rost identifies as the best recent Malay writer, died at Mecca approximately twenty-eight years before publication (c. 1857); his *Journey to Kelantan* and *Pilgrimage to Mecca* are cited as models of Malay style (p. 101).

### Conclusion

Rost's definitive takeaway is that Malay, while structurally simple and admirably suited to its role as a pan-archipelagic medium of communication, has produced a literary tradition that is rich in popular and devotional forms but fundamentally lacking in the sustained imaginative and narrative power characteristic of the great Islamic literatures. He locates the cause not in any inherent deficiency of the language but in the political fragmentation of the Malay peoples, which prevented the emergence of a unifying national epic or a cohesive literary culture capable of absorbing and transforming the models of Arabic and Persian letters.

## Context

- The article is explicitly identified in an editorial note as an extract from the *Encyclopædia Britannica* (1883 edition), republished in the JSBRAS; Rost served as librarian of the India Office Library in London, a position that gave him direct access to the British Museum, India Office, and Royal Asiatic Society collections of Malay manuscripts (p. 101).
- The piece reflects the standard colonial-era philological framework: Malay is treated as a single language with dialectal variants, and its literary value is assessed against the benchmark of Islamic (Arabic/Persian) literary achievement, a comparative frame that Rost himself partially challenges by noting the persistence of Hindu and pre-Hindu undercurrents in Malay fiction (pp. 99–100).
