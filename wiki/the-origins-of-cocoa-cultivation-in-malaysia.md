# The origins of cocoa cultivation in Malaysia

Amarjit Kaur (1995) traces the origins of cocoa cultivation across Peninsular Malaysia, Sabah, and Sarawak from the late eighteenth century through the 1990s, arguing that Malaysia's rise to become the world's third-largest cocoa producer was not a spontaneous market phenomenon but the product of deliberate state intervention, colonial-era research infrastructure, and the political imperatives of the New Economic Policy. The article demonstrates that at each stage of development, the state—whether colonial or national—shaped where, how, and by whom cocoa was grown.

## Summary

Kaur organizes the history of Malaysian cocoa into three phases corresponding to shifts in commodity prices, the investment climate, and the nature of political control. The first phase was one of experimental cultivation, in which cocoa was overshadowed by the more established and profitable plantation crops of rubber and oil palm. The second phase saw the colonial government actively investigate cocoa as a diversification crop following the Second World War, with the private sector entering through the formation of a joint venture between an Agency House, a chocolate manufacturer, and the colonial development agency. The third phase, coinciding with the New Economic Policy, witnessed cocoa's transformation into a major commercial crop driven by both world price incentives and the state's ideological commitment to Malay economic advancement.

The article's central argument is that state policy, rather than market forces alone, determined the trajectory of cocoa development. The colonial government's post-war diversification strategy, the post-independence government's Malay advancement ideology, and the NEP's poverty eradication prong each shaped the institutional frameworks—land settlement schemes, research stations, and public-private partnerships—through which cocoa was promoted. Kaur also highlights the pivotal role of the Cheeseman feasibility study as a document that set the parameters for subsequent policy and investment decisions across all three territories.

The narrative is informed by primary sources including the Cheeseman report, Department of Agriculture trial records, Colonial Development and Welfare Act grant documentation, and Malaysian agricultural statistics. Kaur situates cocoa's development within the broader political economy of the Emergency, the May 1969 riots, and the institutional reforms that followed, including the replacement of RIDA by MARA and the establishment of FELDA.

### Key Findings

- The Cheeseman feasibility study (1948) estimated that an annual output of approximately 100,000 tons of cocoa beans was feasible across the three territories, while cautioning that cocoa would not become a major crop to rival rubber or oil palm (p. 72).
- Malayan Cocoa Limited, formed in 1950 by Harrisons and Crosfield, Cadbury Brothers, and the Colonial Development Corporation, acquired a 1,200-acre lease in Trengganu for the first commercial monoculture of Trinitario cocoa at Jerangau Estate (p. 73).
- Vascular-Streak Dieback struck in early 1958, reducing yields to a third of expectations and particularly affecting the Amelonado variety in Trengganu; the long gap between onset and identification of the causal agent discouraged further plantation investment (p. 75).
- Cocoa acreage in Malaysia expanded from 577 hectares in 1960 to 33,466 hectares in 1976, with Peninsular Malaysia growing from 577 to 20,796 ha and Sabah from 1,538 to 9,827 ha over the same period (p. 78, Table 1).
- Amelonado seedlings imported from Ghana were distributed as follows: 3,649 to the Federation of Malaya, 538 to Sarawak, 570 to North Borneo, and 199 to Singapore (p. 80, n. 9).
- A four-acre smallholding was estimated to yield 750 lbs of dried beans per acre, giving a gross income of M$300 per annum at M$1 per lb (p. 75).

### Conclusion

Kaur's definitive historical takeaway is that cocoa's trajectory in Malaysia was shaped by the interplay of market conditions and state ideology rather than by individual farmer choice or necessity alone. The plantation model served as the institutional vehicle for transferring advanced agricultural techniques to the smallholder sector, and the state's commitment to Malay economic advancement through the NEP provided the political impetus that transformed cocoa from a marginal experimental crop into a major export commodity. Malaysia's decision not to sign the International Cocoa Agreements further underscores the primacy of national expansion strategy over collective price stabilization.

## Context

- The article draws on the Cheeseman feasibility report (1948), Colonial Development and Welfare Act grant records, Department of Agriculture trial data, and Malaysian agricultural statistics compiled by the Ministry of Agriculture and the Department of Agriculture, Sabah.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the literature on colonial economic diversification and post-independence development policy in Southeast Asia, situating cocoa within the broader framework of Malaysia's export-led growth strategy and the political economy of the New Economic Policy.
