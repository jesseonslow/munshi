# Some aspects of the Federation of the Malay States, 1896–1910

Eunice Thio's 1967 article examines the constitutional and administrative development of the Federated Malay States between 1896 and 1910, arguing that the 1895 Agreement was never designed to create a genuine federation and that Sir John Anderson's 1909–1910 reforms—publicly framed as decentralizing—actually deepened centralization within the FMS while shifting authority from Kuala Lumpur to Singapore.

## Summary

Thio addresses three interlocking questions about the FMS: the true nature of the 1895 scheme, the operational relationship between the Resident-General in Kuala Lumpur and the Governor in Singapore, and the significance of Anderson's reforms. Drawing on Colonial Office memoranda, Secretary of State's papers, and official correspondence, she demonstrates that British metropolitan authorities from the early 1890s favoured welding the four states into a single protectorate for administrative and financial reasons, with Ceylon as the explicit model. The 1895 Agreement was merely a transitional instrument, and the term "federation" itself was the product of a clerical correction rather than a deliberate constitutional choice. The resulting amalgamation was therefore neither an accident of poor drafting nor the result of autocratic British personalities alone, but the logical outcome of a policy that had never intended a federal structure.

On the Resident-General–Governor relationship, Thio shows that the balance of power was determined less by the formal scheme than by the calibre of the incumbents. Swettenham's forceful personality allowed him to operate as a de facto independent head of administration despite formal subordination to the Governor, a situation that only resolved when Swettenham himself moved to Singapore in 1901. The friction between Anderson and Taylor in the 1900s, by contrast, involved two evenly matched and assertive figures, making the structural ambiguity untenable.

Thio's most original contribution lies in her reinterpretation of Anderson's 1909–1910 reforms. Against the prevailing view of Emerson, Gullick, Guillemard, and Wilson—that the Federal Council and the Chief Secretary Enactment represented an attempt to create a genuine federal system or restore state powers—she argues that both measures promoted further centralization within the FMS and consolidated the High Commissioner's supremacy. Anderson's public statements about decentralization were either politically expedient (to mollify dissatisfied Rulers) or reflected a later strategic recalibration prompted by the 1909 transfer of the four northern states, which could not be absorbed into a unitary framework.

### Key Findings

- A Colonial Office Assistant Under-Secretary described the 1895 Agreement as an "extraordinarily anomalous federation" possessing no sovereignty either internal or external (p. 4).
- C. P. Lucas's 1895 memorandum for Joseph Chamberlain explicitly envisaged the four states being reduced to "districts of the same state," with Ceylon as the governing model (p. 5).
- The term "federation" entered the Agreement accidentally: the original draft used "confederation" and "federacy"; the Secretary of State corrected "federacy" to "federation" but never amended "Protected Malay States" accordingly, leaving no legal authority for the name "Federated Malay States" (p. 5).
- Colonial Office officials recognized the division of powers in the 1909 Federal Council Agreement as "something new and strange" yet declined to refer it to the law officers, deciding it would "probably work out all right in practice" (p. 10).
- The Chief Secretary Enactment of 1910 downgraded the Resident-General's title and reserved CARCOSA—the official residence in Kuala Lumpur—for the High Commissioner, with a smaller house built for the Chief Secretary on an adjacent site (p. 12).
- By 1910, Anderson's jurisdiction extended to Brunei (1906), Labuan (1906), North Borneo, Sarawak, the four transferred northern states (1909), and Johore's General Adviser (1910), making unified command from Singapore a practical necessity (p. 13).

### Conclusion

Thio's definitive historical takeaway is that from 1895 to 1909, British policy in the FMS was consistently directed toward building a union rather than a federation, and that Anderson's reforms, far from reversing this trajectory, accelerated it. The public rhetoric of decentralization was a veneer over a strategy of consolidation, driven by the widening sphere of British control across the Malay world and the need for a single authoritative centre at Singapore.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on Colonial Office records and Secretary of State's papers held in London, including the 1895 Lucas memorandum and the April 1893 memorandum on federation, alongside official correspondence between the Governor, Resident-General, and Colonial Office.
- Historiographically, the piece challenges the interpretation advanced by Emerson, Gullick, Guillemard, and Wilson that Anderson's reforms represented a move toward genuine federalism, repositioning them instead as instruments of deeper centralization and metropolitan consolidation.
