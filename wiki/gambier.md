# Gambier

Gambier, a dye and pharmaceutical product derived from the leaves of *Garcinia xanthochymus*, was the most significant export-oriented crop cultivated by Chinese agricultural pioneers in southern Malaya throughout the nineteenth century. Together with black pepper, it formed the backbone of a commercial farming system that affected a larger area and achieved a greater degree of success than any other form of export agriculture in the region prior to the advent of rubber. The cultivation was concentrated in Singapore and Johore, where it was practised on a shifting basis across vast tracts of virgin jungle, and its organisational framework—the *Kangchu System*—constituted the most extensive and successful export-oriented enterprise in southern Malaya before the rubber era [[1, pp. 77–78]](#ref-1).

## Scope and Historical Definition

Gambier cultivation in the Singapore and Johore region was inseparable from pepper growing. The two crops were not interplanted but allocated to separate parts of the plantation, and their management was interlocked: the price of gambier was seldom high enough to justify its cultivation alone, yet the almost continuous collecting of gambier foliage could be integrated with the highly seasonal cycle of pepper production, ensuring year-round employment for the labour force required to tend the pepper vines. The refuse from gambier boiling provided virtually the only form of manure available for the pepper vines and was believed to have shaded the soil from intense heat, while the quicker-maturing gambier helped provide an income until the pepper vines reached maturity. As a general rule, the crops were cultivated on a ratio of one acre of pepper to every ten acres of gambier [[1, pp. 79–80]](#ref-1).

The earliest Chinese gambier plantations in Singapore had been established on the hills of the northern, western, and south-western periphery of the new town by the time of the British founding in 1819. Colonel W. Farquhar indicated in 1822 that some twenty gambier plantations, belonging to both Malays and Chinese, existed on the island at that date [[1, p. 78]](#ref-1). The date of the first plantations and the place of origin of the Chinese settlers who worked them are unknown, though V. Purcell suggested that the first Chinese to settle in Singapore came from Malacca and Riau, where Chinese gambier planters had been active in the late eighteenth century [[1, p. 78]](#ref-1). It is highly likely that the system of cultivation and financing associated with these pioneer plantations came with these settlers from Riau, although its original home may lie elsewhere in Southeast Asia [[1, p. 78]](#ref-1).

Carl A. Trocki traced the institutional origins of the *kangchu* system back to approximately 1740, when the Bugis Yang Dipertuan Muda Daeng Celak established gambier plantations worked by Chinese labourers at Riau. The catastrophic disruption of 1784, when the Dutch destroyed the Riau entrepot and the Malay and Bugis rulers fled, left the Chinese planters to govern themselves for over three decades. By the time Stamford Raffles founded Singapore in 1819, the Chinese settlements at Riau had already achieved a degree of economic and military autonomy that prefigured the *kangchu* system [[2, pp. 132–133]](#ref-2). Trocki argued that the system was not a nineteenth-century invention but the product of a long evolution, and that its full significance had been obscured by earlier scholarship which treated it as a Johore-specific phenomenon of the mid-nineteenth century [[2, p. 132]](#ref-2).

## Economic and Ecological Dynamics

The economic trajectory of gambier was characterised by extreme volatility. In Singapore, production rose from an estimated 22,000 *pikuls* in 1836 to about 48,000 in 1839 and to 80,000 in 1848, when the plantations also produced 30,923 *pikuls* of pepper; together these two crops occupied over three-quarters of the total estimated cultivated area and accounted for nearly three-fifths of the total value of agricultural produce on the island [[1, pp. 78–79]](#ref-1). Trocki documented the price fluctuations in detail: gambier stood at $1.30–1.75 per *pikul* in January 1831, rose to $4.00–5.00 in January 1834, then fell to $0.90–1.00 by January 1849 before recovering to $2.85 by 1861 [[2, p. 141]](#ref-2). In 1836, Singapore's gambier trade totalled 39,624 *pikuls*—22,000 of local production plus 19,624 imports, mostly from Riau—with exports distributed to Great Britain (9,921 *pikuls*), Celebes (9,961), Calcutta (7,096), and other destinations [[2, p. 139]](#ref-2).

The ecological cost of the shifting cultivation system was substantial. Gambier was an extremely exhaustive crop, and young pepper vines would not thrive on old, worn-out pepper land. Once abandoned, the land required a long fallow period before it was again suitable for cultivation. A gambier plantation became exhausted and worn out in fifteen years from its commencement, the pepper vines passed their peak of production in their fifteenth or twentieth years, and the fuel supply was expected to last some twenty-five years [[1, pp. 80–81]](#ref-1). To boil the gambier leaves, the planters required large reserves of firewood; it is estimated that an area of forest roughly equal in area to the cultivated holding was cleared to provide the necessary fuel [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1). By 1836, complaints were already being made about the jungle being cut down for firewood and plantations being deserted and allowed to run to *lalang* and *belukar* [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1). By 1850, only three-quarters of the existing plantations in Singapore were cultivated, and it was felt that the cultivation was "retrograding as the older plantations have all become exhausted" [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1).

The labour conditions on the pioneer plantations were extremely unpleasant. Housing was poor, the plantations were isolated, and beri-beri and other diseases were rife. Siah U Chin observed in the late 1840s that many labourers "get their legs and feet hurt with splinters, (and) the broken skin being disregarded, large ulcers are formed," and reports that labourers had been "taken by tigers" were not infrequent [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1). Death and desertion resulted in a rapid turnover of labour. Wages were not fixed but fluctuated in proportion to the price of gambier, a mechanism by which the proprietors could minimise the effects of changing prices [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1).

## The Kangchu System and Administrative Framework

The organisational framework within which gambier and pepper cultivation was conducted—the *Kangchu System*—was the key to the enterprise's success. Chinese agricultural settlers moved into Johore along the main rivers and their tributaries, establishing small villages or "river depots" known as *Kangkars* (literally "foot of the river" in the Teochew dialect), from which cultivation extended only from the river bank to the nearest watershed [[1, p. 82]](#ref-1). Each *Kangkar* and its section of the valley was governed by a semi-feudal Chinese headman, the *Kangchu* ("lord of the river"), who held the land under a title known as a *surat sungei* ("river document"), granted to him by the Malay ruler. The earliest recorded *surat sungei*, issued for part of the Sungei Skudai, is dated 1833 by Jackson [[1, p. 82]](#ref-1), while Trocki, drawing on the Johore Archives, identifies the earliest document in the *Buku Daftar Surat-surat Keterangan Berkebun* as dated 9 October 1844, for Sungai Sekudai, with a second dated 22 October 1844 granting Sungai Tebrau to Kapitan Tan Kee Soon [[2, pp. 144–145]](#ref-2).

The *Kangchu* paid rents or taxes for the whole area and took the responsibility of the territory worked from his *Kangkar* off the shoulders of the government. He was obliged to construct and maintain the paths leading to the various plantations and to provide for the upkeep of river communications. Besides his administrative powers, within his *Kangkar* the *Kangchu* held the opium and gambling farms, together with exclusive rights of pawn-brokering, selling liquor, slaughtering pigs, and selling pork—all lucrative sources of income in pioneer Chinese settlements [[1, pp. 82–83]](#ref-1). The system was well suited to conditions in nineteenth-century Johore, where the State was sparsely populated, almost entirely covered with virgin jungle, and the absence of roads made administration difficult. Each *Kangchu* was officially recognised by the Malay authorities as the *penghulu*, or headman, of the Chinese in his *Kangkar*, and was given virtually a free hand to administer it [[1, p. 82]](#ref-1).

The financing of the plantations was controlled by residents of Singapore town. In 1864, the Chinese merchants of Singapore claimed that they had advanced over one million dollars to develop the Johore plantations, although most of this money had been borrowed originally by them from European merchants. At that date there were one hundred gambier shops and over two hundred provision stores in Singapore which relied almost entirely on the Johore trade [[1, p. 83]](#ref-1). The planters rarely, if ever, became financially independent of the merchant-financiers; indeed, "all their produce and most of their earnings find their way back to Singapore again" [[1, p. 88]](#ref-1). In essence, this was a "truck system" based on a hierarchy headed by the urban-based merchants of Singapore, next in line the *Kangchus*, and in turn the planters in charge of individual *bangsals*, each employing about ten wage-earning labourers [[1, p. 88]](#ref-1).

The system was formally codified by the *Law of the Kangchus* in 1873, which laid out in detail the privileges, duties, and responsibilities of the *Kangchus* in the eyes of the Malay authorities [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1). It was finally abolished by the *Kangchu Rights (Abolition) Enactment* of 1917, under which all remaining *Kangchus* were compensated financially for the loss of their former rights. By this time rubber had come to claim the main interest of Chinese cultivators in Johore [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1).

## Expansion, Peak, and Decline

The declining area available for new plantations in Singapore, and increasing Chinese immigration, stimulated expansion into nearby Johore. This movement may have begun in the late 1820s and rapidly gained momentum. An observer in 1845 noted that within the preceding six months, fifty-two plantations had been commenced on four rivers in Johore, with about five hundred people engaged in them [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1). By the early 1860s there were about 1,200 gambier and pepper plantations in Johore, employing a labour force of some 15,000 [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1). Trocki documented that by 1862, approximately thirty-seven *surat sungai* had been issued covering thirty-two *kangkar* settlements: eighteen rivers in the Johore Strait, nineteen on the Johore River, and two on the west coast [[2, pp. 150–151]](#ref-2).

The 1880s and 1890s represented the peak of the industry. The price of gambier rose from about $5 per *pikul* in 1884 to over $8 for a time in 1889, while black pepper rose from about $12 per *pikul* in 1880 to over $20 in 1889. Johore's exports to Singapore rose from 316,063 *pikuls* of gambier and 56,203 *pikuls* of pepper in 1880 to 400,544 and 117,024 respectively by 1889, when there were an estimated 4,000 gambier factories in the State [[1, pp. 96–97]](#ref-1). In 1894, Dato Abdul Rahman, Secretary to the Sultan of Johore, was moved to say "seven or eight years ago we produced most of the gambier used all over the world" [[1, p. 97]](#ref-1).

The decline was rapid. In Singapore, the total acreage under gambier and pepper fell from approximately 11,000 acres in 1890 to 600 acres in 1912 and 75 acres the following year, as Chinese planters shifted to pineapple growing and rubber cultivation [[1, pp. 94–95]](#ref-1). In Johore, exports of gambier fell by over 40 per cent between 1890 and 1910, and between 1913 and 1917 the value of exports of both gambier and pepper declined by nearly 60 per cent [[1, p. 97]](#ref-1). The death knell of gambier and pepper cultivation was sounded in 1917 with the abolition of the *Kangchu System* [[1, p. 97]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on gambier reflects a shift from early descriptive and administrative accounts to systematic economic and institutional analysis. The earliest contributions, such as those by Newbold (1839) and Balestier (1848), provided statistical snapshots of acreage, production, and prices, while Siah U Chin's 1848 article in the *Journal of the Indian Archipelago and Eastern Asia* offered a rare insider's perspective on the social conditions of the plantations and the dominance of the Teochew dialect group [[1, pp. 78–80]](#ref-1). Jackson's 1965 article represented a major historiographical intervention, arguing that the system's success lay not in the crops themselves but in the organisational framework, and providing the first systematic clarification of the *Kangchu System*'s internal structure and spatial logic, including the distinction between *kangkar*, *bangsal*, and the loosely applied term "plantation" [[1, pp. 87–89]](#ref-1). His evidence base drew on Straits Settlements Blue Books, Annual Reports, the *Singapore Free Press*, the *Singapore and Straits Directory* (1887), the 1887 and 1904 maps of Johore, and Chinese-language sources including Tan Tek Soon's 1902 article and Siah U Chin's 1848 contribution [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1).

Trocki's 1976 article extended the chronological frame back to the 1740s and corrected the earlier assumption, following Coope (1936), that the *kangchu* system was a Johore-specific institution of limited significance. By demonstrating its deep roots at Riau and its centrality to the Singapore economy throughout the nineteenth century, Trocki reframed the system as the foundational economic and political mechanism by which impoverished Malay aristocrats, stripped of their maritime trade base, constructed new mainland states in alliance with Chinese capital and labour [[2, pp. 132–133]](#ref-2). His use of the *Buku Daftar Surat-surat Keterangan Berkebun* (1844–1903) in the Johore Archives, which had only become available to scholars in the 1960s, allowed him to reconstruct the institutional architecture of the system with a precision unavailable to earlier writers [[2, p. 130]](#ref-2). Together, these two studies established the scholarly foundation for understanding gambier not merely as a commodity but as the product of a complex socio-economic and political institution that shaped the development of southern Malaya for more than a century.
