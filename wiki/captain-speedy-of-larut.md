# Captain Speedy of Larut

J.M. Gullick's 1953 monograph-length article reconstructs the career of Tristram Charles Sawyer Speedy (1836–1910), a restless Victorian adventurer who became the first European to administer a Chinese mining district in Malaya, serving as Assistant Resident of Larut in Perak from 1874 to 1877. Set against the backdrop of the Pangkor Treaty and the birth of the British "Residential system," the paper's central thesis is twofold: it examines the pioneer difficulties of governing a turbulent Chinese tin-mining community, and it subjects the character of Speedy to scrutiny in light of the bitter accusations levelled against him by Governor Sir William Jervois.

## Summary

Gullick traces Speedy's peripatetic life from his birth in Meerut, India, through his early military service in the 81st Regiment, his time at the court of King Theodore of Abyssinia, his role as personal interpreter to Lord Napier during the 1868 expedition to Magdala, and his brief stints in New Zealand and Australia, before arriving in the Straits Settlements in 1871 (pp. 1–11). His connection with Larut began as Superintendent of Police at Penang, where he dealt with the Penang repercussions of the Chinese civil war in the Larut mines. In 1873 he resigned his police post to raise a force of Punjabi and Pathan sepoys for the Mentri of Larut, a private venture that the Straits Settlements authorities tacitly endorsed as a means of breaking the military deadlock (pp. 26–30). After the Pangkor Treaty of January 1874 brought Perak under British protection, Speedy was appointed the first Assistant Resident of Larut at a salary of £1,500 per annum, a post for which Governor Clarke judged him uniquely qualified given his knowledge of both the Chinese and Malay parties (pp. 33–34).

During his tenure, Speedy oversaw the restoration of Larut from a devastated war zone to a functioning mining economy. A boundary commission settled disputed mining claims by dividing the area between the Hai San and Ghi Hin factions; stockades were destroyed, arms collected, and forty-five captive women and children restored to their families (p. 36). The Chinese population surged from 4,000 in January 1874 to 27,000 by December, and tin production reached 2,500–3,000 piculs per month (pp. 45–46). Speedy also established a police force, suppressed attempts to form new secret society lodges, laid out the new towns of Taiping and Kamunting, and constructed roads and government buildings (pp. 36–52). His administration was not without faults: he was extravagant, his road-building was haphazard, and he failed to develop an adequate system of political intelligence, as evidenced by his ignorance of the Malay preparations for the Perak War of November 1875 (pp. 41, 51, 55).

The final section of the paper is devoted to dismantling the case made against Speedy by Jervois in 1876. Gullick demonstrates that Jervois's insinuation that Speedy was a disloyal adventurer in the Mentri's pay was "a farrago of improbability" unsupported by any evidence from the Commission of Enquiry (p. 75). The complaint by Brigadier General Ross that Speedy lacked influence over the Chinese is shown to be contradicted by the testimony of Major McNair, who was present at Larut and praised Speedy's "energy" in his memoirs (pp. 67–68). The Colonial Office's attempt to force Speedy's resignation by threatening a salary reduction from £1,500 to £750 per annum is revealed through unpublished minutes as a deliberate "sharp practice" to get rid of an inconvenient officer (p. 72). Speedy resigned in late 1877 and never returned to Malaya, spending his remaining years in England and making three further trips to Abyssinia (pp. 81–88).

### Key Findings

- Larut's Chinese mining population rose from 4,000 in January 1874 to 27,000 in December 1874 under Speedy's administration, and tin production reached approximately 3,000 piculs per month by 1876, making Larut the mainstay of Perak's revenues under the new Residential system (pp. 36, 45–46).
- Total Larut government revenue in 1874 was $101,554, rising to $226,233 in 1875, with tin export duty constituting the largest single source at $70,227 in 1874 (p. 48).
- Jervois's 1876 despatch to the Colonial Office, which alleged Speedy was "lazy, undisciplined and extravagant" and implied disloyalty to the British cause, was never tested by official investigation and was contradicted by the evidence of the Perak Commission of Enquiry, which showed the Mentri was openly hostile to Speedy in 1875 (pp. 73–75).
- The opium farm controversy of April 1875, in which Resident Birch imposed a Singapore-based tax farmer (Cheng Tee) on Larut, caused an estimated 3,000–5,000 Chinese miners to leave the district, a loss that was irreparable (p. 45).
- Speedy's pre-Malayan career included service as personal interpreter to Lord Napier at the 1868 expedition to Magdala, for which Napier described him as "invaluable," and a period of eighteen months as guest of King Theodore of Abyssinia (pp. 3–10).
- The Colonial Office's unpublished minutes reveal that the salary reduction from £1,500 to £750 was deliberately calculated to induce Speedy's resignation: "We reduced it as low as we could in the hopes of getting rid of Captain Speedy" (p. 72).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive historical takeaway is that Speedy represents a type of pioneer administrator whose qualities—exceptional courage, physical presence, personal charm, and an instinctive rapport with Asian subjects—were indispensable in the chaotic early years of British intervention in the Malay States, even though the same qualities made him ill-suited to the routine, economical, and supervised demands of a maturing colonial bureaucracy. The paper vindicates Speedy against the personal vendetta of Jervois while candidly acknowledging his genuine administrative shortcomings, and it uses the Speedy case to illuminate the broader tensions between the "strong hand" approach of the early Residents and the more systematic governance that Hugh Low and his successors would impose in the following decade.

## Context

- The article draws heavily on Colonial Office records at the Public Records Office in London, the files of the *London Illustrated News*, and Sir Hugh Low's unpublished Perak Journal, the latter provided by Miss Sadka of the University of Malaya Department of History (p. i).
- The paper was published in the same volume (JMBRAS 26, pt. 3, 1953) that contained C.D. Cowan's edition of Swettenham's Perak Journals 1874–6, providing a contemporary documentary framework against which Gullick's narrative could be cross-referenced.
