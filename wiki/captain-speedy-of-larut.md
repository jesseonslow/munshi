# Captain Speedy of Larut

C. A. Gibson-Hill, W. W. Skeat, and F. F. Laidlaw published this comprehensive account of the Cambridge University Expedition to the North-Eastern Malay States and Upper Perak in 1953, drawing on Skeat's personal diaries and notes from the 1899–1900 fieldwork. The volume documents a multidisciplinary scientific expedition—encompassing zoology, botany, and ethnography—through the Siamese-controlled states of Patani, Jalor, Raman (Kelantan), Trengganu, Kedah, and Perlis, as well as the Larut district of Perak. Its overarching contribution is to provide an exceptionally detailed ethnographic and natural-historical record of a region at the precise moment when European administrative influence was beginning to transform its social and economic structures.

## Summary

The publication is structured around two personal narratives. Skeat's account, prepared by Sir Richard Winstedt from a manuscript Skeat completed in early 1952 shortly before his death, covers the expedition's journey from Bangkok through the Patani States, Kelantan, and Trengganu, including the ill-fated attempt to reach the summit of Gunong Tahan. Laidlaw's shorter narrative supplements this with his own observations during the party's stay at Kuala Aring and his subsequent climb of Gunong Inas in Upper Perak, in company with R. H. Yapp. Gibson-Hill's introduction situates the expedition within the broader context of Cambridge's anthropological fieldwork programme, following the model of the Haddon Expedition to New Guinea and the Torres Strait.

The ethnographic material is the volume's most substantial contribution. Skeat records in meticulous detail the full cycle of Malay and Siamese-Malay ceremonial life: circumcision rites (masok Jawi), wedding processions and bersanding ceremonies, funeral practices including both temporary and permanent tree-burials, the tolak bala (casting-out-of-evil) ceremonies with their elaborate spirit-vessels, and the main putri (spirit-dance) healing performances. He documents the beliefs and practices of the Samsam (Siamese-Malay) populations of Patani and Kedah, the Phram (Brahmin) community of Lampam with their distinctive sitting-position burials, and the Negrito (Orang Asli) groups encountered in Kelantan and Kedah. The economic and administrative observations—on the bunga mas tribute system, salt-pan operations, rice taxation, currency fluctuations, and the structure of Siamese governance in the Malay states—provide a rare snapshot of pre-colonial administrative practice in these territories.

The natural-history component, while less prominent in the personal narratives, underpinned the expedition's scientific output. Evans's discovery of Peripatus (now Onychophora) at Bukit Besar, Jalor, was a landmark finding for zoological taxonomy. Laidlaw's account of Gunong Inas records several species new to science, including two rats, a keelback snake, a pit-viper, and a millipede. The expedition's collections generated over thirty published scientific papers across zoology, botany, and ethnography, the most significant being Skeat and Bladen's *The Pagan Races of the Malay Peninsula* (1906), which Gibson-Hill identifies as "the most important production to arise from it."

### Key Findings

- The Sleeping Buddha statue in Gua Gambar (Biserat, Jalor) measured approximately 96 feet in total length, with a left arm of 42 feet 7 inches and a mouth 1.5 feet wide; it was carved in solid limestone and flanked by colossal cobra figures, one with seven heads in a single hood (p. 44).
- The bunga mas tribute from Patani comprised gold and silver trees each at least six feet tall, requiring two months to complete; the combined value of the two trees was estimated at over $5,000, with the Jalor contribution valued at $1,500 (p. 77).
- Setul's government farm revenue (Pajak Besar) rose from $10,000 to $26,000 in two years following the opening of the port, while import/export duties (Pajak Chukai) increased from approximately $900–$1,000 to $3,180 over the same period (p. 131).
- The attempt on Gunong Tahan lasted over two weeks (11 August to 16 September 1899); the party carried twenty-six gantang of rice, which was exhausted before the summit was reached, and all thirty match-boxes were ruined by a flood at Kuala Badong, leaving the party unable to make fire for the final days of the descent (pp. 101–108).
- The Kelantan gaol at Kota Bharu contained approximately forty prisoners in cells measuring six feet by six feet by twenty-two inches; the Sultan supplied an average ration of six gantang of rice per day, and prisoners were let out to work in the fields once a week while still chained (p. 112).
- On Gunong Inas, Laidlaw and Yapp collected representatives of sixteen bird species, four mammal species, one lizard, two snakes, and one frog above 3,000 feet, including two rat species new to science (*Rattus edwardsi ciliatus* and *R. alticola inas*) and a keelback (*Natrix inas*, nov. sp.) (pp. 161–164).

### Conclusion

The expedition's definitive historical takeaway is that the Siamese Malay States at the turn of the twentieth century represented a distinct cultural and administrative zone—neither fully Malay nor fully Siamese—whose social structures, religious syncretisms, and economic systems were already undergoing rapid transformation under the dual pressures of Siamese centralisation and encroaching European commercial interest. Skeat's observations on the disintegration of aboriginal social life in Selangor, the decline of traditional crafts and textiles, and the increasing influence of Chinese capital in the gold-mining districts foreshadowed changes that would prove irreversible within a generation.

## Context

- The primary source material consists of Skeat's expedition diaries and field notes (completed as a manuscript in 1935–40, revised 1952), Laidlaw's personal records, and the scientific collections held at the University of Cambridge (Department of Zoology, Herbarium, and Museum of Ethnology and Anthropology).
- The volume's historiographical significance lies in being the only comprehensive, multi-disciplinary field account of the Siamese Malay States from the pre-World War I period; Gibson-Hill notes that no subsequent work has attempted to cover the full ethnographic ground with comparable authority.
