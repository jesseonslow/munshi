# The Sakais of Batang Padang, Perak

G.B. Cerruti published this brief ethnographic sketch in 1904, offering a morphological and cultural survey of the Sakai aborigines inhabiting the highland forests of Batang Padang, Perak. Writing from the position of a colonial observer with no access to Sakai written records, Cerruti constructs a portrait of a people he characterizes as physically distinct, socially simple, and in demographic decline, drawing on direct observation and the fragmentary oral traditions he extracted with difficulty.

## Summary

The article addresses a fundamental historiographical gap: the Sakais possess no written records, and Malay historical sources in Perak reach back only to the sixteenth century, treating the Sakais as slaves and "less than human beings" (p. 113). Cerruti therefore turns to morphological observation and the limited oral traditions available to him, acknowledging that Sakai narratives "very seldom pass beyond the time of a grandfather" and must be regarded as "inexact if not incoherent" (p. 113). The piece is organized as a systematic survey of physical traits, temperament, material culture, religious belief, social institutions, and subsistence practices, rather than as a narrative of events.

Cerruti's central analytical move is to frame Sakai social life through the metaphor of childhood: once trust is established, the Sakai "is like a child, and must be treated as such" (p. 114). This framing extends to their religious life, which he describes as "extremely simple" — devoid of any concept of a Creator or cosmogonic narrative, consisting instead of a binary belief in good and evil spirits, the latter dwelling in ravines, abandoned settlements, and caverns, and manifesting in wind, thunder, and lightning (pp. 115–116). Their marriage customs, he argues, are so informal — no ceremony, no purchase, no elder interference — that the population is "gradually decreasing in numbers" (p. 116). The article closes with a description of death and burial practices, noting the Sakai tendency to abandon a locality after a death, driven by fear that the dead will "drive the timid Sakais miles away" (p. 117).

### Key Findings

- Average male height approximately 5 ft. 3 in.; female approximately 4 ft. 11 in.; hair black, wavy or crisp but "never woolly"; senses described as unusually keen, particularly sight, hearing, and smell in the jungle environment (p. 113).
- Body painting in red, yellow, and black (from plant dyes, gutta, and lime) is not merely decorative but functions as a protective charm, dividing the face into four symmetrical areas and the trunk into two halves (p. 115).
- The Sakai possess no concept of a Creator god; evil spirits are believed to cause famine, disease, and the post-2 a.m. hill breeze, which is considered a "fatal hour" requiring all Sakais to remain awake (pp. 115–116).
- Marriage involves no ceremony, purchase, or elder selection; divorce is common and accomplished "without resentment or apparent jealousy," with causes including temporary sterility lasting "about three durian seasons" or an attack of dangerous disease (p. 116).
- Subsistence relies on maize, sweet potatoes, and tapioca rather than rice; the most primitive groups hunt with the sumpitan (blow-gun) using darts poisoned with an extract of *Ipoh* and a more dangerous poison from the roots of a creeper called *Legop* (p. 117).
- The dead are buried standing erect or sitting in a grave approximately 4 feet deep, accompanied by tobacco, betel-nut, potatoes, fruits, a blow-pipe, and poisoned darts; the community then abandons the area for at least a week (p. 117).

### Conclusion

Cerruti's definitive takeaway is that the Sakais represent a people in terminal demographic and cultural decline, whose informal social institutions — particularly the absence of binding marriage rites and the absence of any transcendent religious framework — render them unable to consolidate the social cohesion necessary for population maintenance. The article implicitly positions them as a vanishing element within the colonial landscape of Perak.

## Context

- Cerruti was a colonial official in Perak; the article is framed as an administrative-ethnographic observation of "aborigines," employing the racial taxonomy of the era (references to "savage races," the Sakais as "less than human" in the Malay view).
- The study draws on no archival collections; its evidential basis is direct field observation and oral testimony, making it a primary ethnographic source for early twentieth-century Sakai material culture in the Batang Padang district.
