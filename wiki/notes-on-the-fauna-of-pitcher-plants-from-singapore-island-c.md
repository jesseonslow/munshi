# Notes on the fauna of pitcher-plants from Singapore Island. C. Dover {and others

R. O. Winstedt published this critical edition of ancient Kedah legal codes in 1928, presenting a manuscript initially misidentified as Kelantan law that he demonstrated to be Kedah law through internal genealogical evidence. The article addresses the problem of textual authentication in early Malay legal manuscripts and provides the first full printed edition of the port-laws of 1650 A.D., a code of 1667 A.D., the Qanun Law of Dato' Kota Star, and a chapter on the *bunga mas* tribute, royal installation ceremonies, and betrothal customs.

## Summary

Winstedt's central contribution is the identification and authentication of a manuscript that had been received as "ancient Kelantan laws" from the Dato' Luar of that state. On examination, the names of rulers embedded in the text—Sultan Rijalu'd-din Muhammad Shah, Sultan Muhiyyu'd-din Mansur, Sultan Jamiwa, and Sultan Ahmad Taju'd-din Halim Shah ibni Sultan Abdullah Mukarram Shah—proved to be Kedah rulers, not Kelantan ones, thereby "exploding the authenticity" of the Kelantan attribution (p. 2). The manuscript, which Winstedt deposited with the School for Oriental Studies in London, contains four distinct legal codes spanning from 1650 to 1784 A.D., along with a chapter on the manufacture of the *bunga mas* (tribute of golden flowers paid to Siam), the installation of rulers, and betrothal customs (p. 2).

The oldest and most substantial code, dated 1060 A.H. (1650 A.D.), is a detailed port-law governing the entry, inspection, taxation, and departure of foreign vessels at the Kedah port. It specifies duties by origin (Kalinga versus Gujarat ships), tonnage brackets, and the chain of officials involved—from the *Panglima Kuala* (river-mouth warden) through the *Shahbandar* (harbour-master) to the *Panglima Bandar* (warden of the port) and ultimately the Raja (pp. 3–7). Winstedt provides both an English outline and the full Malay text of this code, along with the 1667 A.D. code of Dato' Sri Paduka Tuan, which extends into criminal law, agricultural regulation, and Islamic jurisprudence as administered by the *kweng* (village headman) (pp. 9–32).

The final chapter on the *bunga mas* and royal regalia is of particular significance for understanding Kedah's vassalage to Siam and the elaborate ceremonial hierarchy of the court. Winstedt notes that the maritime code section was copied in 1222 A.H. under Sultan Jamal al-'Alam Badr al-munir of Aceh (r. 1703–1726), and that a superior version of the same maritime laws, attributed to Sultan Mahmud Shah of Malacca, exists in an unpublished manuscript previously held by E. A. G. Stuart (p. 3). He also identifies a parallel edition of Kedah laws copied for Raffles in the Batavian Society library, establishing a broader manuscript tradition (p. 3).

### Key Findings

- The oldest code is dated 1060 A.H. (1650 A.D.), the year Zai, Friday the 17th of Jumada'l-akhir, and was enacted under the authority of "Shah 'Alam" with the *Shahbandar* Raja Jalil Putra and *Panglima Bandar* Dato' Laksamana Che' Shahdin (pp. 3, 15).
- Customary port dues were 400 *mas* Patani for ships from Kalinga and 600 *mas* for ships from Gujarat, with the amount varying according to vessel size and cargo (pp. 4, 7, 16).
- A stranger entering the country paid 6 *kupang* in gold for his permit; export taxes were 6 *kupang* gold per buffalo, 1 *paha* gold per elephant, and 1 *emas* per slave (p. 3).
- The 1667 A.D. code (1078 A.H.) was compiled at Kota Palas under the ruler there, with Dato' Sri Paduka Tuan, the Mantri, and Shaikh Alaidin, and incorporated Islamic criminal law administered through the *kweng* system (pp. 9, 27).
- The *bunga mas* (golden flower tribute to Siam) was a copper-wire structure wrapped in gold and silver, with 128 leaves and 37 nutmeg-shaped blossoms, standing one *hasta* (forearm's length) tall; its height was raised in 1222 A.H. when Sultan Ahmad Taju'd-din Halim Shah personally went to do obeisance at Bangkok (pp. 37–39, n. 13).
- The maritime code section was copied in 1222 A.H. (c. 1703–1726 A.D.) under Sultan Jamal al-'Alam Badr al-munir of Aceh; a parallel Achinese copy of the same year is held by the Batavian Society (van Ronkel's *Catalogus*, p. 307) (p. 2).

### Conclusion

Winstedt establishes that the manuscript represents a layered legal tradition of Kedah spanning over a century, from the port-laws of 1650 through the 1784 compilation, and that its Kedah provenance is confirmed beyond doubt by the royal names it contains. The article's broader significance lies in demonstrating that early Malay legal codes were not static texts but living documents, copied, adapted, and reissued across polities (Kedah, Kelantan, Aceh, Malacca), and that authentication requires careful genealogical cross-referencing rather than reliance on external attribution.

## Context

- Winstedt was a senior scholar in the Malayan Civil Service and a leading authority on Malay legal and historical manuscripts; his identification of the Kedah rulers' names as the key to authentication reflects his command of the Kedah royal chronology established in his earlier work (J.R.A.S., S.B. 81, 1920, p. 34).
- The primary source is a single manuscript obtained through W. E. Pepys of the Malayan Civil Service from the Dato' Luar of Kelantan, now deposited at the School for Oriental Studies, London; Winstedt also draws on an unpublished Stuart manuscript and the Raffles copy in the Batavian Society library.
