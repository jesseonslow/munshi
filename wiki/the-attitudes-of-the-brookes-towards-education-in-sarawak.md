# The attitudes of the Brookes towards education in Sarawak

Ooi Keat Gin (1997) examines the educational policies and attitudes of the three White Rajahs of Sarawak across the full century of Brooke rule (1841–1941), arguing that the Brookes treated education as a conservative instrument of material welfare rather than a vehicle for social transformation. Drawing on the Sarawak State Archives, the *Sarawak Gazette*, and missionary correspondence, the article demonstrates how the Brookes' fear of cultural contamination and political destabilisation produced a deliberately limited, practical curriculum for indigenous peoples while leaving Chinese vernacular education largely to its own devices.

## Summary

The article traces the evolution of Brooke educational philosophy from James Brooke's initial utilitarian embrace of missionary activity through Charles Brooke's more systematic (if ambivalent) secular school programme to Charles Vyner Brooke's reinforcement of a practical, vernacular-oriented curriculum. The central historical problem Ooi identifies is the tension between the Brookes' stated desire to improve indigenous living conditions and their deep-seated conviction that Western-style education would erode cultural integrity and, more critically, undermine the political arrangement of ruling through Malay chiefs with a handful of European officers. Charles Brooke's explicit fear that education was "the principal factor of revolution" (p. 56) reveals the political calculus beneath the paternalistic rhetoric.

Ooi shows that the Brookes' approach produced a plural and deeply unequal school system. For the Malays, the Brookes established vernacular schools teaching a practical curriculum in Malay, explicitly designed to keep students within their traditional agricultural livelihoods. For the Dayaks and other indigenous groups, the Brookes delegated education to Christian missionary societies, whose vernacular schools taught carpentry, cash crop cultivation, and animal husbandry—yet reached only a small fraction of the population. For the Chinese, the Brookes adopted a laissez-faire stance, intervening only when nationalist or communist propaganda in imported textbooks threatened political stability, particularly during the 1920s and 1930s.

The primary sources—Rajah's letters, *Sarawak Gazette* supplements, Administrative Reports, and the 1937 Hammond Report—reveal a consistent pattern of official indifference masked by occasional financial support. The Brookes provided land grants and maintenance stipends to mission schools but exercised no meaningful control over curriculum, textbooks, or staffing. The result, as Ooi argues, was an education system that preserved the status quo with remarkable success but failed in its stated objective of raising rural living standards.

### Key Findings

- The 1947 census recorded illiteracy rates of 85% among Malays, 91% among Melanaus, 98% among Sea Dayaks/Ibans, 93% among Land Dayaks, and 98% among other indigenous groups (p. 64).
- In 1936, of a total student population of 14,000 across all schools in Sarawak, only 339 Ibans (2.42%) and 296 Land Dayaks (2.11%) were enrolled in mission schools, while Chinese students numbered 11,279 (80.56%) (p. 61, n.9).
- The 1924 regulations limited government maintenance grants to schools teaching English or Malay as part of the curriculum and mandated the registration of every school, manager, committee member, and teacher, with prohibition on the use of "undesirable" books (p. 56).
- Between 1930 and 1933, the Brooke Government issued at least thirteen separate bans on specific Chinese textbooks deemed subversive, most containing anti-colonial propaganda and pro-China sentiments (p. 66, n.6).
- The post of Government Director of Education, created in 1924, was abolished in April 1933 due to financial constraints from the Depression, fragmenting oversight among the Residents, the Secretary for Chinese Affairs, and the Secretary for Native Affairs (p. 57, n.5).
- By 1937, urban Chinese schools in Kuching, Sibu, and Miri reached only the Junior Middle (Tzu-chung) level, while rural schools reached the Higher Primary Standard (Kao-shio); the Secretary for Chinese Affairs explicitly endorsed this low standard as "exactly suit[ing] the state of development of the country" (p. 58).

### Conclusion

Ooi's definitive takeaway is that the Brookes succeeded in their primary objective—preserving the cultural integrity and traditional subsistence livelihood of the indigenous population—but failed in their secondary objective of materially improving rural living conditions. Education was tolerated as a "necessary evil" and deliberately constrained to prevent the emergence of an educated indigenous class that might challenge the political order. The lop-sided development of educational facilities, concentrated in urban Kuching and serving predominantly the Chinese community, left the indigenous majority largely untouched by formal schooling throughout the century of Brooke rule.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Sarawak State Archives (Rajah's Letters, Rajah's Orders, Sarawak Treasury Ledger), the *Sarawak Gazette* and *Government Gazette*, the 1937 Hammond Report, and the United Society for the Propagation of the Gospel Archives in London.
- The article contributes to the historiography of colonial education in Borneo by shifting focus from institutional description to the political logic underlying the Brookes' educational choices, situating their policies within the broader framework of personal rule and the management of multi-ethnic populations.
