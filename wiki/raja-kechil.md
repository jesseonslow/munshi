# Raja Kechil

Raja Kechil (d. 1746) was a Minangkabau leader who orchestrated the conquest of the Kingdom of Johor in 1718, then the most powerful polity in the Malay world. Claiming to be the son of the murdered Sultan Mahmud Shah of Johor, he exploited a deep legitimacy crisis within the Bendahara dynasty to bring about the kingdom's collapse and establish the new Kingdom of Siak. His origins remain a matter of scholarly debate, but the weight of evidence suggests he was an ingenious Minangkabau figure with genuine connections to the Pagar Ruyong court who used the claim of royal descent as a political instrument [[1, pp. 51]](#ref-1).

## Origins and the Pagar Ruyong Connection

The *Siak Chronicles* preserve an elaborate origin story in which Raja Kechil was conceived by Enche' Pong, a concubine of Sultan Mahmud Shah, on the eve of the sultan's assassination in 1699. The child was secretly raised, passed through the care of the Orang Laut and the Temenggong of Muar, and eventually entrusted to the court at Pagar Ruyong, where the Putri Jamilan brought him up as her own [[1, pp. 59–61]](#ref-1). At Pagar Ruyong, the boy was formally installed with the beat of the royal drums (*nobat*) and given the title Yang dipertuan Kechil and the personal name Raja Beraleh, the four chief ministers of Minangkabau paying him homage [[1, pp. 63–64]](#ref-1).

Andaya argues that this narrative was a later mythologization. The Dutch records contain no reference specifically linking Raja Kechil to the Pagar Ruyong rulers, and in his first formal letter to the Dutch on 22 January 1719, he styled himself simply as "Baginda Raja Kechil who rules over Pulau Gontong and acts as Shahbandar for Baginda Sri Sultan of Pagar Ruyong," omitting any claim to be the son of Sultan Mahmud Shah [[1, p. 72]](#ref-1). The most likely explanation, Andaya concludes, is that Raja Kechil was a Minangkabau leader with genuine but perhaps informal connections to Pagar Ruyong who accurately assessed the depths of dissension within Johor and used the claim of royal descent as a psychological weapon [[1, p. 73]](#ref-1).

## The Conquest of Johor, 1717–1718

By late 1717, Raja Kechil had assembled a force at Bengkalis from among the Minangkabau settlers of east coast Sumatra, armed with a *chap* (a formal letter of authority) from the Pagar Ruyong court calling on all Minangkabaus to accompany him or provide tribute [[1, pp. 64–65]](#ref-1). The Dutch Governor of Malacca, receiving a letter dated 4 December 1717 requesting gunpowder and lead for the invasion, suspected a forgery because it contradicted earlier Pagar Ruyong correspondence affirming unity between Johor and Pagar Ruyong [[1, pp. 52–53]](#ref-1).

The invasion itself was less a military victory than a product of internal Johorese disintegration. The Raja Muda Tun Mahmud dispatched a fleet of seventy vessels downriver to engage the Minangkabau force, but the crews deserted en masse, having first wetted the gunpowder to render the cannons inoperable [[1, pp. 55]](#ref-1). The Bendahara Tun Abdullah, according to the Portuguese eyewitness Captain Joao Tavares, had communicated secretly with Raja Kechil and promised him the cooperation of the Orang Laut who manned Johor's fleets [[1, p. 57]](#ref-1). The Raja Muda fled on 4 March 1718 carrying so much gold that thirty men were required to transport it onto two sloops [[1, p. 58]](#ref-1). The Bendahara's own fortress, armed with fourteen cannons and supported by 4,000 armed men, was outflanked and abandoned [[1, p. 58]](#ref-1). Sultan Abdul Jalil surrendered and was made Bendahara under Raja Kechil [[1, p. 66]](#ref-1).

## Legacy and Documentation

The conquest marked the emergence of the Minangkabaus as a significant political force in the Straits of Malacca. Raja Kechil maintained a loose political unity among the Minangkabau communities of east coast Sumatra and the Malay peninsula until his death in 1746, and his sons Raja Alam and Raja Muhammad continued to raise forces that disrupted Dutch and Malay trade [[1, pp. 73–74]](#ref-1). Notably, later Siak rulers did not claim direct descent from the Malaccan dynasty through Raja Kechil but instead linked their prestige to the Minangkabau rulers of Pagar Ruyong, a decision that may reflect an awareness of his true origins [[1, p. 73]](#ref-1).

The historiography of Raja Kechil rests on a fortunate convergence of sources: the *Siak Chronicles*, the *Tuhfat al-Nafis*, the *Sejarah Raja Riau*, the Portuguese account of Captain Tavares, and the extensive records of the Dutch East India Company, including translated interviews with the refugee Shahbandar and Bendahara of Johor conducted immediately after the conquest [[1, pp. 51]](#ref-1). Andaya's 1972 article established a source-critical method for early Malay historiography by systematically cross-referencing these divergent traditions, demonstrating how the political context of each source shaped its narrative and resolving specific contradictions in the account of the 1718 conquest [[1, pp. 51]](#ref-1).
