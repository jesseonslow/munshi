# Dutch records from Malacca in the India Office Records

Ian Baxter's 1984 finding aid to the Malacca Orphan Chamber and Court of Justice records (series R/9) in the India Office Records, London, provides a comprehensive guide to 98 boxes of Dutch-language administrative, judicial, and financial documents spanning 1685 to 1835. The collection, donated by the Straits Settlements government in 1927 after years of neglect in the Malacca courthouse basement, was only fully sorted and listed in 1981, and Baxter's article serves to make it accessible to researchers for the first time.

## Summary

Baxter's article is a descriptive archival guide rather than a research monograph. Its primary purpose is to orient scholars to the R/9 collection by explaining the administrative structures it documents and then providing a detailed item-by-item inventory of all 44 sub-series. The historical background section traces Malacca's colonial trajectory from its Dutch capture from the Portuguese in 1641 through the British occupation of 1795–1818, the brief Dutch restoration of 1818–1825, and the final British transfer under the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824. This political succession explains why the records are predominantly in Dutch: the British allowed the existing Dutch civil administration to continue during their first occupation, so record-keeping in Dutch persisted until the permanent transfer in 1825.

The bulk of the article is devoted to the itemized listing of the collection's contents, organized under three main administrative bodies. The Orphan Chamber (Weeskamer) records, comprising 36 boxes, document its three functions: educational (maintenance of orphan children with deposited capital sums), fiduciary (administration of intestate estates and wills), and financial (lending on mortgage security, effectively substituting for banking). The Court of Justice (Raad van Justitie) records, in 54 boxes, cover criminal and civil jurisdiction including full trial transcripts, civil case files, petition books, and notarial protocol books recording conveyancing, slave transactions, emancipations, and wills. A single box of Political Council minutes and 7 boxes of miscellanea round out the collection, the latter encompassing slavery registers, police records, census data, shipping documents, and commercial regulations.

Baxter identifies two principal research values: the records illuminate the administration of justice under both Dutch and British rule, and the wills, estate papers, and private documents provide socio-economic information on Malacca's multi-ethnic population, particularly around the turn of the eighteenth century. The listing includes practical notations on physical condition (asterisks marking documents too damaged for consultation) and language (an "(E)" indicating English-language documents from the post-1825 period).

### Key Findings

- The R/9 collection occupies 44 feet of shelving (98 boxes) and was donated to the India Office in June 1927; it was completely sorted and listed only in 1981 (p. 105).
- The records span 1685–1835, with the bulk relating to 1785–1825, covering the final years of VOC administration, the British occupation (1795–1818), and the second Dutch administration (1818–1825) (p. 105).
- The Orphan Chamber's General Ledgers (Grote Boeken, R/9/5) from 1792 to 1820 record the dates of birth of Orphan Chamber pupils, making them a valuable demographic source (p. 113).
- The collection includes 73 criminal trial transcripts (R/9/21) covering charges from piracy and murder to theft and desertion, with defendants drawn from Malay, Chinese, Arab, Buginese, Javanese, and European communities (pp. 121–124).
- Slavery registers for Riau (R/9/40) dated December 1824 and January 1825 list individual slaves by name, age, race, place of origin, and owner, separated into children under 8 and adults over 8 (p. 132).
- The General Protocol Books (R/9/22) span 1786–1825 and were maintained by a succession of notaries including Jacob van Haak, Johannes Hamel, Francois Adam Lefevre, Jan Hendrik Stecher, Anthonij Stecher, and Daniel Koek, recording conveyancing, slave sales, emancipations, wills, and powers of attorney (pp. 124–126).

### Conclusion

Baxter's definitive contribution is the establishment of the R/9 collection as a major primary source for the social, economic, and legal history of colonial Malacca. By providing the first complete and accessible inventory of these records, the article opens the collection to researchers studying Dutch colonial administration, the multi-ethnic society of eighteenth- and early-nineteenth-century Malacca, the history of slavery in the Straits region, and the transition between Dutch and British colonial governance.

## Context

- The primary archival collection is the India Office Records (IOR), London, series R/9, donated by the Straits Settlements government in 1927.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in making a previously inaccessible and unlisted collection available to the scholarly community; it represents standard archival practice of the period in which finding aids were published in society journals to stimulate research.
