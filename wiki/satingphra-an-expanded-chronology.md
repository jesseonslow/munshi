# Satingphra: an expanded chronology

Stanley J. O'Connor's 1966 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* addresses a chronological dispute over Satingphra, a coastal site on the east coast of peninsular Thailand, by arguing that three stone Brahmanical sculptures recovered from the site push its period of cultural significance back to the seventh and eighth centuries—far earlier than the twelfth-century date proposed by ceramic analysis. The article is a focused stylistic and typological study that repositions Satingphra within the broader network of early Brahmanical sculpture production across the Isthmian tract, the Gulf of Siam, and Chen-la.

## Summary

The article arises from a specific scholarly disagreement. In July 1964, three preliminary accounts of the Satingphra antiquities were published simultaneously: Dr. Quaritch Wales placed the site in the Srivijaya period (eighth–thirteenth centuries), O'Connor suggested dates extending from the sixth or seventh centuries through the Srivijaya period, and Dr. Alastair Lamb, working from surface ceramics and bronze images, saw "no good reason to give to Satingphra a date earlier than 1200 A.D." (p. 135). O'Connor identifies the source of the discrepancy: Lamb's evaluation was based entirely on ceramics and bronzes and made no mention of the three stone Brahmanical sculptures held at Wat Majjhimavasa in Song Kla, which O'Connor considers fundamental to any assessment of the site's significance (p. 136).

The core of the argument rests on a broken stone Visnu torso from Satingphra, which O'Connor integrates into the series of long-robed, mitered Visnu images first studied by Pierre Dupont. By demonstrating that the Satingphra torso shares with the famous Takuapa Visnu a distinctive system of stone supports—hands spread away from the hips with reserves extending directly to the base without hip attachments—O'Connor overturns Dupont's earlier view that the Takuapa image was an anomalous sixth-century work close to a Pallava prototype. Instead, both images represent a late, fully evolved stage of the type, paralleling developments in Chen-la where the belt was similarly dropped when hands moved away from the body (pp. 137–138). This allows O'Connor to date both the Satingphra torso and the Takuapa Visnu to the second half of the seventh or the eighth century, and to argue that the Takuapa image was not a full-blown arrival with no local antecedents but the product of a developing Isthmian sculptural tradition (p. 138).

A second, smaller Visnu and a stone Ganesa from the same site reinforce the argument. The Ganesa, carved from reddish stone and approximately fifteen inches high, displays the naturalism and lack of adornment characteristic of early pre-Angkorian representations of the god, consistent with the seventh-to-eighth-century range assigned to the Visnus (pp. 141–142). Together, these sculptures establish that Satingphra was a settlement of considerable cultural complexity by the eighth century at the latest, with local artisans producing monumental stone sculpture in a style connected to Dvaravati and Chen-la workshops (p. 142).

### Key Findings

- The Satingphra Visnu torso shares with the Takuapa Visnu a five-point support system (feet, central drapery fold, and two reserves extending from spread hands to the base) that distinguishes both from other long-robed Visnus on the Peninsula, which use hip-attached reserves concealed by a scarf (p. 138).
- O'Connor dates the Satingphra torso and the Takuapa Visnu to the second half of the seventh or the eighth century, based on parallel developments in Chen-la art, specifically the Visnus of Trung Dien and Sra Prah Theat (p. 138).
- The Satingphra Ganesa, approximately fifteen inches high and carved from reddish stone, lacks a diadem and ornamental jewelry, placing it within the early naturalistic tradition of Ganesa representation documented by Malleret in the Phnom Penh Museum collection (pp. 141–142).
- Dr. Lamb's ceramic-based dating of the site to 1200 A.D. is acknowledged as valid for a later phase of occupation, during which Satingphra functioned as a trading center of some magnitude until approximately 1500 A.D. (p. 143).
- A Burmese administrative list from the 1767 invasion of Siam, identified by David Wyatt, references "Cha-thin" (Chat-thin-phra), indicating the site remained a substantial administrative center into the later eighteenth century (pp. 143–144).
- O'Connor explicitly revises his own earlier estimate (published in the *Journal of the Siam Society*, 1964) that one of the Satingphra Visnus dated from the sixth century, conceding that Dupont's original dating of the Takuapa image to that period was too early (p. 143).

### Conclusion

O'Connor's definitive takeaway is that Satingphra was not merely a Srivijaya-period trading post but a center of early Brahmanical culture with a local sculptural tradition of considerable sophistication by the seventh or eighth century. The site represents the first important new archaeological discovery in Peninsular Thailand since Lajonquière's travels in 1909, and its stone sculptures provide the missing link that integrates the Takuapa Visnu into a coherent regional development rather than treating it as an isolated masterpiece of unknown origin (p. 144).

## Context

- The article draws on the collection of Wat Majjhimavasa in Song Kla and the National Museum in Bangkok, with stylistic analysis grounded in Dupont's published typologies of pre-Angkorian and Dvaravati sculpture.
- Historiographically, the piece challenges the then-prevailing view (associated with Quaritch Wales) of the Malay Peninsula as a zone of cultural passivity and extreme acculturation, arguing instead for indigenous artistic agency in the Isthmian tract during the seventh and eighth centuries (p. 137).
