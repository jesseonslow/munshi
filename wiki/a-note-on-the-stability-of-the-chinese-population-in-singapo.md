# A note on the stability of the Chinese population in Singapore, 1947–1950

Maurice Freedman's 1958 note, drawing on Department of Immigration records from his 1949–50 fieldwork in Singapore, examines the composition and volume of Chinese "visitors" travelling between Singapore and China in the immediate post-war period. His central argument is that while the Chinese population was demonstrably stabilising—evidenced by the rise of the locally-born to sixty per cent by 1947—substantial transnational family ties persisted, and the pattern of who visited China revealed important structural differences between dialect-groups that had implications for the pace of full anchorage.

## Summary

Freedman's starting point is the demographic transformation of Singapore's Chinese community from a largely foreign-born, transient population to one that was increasingly locally rooted. By 1947, sixty per cent of Singapore's Chinese were born in the colony, a dramatic shift from the early twentieth century when the Straits-born (Babas) constituted only about ten per cent. Yet the question remained whether this generational shift would actually sever the active ties to China that had characterised the community for over a century. Freedman addresses this by analysing the flow of Certificates of Admission—documents that allowed Chinese residents to travel to China and return—issued in Singapore between 1947 and 1950.

The article's analytical core is a comparison between the dialect-group, sex, and age structure of certificate-holders and that of the general Chinese population as recorded in the 1947 census. Freedman finds that the visitor population was not a random cross-section of Singapore's Chinese. The Hokkien, who made up nearly forty per cent of the total population, were markedly under-represented among visitors, while the Hainanese, at only seven per cent of the population, were over-represented by a factor of three. This disparity reflected well-known differences in family organisation: Hokkien immigrants tended to bring their wives to Malaya or marry locally, whereas Hainanese men frequently maintained families in China and brought very few women over. The Cantonese presented a distinctive pattern in which women travelled to China in high numbers, a trend Freedman attributes partly to the independent working women who had immigrated in the 1930s and partly to a continuing cultural norm of female transnational mobility.

A further line of inquiry concerns the locally-born among the visitors. Freedman shows that while the locally-born element was relatively young (those under twenty-one outnumbered those over by 28), there were enough older locally-born individuals travelling to China to suggest that the homeland connection would not simply vanish with a generational turnover. The article thus complicates a simple narrative of stabilisation: the population was anchoring demographically, but the social and economic ties that made China a destination for a meaningful proportion of the community remained robust, particularly among certain dialect-groups and among women.

### Key Findings

- The number of valid Certificates of Admission in Singapore rose from 8,584 in January 1947 to a peak of 82,664 in December 1948, then declined to 64,302 by June 1950, reflecting both the pent-up wartime demand and the deteriorating political situation in China (p. 3).
- After adjusting for non-Chinese holders, seamen, regular Indonesia travellers, and Federation residents, Freedman estimates that approximately 7.5 per cent of Singapore's Chinese population at the 1948 peak was either in China, about to go, or recently returned; by mid-1950 this had fallen to about five per cent (p. 8).
- Between January 1948 and March 1950, 13,781 applications for entry permits (for dependants in China) were made in Singapore; 7,118 were granted, a small fraction compared to the free-immigration era (p. 2).
- The 1947 census recorded 730,133 Chinese in Singapore, with the Hokkien (39.6 per cent), Cantonese (21.6 per cent), and Tiuchiu (21.5 per cent) as the three largest dialect-groups; the overall sex ratio was 882 females per 1,000 males (p. 9).
- Among certificate-holders in December 1948, the Cantonese sex ratio was 962 females per 1,000 males (compared to 1,219 in the general population), and the Hakka ratio was 385 (versus 768 generally), indicating a disproportionate presence of women in these groups' visitor flows (p. 10).
- Of the locally-born visitors identified in the sample, 146 were under twenty-one and 118 were twenty-one or over, suggesting that while the locally-born visitor element was young, it was not exclusively so, and that transnational ties would persist beyond a single generational shift (p. 12).

### Conclusion

Freedman's definitive takeaway is that the stabilisation of Singapore's Chinese population was real but incomplete and unevenly distributed across dialect-groups. The demographic shift toward a locally-born majority did not automatically dissolve the social infrastructure of transnational family life. China continued to function as a meaningful destination for a significant minority of the population, and the persistence of locally-born visitors—particularly among the Hokkien, who had the highest proportion of locally-born certificate-holders at nineteen per cent—indicated that the homeland connection was a cultural and economic reality that would outlast the first generation of local birth.

## Context

- The primary data derive from records in the Singapore Department of Immigration, accessed during Freedman's Colonial Social Science Research Council-funded study of Chinese family organisation (1949–50).
- The article is a companion piece to Freedman's monograph *Chinese Family and Marriage in Singapore* (H.M.S.O., 1957), of which it forms a demographic supplement; it contributes to the early quantitative literature on Chinese diaspora settlement patterns in the Straits Settlements.
