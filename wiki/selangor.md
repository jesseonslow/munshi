# Selangor

### Scope and Historical Definition

Selangor is a state on the west coast of the Malay Peninsula whose modern identity was forged in the nineteenth century through the convergence of tin mining, Chinese immigration, Malay royal politics, and British colonial administration. The territory's transformation from a Bugis fief under nominal Johor suzerainty into a sovereign Malay sultanate, and thence into a British-administered state within the Federated Malay States, constitutes one of the most thoroughly documented political and economic narratives in the colonial literature of the Malay world. The state's economy was dominated by tin extraction from the mid-nineteenth century onward, with the Selangor River Valley, the Klang Valley, and the interior districts of Ulu Selangor and Ulu Langat forming the principal mining and agricultural zones [[1, pp. 1–16]](#ref-1), [[2, pp. 83–98]](#ref-2). By the late nineteenth century, Selangor had become the most economically dynamic state on the peninsula, a status driven by the concentration of Chinese mining capital, the construction of the Klang–Kuala Lumpur railway, and the emergence of Kuala Lumpur as a commercial centre [[1, pp. 89–99]](#ref-1), [[2, pp. 91–92]](#ref-2).

The political foundations of the state were laid in 1766, when Raja Lumu, a Bugis prince of Luwu descent, was installed as the first Sultan of Selangor at Kota Lumut in Perak, receiving the title "Sultan Salehuddin" and the *nobat* (royal musical ensemble) from Sultan Mahmud of Perak [[3, pp. 47–48]](#ref-3), [[4, pp. 7–8]](#ref-4). This act severed Selangor's ties with the Johor–Riau polity and anchored its legitimacy in the older Melaka royal line, a move that contemporary Malay political actors considered essential to the recognition of kingship [[3, pp. 53–54]](#ref-3), [[4, pp. 7–8]](#ref-4). The dynasty thus established continued through a succession of sultans to the present day, with the original crown of Hindu design from Celebes in use at coronations until it was altered to reflect Islamic influence by the reign of Sultan Muhammad (1826–1857) [[4, p. 11]](#ref-4).

### Political Transformation and the Civil War

The period from the 1850s to the 1870s was defined by a succession crisis and a devastating civil war that reshaped the state's political architecture. Sultan Abdul Samad, who had ascended the throne in 1857 under circumstances that Gullick characterises as semi-usurpation, faced a challenge from Raja Mahdi, who seized Klang by force in 1867 and withheld its revenues from the royal exchequer [[5, pp. 6–11]](#ref-5). The Sultan's response was to delegate authority to Tunku Kudin (Dhiauddin) of Kedah, a kinsman who arrived in Selangor in 1868 with a force of 500 men and a war chest advanced by his mother of $60,000 [[5, pp. 11–12]](#ref-5). The title "Viceroy" (*Wakil Yam Tuan*) under which Kudin operated was, as Gullick demonstrates, an ad hoc invention by the British official C.J. Irving during the July 1871 negotiations at Kuala Langat, coined to avoid "troublesome territorial and constitutional questions" rather than reflecting any established Malay constitutional concept [[5, pp. 10–11]](#ref-5).

The civil war (1866–1873) was fundamentally an economic struggle over tin revenues, fought between the Fei Chew (Hai San) and Kah Yeng Chew (Ghi Hin) Chinese factions with Malay chiefs aligned on both sides [[1, pp. 32–36]](#ref-1). The Chinese mining magnate Yap Ah Loy, a Hakka from Kwangtung who had arrived in Malaya in 1854 with eighty dollars, emerged as the principal military and economic actor on the Fei Chew side, directing strategy from Kuala Lumpur while his general Chung Piang commanded in the field [[1, pp. 12–16]](#ref-1). The war reached its nadir in August 1872, when Sayid Mashhor and Raja Asal captured Kuala Lumpur, killing Ah Loy's close associates and forcing his retreat to Klang; approximately 1,700 of his men perished during the retreat, and the European garrison under van Hagen was annihilated at Sungei Besi [[1, pp. 74–77]](#ref-1). The turning point came in March 1873, when Ah Loy, reinforced by Pahang troops, retook the town after a two-day battle [[1, pp. 79–80]](#ref-1).

The war's conclusion precipitated British intervention. The Davidson mining concession of 8 March 1873, granting exclusive rights over a large part of Selangor, was the factor that brought the state within the orbit of the Colonial Office, and the Pangkor Treaty of 1874 established the Residential System [[5, pp. 28–29]](#ref-5), [[6, p. 124]](#ref-6). The first Resident, James Davidson, took control of state revenues in 1875, and his successor, W. Bloomfield Douglas, served from 1876 to 1882 [[7, pp. 1–2]](#ref-7). Douglas's private diary, edited by Gullick, reveals a period of intense administrative activity in which the Sultan, far from being the passive figure depicted in Swettenham's writings, remained an alert and well-informed ruler who actively engaged with revenue matters, succession, boundary disputes, and judicial decisions [[7, pp. 7–9]](#ref-7). The diary also documents the precarious position of Tunku Kudin, whose direct dealing with the Sultan by Douglas rendered his position as Viceroy redundant, culminating in his departure in mid-1878 on a pension of $500 per month [[7, pp. 19–21]](#ref-7), [[5, pp. 32–33]](#ref-5).

### Economic Development and Infrastructure

The post-war economy of Selangor was shaped by the interplay of tin prices, transport infrastructure, and immigration. The London tin price fell from £90 per ton in 1875 to £66 in 1878 before recovering to £117 by 1888; the approximate break-even point for many mines was £70, meaning the valley's mines were unprofitable for much of the early colonial period [[2, pp. 87–88]](#ref-2). Yap Ah Loy's post-war recovery strategy combined government loans of $25,000 at 10 per cent interest, a special tin levy of $1 per *bahara* (yielding approximately $16,000 over two years), and the Kuala Lumpur revenue farms, which generated an estimated $8,500 profit in 1878 [[1, pp. 90–91]](#ref-1). By 1879 the tin price had doubled above $100 per *bahara*, and Ah Loy was never in financial straits again [[1, p. 91]](#ref-1).

The construction of roads and the railway was the decisive catalyst for demographic and economic revival in the interior. The carriage road from Damasara to Kuala Lumpur, 17 miles in length, was completed for only about 12 miles at the time of Hornaday's visit in 1878, the remainder requiring coolie portage through dense forest [[6, p. 126]](#ref-6). A systematic programme of road-making in the 1880s converted jungle paths into bridle tracks and eventually cart roads, opening the Ulu Selangor district to settlement and trade [[2, pp. 89–90]](#ref-2). The first 20 miles of railway from Kuala Lumpur to Rawang, completed in 1892, cost $800,000; the largest cutting was 50 feet deep and the biggest embankment 48 feet high [[2, pp. 91–92]](#ref-2). The extension of the railway to Kajang in 1897, combined with the first European estate land applications in 1894 and the Kindersley brothers' experimental rubber planting in 1896, fundamentally altered the economic trajectory of the Ulu Langat district [[8, pp. 14–15]](#ref-8).

The mining entrepreneur Loke Yew became the dominant figure in the Selangor River Valley in the 1890s, commanding a labour force at Serendah that peaked at 12,000 men, pioneering the use of electric power for mine machinery at his Rawang mines in 1894, and building a private road over the central range to found Bentong in Pahang [[2, pp. 90–91]](#ref-2). Population figures illustrate the scale of transformation: the 1884 census recorded 3,695 inhabitants in Ulu Langat (1,808 Malays, 1,675 Chinese), rising to 8,015 by 1891 (3,166 Malays, 4,664 Chinese) [[8, pp. 11–12]](#ref-8). In the Selangor River Valley, the 1884 survey recorded 3,572 inhabitants in Kuala Selangor district and 3,824 in Ulu Selangor, with 2,000 acres under cultivation in the former and 837 in the latter [[2, p. 90]](#ref-2).

The contrast between the mining-driven Ulu Selangor, which boomed rapidly once connected by rail, and the agricultural Kuala Selangor district, which lacked the stimulus of mining and remained dependent on fishing, coconut, and a *padi* (rice) cultivation repeatedly thwarted by the inability to control water supply, illustrates the uneven geography of colonial development [[2, pp. 93–94]](#ref-2). A complete *padi* crop failure occurred in Kuala Selangor in 1890, and by 1900 rice cultivation in the district was "practically given up," with peasants turning to coconut and fishing as more reliable livelihoods [[2, pp. 93–94]](#ref-2).

### Natural History and the Colonial Landscape

The earliest scientific accounts of Selangor's natural environment reflect the colonial assumption that scientific collecting and territorial consolidation were mutually reinforcing enterprises. W.T. Hornaday's 1879 field account, read before the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, documents a six-week expedition to the newly administered territory, organised around two field stations: the coastal village of Jerom on the Sungei Bulu, devoted to crocodiles, and the inland village of Batu in the tin-mining district, focused on large mammals [[6, pp. 124–131]](#ref-6). The largest crocodile obtained (*Crocodylus porosus*) measured 12 feet in length and weighed 415 pounds; ten specimens were collected in total over twelve days [[6, p. 125]](#ref-6). A tusked male elephant, not yet full grown, was killed at Batu after a stalk of approximately fifteen paces; the animal's back was "completely encrusted with a hard cake of dry mud" as protection against flies [[6, pp. 127–128]](#ref-6). *Rhinoceros sumatranus* was observed in the territory, with one individual noted as having been caught alive [[6, p. 131]](#ref-6).

Hornaday's account is notable for its frank assessment of the relationship between colonial infrastructure and indigenous populations. He explicitly states that "the making of good roads is the subjugation of Malays," linking the construction of the Damasara road to the extension of governmental authority into the interior [[6, p. 126]](#ref-6). He also offers a pragmatic recommendation that the government should offer a bounty for dead elephants, which he considers a growing nuisance to native agriculture and unlikely candidates for capture and training [[6, p. 130]](#ref-6). The government had established a reward of $50 for each black panther (*Felis perniger*), which Hornaday describes as "a pest" [[6, p. 130]](#ref-6).

A geographical note by A.M. Skinner and W. Bloomfield Douglas (1879) documents routes across the Selangor–Pahang range and observations in the Ulu Klang district. Datu Manku described the Sungei Roh route as rich in minerals and revenue, while Haji Mustapha outlined the Ulu Bernam crossing, noting the narrow water-shed and abundance of tin and gold [[9, pp. 133–134]](#ref-9). Douglas recounted his journey to Sungei Tata, documenting the ascent to Bukit Lalu, the descent to the mining settlement, and the discovery of sulphur hot springs near the Klang–Tata junction, which natives utilised for rheumatism [[9, p. 135]](#ref-9).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Selangor spans nearly a century and reflects shifting priorities from natural history and ethnography in the 1870s and 1880s to political and economic history in the twentieth century. The earliest contributions, Hornaday's 1879 field account and the Skinner–Douglas geographical notes, are products of the immediate post-Pangkor period, when the territory was still being physically mapped and its fauna catalogued [[6, pp. 124–131]](#ref-6), [[9, pp. 133–135]](#ref-9). The 1884 library catalogue of the Straits Branch records the Society's holdings at this formative stage, including the *Hikayat Abdullah* in its 1883 French publication, the *Maritime Code of the Malays* (Singapore, 1877), and *Makota Radja-Radja* (Paris, 1878), evidencing early institutional interest in Malay legal and literary texts [[10, pp. 2]](#ref-10).

The historiographical centre of gravity shifted decisively in the 1970s and 1980s with the work of J.M. Gullick, whose multi-volume research programme on colonial Selangor produced a series of articles that re-examined the period from primary sources. His 1975 edition of the Douglas diary challenged the conventional Swettenham-derived image of Sultan Abdul Samad as a passive ruler, demonstrating instead an alert and well-informed monarch who actively engaged with the affairs of his state [[7, pp. 1–9]](#ref-7). His 1986 study of Tunku Kudin re-examined the origin of the "Viceroy" title, showing it to be Irving's ad hoc invention rather than a genuine Malay constitutional concept, and used Malay eyewitness accounts to reconstruct military details of the siege of Klang [[5, pp. 10–11]](#ref-5). Middlebrook's 1951 biography of Yap Ah Loy, completed posthumously and edited by Gullick and Gibson-Hill, remains the definitive account of the Chinese mining magnate's role in the making of modern Selangor, drawing on seven primary sources including a Chinese-language "Record" dictated for Ah Loy and the Selangor Government Records for 1876–1886 [[1, pp. 120–125]](#ref-1).

More recent scholarship has extended the narrative to the pre-colonial period and to district-level economic history. Andaya's 1974 article on the 1766 installation of the first Sultan demonstrated a method for reading indigenous Malay and Bugis texts not as transparent windows onto "objective reality" but as products of specific political and genealogical agendas, using the Dutch VOC documentary record as an independent chronological framework [[3, pp. 41–57]](#ref-3). Khoo Kay Kim's 1985 Silver Jubilee Lecture reinforced this approach, arguing that the Melaka Sultanate's tradition of conferred legitimacy (*daulat*) remained the operative principle of Malay statehood even after the kingdom's fragmentation [[4, pp. 1–24]](#ref-4). Gullick's 2007 and 2008 articles on Ulu Langat and the Selangor River Valley respectively provide granular district-level economic narratives that complement broader state histories, drawing on Swettenham's journals, the Douglas diary, the *Selangor Journal*, census returns, and boundary treaty documents [[8, pp. 1–18]](#ref-8), [[2, pp. 83–98]](#ref-2). Hassan's 1967 study of Raja Bot bin Raja Jumaat exemplifies the use of State Council Minutes and Secretariat Files to document the administrative role of Malay chiefs in the post-1874 period, placing a previously under-examined figure at the centre of both the Lukut boundary dispute and the early Residential System [[11, pp. 68–93]](#ref-11). Gullick's 2008 article on Raja Muda Sulaiman traces the formative years of the future sultan, arguing that his path to the throne was shaped by the demands of an emerging colonial bureaucracy as much as by genealogy [[12, pp. 1–18]](#ref-12).
