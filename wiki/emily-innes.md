# Emily Innes

Emily Innes (1843–1927) was a Victorian author whose two-volume work *The Chersonese with the Gilding Off* (1885) provides a first-hand account of life in the Malay States during the period of early British administrative intervention. Written from the perspective of a district officer's wife stationed at Kuala Langat and Durian Sabatang between 1876 and 1882, the book presents observations on Malay court life, village society, and the daily hardships of European residence in the tropics. It was conceived as a deliberate counterpoint to Isabella Bird's *The Golden Chersonese* (1883), which Innes acknowledged as "perfectly and literally true" while asserting that the two authors had seen the country "under totally different circumstances" [[1, pp. 87–88]](#ref-1).

### Life and Background

Emily Anne Robertson was born on 5 March 1843 at Boxley, Kent, the third of six children of the Reverend James Craigie Robertson, who became a leading historian of the Christian church and Professor of Ecclesiastical History at King's College, London [[1, p. 88]](#ref-1). As the youngest daughter, she was expected to remain at home as companion to her mother, and it was not until she was almost thirty-two that she married James Innes, a Scottish lawyer's son who had served as Treasurer to the Government of Sarawak [[1, pp. 89–92]](#ref-1). The couple arrived in Sarawak in April 1875, but Innes was ordered to resign his post in March 1876 after a clerk absconded with government revenue and the year's accounts showed a deficit of approximately seven per cent [[1, p. 96]](#ref-1). They subsequently took up a posting in Selangor, where Innes served as District Officer at Kuala Langat and later as Superintendent of Lower Perak at Durian Sabatang, before resigning from the Selangor service in 1882 [[1, pp. 97–98]](#ref-1).

### The Book and Its Publication

Innes wrote the book from memory after returning to London, most of her letters home having been lost or destroyed [[1, p. 110]](#ref-1). She chose Richard Bentley & Son as publisher, a firm described on the title page as "Publishers in Ordinary to Her Majesty the Queen" [[1, p. 110]](#ref-1). The Bentley records reveal a memorandum of approximately thirty passages flagged for potential libel between 29 May and 4 June 1885, with instructions such as "alter name" against a passage identifying a forger; the published text was a considerably modified version of the original manuscript [[1, p. 110]](#ref-1). The book appeared on 19 October 1885 at a production cost of about £200, with a print run of 500 bound copies [[1, p. 111]](#ref-1). Sales were poor: by 1887 only 30 bound copies remained, and Innes's share of the profits over the ensuing years amounted to £41 9s. 0d. [[1, p. 111]](#ref-1). No reviews in the literary journals have been traced [[1, p. 111]](#ref-1).

### Assessment and Later Life

The book has long been regarded with scepticism. Professor Khoo Kay Kim, in his introduction to the 1974 reprint, accepted that Innes's "descriptive information" was valuable but noted that "her bitter experiences unavoidably clouded her vision"; Professor Heussler observed that the work "bears the mark of acute paranoia" [[1, p. 87]](#ref-1). The discovery of the private diary of Bloomfield Douglas, Resident of Selangor, has enabled a more nuanced assessment. Douglas's entries show that his initial relationship with the Innes was cordial — he recorded "Mrs. Innes very pleasant indeed" and noted that "it was quite a relief to enjoy ladies society" — before the relationship deteriorated into open hostility [[1, pp. 104–105]](#ref-1). The diary confirms that the "Boycotting" period at Kuala Langat (1880–1882), during which Innes received only five European visitors in nearly two years, was a product of mutual alienation rather than one-sided persecution [[1, p. 107]](#ref-1). After James Innes's death in 1901, Emily retired to Nairn in Scotland, where she died on 9 November 1927 at the age of eighty-three [[1, p. 113]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature has addressed Emily Innes primarily through J.M. Gullick's 1982 biographical reassessment in *JMBRAS* 55(2), which constituted the first systematic cross-verification of Innes's published account against an independent contemporary source [[1]](#ref-1). Gullick drew on the Bloomfield Douglas diary, the Richard Bentley publishing records, the John Murray archives, Colonial Office files, and Sarawak records to separate the factual content of Innes's narrative from the distorting effects of her personal grievances [[1, pp. 87]](#ref-1). The article established a framework for assessing the reliability of her observations chapter by chapter, correcting the earlier critical consensus that her account was uniformly unreliable [[1, p. 114]](#ref-1). Prior to this, the Society's journal had not published a dedicated study of Innes or her work, and the 1974 reprint of *The Chersonese with the Gilding Off* with Khoo Kay Kim's introduction remained the principal scholarly engagement with the text [[1, p. 87]](#ref-1).
