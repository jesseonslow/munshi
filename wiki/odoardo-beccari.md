# Odoardo Beccari

This 1921 obituary by I.H. Burkill and J.C. Moulton, published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, commemorates the Italian naturalist Odoardo Beccari (1843–1920), whose four decades of fieldwork across the Malay Archipelago, New Guinea, and Eritrea produced one of the most extensive natural history collections of the nineteenth century. The piece serves as both a biographical account and a historiographical assessment of Beccari's place in the scientific exploration of Malesia.

## Summary

The article is structured in two complementary parts. Burkill's section provides a chronological account of Beccari's four major expeditions (1865–1878) and his subsequent career as a systematist in Florence, emphasizing the sheer volume of material collected and the major publications that resulted. Moulton's section shifts focus to Beccari's intellectual legacy, drawing heavily on his 1902 memoir *Nelle foreste di Borneo* to assess his theories on evolution, the origin of man, and the natural history of Sarawak.

A central thread is Beccari's Lamarckian evolutionary theory, which the authors present with evident respect but also note its rejection by the mainstream. Beccari argued that environment was the principal agent in shaping species, that heredity was an obstacle to variation in the present, and that a "plasmative" primordial epoch had allowed free adaptation. His views on human origins—specifically that Borneo's forestal environment could not have produced bipedal man, and that the orang-utan was a collateral rather than progenitor of humanity—were informed by Sir Charles Lyell's suggestion to explore Bornean caves for fossils, a project later undertaken by A.H. Everett under a Borneo Caves Exploration Committee (pp. 5–6).

The article also situates Beccari within the broader history of Sarawak under the Brooke Rajahs, noting his visits to Santubong and Mount Serambu a decade after Alfred Russel Wallace's formative stays there. Moulton argues that Sarawak deserves recognition as the "germinating ground" for Wallace's theory of natural selection, and that Beccari's narrative, alongside those of Hugh Low, Spenser St. John, and Wallace, constitutes the primary documentary record of the territory's early colonial period (pp. 5, 9).

### Key Findings

- Beccari's first expedition (1865–1868) yielded 20,000 botanical specimens representing 3,300 species of Higher Plants, 800 fruits in spirit, a large timber collection, and 48 orang-utans (p. 2).
- His reptile collection from Borneo contained 88 species, 19 of which were new to science; his bird-skin collection totalled approximately 800 specimens representing 226 species (p. 7).
- His 300-mile overland journey from Bintulu to Kuching in 1867 (September 15 to November 20) followed the route up the Bintulu River, across to Belaga, down the Rejang to Sibu, and across country to Kuching (p. 7).
- *Malesia*, his major illustrated work on Malayan plant groups, was published in three volumes: the first at Genoa in 1877, the second from 1884–1886, and the third from 1886–1890, with reproduction costs partly met by a grant from the Bentham Trustees in London (p. 3).
- Beccari discovered *Amorphophallus titanum* in Sumatra; its tuber was so heavy it required two men to carry, and seeds sent to the Marquis Corsi Salvatori produced a flowering specimen at Kew in 1889, eleven years after discovery (p. 2, n. 1).
- His two monographs on rattan-palms were published by the Royal Botanic Gardens, Calcutta, in 1908 and 1914, with plates executed from photographs taken using an ingenious shadow-removal apparatus (p. 3).

### Conclusion

The authors' definitive takeaway is that Beccari's significance transcends his role as a collector or systematist; he was a "born naturalist" whose narrative ranks with the great nature-diaries of Darwin, Wallace, Bates, and Belt, and whose death removed one of the last living links to the romantic early period of Sarawak under the White Rajahs (pp. 4, 9).

## Context

- The article draws primarily on Beccari's own published memoir *Nelle foreste di Borneo* (1902/1904) and on the authors' personal acquaintance with him through the Singapore Botanic Gardens correspondence.
- Historiographically, the piece contributes to the early twentieth-century effort to consolidate the narrative of European scientific exploration in the Malay Archipelago, and Moulton's argument for Sarawak's role in Wallace's intellectual development anticipates later work on the geography of evolutionary thought.
