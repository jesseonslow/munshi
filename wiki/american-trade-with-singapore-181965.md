# American trade with Singapore, 1819–65

Sharom Ahmat's 1965 article traces the legal and commercial evolution of American trade with Singapore from the port's founding in 1819 through the post-Civil War period, arguing that the exclusion of American vessels was an artifact of the 1815 Anglo-American Convention of Commerce and Navigation that was gradually overcome through diplomatic pressure, commercial pragmatism, and shifting British imperial policy. The study is grounded primarily in the consular dispatches of Joseph Balestier and Straits Settlements administrative records.

## Summary

The central problem Ahmat explores is a legal ambiguity created by the 1815 Convention of Commerce and Navigation, which granted American vessels access to specific British East Indian ports—Calcutta, Madras, Bombay, and Prince of Wales' Island—but not to Singapore, which did not yet exist. This omission placed American traders in a grey zone: they were commercially motivated to use Singapore as a free port and entrepôt, yet the letter of the treaty appeared to forbid it. Ahmat shows how American merchants initially worked around the restriction through transhipment at Riau and anchorage at Bulang Bay, while British authorities in Singapore and India, though bound by the Convention's text, were in practice sympathetic and even encouraged American participation in the port's commerce.

The article then follows the protracted diplomatic effort—driven by Balestier as American Consul, by Singapore's mercantile community, and by Raffles himself in London—to secure a formal legal ruling. The Court of Directors' 1836 decision confirmed American trading rights at Singapore, but the re-imposition of the Convention's direct-trade clause (requiring American vessels to carry goods only to U.S. ports) and the devastating U.S. Tariff Act of 1842 (imposing a 20% differential duty on teas and coffees from non-producing places) repeatedly disrupted the trade. Ahmat uses Balestier's dispatches to illustrate how these external shocks—American tariff policy, the suspension of trade in northern China, and the Confederate cruiser *Alabama*'s disruption of American shipping in the 1860s—shaped the trajectory of American-Singapore commerce more than any single legal ruling.

Primary sources inform the narrative at every stage: Balestier's consular returns provide the quantitative backbone of trade volumes, while correspondence between the Straits Settlements government, the Supreme Government of India, and the Court of Directors reveals the internal British deliberations on American trading rights. The article also draws on the *Singapore Chronicle*, the *Free Press*, and the *Straits Times* to capture the mercantile community's perspective on the legal dispute.

### Key Findings

- Pre-legalization American trade with Singapore was minimal: $7,600 in 1823–24 and $4,000 in 1824–25, reflecting the deterrent effect of the 1825 seizure of the *Governor Endicott* by HMS *Larne* (p. 3).
- After the Court of Directors' 1836 ruling, trade surged: total cargo value on arrival reached $276,000 and on departure $613,000 between May 1834 and June 1836, with 9,272 tons of American vessels calling at the port (p. 9).
- The U.S. Tariff Act of August 1842, which imposed a 20% differential duty on teas and coffees imported from non-producing places, caused American trade to collapse from $165,024 in 1842–43 to $34,524 in 1843–44 (p. 15).
- Singapore had been receiving an average of 3 million pounds of coffee and 4½ million pounds of tea annually, commodities central to the American barter trade that the 1842 Act effectively destroyed (p. 15).
- The Confederate cruiser *Alabama* caused American trade to plunge from $1,203,681 in 1860–61 to $291,676 in 1861–62; by early 1863, eighteen large American ships lay idle in Singapore harbour, and more than half subsequently changed to the British flag (p. 17).
- The 1838 Court of Directors regulation permitted foreign ships to export goods from British Indian ports to any foreign country, and the 1839 rescission of the restriction limiting imports to a ship's own country's produce removed the last major legal barrier to American participation in Singapore's entrepôt trade (pp. 13–14).

### Conclusion

Ahmat's definitive takeaway is that American trade with Singapore was governed less by commercial logic than by the interplay of inherited legal frameworks, diplomatic negotiation, and external economic shocks. The free-port character of Singapore made it inherently attractive to American merchants, but the 1815 Convention's text created persistent friction that could only be resolved through a combination of British administrative pragmatism and American diplomatic persistence. The repeated disruptions caused by U.S. tariff policy and the Civil War underscore that the port's commercial success depended on conditions beyond the control of either party.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the T-128 series (26 volumes of U.S. consular dispatches from Singapore, 1833–1906) held at the National Archives and Records Service, Washington D.C., and the Straits Settlements Records at the National Library, Singapore (p. 241, n. 2; p. 248, n. 19).
- As one of the earliest studies to reconstruct Singapore's commercial history from American consular records rather than British administrative sources alone, the article provides a valuable complement to the British-side narrative and highlights the role of American commercial actors in the port's early development.
