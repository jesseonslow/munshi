# English-language fiction relating to Malaysia, Singapore and Brunei: a check-list

W.R. Roff's 1982 check-list in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 55, No. 242) is a cumulative bibliographic compilation of approximately 368 titles of English-language fiction set in or relating to Malaysia, Singapore, and Brunei, spanning from the 1890s to the late 1970s. The work serves as a reference tool for scholars of colonial and postcolonial literature, documenting the breadth of fictional engagement with the region by both expatriate and local writers.

## Summary

Roff's check-list is organized alphabetically by author and covers a wide range of genres, including adventure novels, romance, children's literature, war fiction, and literary fiction. The earliest entries date to the 1890s, with R.W. Wildman's *The Panglima Muda* (1894) and *Tales of the Malayan Coast* (1898) among the first, while the latest entries include Paul Theroux's *The Consul's File* (1977) and Shahnon Ahmad's *Rope of Ash* (1980). The list reveals that the majority of entries are by British or Western authors writing during the colonial period, with a significant concentration in the 1920s through 1950s, reflecting the era of greatest British literary production about the region.

The compilation includes works by major literary figures such as Joseph Conrad (eight titles, including *Almayer's Folly*, *Lord Jim*, and *Victory*), W.S. Maugham (five titles, including *Ah King* and *The Casuarina Tree*), Anthony Burgess (five titles forming his Malayan trilogy), and Nevil Shute (two titles). It also documents a substantial body of pulp adventure fiction, particularly the "Chandu" series by Own Rutter and the numerous thriller novels set in Singapore and Malaya. Pseudonymous authors are identified where known, such as Gavin Black (Oswald Wynd), Mark Derby (Harry Wilcox), and Michael East (Morris L. West).

Local and regional authors are also represented, including Shahnon Ahmad (four translated titles), Lim Thean Soo (five titles), Aminurrashid Harun (two translated titles), and various Chinese and Malay writers publishing in Singapore and Kuala Lumpur. The list notes juvenile fiction where identifiable and flags uncertain dates in square brackets. Roff acknowledges the assistance of Professors John M. Echols and David K. Wyatt of Cornell University and the Wason Library and Echols Collection in checking and supplementing entries (p. 62).

### Key Findings

- The check-list documents approximately 368 titles spanning 1894–1980, with the densest period of publication falling between the 1920s and 1950s (pp. 62–76).
- Joseph Conrad contributes the largest single-author corpus with eight titles, all set in the Malay Archipelago, establishing the region as a central setting in his literary output (p. 65).
- A significant proportion of entries are adventure or thriller fiction, particularly those set in Singapore, including the "Chandu" series by Own Rutter (1913–1926) and numerous 1950s–1960s crime novels (pp. 69, 73).
- The list identifies multiple pseudonymous authors, revealing that several prolific writers of Malayan-set fiction published under pen names, including Gavin Black (Oswald Wynd), Mark Derby (Harry Wilcox), and Michael East (Morris L. West) (pp. 63–64, 66, 67).
- Local Malay and Chinese authors appear in translated form, with Shahnon Ahmad contributing four titles (1972–1980) and Lim Thean Soo five titles (1970–1978), indicating a growing body of indigenous English-language fiction in the post-independence period (pp. 71, 74).
- The bibliographical reference sources cited at the end include earlier partial lists by Habibah binte Dato Salleh (1968), Donn V. Hart (n.d.), and Robin Winks (c. 1976), positioning Roff's compilation as a synthesis and expansion of prior scholarly efforts (p. 77).

### Conclusion

Roff's check-list functions as a foundational reference for the study of English-language fiction set in the Malay world, documenting the region's literary representation from the late colonial period through the early post-independence era. Its scope—encompassing both canonical literary fiction and popular genre writing—reveals that Malaya and Singapore served as a remarkably fertile setting for English-language narrative, attracting writers from Conrad to Theroux across nearly a century of production.

## Context

- The list draws on the Echols Collection at Cornell University, a major repository of Southeast Asian literary materials, and builds on earlier partial bibliographies compiled at the University of Malaya and Syracuse University (p. 77).
- The publication in JMBRAS reflects the journal's long-standing commitment to documenting the intellectual and cultural history of the Malay world, including its literary traditions in English.
