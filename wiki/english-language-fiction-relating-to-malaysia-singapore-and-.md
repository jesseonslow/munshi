# English-language fiction relating to Malaysia, Singapore and Brunei: a check-list

Kobkua Suwannathat-Pian's 1982 article examines a Thai-language manuscript of official correspondence between Kedah and Siam (1890–1898) held at the Arkib Negara, arguing that these years represent the zenith of cordial Kedah-Siamese relations, characterised by mutual trust, personal royal ties, and diplomatic cooperation that stood in sharp contrast to the hostility of the earlier nineteenth century. The study centres on the manuscript SPP/K/II;509/67, a file of approximately 190 copied letters maintained by Kedah's Royal Scribes Department, and uses it to illuminate the political manoeuvres surrounding the Perlis question and the introduction of the Siamese *thesaphiban* provincial system.

## Summary

The article is structured in two parts. Part I provides a contextual and analytical framework for understanding the correspondence, situating it within the broader history of Kedah-Siamese relations from the 1821 Siamese invasion through the reconciliation of 1841 and the subsequent period of rapprochement. Suwannathat-Pian demonstrates that the 1890s witnessed a remarkable degree of personal intimacy between the two courts: Kedah royal children were educated in Bangkok, Sultan Abdul Hamid accompanied King Chulalongkorn on the latter's 1891 tour of the Malay Peninsula, and the Sultan's mother, Wan Hajar (known to the Siamese as Wan Teh), cultivated direct relationships with the Siamese Queen. The author emphasises that Kedah rulers went to extraordinary lengths to demonstrate loyalty, including participation in the Brahmin-Buddhist Water of Allegiance Ceremony, which was contrary to Islamic teachings, and the prompt dispatch of *bunga mas* tribute gifts every three years.

Part II presents translations of ten letters directly concerning the Perlis affairs, spanning 1892 to 1897. These letters reveal a sustained diplomatic campaign by Sultan Abdul Hamid, his brother the Raja Muda (Tengku Abdul Aziz), and his mother Wan Hajar to secure the return of Perlis to Kedah following the death of the second Raja of Perlis in February 1897. The correspondence shows a methodical strategy: written petitions followed by a personal mission to Bangkok, where the Raja Muda and the Chief Minister Wan Mat Saman lobbied the Siamese Ministry of the Interior over a period of two months. The outcome was the establishment of *monthon* Saiburi on 7 April 1897, with Sultan Abdul Hamid appointed *khaluang thesaphiban* (Superintendent Commissioner) over Kedah, Perlis, and Setul. However, the final letter in the series reveals that the arrangement was already fracturing, as the new Phraya Perlis refused to accept Kedah's supervisory authority and ordered his officials to stop working.

The manuscript itself is described as a hard-cover exercise book of foolscap size, containing long-hand copies in the scribes' hand. It is significantly damaged, with the first few pages beyond repair, and the letters are not strictly arranged chronologically. Suwannathat-Pian corrects the Arkib's dating of the document (listed as 1887–1895) to 1890–1898, based on the earliest surviving letter dated 25 March 1890 and the latest dated 21 January 1898.

### Key Findings

- The manuscript SPP/K/II;509/67 contains approximately 190 letters and 18 royal decrees (dated A.H. 1297–1313 / A.D. 1880–1896), covering the period 25 March 1890 to 21 January 1898 (pp. 62, 67).
- In 1892–93, Kedah took a loan of 100,000 dollars from Bangkok, repayable within 5–6 years; this was part of a broader financial deterioration that eventually led to a loan exceeding 2,000,000 dollars and the acceptance of a Siamese Financial Adviser in 1905 (pp. 62, 68).
- The traditional *bunga mas* tribute gifts, as recorded in letters of 1894 and 1897, comprised: 3 pairs of spears with silver-gilded sheaths, 15 pairs of long swords with gold-gilded sheaths, 2 pairs of swords with carved-wooden sheaths, 10 pairs of knives with rattan sheaths, 40 rolls of white cloth (80 *sawk* length), 40 rolls (50 *sawk*), 80 rolls (24 *sawk*), and 60 rolls of white-saklad material (24 *sawk*) (p. 69, n. 14).
- *Monthon* Saiburi was formally established by decree on 7 April 1897 (R.S. 116), appointing Sultan Abdul Hamid as *khaluang thesaphiban* with supervisory authority over Kedah, Perlis, and Setul; the decree specified that revenue from Perlis and Setul would be kept separately and that the *bunga mas* from all three states would be delivered by the Sultan to Bangkok (pp. 67, 80, 85, n. 73).
- Nakhon Sri-thammarat was removed as the intermediary *Phraya* overseeing Kedah in 1896, a change that marked the peak of direct Kedah-Bangkok relations; the Sultan was elevated to the rank of *Chao Phraya* in a grand ceremony in Bangkok the same year (pp. 63, 68, 83, n. 45).
- The final letter (23 Jamadilawal A.H. 1315 / 20 October 1897) records that Phraya Perlis refused to affix his seal alongside the Sultan's on land-grant documents, declared he was "in the service of His Majesty the King" and could not govern as Kedah directed, and ordered all his officials to stop working (pp. 81–82).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian's definitive takeaway is that the years 1890–1898 constitute the high point of Kedah-Siamese relations, a period of genuine mutual understanding and personal closeness between the two courts that was achieved against the backdrop of intense Anglo-Siamese rivalry in the Peninsula. The Perlis affair, while ultimately only a temporary and illusory satisfaction of Kedah's territorial claims, demonstrates both the considerable influence Kedah leaders could exert on Siamese policy and the limits of that influence when confronted with the structural imperatives of Bangkok's centralising *thesaphiban* reform.

## Context

- The primary source is the Thai manuscript SPP/K/II;509/67 at the Arkib Negara, Malaysia, supplemented by Siamese official publications (*Prachum Phongsawadan*, Chulalongkorn's *Royal Tour* journals) and British Foreign Office records (F.O. 422 series).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Siamese provincial administration and the Western Malay States by providing the first detailed English-language analysis of Kedah's own diplomatic correspondence with Bangkok, offering a Kedah-centric perspective that complements and at times corrects the British colonial record (e.g., contradicting Swettenham's claim that the Sultan did not speak Siamese).
