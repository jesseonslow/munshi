# The collective memory of the Sook Ching massacre and the creation of the civilian war memorial of Singapore

Kevin Blackburn's 2000 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* traces how the Sook Ching Massacre of 1942 was transformed from a Chinese communal grievance into the foundational narrative of Singapore's national identity, culminating in the Civilian War Memorial dedicated in 1967. Drawing on war crimes trial transcripts, Chinese-language press archives, and diplomatic records, Blackburn argues that the Singapore government deliberately reshaped the memory of a single-ethnicity atrocity into an ideology of collective suffering to serve the political project of nation-building in a multi-ethnic state.

## Summary

The article begins with the theoretical framework of David Lowenthal and Ernest Renan on how states construct national heritage around common suffering to bind diverse populations. Blackburn then moves to the historical event itself: the Japanese army's systematic massacre of Chinese adult males in Singapore in February–March 1942, which he situates within the broader pattern of Japanese military atrocities in China. The screening process at mass identification centres, the indiscriminate nature of the killings, and the subsequent demand for $50 million in tribute money are reconstructed from testimony given at the 1947 War Crimes Trial and from contemporary Chinese press reporting.

The second major thread of the article follows the post-war Chinese community's campaign to locate mass graves, rebury the dead, and exact what the Chinese press called the 'blood debt' from Japan. Blackburn shows how the 1947 trial failed to satisfy the victims' families—only seven officers were charged, and the Nuremberg defence of following orders was effectively accepted—leaving a persistent sense of unresolved grievance that resurfaced in public demonstrations as late as 1958. The accidental discovery of mass graves at Siglap in February 1962 reignited the campaign and brought it into direct collision with the political imperatives of Lee Kuan Yew's government, which was simultaneously managing communal tensions, preparing for merger with Malaya, and courting Japanese investment.

The article's central argument is that the government co-opted the Chinese community's memorial campaign and subordinated it to a nationalist narrative of shared suffering across all ethnic groups. The Chinese Chamber of Commerce was compelled to accept conditions that reframed the memorial as a national monument rather than a Chinese one, the proposed Chinese cemetery at Siglap was blocked, and the final design—four pillars representing the four major ethnic groups—erased the specific history of the Sook Ching Massacre from the monument's public inscription. The result, Blackburn contends, was a national heritage that served present-day political needs at the expense of historical accuracy.

### Key Findings

- The Japanese military targeted 50,000 'anti-Japanese' Chinese adult males for execution; the massacre was called off on 3 March 1942 after approximately two weeks. Estimates of the dead range from the Japanese official figure of 5,000 to 100,000 in Chinese community rumour, with testimony from Major Hayashi Tadahiko suggesting a figure between 25,000 and 50,000 (pp. 73–75).
- The 1947 War Crimes Trial charged only seven Japanese officers; Lieutenant Generals Kawamura Saburo and Oishi Masayuki were sentenced to death, while the other five received life imprisonment. The victims' families declared themselves unsatisfied, and the 'blood debt' remained unresolved in collective memory (p. 78).
- Between February 1962 and August 1966, approximately 100 mass graves were exhumed, yielding enough human remains to fill 600 large funeral urns, of which 428 came from the Siglap site alone (pp. 81–82).
- Over 120,000 people gathered at City Hall on 25 August 1963 to demand $50 million (US$17 million) in compensation from Japan; a subsequent boycott of Japanese goods lasted less than a week (p. 85).
- Japan agreed on 25 October 1966 to pay $50 million in compensation—structured as $25 million grant and $25 million loan—without an official apology. The Singapore government considered the matter closed to protect Japanese investment (p. 87).
- The Civilian War Memorial cost $750,000 to build, funded equally by the government and the Chinese Chamber of Commerce. Its four pillars, 67.4 metres high, were dedicated on 15 February 1967 to all civilian victims of the Japanese Occupation (pp. 72, 87).

### Conclusion

Blackburn's definitive takeaway is that the Civilian War Memorial stands as a case study in what Lowenthal calls 'fabricating heritage': the very forces that drove the monument into existence—the Chinese community's demand for a memorial to the Sook Ching victims—were systematically denied in the monument's final form because the government required a symbol that matched the political demands of 1960s nation-building. The memorial represents a past that all Singaporeans supposedly shared, and in doing so, it required the forgetting of the specific ethnic targeting that had originally motivated its creation.

## Context

- Primary sources include the 1947 War Crimes Trial record (WO 235/1004, Public Record Office, London), Australian Department of External Affairs diplomatic cables (Canberra), Legislative Assembly Debates of the State of Singapore (1962), and extensive Chinese-language press coverage (*Sin Chew Jit Poh*, *Nanyang Siang Pau*, *Nanfang Evening Post*, *Utusan Melayu*).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Singapore's national memory by demonstrating how the state's multi-racial ideology actively suppressed the ethnic specificity of wartime atrocities in official commemoration, a process that remains visible in the memorial's design and the annual 15 February school ceremonies.
