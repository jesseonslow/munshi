# Transformation of Mandailing cultural identity and leadership

Abdur-Razzaq Lubis, a Mandailing-descended scholar and project leader of a Toyota Foundation research grant on Mandailing migration, published this article in 2003 tracing the transformation of Mandailing cultural identity and the traditional governance institution of *Namora-Natoras* from the pre-colonial period through the Padri War, Dutch and British colonialism, nationalist movements, and into the age of globalization. The overarching thesis is that Mandailing identity has been repeatedly constructed, suppressed, and reinvented by successive external forces—Wahhabite Islamization, colonial administrative reclassification, nationalist state-building, and global economic integration—each of which progressively dismantled the traditional consultative governance system while simultaneously provoking new assertions of ethnic distinctiveness.

## Summary

Lubis begins by establishing the Mandailing as a historically hybridized people whose cultural identity rests on centuries of incorporation of Chinese, Bugis, and Indian elements into their gene pool and material culture. The traditional governance institution, *Namora-Natoras*, functioned as a parliamentary and judicial assembly in which nobles (*namora*) and elders (*natoras*) governed within the constraints of *adat Dalihan Na Tolu*, a body of customary law. The *huta* (fortified settlement) served as a self-governing "village republic" with a transparent, open-air council hall (*sopo godang*) and a courtyard of justice (*alaman bolak silangse utang*) where any person could seek refuge. This system was first disrupted by the Padri War (1816–33), which brought Wahhabite Islam to Mandailing at the point of the sword and triggered the most extensive exodus of Mandailing peoples to the west coast of the Malay Peninsula. The Mandailing who migrated did so in whole-clan units under the command of *namora-natoras*, maintaining their consultative governance structures in their new settlements and becoming deeply embroiled in the wars of nineteenth-century Malaya.

The article's central analytical move is to show how colonial powers on both sides of the Strait of Malacca reclassified Mandailing identity for administrative convenience: the Dutch subsumed them under "Batak" and progressively stripped the *namora-natoras* of judicial powers, legal immunities, and hereditary rights between 1875 and 1908, while the British classified them as "Malays" in the censuses of 1911 and 1921, a category that was then reinforced by the Malay Reservation Enactment of 1913 and the Federal Constitution upon independence. In Sumatra, the Mandailing fought back against the Batak label through the Sungai Mati cemetery dispute of 1922–26, which produced the landmark text *Asal-Oesoelnja Bangsa Mandailing* and a successful petition to the 1930 census. In Malaya, by contrast, the *namora-natoras* did not object to their reclassification as Malays, and the institution died with the last great leader, Raja Haji Muhammad Ya'qub, during the Japanese Occupation. The nationalist movements of the 1920s–40s further marginalized traditional leadership, and post-independence state hegemony in both Indonesia and Malaysia completed the erasure of Mandailing as a distinct political category.

Lubis draws on a wide range of primary sources including Colonial Office files, the *Hikayat Pahang*, *Pesaka Selangor*, *Tarikh Raja Asal dan Keluarganya*, the *Riwayat Hidup Tuan Abu Bakar*, and the 1926 *Asal-Oesoelnja Bangsa Mandailing*, alongside Dutch colonial administrative records and the native press. The article's final section addresses the contemporary revival of Mandailing identity through organizations such as IMAN (established 1979) and the formation of the Madina district in 1992, while noting the unresolved tensions between the competing labels of Mandailing, Batak, and Malay that continue to structure political and cultural life on both sides of the Strait.

### Key Findings

- In 1845, each *huta* in Mandailing consisted of between 350 and 400 people, governed by a *raja* from the land-owning clan (p. 57).
- The Dutch systematically dismantled *Namora-Natoras* powers: major judicial powers removed in 1875, hereditary principle departed from after 1890, legal immunities (*forum privilegiatum*) abolished around 1900, and direct taxation introduced in 1908 (p. 63).
- In the Federated Malay States census of 1911, "Mendeling" was listed under "Malay Population by Race"; by 1931 the term was removed altogether from the census (p. 67).
- The Sungai Mati cemetery dispute (1922–26) was resolved in favour of the Mandailing by the *Raad van Justitie*, which reversed the Governor-General's decision, and produced the 1926 publication *Asal-Oesoelnja Bangsa Mandailing* (p. 66).
- IMAN registered 1,500 members by 1982, against an estimated 30,000 Angkolan and Mandailing in Malaysia; in 1992 it proposed a reconstruction of Raja Asal's village at Changkat Piatu on 120–400 hectares at a cost of RM10 million (pp. 71–72).
- The Japanese military abolished the territoriality of the *huta* and *banua* in 1942, and the institution of *Namora-Natoras* in Perak died with Raja Haji Muhammad Ya'qub during the Occupation (p. 69).

### Conclusion

Lubis argues that the Mandailing have reached a new turning point in which globalization, paradoxically, creates conditions for the recovery of cultural identity that was suppressed during the ages of Islamization, colonialism, and nationalism. The revival of the *gordang sambilan* drums, the formation of the Madina district, and the re-establishment of cultural ties between Malaysian and Indonesian Mandailing represent a dialectical response to global homogenization. The author's definitive takeaway is that the very qualities of Mandailing culture that were marginalized—consultative governance, the primacy of *adat* alongside Islamic law, and the principle of impartial justice—will prove essential to the community's survival and agency in the face of global economic integration and environmental degradation.

## Context

- The article draws on Colonial Office files (CO 271, CO 273 series), Dutch colonial administrative records, the *Politieke Inlichtingen Dienst* archives, and a range of Mandailing-language and Malay-language primary texts including *Tarikh Raja Asal dan Keluarganya* (1934) and *Asal-Oesoelnja Bangsa Mandailing* (1926).
- Historiographically, the article challenges the anthropological convention of treating "Batak" as a fixed ethnic category, arguing instead that the label was a colonial and academic construction that the Mandailing themselves actively resisted. It also intervenes in the Malaysian historiography of ethnic identity by demonstrating that the "Malay" classification of Mandailing migrants was an administrative imposition rather than a reflection of self-identification.
