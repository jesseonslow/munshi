# Prince Damrong’s introduction to the “Dispatches of Luang Udom Sombat”

Nathan Porath's 2011 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* applies René Girard's theory of mimetic desire to the *Hikayat Patani*, a six-book Malay manuscript composed between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries. Porath argues that the text's narrative structure reveals Patani's political identity as fundamentally shaped by its mimetic relationship with Ayutthaya—an Indic kingdom to which Patani was culturally drawn—while simultaneously fracturing its resemblances with the Malay kingdom of Johor.

## Summary

Porath reads the *Hikayat Patani* not as a straightforward historical chronicle but as a "mimetic text" that encodes the power relations of the pre-modern Southeast Asian kingdom world. Drawing on Girard's concept of mimesis as a competitive power relationship in which a subject desires what its model possesses, Porath maps the text's narrative onto the hierarchical mandala system of Indic kingdom politics. The first book of the *hikayat*, which Bradley has identified as originally oral in composition, is structured around two axes: an affirmation of similarity with Ayutthaya and a deliberate fracturing of similarity with Johor. This dual strategy, Porath contends, reveals that Patani's prestige in the Malay-speaking world was derived in part from its association with the more cosmically powerful Ayutthaya, while its differentiation from Johor was necessary to maintain internal political identity among recognizably similar Malay polities (pp. 45–48).

The article then traces how these mimetic dynamics play out across the text's major narrative episodes. The founding legend and the conversion story are read through the lens of Jordaan and Josselin de Jong's work on skin-disease myths as metaphors for the social contract between ruler and realm. The conversion narrative, in particular, is interpreted as encoding a political and social contract between the kingdom and the Islamic faith, with the king's cure made conditional on his conversion—a condition that elevated the Muslim community from a subordinate religious minority to the basis of the kingdom's legitimacy (pp. 48–50). The relationship with Johor is then analysed through the text's repeated deflation of the Johor raja and its climactic scene in which the envoy Raja Lela is made to articulate the difference between Patani and Johor despite their fundamental similarity, a moment Porath identifies as the text's most explicit act of "fracturing resemblances" in Harrison's sense (pp. 52–54).

The most substantial section of the article concerns Patani's two rebellions against Ayutthaya. Porath reinterprets the first rebellion of Sultan Mudhaffar Syah (c. 1564) not as an act of secession but as a failed usurpation within the Indic framework of mimetic kingdom-hood, arguing that the sultan's attack on the Ayutthaya palace was an attempt to appropriate the cosmic power of a weakened rival during the Burmese siege. A key piece of evidence is the sultan's statement in the original Malay—"that king is not different from us"—which Porath reads as an affirmation of mimetic identity rather than a declaration of independence. He further argues that the cryptic phrase "rather than one country, two is better" indirectly references the Ayutthaya–Burmese war and the fear that the two kingdoms would be merged into "one golden country" under Burmese domination (pp. 55–58). The second rebellion under Queen Raja Ungu (c. 1630s) is interpreted as a rejection of Ayutthaya's authority made possible by the usurpation of King Prasat Thong, whose illegitimacy gave the queen grounds to refuse her Thai royal title and assume the Islamic title Paduka Syah Alam (pp. 59–61).

### Key Findings

- The *Hikayat Patani* manuscript was written in 1839 and comprises six books composed at different periods between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries, following the end of Patani's founding dynasty and during its decline under Kelantanese kings (p. 46).
- Sultan Mudhaffar Syah's attack on Ayutthaya (c. 1564) is confirmed by the Luang Prasoert chronicle, which records the sultan riding one of King Cakkraphat's white elephants in the palace plaza, and by van Vliet, who identifies the motive as the sultan's perception of Ayutthaya's weakness under Burmese pressure (pp. 56–57).
- Queen Raja Ungu rejected her Thai royal title *Phra Nang Chaou Ying* and assumed the Islamic title Paduka Syah Alam, an unprecedented severing of Patani's ties with Ayutthaya that van Vliet described as "the ambition to obtain the highest power" (p. 60).
- King Prasat Thong mobilized 60,000 men for the 1632–36 campaign against Patani, headed by the Governor of Ligor, Okya Kalahom, the Pra Klang, and a Muslim official named Rabisit; the king also requested armed ships from the Dutch in Batavia, which arrived too late (p. 61).
- The *hikayat* states that the second rebellion was the first time the governor of Ligor and Siam had attacked Patani, indicating a radical shift in the Patani–Ayutthaya relationship between the two rebellions (p. 61).
- Bradley's revision places the deposition of Raja Kuning by the King of Kelantan in 1649 and the installation of his son in 1651 with Ayutthaya's support, rather than the later date of 1688 previously accepted from Teeuw and Wyatt (p. 52).

### Conclusion

Porath's definitive takeaway is that the *Hikayat Patani* should not be read through a modernist ethnicist lens that would interpret Patani's rebellions as proto-nationalist assertions of Malay identity against Thai rule. Instead, the text reveals that Patani's kingdomhood was constituted through its mimetic relationship with Ayutthaya—a shared Indic cosmos inherited from its Buddhist past—and that the text's ultimate political message is that the viability of the Patani *kerajaan* depended on the maintenance of hierarchical order and peace with that northern kingdom. The text's refusal to moralize against Ayutthaya, even after sixty years of warfare, and its affirmative portrayal of similitude with the Siamese court, stand in stark contrast to other Malay *hikayats* that deny resemblance with Siam and portray it in negative terms (pp. 62–63).

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Teeuw and Wyatt (1970) edition of the *Hikayat Patani*, the Thai version edited by Wyatt (1967), van Vliet's *Siam* (Baker et al. 2005), the Royal Chronicles of Ayutthaya (Cushman 2006), and Bradley's (2009) contextualization of the text.
- Historiographically, the article extends Bradley's (2009) work on the *hikayat* as a political charter by introducing Girardian mimesis and Harrison's concept of "fracturing resemblances" as analytical tools for understanding how the text constructs Patani's identity in relation to both Ayutthaya and Johor.
