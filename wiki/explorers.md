# Explorers

The exploration of the Malay Peninsula and its surrounding regions by European travellers, missionaries, and surveyors between the 1870s and the early twentieth century constituted a sustained effort to chart, classify, and comprehend a landscape that, by the mid-nineteenth century, remained largely unrecorded in Western geographical literature. From the forested Siamese-Lao frontier to the mountainous interior of Perak, from the river systems of Pahang to the highlands of Aceh, a succession of individuals—government surveyors, Catholic missionaries, university scientists, and independent adventurers—produced the first systematic accounts of terrain, population, geology, and indigenous culture across the region. Their work was driven by overlapping imperatives: the administrative needs of the British protectorate system established after the Pangkor Engagement of 1874, the scientific ambitions of metropolitan institutions, the commercial interests of mining and plantation capital, and the missionary and colonial projects of rival European powers. The cumulative effect was the progressive transformation of what one contemporary observer described as a "closed volume" into a mapped, administered, and increasingly exploited territory [[1, p. 15]](#ref-1).

## Scope and Historical Definition

The term "explorer" in the context of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo encompasses a diverse cast of figures whose motivations and methods varied considerably. At one end of the spectrum stood the government surveyor, whose primary task was the determination of boundaries and the compilation of topographical maps for colonial administration. D. D. Daly, appointed surveyor for the Native States of the Straits Settlements in May 1875 by Governor Sir Andrew Clarke, exemplifies this category. His surveys of Selangor, Moar, Pahang, Sungai Ujong, and Perak between 1875 and 1877 were conducted with prismatic compasses, sextants, and base-line measurements, and were explicitly framed as instruments of territorial governance [[1, pp. 2]](#ref-1). The political context was urgent: the Pangkor Engagement of 1874 had initiated British intervention in the internal affairs of the Malay states, and the new protectorate system required precise knowledge of boundaries that had previously been defined only by vague Malay phrases such as "as far as the sound of a gunshot may be heard from this particular hill" [[1, p. 6]](#ref-1).

At the other end of the spectrum stood the independent traveller or missionary, whose primary purpose was not administrative but personal, religious, or scientific. G. Dabin, a French Catholic missionary of the Missions Étrangères de Paris, undertook a 48-day overland journey from Bangkok to Ubon in February–March 1884 to replace an invalided confrère at the Catholic station on the Seimoun [[2, pp. 103–104]](#ref-2). His account, published in 1885, is a day-by-day journal of material hardship—broken wheels, missing bullocks, the search for water—framed within the broader project of French missionary penetration into what the introduction characterises as "savage tribes" [[2, p. 103]](#ref-2). Similarly, Xavier Brau de Saint-Pol Lias, a French explorer and sometime diplomat, travelled to Aceh in the early 1880s aboard Dutch naval vessels, accompanying the mining engineer John Errington de la Croix, and produced a vivid ethnographic and military narrative of the west coast of Aceh and the interior Gayo highlands [[3, pp. 205–206]](#ref-3).

A third category comprised the university-led scientific expedition, of which the Cambridge University Expedition to the North-Eastern Malay States and Upper Perak of 1899–1900 is the most substantial example in the available records. Conceived by W. W. Skeat during his service in the Selangor and Perak Civil Services, the venture was modelled on the Haddon Expedition to New Guinea and the Torres Strait, and was funded largely by the Worts Fund of the University of Cambridge [[4, pp. 5–6]](#ref-4). The scientific party comprised Skeat (leader), Nelson Annandale (zoologist), Richard Evans (morphologist), D. T. Gwynne-Vaughan (botanist), F. F. Laidlaw (medical practitioner and naturalist), and R. H. Yapp (botanist), accompanied by nine Malays and a Javanese servant [[4, p. 6]](#ref-4). The expedition's significance lay in the extraordinary breadth of ethnographic, zoological, and botanical material gathered from a region then under Siamese suzerainty and largely unexplored by Western science [[4, p. 1]](#ref-4).

## Administrative and Geographical Frameworks

The administrative impetus for exploration was inseparable from the establishment of the British protectorate system. When Sir Andrew Clarke arrived as Governor of the Straits Settlements in 1873, he found that the Geographical Society in London possessed "absolutely no such information of the least value" in its archives regarding the peninsula, and that the local office was equally deficient [[1, p. 16]](#ref-1). This vacuum of geographical knowledge was both a practical obstacle to governance and a source of anxiety: the boundaries between the Native States, the Siamese tributary territories, and the British settlements were vaguely defined, and the interior was, in the common impression of the time, "a malarious marsh" on one half and "a vast jungle inhabited by wild beasts" on the other [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1).

Daly's surveys addressed this deficit directly. In Selangor, he fixed the positions of the principal tin mines—Kanching, Ulu Selangor, Ulu Bernam, Ulu Gombah, Ulu Klang, Ulu Langat, Sungie Puteh, Recko, Kajang, and intermediate places—none of which had been previously charted [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). In Moar, he encountered active resistance: at Gumatie, he was surrounded by approximately 30 armed Malays who took him prisoner and threatened his life, and his Chinese servants were detained and starved for two days [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). The Dato of Moar expressed the local suspicion in a memorable phrase: "If we let the needle in, the thread is sure to follow," meaning that if an Englishman was allowed to enter their country, British annexation would be the natural sequence [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). In Pahang, Daly was received hospitably by the Bandaharah, who stated that he was the first white man to have visited the country from the interior [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1). The Perak–Kedah boundary survey of 1877 involved a party of 42 men and seven elephants lent by the Sultan of Kedah, and included the measurement of a four-mile base line at Kwala Kupang to fix the position of Gunung Inas and the Titi Wangsa range [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1).

The geographical knowledge produced by these surveys was considerable but incomplete. Daly's map, accompanying his 1882 paper to the Royal Geographical Society, showed substantial areas of the interior as unexplored [[1, p. 1]](#ref-1). He noted that "more than half" of the Malayan Peninsula remained unexplored, of which only the coast-line was known, and that of the internal government, geography, mineral products, and geology of these regions "we do not know anything" [[1, p. 15]](#ref-1). This assessment was reinforced by the remarks of Sir Andrew Clarke in the recorded discussion, who stated that the peninsula comprised approximately 33,000 square miles with only one acre in every 300 cultivated, and a density of five to six Malays per square mile, compared with 125 per square mile in the British settlements [[1, p. 17]](#ref-1).

## Scientific and Ethnographic Contributions

The scientific yield of the exploratory expeditions was substantial and multi-disciplinary. The Cambridge University Expedition of 1899–1900 collected material that yielded over twenty published zoological papers, including new species of mammals (*Rattus inas*, *Rattus ciliatus*), reptiles (*Tropidonotus inas*, *Trimeresurus popeorum*), amphibians (*Rana larutensis*), birds (sixteen species from Gunung Inas alone), and invertebrates, as well as botanical specimens from which new species were later described [[4, pp. 171–173]](#ref-4). Skeat's ethnographic observations ranged across burial customs (Siamese tree-burials, Phram sitting-burials, Malay Muslim rites), wedding and circumcision ceremonies, spirit-halls and trance performances, the manufacture of the *bunga mas* tribute, salt-pan operations, pottery, rice cultivation, and the folklore of vampires, giants, and cave-princesses [[4, pp. 1–147]](#ref-4). The *bunga mas* tribute from Patani, which Skeat documented in detail, consisted of a gold tree and a silver tree, each at least six feet tall with stand, requiring two months to complete; the combined value was estimated at over $5,000, with the Jalor contribution alone at $1,500 [[4, pp. 77–78]](#ref-4).

The Malayan Nature Society's year-long expedition to the Endau-Rompin area in southern Peninsular Malaysia, reported by H. S. Barlow in 1985, represents a later phase of exploration in which the primary objective shifted from initial charting to ecological inventory and conservation advocacy. Barlow framed the expedition within a long history of exploration of the Endau River catchment, beginning with D. F. A. Hervey's 1881 account and H. W. Lake's 1894 journey to the source of the Endau [[5, p. 135]](#ref-5). The scientific work encompassed botanical, entomological, and zoological surveys along the Endau and Jasin Rivers, including a 36-kilometre traverse by Tunku Mohd. Nazim Yaacob from Bekok to Kampong Jasin [[5, p. 138]](#ref-5). The expedition reported on vegetational zonation along the Janing Barat Ridge, the discovery of the endemic climbing bamboo *Racemobambos setifera*, and the first peninsular records of several moth species previously known only from Sumatra and Borneo [[5, pp. 138–142]](#ref-5). A nine-storey tree tower reaching 103 feet above the ground was erected in a giant forest *keruing* by the Malaysian Armed Forces for microclimate studies [[5, p. 138]](#ref-5).

The ethnographic contributions of the earlier explorers were equally significant, though framed within the assumptions of their respective eras. Brau de Saint-Pol Lias recorded the "Twelve Colonies" tradition, which holds that twelve Malay boats from the Kingdom of Menangkebow (Padang) founded settlements along the Aceh coast before the thirteenth century, and that their descendants at Tampat-Touan maintained racial purity, disowning any man who married an Acehnese woman [[3, p. 213]](#ref-3). He also recorded the first reference to the "empire of the Karo," a region south of the Gayo between the Alas and the Batak, provided by a Gayo man named Si-Maoulout to the Dutch Controller [[3, p. 211]](#ref-3). Daly compiled a vocabulary of the language of the Orang Semang in Perak, and noted their physical characteristics—thick lips, skin nearly black, nose with large nostrils, prominent upper lip, hair short and curly, with a curly top-knot—comparing them to the Papuans of New Guinea whom he had seen in Torres Straits [[1, p. 15]](#ref-1).

## The Transition from Exploration to Conservation

The final phase of exploration documented in the available records marks a fundamental shift in purpose and methodology. By the 1980s, the primary concern was no longer the charting of unknown territory but the protection of what remained of the primary forest. Barlow's 1985 report on the Endau-Rompin expedition explicitly argued that the area constituted the last extensive tract of lowland primary forest in the south of the peninsula and warranted urgent protection [[5, p. 135]](#ref-5). The political context was the progressive erosion of wildlife reserves established by Sultan Ibrahim of Johore in the 1930s—the Endau-Kluang Wildlife Reserve in 1933 and the Segamat Wildlife Sanctuary in 1937—through development pressure in the 1950s through 1970s, compounded by the failure of the 1980 National Parks Act (Act No. 226) to be implemented due to state governments' reluctance to cede land to federal park designation [[5, pp. 135]](#ref-5). The public outcry generated by a half-page advertisement in the *New Straits Times* of 7 May 1977 was presented as a turning point that demonstrated Malaysian public commitment to conservation [[5, p. 138]](#ref-5). The expedition was extended from six months to a full year, with a further six-month extension to May 1986, to allow a more thorough survey and inventory of the area [[5, p. 142]](#ref-5).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature documents the history of exploration in the region across a span of nearly a century, with shifting priorities reflecting the broader transition from imperial expansion to scientific inquiry and, ultimately, to conservation. The earliest records in the available corpus—Dabin's 1885 journal and Daly's 1882 paper to the Royal Geographical Society—belong to the period of initial British administrative intervention and French missionary and colonial penetration. Both are characterised by a practical, often unselfconscious tone: Dabin records broken wheels and the search for water; Daly records compass bearings and base-line measurements. Both, however, are framed within explicit civilising narratives. Dabin's introduction characterises the Laotian peoples as "savage tribes" requiring conversion [[2, p. 103]](#ref-2), while Daly's paper, and the remarks of Clarke and Adamson in the recorded discussion, explicitly frame British intervention as a developmental and civilising project [[1, pp. 16–18]](#ref-1).

The Cambridge University Expedition of 1899–1900, published in 1953, represents a more self-consciously scientific phase. Skeat's motivation was explicitly the fear that contact with outside cultures was rapidly disintegrating indigenous traditions, and the expedition was designed as a rescue operation for ethnographic knowledge [[4, pp. 5–6]](#ref-4). The volume's historiographical contribution lies in providing the only contemporary, multi-disciplinary account of the Patani States, Kelantan, Trengganu, Kedah, and Upper Perak from the field, predating the administrative reorganisation that followed the 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty [[4, p. 1]](#ref-4). The primary archival basis consists of Skeat's field diaries and notes, completed as a manuscript in 1952 and abridged by Sir Richard Winstedt, Laidlaw's personal narrative, and the scientific publications drawn largely from the *Proceedings of the Zoological Society of London* and the Cambridge University collections [[4, p. 174]](#ref-4).

The later records—Barlow's 1985 expedition report and the 2020 publication of Brau de Saint-Pol Lias's Aceh account—illustrate the Society's ongoing role in making historical exploration literature available to contemporary readers. The Aceh paper, originally presented to the Société de Géographie in June 1883, was translated by Colin Dyer and published with an editor's note situating it alongside two other French visitors to the region in the early 1880s: John Errington de la Croix, engaged in tin mining in Perak, and Jacques de Morgan, a mining engineer seeking a concession [[3, p. 205]](#ref-3). The editor's note to Daly's paper similarly situates it alongside de Morgan's 1884 explorations in Perak, establishing a small corpus of near-contemporary European accounts of the peninsular interior at the moment of British administrative transition [[1, p. 1]](#ref-1). The 2020 English translation of de Morgan's explorations, published by the Society, further extends this corpus [[1, p. 1]](#ref-1).

The obituary of John Waterstradt (1869–1944) by H. S. Barlow, published in 1969, commemorates a figure associated with Malaya whose contributions warranted recognition by the Society, though the full body of the obituary is not included in the available records [[6]](#ref-6). The existence of such a substantial fifteen-page treatment suggests that Waterstradt's work occupied a significant place in the Society's understanding of the region's exploratory history, even if the specific details of his contributions cannot be assessed from the available evidence.
