# Pulai: memories of a gold mining settlement in Ulu Kelantan

Sharon A. Carstens (1980) reconstructs the history of Pulai, a Hakka Chinese gold mining settlement in Ulu Kelantan, from the late eighteenth century to 1940, arguing that the community's survival as a distinct, isolated settlement was determined by its transition from gold mining to subsistence rice farming and the resulting self-sufficiency in food and marriage partners. The article addresses the methodological challenge of reconstructing a community's past when written sources are sparse, compiled by outsiders, and often based on second-hand information, while oral traditions are fragmented, serve multiple social functions, and resist straightforward chronological ordering.

## Summary

Carstens begins by surveying the limited written record of Chinese gold mining in Kelantan, drawing on Chinese travellers' accounts (notably Hsieh Ching-Kao's late eighteenth-century description), nineteenth-century British and Siamese administrative reports, and colonial-era observations by Waterstradt (1902), Graham (1908), and Middlebrook (1933). These sources establish the broader political and economic context: small groups of Chinese miners working alluvial gold in scattered locations, dependent on outside rice supplies and vulnerable to the taxation and political machinations of local rulers. The written record documents two critical episodes of conflict between Pulai miners and the Kelantan Sultans in the early nineteenth century, including a massacre around 1825 that Graham described in lurid detail, after which the settlement gradually recovered and its inhabitants shifted toward rice cultivation.

The bulk of the article, however, is built on oral histories collected during Carstens' year of residence in Pulai (February 1978 to February 1979). She is candid about the limitations of this material: stories vary with tellers, some are recited for didactic purposes, others serve as tools of continuing family rivalries, and the dispersed nature of the settlement meant that knowledge was not systematically transmitted across generations. Nevertheless, personal reminiscences of elderly residents about their own lives seventy or more years ago provide the most reliable window into the past. From these accounts, Carstens constructs a picture of a loosely structured, egalitarian community in which social differentiation derived not from wealth or lineage but from specialized knowledge and skills—medical practitioners, shamans, musicians, and martial arts instructors each occupied recognized but non-hierarchical roles.

The article's central analytical contribution lies in explaining why Pulai remained culturally Chinese while other early Chinese settlements in Kelantan and Trengganu underwent significant acculturation. Carstens argues that the near-total absence of Malay residents in the Pulai area meant that the typical markers of acculturation—adoption of Malay housing styles, food customs, clothing, and language—simply did not apply. Instead, adaptations were driven by the natural environment: jungle products supplemented the diet, blow-pipes were adopted from the Orang Asli for hunting, and the scarcity of women led to intermarriage with Orang Asli and Siamese women, though these women were themselves assimilated into the Hakka Chinese culture of the settlement.

### Key Findings

- By 1902, Waterstradt recorded a Pulai population of only 200, many of whom had already switched to rice farming; by Middlebrook's 1933 visit, the population had recovered to between 700 and 800 Hakka-speaking Chinese (pp. 53–54).
- The massacre of Pulai miners under Sultan Muhammad I, probably around 1825, was triggered when the miners killed the Temangong (the Sultan's son) who had enforced a rice monopoly and blocked supply boats up the river; Graham reported that "the river ran red with blood" and the gold mining industry of Kelantan "came to a sudden end" (p. 52).
- R.W. Duff acquired a mineral and development concession covering almost one quarter of Kelantan in 1900, and gold dredging operations were established in the Galas River near Pulai, but these ceased by 1915 due to diminishing returns with no discernible impact on the Pulai community (p. 53).
- Interviews with 80 of 100 Pulai households revealed that approximately 80 percent of families had been in Pulai for four to six generations, and 90 percent of those interviewed identified their forefathers as Hakka (p. 56).
- The Pulai Capitan position was first noted by Clifford in 1895, though Chinese Capitans had been appointed by the Kelantan Sultans as early as 1823; by 1933 the Capitan no longer resided in Pulai and appeared to have little authority outside his own family (pp. 54, 61).
- The conflict with the Pang clan, the most frequently told story in Pulai, occurred approximately 150 years before Carstens' fieldwork (i.e., c. 1830s) and involved several hundred cooperative miners who were forced out of the area after a period of bullying and fighting; the remnant of a cannon from this battle is still worshipped at the annual Kuan Yin temple festival (pp. 57–58).

### Conclusion

Carstens concludes that Pulai's survival as a distinct community to the present day was a function of increasing self-sufficiency. As long as the miners depended on outside rice supplies and marriage partners, they were forced to maintain relations with other groups and remain vulnerable to political interference. Once the community became self-sufficient in both food and women, there was little reason or incentive to travel outside, and the geographical isolation of the Ulu Kelantan interior reinforced this withdrawal. The Pulai people themselves frame this history in terms of *fu ch'i* (good fortune), viewing their ancestors not as lazy or self-indulgent but as destined by a lack of fortune to remain poor—a narrative that simultaneously acknowledges hardship and preserves community pride.

## Context

- Fieldwork was conducted in Pulai from December 1977 to February 1979 under a Fulbright-Hays Doctoral Dissertation Grant; the article draws on informal conversations with residents rather than formal interviews, supplemented by a review of colonial-era written sources (pp. 50, 55).
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the study of overseas Chinese communities in Malaysia by documenting a case that defies the typical pattern of acculturation observed in Trengganu and northern Kelantan, and by demonstrating the value of oral history as a complement to the sparse written record for understanding the social and cultural life of isolated Chinese settlements.
