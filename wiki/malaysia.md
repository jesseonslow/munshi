# Malaysia

Malaysia, spanning the southern Malay Peninsula and the northern coast of Borneo, has been a subject of sustained scholarly investigation across political, economic, and cultural domains. The Society's literature reveals a country whose modern identity has been shaped by contested narratives of sovereignty, state-engineered commemoration, and the economic restructuring that followed independence. From the tributary relations that bound northern Malay states to Siam through the nineteenth century, to the post-independence construction of national memory through museums and memorials, the archival record documents a nation continuously negotiating the meaning of its own past.

### National Memory and Commemorative Spaces

The construction of national memory in Malaysia has been a central preoccupation of the Society's literature, with scholars examining how the state selectively foregrounds certain historical episodes while marginalising others. Abu Talib Ahmad's systematic study of state museums across seven states—Kelantan, Terengganu, Kedah, Penang, Selangor, Kuala Lumpur, and Sabah—demonstrates that these institutions function as instruments of nation-building that project an official version of history, favouring Malay achievement and the narrative of the victors in the nationalist struggle [[1, pp. 45–46]](#ref-1). His analysis of the Kedah State Museum, for instance, reveals a list of eight individuals who achieved "a first" in the country, all of whom are Malay, while the contributions of Chinese and Indian communities to the state's economic development receive only passing mention [[1, p. 51]](#ref-1). The museum's treatment of the Japanese Occupation is similarly selective, containing only three original exhibits and a narrative of four paragraphs that emphasises political awakening while omitting the suffering of the local population [[1, p. 61]](#ref-1).

The Tun Abdul Razak Memorial Museum, opened in 1982 at the former official residence Sri Taman in Kuala Lumpur, exemplifies what Ahmad terms "state-engineered nationalism," systematically foregrounding the second Prime Minister as "father of development" while omitting his membership in the British Labour Party and Fabian Society, the role of leftist intellectuals in formulating the New Economic Policy, and the University and University College Act of 1971 [[2, pp. 1–32]](#ref-2). The memorial's primary audience is schoolchildren on organised tours, for whom the experience largely replicates the school history textbook [[2]](#ref-2).

The politics of memory extends to the treatment of political violence. POR Heong Hong's examination of the mass burial site for victims of the May 13, 1969 riot, located within the Sungai Buloh Leprosarium, reveals how the management of dead bodies and the siting of the burial ground functioned as instruments of state control over collective remembrance [[3, pp. 35–54]](#ref-3). The National Operations Council report records 196 deaths, yet its burial accounting totals only 129, leaving the disposition of the remaining bodies unexplained [[3, pp. 39]](#ref-3). Bodies were buried naked in four-foot-deep trenches pre-dug by leprosy patients, with no coffins or wrapping, making later exhumation practically impossible [[3, pp. 41–42]](#ref-3). Of the 103 tombstones at the site, 18 belong to unidentified persons, and only approximately 27 show traces of family visitation [[3, pp. 41]](#ref-3). William Roff has noted that the terms "art" and "artist" are entirely absent from the broader literature on Malaysian history, and that colonial Malaya saw no attempt to confront threats to identity through public art, with only modest expressions of shared national identity emerging in the early post-independence period [[4, pp. 99–100]](#ref-4).

### Economic Transformation and the New Economic Policy

The economic restructuring that followed independence, particularly the New Economic Policy implemented from 1971, has been a significant focus of the Society's literature. The NEP raised the Malay economic stake from approximately 3 per cent to 17–18 per cent by the 1980s, while simultaneously fostering crony capitalism and a new business elite [[2, p. 21]](#ref-2). FELDA, the signature rural development project of Tun Abdul Razak's administration, opened 145,000 acres of virgin jungle for agricultural settlement in its first ten years, predominantly for Malay rural poor [[2, p. 20]](#ref-2).

The process of "Malaysianisation"—the transfer of economic control from foreign to domestic hands—has been examined through the case of the Barlow Boustead Estates Agency, a joint venture between the Barlow and Boustead families. Rob Glew and Chander Velu document how the investment climate became progressively less attractive to foreign investors under the NEP, ultimately resulting in nationalisation "by default" [[5, p. 65]](#ref-5). The case illustrates divergent strategies among shareholders: some pursued divestment and capital remittance, while others maintained confidence in the government's stewardship and sought to grow their holdings at discounted valuations [[5]](#ref-5). BB EA accounts show dividend growth from M$600,000 in 1974 to M$2,572,000 in 1980, alongside a 50 per cent increase in asset value over the same period [[5, p. 65]](#ref-5).

### Historical Sovereignty and Tributary Relations

The Society's literature also illuminates the long history of personalised relations between Malay rulers and their external overlords.
