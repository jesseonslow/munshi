# Further notes on the rainfall of Singapore

Roland Braddell's "Notes on Ancient Times in Malaya" (Part II), published in 1947 in the Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, continues his investigation into the prehistoric and ancient connections between the Malay Peninsula and the wider Indian Ocean world. The central thesis of this section is that the ancient beads recovered at Kota Tinggi and Johore Lama in Johore were not locally manufactured but were importations carried by Sabaean (South Arabian) seafarers who dominated Indian Ocean trade from before the Christian era through the early centuries A.D.

## Summary

Braddell takes up the bead evidence gathered by G. B. Gardner at Kota Tinggi and Johore Lama—some 600 or more specimens—and subjects it to careful typological and comparative analysis. He corrects a misattribution by Dr. Quaritch Wales, who had lumped a distinctive black glass "eye-bead" found in Johore with the generic "Roman" beads; Braddell demonstrates, by reference to Seligman and Beck's publication in the *Bulletin of the Museum of Far Eastern Antiquities*, that this bead is in fact a pre-Christian type (6th–7th century B.C.) identical to examples from Cumae and Pozzuoli. Together with a Hittite bead and two Phoenician or Cypriot beads also in Gardner's collection, this gives four pre-Christian specimens out of roughly 600, a ratio Braddell finds remarkable. He further notes 80 early Indian stone beads, which he argues must predate the glass bead trade, implying an earlier Indian commercial link to Johore that was later superseded by Western glass bead imports.

Having established that the beads were almost certainly importations, Braddell turns to the question of their carriers. He dismisses the Phoenician hypothesis for want of evidence, noting that the Ophir debate remains unresolved and that the Phoenicians, while capable of ocean voyaging, left no trace in Malaysian waters. He then advances the Sabaean hypothesis with considerably more force. Drawing on the authoritative synthesis by Steiger, Otley Beyer and Benitez, he shows that South Arabian (Sabaean) ships were the dominant carriers in the Indian Ocean from at least the first century B.C., that they had reached South China by the first century A.D., and that the monsoon sailing routes made extension from India to the Strait of Malacca and the Malay Peninsula a natural and gradual progression. He reinforces this with toponymic evidence—Sabah, Sabak, Sabam (Kundur Island), and Ptolemy's Sabara, Sabarakos Gulf, Sabana, and Zabae—and with Chinese records of an Arab counting-house at Canton by 300 A.D.

The final section provides a compressed survey of ancient South Arabian history to contextualize the Sabaean maritime enterprise. Braddell traces the succession of the Minaean, Sabaean, and Himyarite kingdoms, the geography of the Hadramaut frankincense trade, the great Ma'rib dam, and the eventual Roman encroachment on the Red Sea and Indian Ocean routes that undermined Sabaean commercial supremacy. He notes the Bugis royal tradition claiming descent from Queen Bilkis of Sheba as a matter of interest but explicitly disclaims any theoretical weight for it.

### Key Findings

- Gardner's collection at Kota Tinggi and Johore Lama comprised approximately 600 beads, of which 4 were pre-Christian (one Hittite, two Phoenician/Cypriot, one "eye-bead" of 6th–7th century B.C. type) and 80 were early Indian stone beads (pp. 61–63).
- The Johore "eye-bead" is typologically identical to a specimen from Cumae illustrated by Seligman and Beck, with comparable examples in the British Museum dating to the 6th or 7th centuries B.C. (p. 62).
- Quaritch Wales' excavations at Kota Tinggi yielded blue and white Ming porcelain and stamped pottery throughout the deposits, complicating the dating of the associated Kuala Selinsing-type beads (p. 61).
- Chinese records document an Arab counting-house at Canton by 300 A.D., and Cary and Warmington place Sabaean contact with China in the first century A.D.; Ptolemy's geography (c. 150 A.D.) was based on the sailing directions of a Phoenician captain, Marinas, active c. 75 A.D. (pp. 67–68).
- The Minaean kingdom flourished c. 1300–650 B.C.; the first Sabaean period is dated 950–650 B.C.; the second Sabaean period 650–115 B.C.; the Himyarite kingdom 115 B.C.–c. 300 A.D. (first period) and to c. 525 A.D. (second period); the Ma'rib dam failed between 542 and 570 A.D. (pp. 71–73).
- In the first century B.C., caravans of two to three thousand camels (extending some twenty miles on the march) were arriving in the southern Mediterranean, illustrating the scale of the incense trade (p. 71).

### Conclusion

Braddell's definitive takeaway is that the Johore beads constitute evidence of a sustained bead trade into the Malay Peninsula from before the Christian era, continuing for at least two centuries after it, and that the most probable carriers were Sabaean ships operating the monsoon routes from South Arabia through India to the Strait of Malacca. He frames this not as a proven theory but as a reasoned suggestion, emphasizing that the ancient history of Malaya cannot be told in isolation but only as one segment of the long sea-routes stretching from the Mediterranean to China.

## Context

- The article draws on the bead collections of G. B. Gardner (Johore), H. C. Beck (Sarawak), and the excavations of Dr. Quaritch Wales (Kota Tinggi), as well as the published analyses of Seligman and Beck in the *Bulletin of the Museum of Far Eastern Antiquities* (Stockholm).
- Historiographically, the piece represents Braddell's attempt to integrate South Arabian, Indian, and Chinese source traditions into a unified account of prehistoric maritime connections, a approach that was relatively novel in Malayan historiography of the 1940s and which anticipated later work on Indian Ocean trade networks.
