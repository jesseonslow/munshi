# Journal kept during a journey across the Malay Peninsula

F.A. Swettenham's 1885 journal records his sea-to-sea crossing of the Malay Peninsula from the Bernam River to the Pahang River—a route of 402 miles traversing the main mountain range—undertaken in April–May 1885 to survey a proposed trunk road through Perak and to assess the commercial and administrative potential of Pahang. The account functions simultaneously as a travel narrative, a geographical survey, and a colonial policy memorandum.

## Summary

The journal documents a multi-modal journey: up the Bernam River by boat to Kuala Slim, overland across the main range (reaching 3,150 feet at the Perak–Pahang boundary), then down the Lipis and Pahang Rivers by raft and boat to the Sultan's residence at Pekan. Swettenham's stated purpose was to examine the trace of a trunk road, but the account expands into a comprehensive survey of Pahang's governance, economy, and physical geography. He records in detail the tax systems (hasil banchi, serah, forced labour, debt-slavery), the near-absence of Chinese settlers, the Sultan's ill health, and the political tension between the Yam Tuan and his brother the Raja Muda, whose recent attempt to enter the country had unsettled the upper districts. Topographical observations—geology of the main range, river widths, elevations, the hydrology of the Pahang basin, and the geology of the Kota Kelanggi caves—run throughout.

The narrative is framed by Swettenham's colonial administrative concerns. He repeatedly contrasts Pahang's governance with the protected states of the west coast, and closes with a concrete infrastructure proposal: a 130-mile road from Johor Bahru to Pekan, estimated at under $150,000, that would link Singapore's resources directly to Pahang's interior. The journal thus serves as both a field report on the trunk road and an argument for opening Pahang to British commercial penetration.

### Key Findings

- Total journey distance: 402 miles from Kuala Bernam to Kuala Pahang; the straight-line distance is only 170 miles (p. 35).
- The Perak–Pahang boundary at the main range summit: 3,150 feet above sea level; the gap 2,854 feet above Kuala Geliting (p. 4).
- Pahang's Chinese population: approximately 80 at Pekan and no more than 200–300 in the entire state, attributed to intolerable taxation and arbitrary seizure of goods by chiefs (pp. 31–32).
- Taxation system documented: hasil banchi of $1.33 per head annually; the "serah" (compulsory purchase at a named price); a $3 tax on every exported buffalo; a gambling farm paying $50 per month to Toh Kaya; a one-tenth tax on imported cloth (pp. 7, 11).
- The Yam Tuan's principal house at Pekan cost $25,000 to build (p. 31).
- Proposed road from Johor Bahru to Pekan: 130 miles, estimated cost under $150,000 for a first-class bridle road, constructible in eighteen months (p. 36).
- Cholera among the party: at least three deaths (one Bernam coolie, one girl left at Batu Talam, one at Permatang Linggi) and at least three others sickened over a two-day period (pp. 2, 10, 12–13).

### Conclusion

Swettenham's definitive historical takeaway is that Pahang possesses "great resources and unusual capabilities" for supporting a large population, yet is held back by its own governing class, whose arbitrary exactions deter legitimate enterprise. He argues that the state's salvation lies not in internal reform under the existing regime but in external connectivity—specifically a road from Johor Bahru—that would bring Singapore's capital and markets to bear on Pahang's interior, while expressing doubt that the current rulers would be capable of utilising such opportunities for the best interests of the State.

## Context

- Swettenham was a senior British colonial administrator (later High Commissioner for the Federated Malay States); this journey was undertaken in his official capacity to survey a trunk road for the Perak government, accompanied by Captain Giles, R.A., and others.
- The journal's historiographical contribution lies in providing the earliest detailed European description of the Pahang interior's governance, economy, and topography, and in documenting the political tensions (the Raja Muda's attempted entry) that would later culminate in Pahang's acceptance of a British Resident in 1888.
