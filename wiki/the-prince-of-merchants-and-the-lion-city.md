# The prince of merchants and the Lion city

Nicholas Tarling's 1964 article examines the correspondence of John Palmer, the Calcutta merchant dubbed the "Prince of Merchants" by Governor-General Lord Hastings, to illuminate the British strategic and commercial calculus behind the acquisition of Singapore in 1819 and the broader Anglo-Dutch contest for the Straits of Malacca. Drawing on Palmer's letters held in the India Office Library, Tarling argues that these documents provide a distinctive Calcutta vantage point on the diplomatic and commercial negotiations that culminated in the Treaty of 1824, one less commonly exploited than the London, Bencoolen, or Batavia perspectives.

## Summary

The article centres on Palmer's role as a political intermediary between the Governor-General's Council in Calcutta and Dutch officials in India and Java. Tarling demonstrates that Palmer was not merely a passive conduit of information but held coherent strategic views: he was sceptical about the commercial potential of the East Indies yet deeply committed to securing the maritime route to China. His letters reveal a consistent preference for a British foothold at the western end of the Straits of Malacca—Malacca or a position near it—rather than at the eastern end, a view that placed him at odds with Raffles' vision for Singapore. Palmer's strategic logic was that a western station, combined with a firm footing at Aceh, would "hermetically seal" the Straits in war and secure British influence throughout the Archipelago in peace (p. 24).

Tarling traces Palmer's sustained diplomatic efforts to prevent Dutch retaliation against British encroachments, particularly Raffles' acquisition of Singapore. Palmer proposed various schemes of compensation and reciprocity—opium allotments at cost, duty reductions, territorial swaps—to secure Dutch acquiescence while preserving the essentials of the British position. He was personally invested in these negotiations as the Dutch commercial agent in British India, and his letters reveal the tension between his commercial self-interest and his political convictions. The article also illuminates internal British tensions between the Penang establishment (Farquhar, Bannerman) and Raffles, with Palmer navigating between these factions while trying to keep the mission on course.

The article closes with the Treaty of 1824, which largely achieved what Palmer had long advocated: Dutch withdrawal from the Straits of Malacca, British retention of Singapore, and reciprocal commercial arrangements. Palmer's single recorded comment on the treaty expressed satisfaction that trade privileges at Dutch ports made a British settlement on Sumatra unnecessary, but lamented the disregard for native engagements and the "contempt of the Feelings of the Natives" (p. 40).

### Key Findings

- In May 1818, Palmer proposed to the Dutch Commissioner J. A. van Braam that the Dutch transfer all their continental Indian factories to Britain in exchange for equality of customs duties, opium and saltpetre at actual cost, and permission for Dutch merchants to reside at former factory locations (p. 21).
- In April 1820, Palmer suggested the Dutch apply for 400 chests of opium yearly at cost and charges, in return for prohibiting foreign opium importation into Java and reducing duties on Bengal opium from 100 to 50 dollars per chest, with Singapore "chucked into the sale" as further compensation (p. 32).
- Palmer's strategic preference was consistently for a station at the western entrance of the Straits of Malacca, which he considered better positioned to guard the channel than Singapore at the eastern end, noting that Singapore "must owe its support and safety to Pinang, for it is too distant for our protection even if hostile positions did not intervene" (pp. 35–36).
- The Treaty of 17 March 1824 resulted in Dutch surrender of Singapore, their continental possessions, and Malacca, with reciprocal commercial treatment and a boundary line drawn down the Straits of Malacca (p. 40).
- Palmer sent his natural son, Claude Queiros, to Singapore in 1820 to open a commercial office, indicating his personal financial stake in the settlement's success and his willingness to "continue a steady Support" even at the hazard of its surrender to the Dutch (p. 33).
- Palmer's letter to Farquhar of 4 December 1818 articulated the overarching strategic logic: "with a strong position at Riau, and a firm footing at Aceh, the Straits would be hermetically sealed in war, and the whole influence in them and the contiguous Archipelago, in our hands in peace" (p. 24).

### Conclusion

Tarling's definitive takeaway is that Palmer, despite his commercial connections with the Dutch, maintained a consistent political position favouring British dominance of the Straits of Malacca and the security of the China trade route. His letters demonstrate that the acquisition of Singapore was not simply Raffles' personal venture but was embedded in a broader strategic framework that Palmer had been articulating from Calcutta since 1818. The Treaty of 1824 vindicated this framework, though Palmer's preferred solution—a western Straits station—was not fully realised, and his final comment on the treaty carried a note of moral unease about the treatment of native allies.

## Context

- Primary source base: Palmer's letters in the India Office Library (Eng. lett. series, vols. 87–102), supplemented by Dutch archival sources (van der Kemp, van Deventer) and the author's own earlier monograph, *Anglo-Dutch Rivalry in the Malay World, 1780–1824* (1962).
- Historiographical contribution: The article provides the Calcutta perspective on Anglo-Dutch negotiations, complementing the more commonly used London, Bencoolen, and Batavia viewpoints, and deepens understanding of the commercial and personal dimensions of diplomatic decision-making in the early colonial period.
