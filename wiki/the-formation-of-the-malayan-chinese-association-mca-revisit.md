# The formation of the Malayan Chinese Association (MCA) revisited

## Abstract

This article revisits the formation of the MCA, looking in particular at the roles played by Tan Cheng Lock and Lee Hau Shik (H. S. Lee). Their efforts to get such an organization off the ground in a Malayan Chinese society fragmented by language, political loyalties and economic interests highlight the communal and factional divisions they had to overcome, and explain how Tan Cheng Lock, a locally-born Peranakan who spoke no Chinese, became the body’s leader.

## Summary

Tan Miau Ing revisits the historiographical debate over who founded the Malayan Chinese Association (MCA), a question that has produced competing attributions to Tan Cheng Lock, H. S. Lee, High Commissioner Henry Gurney, and the Chinese members of the Federal Legislative Council. Drawing on personal correspondence, Colonial Office dispatches, and Chinese government archives, the article argues that the more consequential question is not who proposed the MCA but how the proposal was executed in a Chinese community fragmented by dialect, political loyalty, and economic interest. The formation process required bridging a deep divide between the Peranakan, English-educated faction (Yong Shook Lin, Khoo Teik Ee, Tan Siew Sin) and the China-born, Cantonese-speaking business and mining faction led by H. S. Lee, while also navigating the suspicions of UMNO and the colonial administration.

The article traces how the two factions in the Federal Legislative Council converged in late 1948, how Gurney's immediate endorsement at a dinner on 15 December 1948 catalysed action, and how Lee's extensive network of chambers of commerce and dialect associations provided the organizational infrastructure for rapid mobilization across the states. Primary sources—particularly Lee's speeches, Gurney's reports to London, and the correspondence between Tan, Lee, and Walter Fletcher—reveal that the MCA's success depended on a deliberate strategy of enlisting the backing of the *huiguan* and chamber establishment while avoiding antagonism toward Malay political leadership.

A central puzzle the article addresses is why Tan Cheng Lock, a Peranakan who spoke no Chinese and had not publicly supported the MCA's creation, was elected its first president. The answer lies in his unique position: his pre-war service in the Straits Settlements Legislative and Executive Councils gave him high visibility in colonial politics; his identity as a Straits Chinese placed him outside any dialect *bang*; and his complete absence of KMT connections made him acceptable to a Malaya-centred organization at a time when Chinese political loyalties to China were a source of anxiety for both the British and UMNO.

### Key Findings

- The Overseas Chinese Association (OCA), the MCA's precursor, was formally established in September 1943 in India with Tan Cheng Lock as president and Tan Siew Sin as secretary-treasurer; it became moribund within months due to disapproval from the Chinese Consul General in India and reluctance among non-Malayan Chinese to join a body dominated by Malayan members (pp. 107–109).
- H. S. Lee raised the idea of a Chinese political party to High Commissioner Henry Gurney at a dinner hosted by Yong Shook Lin on 15 December 1948; Gurney immediately agreed and urged the Chinese council members to proceed at once (pp. 114–115).
- The MCA inaugural meeting was held on 27 February 1949 at the Selangor Chinese Assembly Hall, attended by approximately 160 delegates representing all states except Kelantan (p. 117).
- State MCA branches were formed before the headquarters existed: MCA Perak on 13 February 1949, MCA Penang on 15 February 1949, while MCA Malacca—chaired by Tan Cheng Lock—was not established until 10 March 1949, after the headquarters was founded (p. 117).
- Tan Cheng Lock was officially elected first MCA president on 3 April 1949; he had initially declined the offer and had not publicly supported the MCA's formation, with O. H. Morris of the Colonial Office noting three days before the inaugural meeting that Tan "has not yet come out in support of the Malayan-Chinese Association" (pp. 111, 119).
- H. S. Lee was not elected president because representatives from Perak, Negeri Sembilan, and Johor opposed him; his KMT colonel rank, Guangdong *bang* affiliations, and limited influence outside Selangor made him unacceptable to other factions (pp. 118, 121).

### Conclusion

Tan Miau Ing's definitive takeaway is that the MCA's formation was a collective achievement whose success depended less on the identity of its originator than on the strategic mobilization of the Chinese association network and the careful selection of a president who could transcend factional divisions. Tan Cheng Lock's election was a compromise that reflected the organizational need for a figure unaffiliated with any dialect group or Chinese political party, capable of holding together a community whose loyalties were split between Malaya and China.

## Context

- **Archival basis:** The study draws on Colonial Office records (CO 537/3757, CO 537/4242, CO 537/4741, CO 537/4761, CO 825/35/15), the Tan Cheng Lock and H. S. Lee personal paper collections, the National Archives of India, and the Foreign Ministry Archives at Academia Historica Taiwan.
- **Historiographical contribution:** The article complicates the earlier attributions in Roff (1965), Tregonning (1979), and Heng (1988) by demonstrating that the MCA's formation was a multi-actor process and that Tan's presidency was a strategic compromise rather than a reflection of his role as founder.
