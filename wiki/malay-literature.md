# Malay literature

Malay literature encompasses the written and oral literary traditions of the Malay-speaking world, spanning the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Borneo, and the broader Nusantara archipelago. The surviving corpus of traditional Malay literature comprises approximately 5,000 manuscripts representing some 800 distinct titles, with the largest single collections held at Leiden University (c. 1,500 items) and the Jakarta Museum (c. 919 recorded in 1909) [[1, pp. 8–9]](#ref-1). The tradition ranges from the earliest extant written texts in "Malacca" Malay, composed in the fourteenth century, through the classical court chronicles and verse romances of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, to the modern novel and short story that emerged in the early twentieth century through the constitutive influence of translation from European, Chinese, Urdu, and Arabic sources [[2, pp. 91–93]](#ref-2), [[3, pp. 40–41]](#ref-3). Malay literature has served simultaneously as a vehicle for political legitimation, religious instruction, aesthetic pleasure, and social commentary, and its study has been shaped by colonial administrative needs, philological method, and the evolving demands of post-colonial national identity [[4, pp. 30–32]](#ref-4), [[1, pp. 1–2]](#ref-1).

### Scope and Historical Definition

The boundaries of Malay literature as a field of study have been contested since the discipline's institutionalisation in the nineteenth century. Ismail Hussein, in his 1966 Annual Lecture to the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, argued that a century of European scholarship had been "fundamentally non-literary in method—dominated by source-tracing, historical, and ethnological approaches," and that the field urgently required a structural, literary-scientific treatment grounded in the Nusantara cultural context rather than in the comparative frameworks of Greater India or Greater Arabia [[1, pp. 1–2]](#ref-1). Hussein traced the institutional engine of pre-war Malay literary studies to the Leiden University tradition, shaped by Hendrik Kern and Snouck Hurgronje, noting that this tradition was structurally modelled on the Greek-Latin twin-civilisation paradigm of Dutch classical education, which generated a persistent and misleading assumption that Malay culture could be understood through its Indian and Islamic "sources" rather than as an autonomous system [[1, pp. 3–11]](#ref-1).

The foundational text of the field, Richard Winstedt's *A History of Malay Literature* (1940), imposed a tripartite cultural schema—Shaman (primitive), Saiva (Hindu), Sufi (Islamic)—that compelled a compartmentalised reading emphasising borrowing at the expense of native creative ingenuity [[1, pp. 12–13]](#ref-1). Hussein's critique of Winstedt's treatment of the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* as a Javanese-inspired Panji derivative, rather than a literary creation in its own right, exemplified the broader failure to apply literary norms [[1, pp. 12–13]](#ref-1). Hussein insisted that Malay literature must be studied first within the Nusantara linguistic and cultural sphere—where motifs, forms, and literary practices recur across Sumatran, Javanese, Bornean, and Celebes traditions with characteristic modifications—before any comparative work with Indian, Persian, or Arabic literatures is attempted [[1, pp. 15–19]](#ref-1).

The earliest extant literature in "Malacca" Malay is the *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai*, a chronicle of roughly 30,000 words narrating the islamisation of the north Sumatran kingdom of Semudera-Pasai. A.H. Hill, in his 1960 revised edition, argued that the text was composed in at least three distinct phases between approximately 1330 and 1390, and that it preserves a hard core of historical fact within a semi-historical romance framework [[3, pp. 40–41]](#ref-3). The sole surviving manuscript was copied for Raffles in 1814 from an original belonging to Kiai Suvadimanggala, senior regent of Demak [[3, pp. 4]](#ref-3). Blagden noted in 1921 that no existing Malay manuscript is much older than the early seventeenth-century Bodleian manuscript from which the *Seri Rama* was printed in 1916 [[4, p. 33]](#ref-4).

### Poetry, Prose, and the Classical Canon

The classical Malay literary canon is dominated by two broad genres: the *hikayat* (prose narrative) and the *syair* (verse romance). Overbeck's 1934 study of Malay animal and flower *syair* demonstrated that these verse romances, featuring animals and flowers as protagonists, are not mere fables or erotic verse but veiled accounts of real human love-tragedies and court intrigues, composed in the Malay tradition of allusion to avoid naming real persons [[5, pp. 108–148]](#ref-5). Overbeck identified recurring narrative patterns across seventeen such poems: the dream that initiates the love, the go-between (often a bird or insect of lower rank), the use of love-charms (*ilmu ma'rifat*), the refusal or obstacle (difference in rank, an existing marriage, the lady's master), and the tragic or bittersweet resolution [[5, pp. 108–148]](#ref-5). He noted that the *syair* were often composed orally and transcribed carelessly by scribes, which accounts for the frequent incoherence, missing passages, and abrupt endings [[5, pp. 108–148]](#ref-5).

The *pantun*, a four-line quatrain form, has been a central puzzle in Malay poetics since 1868. Hussein identified its most promising explanatory key not in Malay cultural life but in the secret leave-language of the Batak and the magical recitation practices of the Javanese, demonstrating the necessity of a Nusantara-wide comparative framework [[1, pp. 19–20]](#ref-1). The Toraja literary tradition of Central Celebes, documented by Adriani and Kruyt in 1914, represents the most primitive Nusantara literary stage, in which recitation is a taboo-bound magical act tied to specific calendar periods, providing a developmental baseline for understanding the evolution toward the professional *penglipur lara* (oral storyteller) tradition and ultimately written literature [[1, pp. 17–18]](#ref-1).

In the prose domain, the *Sejarah Melayu* stands as the pre-eminent court chronicle. T. Iskandar's 1967 study established that the work was commissioned on 12 Rabi'ul-awal A.H. 1021 (13 May 1612) during the reign of Sultan 'Ala'ud-din Ri'ayat Shah of Aceh, while the Johore court was in captivity there, and was likely completed by 1615 [[6, pp. 38–42]](#ref-6). Iskandar challenged Winstedt's long-standing argument that Tun Bambang, rather than Tun Seri Lanang, was the true author, reconstructing a coherent reading in which the Bendahara—Tun Seri Lanang—receives the commission directly from the Raja di-Hilir [[6, pp. 38–42]](#ref-6). The work was conceived as a Johore political response to the Acehnese *Tajus-Salatin* of 1603, serving to vindicate the legitimacy of the Johore royal house at a moment of vulnerability to both Portuguese and Acehnese pressure [[6, pp. 38–42]](#ref-6).

G.L. Koster's 2005 study demonstrated how seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Malay historiographical texts constructed literary images of the Dutch VOC as a form of "damage control" to defend the Malay world order of *kerajaan* (sovereign authority) and Islam against the challenge posed by Dutch treaties and monopolies [[7, pp. 59–96]](#ref-7). In the *Hikayat Hang Tuah*, Johor's voluntary treaty with the Dutch (1639) is presented as a favour done to the impotent Dutch by mighty Johor, and the conquest of Malacca is attributed almost single-handedly to the Johorese nobleman Abdul Jamil [[7, pp. 59–96]](#ref-7). The *Syair Perang Mengkasar*, written in Malay rather than Macassarese, was explicitly intended as propaganda for the surrounding kingdoms, its narrator stating the poem was written "so that other kingdoms might hear what has happened" [[7, p. 17]](#ref-7).

### The Modern Turn: Translation, Reform, and the Novel

Holger Warnk's 2007 article argued that translations from European, Chinese, Urdu, and Arabic sources were a foundational but systematically overlooked force in the emergence of modern Malay literature between 1850 and 1950, serving as the vehicle through which the novel form, realistic prose style, and modern concepts of authorship entered both the Malay Peninsula and the Netherlands East Indies [[2, pp. 91–113]](#ref-2). Warnk challenged the prevailing historiographical narrative that modern Malay literature developed *ex nihilo* from traditional forms, arguing instead that translations were an active, constitutive element of literary modernity [[2, pp. 91–113]](#ref-2). The first two modern Malay novels—Muhammad bin Muhammad Said's *Ceritera Kecurian Lima Million Ringgit* (1922, Kota Bharu) and Syed Syeikh al-Hadi's *Hikayat Faridah Hanom*—were both perceived by contemporary readers as translations, though their source texts have never been identified [[2, p. 101]](#ref-2).

The institutional infrastructure for modern Malay prose was built upon translated models. The Sultan Idris Training College's Malay Translation Bureau, established in 1924, produced at least 48 titles in its Malay School Series (1924–1936) and 64 titles in its Malay Home Library Series (abandoned 1957), with print runs of 5,000 to 10,000 copies per title [[2, pp. 102–103]](#ref-2). Syed Mahmud b. Syed Abdulkadir al-Hindi (1865–1913) authored the first Malay–Arabic dictionary and produced numerous schoolbook translations from English that remained in use in government schools for over twenty years [[2, p. 96]](#ref-2). The *Syair Indera Sebaha*, a Malay adaptation of the Urdu musical comedy *Inder Sabha*, saw six editions between 1889 and 1901; its publisher Haji Muhammad Siraj registered the work with colonial authorities and threatened legal action against unauthorised copyists, representing an early assertion of copyright in the Malay Peninsula [[2, pp. 97–99]](#ref-2).

Virginia Matheson Hooker's 1994 study of two early Malay novels—*Faridah Hanom* (1925–26) by Sayyid Shaikh al-Hadi and *Hikayat Percintaan Kasih Kemudaan* (1927) by Ahmad Kotot—demonstrated how Egyptian reformist Islamic thought, particularly the teachings of Muhammad 'Abduh and the journal *al-Manar*, was transmitted into Malay literary culture through the narrative device of the love story [[8, pp. 93–118]](#ref-8). Hooker argued that both novels function as vehicles for a specifically social (rather than doctrinal) reformism, using the moral authority of their female protagonists to articulate a system of values—loyalty (*setia*), duty (*wajib*), and humanism (*kemanusiaan*)—that positioned Islam as the foundation for modernisation without Western secularism [[8, pp. 93–118]](#ref-8). The novel *Faridah Hanom* is set in Cairo in 1894 and references real historical figures including Muhammad 'Abduh, the journal *al-Manar*, and Egyptian feminists such as Huda Sha'arawi [[8, pp. 96–97]](#ref-8). The fatwa that resolves Faridah's marriage impediment is based on the principle of *maslaha* (welfare/utility), which Kerr identifies as "the most liberal principle of legal interpretation in traditional currency" [[8, p. 102]](#ref-8).

### Texts, Translations, and the Manuscript Tradition

The material conditions of the Malay manuscript tradition have profoundly shaped the field's evidential base. Ian Proudfoot's 2003 article examined an 1821 manuscript-collecting expedition dispatched by the Dutch colonial administration in Malacca, arguing that Malay philology has been fundamentally shaped by the political interests of its patrons and the material fragility of the manuscript tradition it studies [[9, pp. 1–53]](#ref-9). The expedition's agent, Abdul Kadir, was authorised on 3 April 1821 with a budget of $500 and a list of 70 titles to collect manuscripts in Riau, Lingga, and Terengganu [[9, pp. 7]](#ref-9). In Terengganu, a religious fatwa condemning royal romances had created a peculiar buyers' market, as owners disposed of texts that could no longer be read in company [[9, pp. 8]](#ref-9). Of the approximately 30–34 books collected, only one manuscript is known to have survived to the present: a copy of *Gangga Sakti* (identified as *Kumala Bahrain*) now in the National Library of Indonesia [[9, pp. 27]](#ref-9). Proudfoot used this case to make a larger epistemological argument: the surviving corpus of Malay manuscripts is so heavily filtered through nineteenth-century European collecting that our vision of the Malay past is "markedly foreshortened" [[9, pp. 1–53]](#ref-9).

The Leiden University Library's Malay manuscript holdings grew from 7 items in c. 1850 to 107 in 1864, 401 in 1899, and 1,168 in 1921, reflecting the intensive collection drive of the nineteenth century [[1, p. 5]](#ref-1). By 1966, only 25 texts (about 0.03% of the total) had been edited to a scientifically reliable standard; a further approximately 100 had been printed but from imperfect manuscripts and were not authoritative [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1). The Malay Literature Series, begun in 1906 under the auspices of the Committee for Malay Studies in the Federated Malay States, had issued 15 texts in the Roman character by 1921, including the classical *Sejarah Melayu*, *Hang Tuah*, writings of Abdullah bin Abdulkadir, and previously unpublished traditional and modern texts [[4, pp. 32–33]](#ref-4). The deliberate policy of Roman-script publication was intended to overcome the barrier that the Arabic script (hallowed by religious prestige but phonetically ill-suited to Malay) posed to the non-Muslim population [[4, pp. 32–33]](#ref-4).

### Research and Documentation

The study of Malay literature has undergone several distinct phases, each shaped by institutional imperatives and methodological paradigms. The earliest phase, driven by colonial administrative needs, produced the great manuscript collections of the nineteenth century and the foundational grammars and dictionaries of the early twentieth. Blagden's 1921 report framed the emergence of organised Malay studies as a response to an administrative problem: as British control over the Peninsula expanded, the old practice of each officer independently acquiring local knowledge had become inadequate to the needs of governance [[4, pp. 30–32]](#ref-4). The Committee for Malay Studies in the Federated Malay States, with R. J. Wilkinson as its principal intellectual force, coordinated the systematic collection and publication of information on Malay life, customs, history, language, and literature [[4, p. 31]](#ref-4).

The mid-twentieth century saw a methodological crisis. Hussein's 1966 lecture represented a decisive break with the source-tracing paradigm, calling for structural and philological analysis that treats each work as a self-contained aesthetic unit [[1, pp. 21–22]](#ref-1). He rejected both Kassim Ahmad's elevation of Hang Jebat as revolutionary hero and the Russian scholar Parnickel's Marxist class analysis as non-literary impositions of contemporary social needs onto a sixteenth-century text [[1, pp. 21–22]](#ref-1). The post-war turn toward nationalist and ideological readings of the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* was, in Hussein's view, a symptom of the same non-literary method that had characterised the field since its inception [[1, pp. 21–22]](#ref-1).

The late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries have witnessed a further shift. Warnk's 2007 article challenged the "ex nihilo" narrative dominant in Malaysian literary historiography, aligning with a broader postcolonial turn that recognises local agency and cross-cultural literary exchange in the formation of modern literatures [[2, pp. 91–113]](#ref-2). Graf's 2007 survey of German-language scholarship traced the evolution from pre-war folk and court literature to a post-1990s phase in which Malaysian institutions—Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka, ATMA, IKON, and the European Chair of Malay Studies in Leiden—began to set the agenda for international Malay studies, organising conferences, funding translations, and critically re-examining Western Orientalist approaches [[10, pp. 51–65]](#ref-10). Graf framed this as a post-Orientalist deconstruction carried out from the Malaysian side, marking a qualitative break from the earlier pattern in which European scholars defined the terms of engagement [[10, pp. 51–65]](#ref-10). By 2003, German academic publications on Malay literature were concentrated in only four universities—Cologne, Bonn, Frankfurt, and Hamburg—reflecting a marked contraction of the field following the Asian financial crisis and German university budget cuts [[10, p. 58]](#ref-10). The field's future, as these records suggest, lies in the continued integration of philological rigour, literary-scientific method, and the recognition of translation and cross-cultural exchange as constitutive rather than peripheral to the Malay literary tradition.
