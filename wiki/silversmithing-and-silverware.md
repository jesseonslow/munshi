# Silversmithing and silverware

## Silversmithing and Silverware

Malay silversmithing is a traditional metalworking craft that, by the mid-twentieth century, survived in a concentrated and economically significant form in Kelantan, with scattered activity in Trengganu and other east-coast states. The craft produced a range of domestic and ceremonial objects—spoons, cigarette boxes, coffee sets, bracelets, and belt buckles—using techniques of casting, filigree, and engraving that had been refined over generations under royal patronage. By 1951, the industry in Kelantan employed 74 Malay silversmiths, of whom 54 worked in Kota Bharu, and represented one of the few surviving centres of Malay metalwork on the peninsula, where the old silversmiths had largely died out elsewhere, leaving only scattered specimens and what A. H. Hill described as "meretricious imitations" of former skill [[1, p. 134]](#ref-1), [[2, p. 97]](#ref-2). The craft's economic significance lay not only in its output for the domestic Malay market but also in its adaptation to non-Malay and European demand, a shift that had begun in the early 1930s and accelerated after the Japanese Occupation [[1, pp. 146–147]](#ref-1), [[2, p. 97]](#ref-2).

## Technical Methods and Materials

The production of Kelantan silverware followed a sequence of operations that combined local ingenuity with imported materials. Silver was alloyed with ten per cent copper to improve hardness and workability, and was melted in locally made clay crucibles or imported alternatives [[2, p. 98]](#ref-2). Weights were measured using the traditional *saga keneri* system, based on the seeds of *Abrus precatorius*, in which thirty-two *saga* equaled one *real* (four *saga* making one *kupang*, worth twelve and a half cents) [[2, p. 98]](#ref-2).

The most distinctive technical feature of Kelantan silverwork was the cuttlefish (*sotong*) mould technique, used for producing exact replicas of small flat objects such as spoon handles. A dried cuttlefish carcase was leached in running water for ten days before being employed as a one-use mould; Hill described the method as "the nearest thing to mass production in the industry" [[2, p. 99]](#ref-2). This technique allowed craftsmen to produce multiple identical pieces without the need for permanent metal moulds, a practical solution in a context where silver itself was scarce and expensive.

Filigree work involved the mechanical drawing of silver wire to fine gauges, followed by the production of *dawai pintal* (twisted wire), achieved by applying torque via a sliding board to a flattened wire. At a maximum of thirty to forty turns per inch, the wire "looks almost like a row of very fine silver beads" [[2, p. 100]](#ref-2). Engraving was executed with a set of nine distinct gravers (*pahat*), each designed for a specific surface effect: *pahat pasir* for a knobbled surface, *p. kuku* for bold curves, *p. pantat lipas* for straight or curved outlines, *p. betul* for straight lines, *p. jari chichak* for crows' feet patterns, *p. bunga* for rosettes, *p. bintang* for stars, *p. chichang* for levelling borders, and *p. mata* for circular indentations [[2, p. 101]](#ref-2).

The principal decorative motifs included *bunga jawa*, *setapak lembing*, *naga*, and *undok-undok*, which Hill traced to a combination of Majapahit sculpture, Siamese shadow-play figures, Indian lotus conventions, and simple natural prototypes such as the half-coconut shell [[2, pp. 103–104]](#ref-2). These patterns were conservative in character, passed down through apprenticeship rather than written record, and formed the visual vocabulary that distinguished Kelantan work from other regional traditions.

## Economic Structure and Labour

The 1951 survey conducted under the Rural and Industrial Development Authority (RIDA) by T. W. T. Bangs and two Malay assistants provided the first systematic economic data on the Kelantan silversmithing industry [[1, p. 134]](#ref-1). Of the 74 workers recorded, 27 were master-craftsmen operating on their own account, while 47 were paid assistants—journeymen and apprentices—of whom 35 worked at piece rates and 12 on daily wages [[1, p. 145]](#ref-1). Piece rates were evidently preferred by both employers and workers; the twelve daily-wage earners were the newest recruits to the industry [[1, p. 145]](#ref-1).

Master-craftsmen typically earned between $100 and $150 per month, with the most skilled reaching $300 [[1, p. 145]](#ref-1). Journeymen on piece rates generally earned between $50 and $100 per month, while apprentices on daily wages received $30 to $60 [[1, p. 145]](#ref-1). The data revealed a clear correlation between years of experience and earnings: few silversmiths with less than ten years' practice could command more than $100 per month, while after ten years most reached the $100–$150 category, and a few outstanding craftsmen earned more than $150 [[1, p. 146]](#ref-1). No Kelantan silversmith claimed more than nineteen years' practice, a figure that dated the oldest practitioners to 1932 [[1, p. 146]](#ref-1).

Silver was supplied to the craftsmen on credit by the Kelantan (Malay) Arts & Crafts Society or by commercial middlemen, while the simple hand tools used in production were the smiths' own property [[1, p. 144]](#ref-1). The majority of workers were based in Kampong Sireh, an upriver suburb of Kota Bharu, with a few on the west bank of the Kelantan River [[2, p. 97]](#ref-2). The Kelantan Arts and Crafts Depot, directed by Mrs. Rentse, provided marketing, pricing, and design suggestions while leaving craftsmen free to use their traditional patterns [[2, p. 97]](#ref-2).

## Historical Trajectory and Regional Context

The craft had been in danger of dying out in the early 1930s, and was revived through the introduction of new object types—teaspoons, cigarette boxes, coffee sets—that retained traditional motifs but adapted them to forms suitable for the non-Malay market [[1, p. 147]](#ref-1). The Japanese Occupation represented a severe set-back; the shortage of silver was almost complete in 1945 and early 1946, and some older smiths, anxious to resume work, began using copper obtained for them by the late Anker Rentse, producing a number of cigarette-tin cases in that medium before silver became readily available again [[1, p. 147]](#ref-1). Since 1946, the industry had increased appreciably, and the various centres selling Kelantan silverware had no difficulty disposing of their stock [[1, p. 147]](#ref-1).

The post-war recruitment pattern was evident in the age and experience data: the 0–5 years' experience group contained 48 individuals, reflecting a large intake after 1945, while the 6–10 years group contained only three, a direct consequence of the Occupation's disruption [[1, p. 147]](#ref-1). A useful body of young men with 11–15 years' experience constituted a core of skilled craftsmen likely to sustain the industry for decades [[1, p. 147]](#ref-1).

In regional comparison, Hill contrasted Kelantan silverwork with the niello-ware (*chutam*) tradition of Ligor (Nakun Sri Thamarat) in southern Siam, where he visited the state-supported Rong Rien Art School in February 1950. The Ligor school, with Siamese instructors and apprentices aged fourteen to eighteen on a three-year course, averaged a monthly output of 400 bracelets and 200 belt buckles [[2, p. 105]](#ref-2). Its gold-inlay process involved dissolving gold leaf or dust in mercury to form an amalgam, rubbing it onto the silver surface, and heating in a forced-draught furnace to volatilize the mercury and leave a silver-gold amalgam [[2, p. 106]](#ref-2). Hill judged Kelantan work to be superior in originality and workmanship to comparable Malay and Siamese production, attributing this to the craft's roots in feudal royal patronage and its conservative but skilled transmission [[2, p. 97]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Malay silversmithing reflects a progression from general ethnographic description to focused technical and economic analysis. The earliest published accounts—Wray (1902, 1906) and Skeat (1902)—were based on experience in Perak and Selangor and did not provide a direct account of work and methods in Kelantan or Trengganu [[1, p. 148]](#ref-1). Winstedt's *Malay Industries* (1909) and his later *The Malays, a Cultural History* (revised 1950) offered broader treatments of arts and crafts but remained general in scope [[1, p. 148]](#ref-1).

The first substantive technical study of Kelantan silverwork specifically was Hill's 1951 article, which documented the full production sequence, the local system of weights, the cuttlefish mould technique, the nine gravers, and the principal decorative motifs, and provided a comparative account of the Ligor niello-ware tradition [[2, pp. 99–108]](#ref-2). This was followed almost immediately by Gullick's 1952 economic survey, which supplied quantitative data on workforce size, earnings distributions, age profiles, and the structure of employment relationships [[1, pp. 144–147]](#ref-1). Together, these two articles—published in consecutive volumes of the *JMBRAS*—established the baseline against which later studies of the industry could measure change.

Gibson-Hill and Hill's 1951 summary, prepared for the Singapore Art Society's exhibition and reprinted for the Malay Arts and Handicrafts Association (MAHA), provided a broader contextual framework for Malay arts and crafts in general [[1, p. 148]](#ref-1). The archival record indicates that the Society's interest in this subject was concentrated in the immediate post-war period, when colonial-era institutional support for Malay crafts was being reorganized and the Kelantan Arts and Crafts Depot represented one such intervention [[2, p. 97]](#ref-2). No subsequent articles in the available records address the craft in comparable depth, suggesting that the 1951–1952 publications remain the principal scholarly documentation of Kelantan silversmithing in the Society's literature.
