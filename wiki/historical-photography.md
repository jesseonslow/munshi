# Historical photography

Historical photography in the Malay Peninsula and Borneo constitutes a small but significant body of visual evidence for reconstructing the physical, political, and social landscapes of the region during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Because very few photographs were taken in the Malay States before the mid-1890s, when professional photographers established themselves in Kuala Lumpur and elsewhere, each surviving image carries disproportionate evidentiary weight [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1). The practice of photography in the region was a complex exercise in practical chemistry, requiring both expertise and equipment, and most of the early work was in the hands of professional photographers based in Singapore, among whom the firm of G. R. Lambert & Co. were preeminent [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1). The surviving photographic record spans from the 1870s, when colonial administrators such as J. W. W. Birch took photographs during official tours, through the late 1940s and early 1950s, when Carl A. Gibson-Hill produced a major photographic survey of the region at the close of direct colonial rule [[2, pp. 1–2]](#ref-2). These images serve simultaneously as historical documents, objects of state propaganda, and artefacts whose own material history—misattribution, loss, and reinterpretation—reflects the political and institutional forces that shaped their production and reception.

### The Material Conditions of Early Photographic Practice

The technical and logistical constraints of photography in the Malay States during the 1870s and 1880s shaped both the quantity and the character of the surviving record. Photography was a complex exercise in practical chemistry, for which both expertise and equipment were required, and the scarcity of practitioners meant that individual images were vulnerable to misattribution and loss [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1). J. W. W. Birch, Colonial Secretary (1870–1874) and then first British Resident in Perak (1875), had taken a keen interest in photography since his period in the Ceylon Civil Service in the 1860s, when views taken by him of the ruined city of Polonnaruwa were marketed by the firm of Skeen & Co. [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1). During his tour of Selangor and Perak in 1874 he took along an unidentified photographer as well as taking photographs himself; sets of these prints are preserved in the Foreign and Commonwealth Office Library, London, and in Rhodes House Library, Oxford [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1).

The professional photographic infrastructure was concentrated in Singapore. The firm of G. R. Lambert & Co. was preeminent among the professional photographers based there, and it is suggested that they may have provided a photographer to accompany Sir William Jervois on his 1875 tour of Perak, of which three photographs survive [[1, pp. 33–34]](#ref-1). However, the small number of surviving images from that tour—compared with the more extensive records Lambert & Co. produced for the first Durbar of FMS Rulers at Kuala Kangsar in 1897 or for Sir John Anderson's 1909 visit to Kelantan and Terengganu—suggests that the 1875 photographs were more likely the work of the amateur enthusiast Birch rather than of professionals who would be expected to take and preserve a larger number [[1, p. 33]](#ref-1).

By the late 1940s and early 1950s, the material conditions of photographic practice had changed considerably, though they remained constrained by the political circumstances of the Malayan Emergency. Carl A. Gibson-Hill, a former director of the Raffles Museum of Singapore, photographed the region during this period of crisis and the beginning of the end of direct colonial control [[2, pp. 1–2]](#ref-2). The Emergency directly curtailed his movement: he cancelled a planned trip to the Federation when told a police escort would be required, was forced to use "the tail end of film from a newsreel camera" upon his return to Singapore, and had to wash prints in buckets of water for lack of a darkroom [[2, pp. 10–11]](#ref-2). Film shortages and tropical humidity degraded his negatives, and his government position at the Raffles Museum enabled the work in the first place [[2, pp. 10–11]](#ref-2).

### Photographic Evidence and the Reconstruction of Urban History

The identification and dating of early photographs of Malayan towns has become a significant sub-discipline within the study of the region's physical history. J. M. Gullick's 1959 identification of an undated photograph from Sir Roland Braddell's family album as a view of Kuala Lumpur taken from the slopes of Bukit Nanas in or about 1883–84 exemplifies the methodological approach: internal topographical and architectural evidence permits confident identification where no external caption or date is available [[3, pp. 198–202]](#ref-3). Gullick's identification rests on a convergence of features—a confluence of two comparatively small rivers, a central block of Chinese-style buildings, a mining pool, government or police buildings on higher ground behind the Padang, and a substantial timber bridge—which he cross-references against a modern map to show that the relative positions correspond to the Klang/Gombak confluence, the Market Street bridge, and the Selangor Government offices on Bluff Road [[3, pp. 200–201]](#ref-3).

The dating is narrowed further by what is absent from the image. The Klang River widening works of 1884 are not visible, and the $54,000 bridge built above the junction in 1884 is also missing; the visible bridge is therefore identified as the first Market Street bridge, built of timber and 150 feet long in 1883, and later replaced by an iron bridge in 1890 [[3, p. 201]](#ref-3). The central block of Chinese houses corresponds to buildings known to have stood on the west bank of the Gombak from the early 1880s until their staged demolition up to 1994 for the Secretariat building [[3, pp. 200–202]](#ref-3). The photograph is significant as one of the very few extant images of Kuala Lumpur before its rapid growth in brick and tile during the later 1880s [[3, p. 199]](#ref-3).

A parallel case of photographic misattribution is the Bandar Bahru group photograph, long confused with a record of the Pangkor meeting of January 1874. Falconer and Gullick (1989) demonstrated through documentary and visual evidence that the photograph was in fact taken at Bandar Bahru in Lower Perak on 15 September 1875 during the visit of Sir William Jervois, Governor of the Straits Settlements [[1, pp. 21–22]](#ref-1). The confusion arose because, in the ensuing thirty years before the first publication of the photograph in 1908, the generation of officials who remembered the events of 1874–75 retired from service, memories became blurred, and the historical importance of the Pangkor Agreement induced a need to find a pictorial record of the event which formalized the intrusion of British colonial authority into the Malay States [[1, p. 22]](#ref-1). The photograph was first published in Wright and Cartwright's *Twentieth Century Impressions of British Malaya* (1908), which identified the seated figure as Jervois but still captioned the image as showing officials "concerned in enforcing the provisions of the Treaty of Pangkor" [[1, p. 26]](#ref-1). The issue was finally settled by a letter which Swettenham himself wrote in 1941 to Dr. Mubin Sheppard, then editor of the Society's journal, stating that the photograph "has nothing to do with Pangkor, or Sir A. Clarke. It was taken at Mr. Birch's house at Bandar Bharu and the Governor in the centre is Sir William Jervois" [[1, p. 23]](#ref-1).

### Photography as State Propaganda and the Politics of Representation

The relationship between photography and state power in British Malaya is most clearly documented in the case of Carl A. Gibson-Hill's book *Malaya* (1952), a collection of photographs with accompanying text by Gerald Hawkins, published by the Singapore government during the Malayan Emergency [[2, pp. 1–2]](#ref-2). Brendan Luyt's analysis argues that the book functioned as an exercise in state propaganda, produced at a moment when the colonial government needed to cultivate public opinion in the United Kingdom supportive of its counter-insurgency policies [[2, pp. 7–8]](#ref-2). The photographs, dominated by empty landscapes, traditional sailing boats, and villages, project an idyllic, conflict-free Malaya, while Hawkins' text supplies a narrative of development and modernization that justifies continued British presence [[2, pp. 7–8]](#ref-2).

The photographic content is heavily skewed toward peaceful, traditional subjects: 34 general landscapes, 15 boats (all traditional sailing craft, no motorized vessels), 14 rivers, and 9 villages, while only 2 photographs depict tin mining and 4 show rubber cultivation, despite these being Malaya's principal exports [[2, p. 13]](#ref-2). Conspicuous absences include the rainforest and its indigenous inhabitants, scenes of conflict or political unrest, schools and hospitals, and urban street scenes of Malacca or Penang; the only photographic hint of the Emergency is a single smiling portrait of a special Malay constable [[2, pp. 12–14]](#ref-2). Hawkins' text constructs a hierarchy of development among the Malay states, placing Perak at the top ("the best-balanced state in the peninsula" with "a modern mentality") and Trengganu at the bottom ("the least developed of all the Malay states"), while framing even the least developed states as showing promise of future industrialization [[2, pp. 15–16]](#ref-2). The text employs what Mary Louise Pratt terms "anti-conquest discourse": it presents British intervention as a response to local rulers' requests for help with "ruinous dynastic struggles," omitting the role of British merchants in exacerbating those conflicts and pressuring the Straits Settlements government to intervene [[2, pp. 16–17]](#ref-2).

The publication history of the book itself reflects the intersection of individual and state interests. Initially proposed to the ornithological publisher A. F. and G. Witherby in late 1948, the project was rejected on cost grounds until Acting-Governor Sir Patrick McKerron intervened, with the Government Printer producing a first run of 5,120 copies at a price of eight shillings; by the end of 1955, 19,000 copies had been sold [[2, pp. 4–6]](#ref-2). Gibson-Hill received no royalties from the book; his stated motivations were preservation of his work (having lost at least three manuscripts during the Japanese occupation), reaching readers in the UK, and ensuring his photographs were not attached to "maliciously inaccurate" texts about Malaya [[2, pp. 8–9]](#ref-2).

### The Afterlife of Photographic Records: Misattribution, Loss, and Reclamation

The history of individual photographs in the Malay States is frequently a history of misattribution, loss, and contested interpretation. The Bandar Bahru photograph illustrates how the scarcity of early images made them vulnerable to being co-opted by later historical narratives. Four surviving prints of the photograph are known, each bearing different handwritten identifications, and three of them expressly identify the seated figure as Clarke, implying the Pangkor attribution [[1, pp. 22–23]](#ref-1). The most likely explanation, as Falconer and Gullick argue, is that at some stage the photograph was annotated or adapted by persons who believed it represented Clarke and his party at Pangkor, and were over-eager to put the point beyond doubt [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1).

The material condition of photographs also bears on their evidentiary value. Datuk Lim Chong Keat, who acquired one print in 1972, reported that scrutiny of the original photograph revealed earlier inscriptions possibly on the negative within the actual picture area, and that the photograph had been touched up on the different prints, adding buttons and improving light and shade contrasts [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1). The possibility of tampering—adding-in of figures or details—was by no means unknown in nineteenth-century photographs, and Plow's figure in the Bandar Bahru group seems to stand out in the background in a manner suggestive of superimposition [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1).

In a different register, the use of segregated colonial-era spaces for the burial of political victims demonstrates how the spatial logic of colonial institutions continued to shape the management of memory long after independence. The Sungai Buloh Leprosarium, a colonial-era quarantine facility designed to segregate persons with leprosy from the general population, was chosen as the burial site for 103 identified victims of the May 13, 1969 riot [[4, pp. 39]](#ref-4). The uniform grey headstones—each inscribed only with the deceased's name, date of passing, and the phrase "BY THE COURTESY OF THE MALAYSIAN GOVERNMENT"—strip victims of individuality and family connection, reducing them to anonymous casualties of state-acknowledged violence [[4, p. 51]](#ref-4). Families of at least 27 victims engaged in acts of reclamation—rebuilding stones, adding portraits, flanking with Earth God figures, painting inscriptions—while ghost narratives served as a vehicle for expressing unresolved grief and injustice in the absence of a legitimate public space for remembrance [[4, pp. 51–52]](#ref-4).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's journal has served as the principal venue for the scholarly examination of historical photography in the Malay Peninsula across more than a century of publication. The earliest significant contribution is the 1908 publication of the Bandar Bahru photograph in Wright and Cartwright's *Twentieth Century Impressions of British Malaya*, which, while identifying the seated figure correctly as Jervois, perpetuated the erroneous Pangkor attribution that would persist for decades [[1, p. 26]](#ref-1). The Society's journal itself reproduced the photograph in 1951 and 1953, using Print B (in the Singapore National Museum), with the then editor Dr. C. A. Gibson-Hill accepting the Clarke identification but noting the problem that Birch was absent from the Pangkor meeting [[1, p. 26]](#ref-1). The definitive correction came in 1989 with Falconer and Gullick's forensic re-examination, which drew on four surviving prints, the Kuala Kangsar group photograph from Swettenham's collection, the 1873 Legislative Council photograph, and Swettenham's 1941 correspondence [[1, pp. 21–34]](#ref-1).

Gullick's 1959 identification of the Kuala Lumpur photograph from Braddell's album represents a different methodological approach: the use of internal topographical evidence to date and locate an undated image, supplementing written administrative records with photographic evidence of the town's topography before the major river-modification and embankment works of the 1880s and 1890s [[3, pp. 198–202]](#ref-3). This work builds on Dr. Gibson-Hill's earlier internal-evidence dating of the other prints in the Braddell collection, which had narrowed the Singapore views to the 1875–1885 window and placed several Malayan views more precisely [[3, p. 198]](#ref-3).

The most substantial recent contribution is Luyt's 2019 analysis of Gibson-Hill's *Malaya*, which situates the book within the broader literature on colonial photography while arguing that British Malaya's photographic heritage has been comparatively neglected by scholars [[2, pp. 1–2]](#ref-2). Luyt's work represents a shift in the Society's literature from the identification and dating of individual images toward a critical analysis of photography as a medium of state power and cultural representation. His 2022 excerpt on Gibson-Hill's historical writing further contextualizes the photographer's work within the institutional transition from colonial museum-based scholarship to professionalized university-based history writing in the 1950s and 1960s [[5, pp. 89–94]](#ref-5). The Gibson-Hill correspondence is held at the Asian Civilisations Museum Library in Singapore, while government documents including the Colonial Secretary's files on the book's publication are in the National Archives of Singapore [[2, pp. 4–6]](#ref-2).

The body of work documented in the Society's journal thus traces a clear evolution: from the initial publication and misattribution of individual photographs in the early twentieth century, through the mid-century identification and dating of specific images using topographical and architectural evidence, to the late-twentieth and early-twenty-first century critical analysis of photography as a site of political contestation, state propaganda, and memory management. The key debates have concerned the reliability of photographic evidence in the absence of external documentation, the conditions under which early photographs were produced and circulated, and the ways in which the scarcity of the visual record has made individual images vulnerable to appropriation by later historical narratives.
