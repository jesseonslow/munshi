# Tin

## Tin

Tin, in its ore form known as cassiterite (stannic oxide), is one of the most economically significant metals extracted from the Malay Peninsula, a region that by the late nineteenth century had become the world's leading producer of the metal. The peninsula's tin resources are concentrated in alluvial deposits formed by the gravitational sorting action of mountain streams over geological time, with the richest workings occurring in the granite-rich districts of Perak, Selangor, and Pahang, as well as in the Siamese tributary states of Kelantan and the former Patani territory [[1, p. 230]](#ref-1). The metal's economic importance is underscored by production figures that, by the 1880s, placed the peninsula's output at over 24,000 tons annually—more than half the world's total [2, p. 199, n. 1]. Beyond its commercial value, tin has carried deep cultural and ritual significance in the Malay world, where specially shaped ingots served as sacred ceremonial objects rather than mere commodities [[2, p. 103]](#ref-2).

## Geological Formation and Mining Methods

The geological basis of the peninsula's tin wealth was first systematically explained by J. E. Tenison-Woods, an Anglican clergyman and Fellow of the Geological Society and the Linnean Society, in two lectures delivered at Taiping in April 1884. Tenison-Woods argued that cassiterite originated at the junction of the region's ancient granite with overlying Paleozoic clays—probably of Ordovician age—and was subsequently liberated by erosion and concentrated into workable deposits by the sorting action of mountain streams [[1, pp. 224–230]](#ref-1). The critical mining inference was that tin ore is richest at the contact zone between these two formations, and that it is only after the clays have been denuded and the tin washed, sifted, and concentrated by streams that the workable stream tin deposit appears in the alluvial drift [[1, p. 229]](#ref-1). In the Thaipeng mine sections, the tin-bearing stratum lay at a depth of 20 to 30 feet below the surface, was seldom more than four feet thick, and rested upon white or blue clay of either Paleozoic or granitic derivation [[1, p. 229]](#ref-1). Stream tin generally did not travel more than a mile from its point of origin, owing to the high specific gravity of cassiterite, though it could be found further out in the plains where outlying hillocks of granite or Paleozoic clay had once existed and been subsequently washed away [[1, p. 230]](#ref-1).

The practical mining operations observed by European visitors in the late nineteenth century were overwhelmingly Chinese in character. Xavier Brau de Saint Pol Lias, a French naturalist who visited the Perak mines in the late 1880s, described the process in detail: after clearing the ground, the surface and subsoil were removed for one, two, or three metres until the tin-bearing bed was exposed; the mineral was carried in baskets up cocoa-trunk ladders to a wooden flume washed by a current of water; and the washing was done by coolies who, with a rake, removed stones and worked up the material to eliminate light sands until only twenty-five or thirty-five per cent of foreign matter remained [[3, p. 194]](#ref-3). The enriched ore was then melted in small brick furnaces with the aid of a bamboo bellows worked by a coolie, and the white metal was cast into the well-known cubic ingots with one side flaring over the edges to form a pair of "ears" for handling [[3, p. 194]](#ref-3). Lias noted that a great deal of metal was wasted in this process, and that the Chinese and Malays called this lost metal "young tin," which was returned to the earth to ripen because it was not yet old enough to stay in their primitive machines [[3, p. 194]](#ref-3).

By the early twentieth century, more mechanised operations had appeared. E. W. Birch, C.M.G., visiting the Klian Intan district in 1909, recorded that Mr. Kemp's Stockwerk mine operated 25 head of stamps on a 2,300-foot hill, with a 3,800-foot overhead wire rope carrying 11 cages simultaneously, on a 2,200-acre property estimated to yield 10,000 *pikuls* of tin in the following twelve months [[4, p. 142]](#ref-4). Mr. Pearse's Rahman Hydraulic Mine covered approximately 600 acres, with water channelled by open ditch over eight miles, and was estimated to produce 4,000 *pikuls* in the next twelve months [[4, p. 142]](#ref-4).

## Economic and Political Dimensions

The tin trade was a central pillar of the Straits Settlements' economy, and its fluctuations shaped colonial policy. C. D. Cowan's 1950 study of Governor John Bannerman's 1818–19 tin scheme documents how Penang's tin exports peaked at $336,356 in 1816–17, fell to $241,845 in 1817–18, and dropped further to $221,458 in the year ending June 1818, following the Dutch recapture of Java and the return of Malacca to Dutch control [[5, pp. 5–6]](#ref-5). Bannerman, newly arrived from a seat on the Court of Directors, responded by negotiating commercial treaties with Perak, Selangor, and Johore in 1818 to forestall Dutch monopoly claims, and then appointed John Anderson as "Agent for Tin" to purchase ore directly on the Company's account [[5, pp. 10–12]](#ref-5). The Company's contracts comprised 500 *bharas* annually from Selangor at $43 per *bhar*, 100 *bharas* from Perak, and 200 *bharas* offered from Kroh at $40 per *bhar* [[5, pp. 10–12]](#ref-5). The venture encountered intractable obstacles: in the north, a Chinese tax farm on the Muda River imposed duties of $15 per *bhar*—over 37 per cent of the Company's purchase price—and the Raja of Kedah refused to revoke it for fear of Siamese displeasure; in the south, a Dutch mission to Selangor in 1819 succeeded in reviving the 1786 monopoly treaty, which had fixed tin delivery prices at $36–38 per *bhar*, far below the $43–54 the Company was paying [[5, pp. 18]](#ref-5). The Company's collected tin was sold at auction in August 1819 at $18 per *picul* ($54 per *bhar*), yielding a clear profit of $5,396.41 after all expenses [[5, p. 30]](#ref-5). Bannerman's broader vision—acquiring Pangkor Island as a collecting depot and eventually Junk Ceylon to make Penang "the Great Tin Mart of the East"—was never realised, and his death on 8 August 1819 left his successors unwilling to pursue the more ambitious elements of the scheme [[5, pp. 28–30]](#ref-5).

The tin trade also generated significant revenue for Malay rulers and Siamese authorities. C. F. Bozzolo, an Italian-born official in the British administrative service in Perak, recorded in his 1888 report that the Sultan of Kelantan's personal share from the mines was 130 *gantangs* of rough gold, collected via a tax of one tael per miner leaving for China [[6, p. 111]](#ref-6). In the Tomoh gold-mining settlement, Towkay Chiong's *quassa* (royal warrant) from Siam required payment of 30 catties of gold every three years to Bangkok; his son Towkay Ah Fat secured the transfer of this authority by presenting "plenty of gold and a quantity of tin" to the Siamese Government [[6, p. 132]](#ref-6). Bozzolo's assay of tin ore specimens from Negri Say yielded a medium-grained sand with specific gravity 5.55 containing 40.7 per cent metallic tin, and a fine-grained sample with specific gravity 5.4 containing 29.6 per cent metallic tin [[6, p. 142]](#ref-6).

The political geography of tin mining was further shaped by the Anglo-Siamese Treaty of March 1909, which transferred the disputed Reman territory from Patani to Perak, adding a tract of not less than 1,000 square miles and a population of at least 3,000, and bringing the entire 260-mile length of the Perak River under Perak territory [[4, p. 139]](#ref-4). The Intan and Endak mines in this district had originally been opened by a Perak Malay, Pawang Sering, and thereafter became a persistent flashpoint between Perak chiefs and the Patani power, with control passing back and forth through border skirmishes until the 1817–18 war with Kedah placed the mines permanently in Patani hands [[4, p. 137]](#ref-4). Prior to 1824, the mines had produced approximately 50 *bharas* (1 *bhar* = 400 lbs) monthly from Kroh and 200 from Intan; at the time of Birch's visit, only about 40 men worked the Intan mines [[4, pp. 137–138]](#ref-4).

## Cultural and Ritual Significance

Tin in the Malay world was not merely a commodity but a medium of ritual and symbolic expression. W. Linehan, a colonial administrator and historian of Pahang, published a brief note in 1950 describing a unique inscribed tin-ingot of tortoise-shell shape held in the Perak Museum, arguing that such objects were not currency but sacred ceremonial items whose magical significance had been obscured by later misidentification [[2, p. 102]](#ref-2). The ingot in question, donated to the Perak Museum in 1885 by A. Hale, Inspector of Mines, and found in a tin mine at Kuala Dipang in the Kinta District, weighed 1 lb. 14¼ ozs. and showed no signs of deterioration, indicating pure tin content [[2, p. 102]](#ref-2). It bore a short, faintly written legend that has resisted definitive decipherment: a Siamese priest in Singapore identified it as a *mantra* in Burmese characters, while the French epigrapher George Cœdès, working from a cast and photograph, could not read it but concurred that it was almost certainly a mantra, with the script possibly Burmese or Mon [[2, p. 102]](#ref-2).

Linehan's central argument was that the tortoise-shell ingot (*kura-kura*) was never a form of currency, contrary to the view advanced by Temple in his 1914 study of obsolete tin currency [[2, p. 103]](#ref-2). Drawing on the testimony of the Malay trader Haji Mat Arshat, recorded by Laidlaw, Linehan established that these ingots were cast in duplicate as the "eldest-born of the smelting-furnace" or "eldest-born of the mines," and were used in ceremonies attending the erection of the first pillar of a house and bequeathed as heirlooms [[2, p. 103]](#ref-2). The practices associated with these ingots, though sometimes attributed to Chinese miners, emanated largely from Malay medicine-men (*pawang*) who performed the appropriate ceremonies [[2, p. 103]](#ref-2). The tortoise carried symbolic weight across the region's cultures—avatar of Vishnu in Indian tradition, symbol of longevity in Chinese tradition—reinforcing its suitability as a vessel for sacred invocation at the commencement of new undertakings [[2, p. 103]](#ref-2). In Pahang, during the month of Muharram, the four great chiefs presented tin wrought into the shape of tortoises or other animals at the Ruler's court in Pekan, confirming the ceremonial rather than monetary function of such objects [[2, p. 102]](#ref-2).

In a companion note published in 1951, Linehan documented a small group of anomalous tin ingots in the Perak Museum that bore no resemblance to the well-known *tampang* (flat, stamped ingots) or the "animal" types and lacked any local traditional association [[7, p. 69]](#ref-7). These included mound-shaped specimens with circular bases and tooth ornamentation, octagonal ingots with longitudinal ridges (*belimbing segi*), and tubular or cannon-barrel-shaped specimens [[7, pp. 69–70]](#ref-7). Linehan suggested a possible solar-cult association for the mound forms, and tentatively identified the cylindrical specimens as representations of the *linga*, citing Professor L. Renou's treatment in *L'Inde Classique* [[7, p. 71]](#ref-7). The article's broader question—whether any of these objects connected to the mound or ant-hill that featured prominently in the marriage ceremonies of the Malayan aborigines—was left open, with Linehan appending substantial extracts from Skeat and Blagden's *Pagan Races of the Malay Peninsula* documenting the construction of a truncated-cone clay mound used in Besisi wedding rites [[7, p. 72]](#ref-7).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on tin spans more than a century and reflects shifting priorities from geological science and commercial policy to cultural anthropology and archival reconstruction. The earliest contribution in the record is Tenison-Woods's 1884 lectures, which applied the latest European geological science—particularly Daubrée's experimental work on pressure metamorphism and the solvent action of superheated water—to a colonial mining context, with the explicit expectation that scientific knowledge would serve the economic development of the tin-mining state [[1, pp. 221–222]](#ref-1). Lias's 1890 popular-science dispatch, reprinted as a facsimile in 2020, represents a different register: a French naturalist writing for a general audience, whose etymological and civilisational claims—particularly the thesis that the Malay Peninsula may have been the original cradle of tin metallurgy from which knowledge diffused to Europe—reflect the speculative diffusionism common in late-nineteenth-century French science [[3, pp. 197–198]](#ref-3). The editor's note to the facsimile clarifies that "Malacca" in nineteenth-century usage referred to the Malay Peninsula as a whole, and that the illustrations depict workings of the British Pahang Corporation [2, p. 199, n. 2].

The early twentieth century saw a turn toward administrative and political documentation. Bozzolo's 1888 report, printed for circulation among government officials but not published for general distribution until its appearance in the Society's journal in 2018, was submitted to the Colonial Office as a confidential dispatch and contains his journal, six sketch maps, a drawing of Malay mining methods, and the assay report by L. W. Jr. [3, Archival Notes]. Birch's 1910 account of Klian Intan occupies a distinctive position as part travelogue, part administrative briefing, and part historical argument, incorporating verbatim passages from Sir William Maxwell's 1876 journey to layer a contemporary colonial field report over an earlier British administrative account of the same region [5, Archival Notes].

The mid-twentieth century brought a new emphasis on cultural interpretation and archival reconstruction. Linehan's two short notes of 1950 and 1951, drawn entirely from the Perak Museum's own holdings and published ethnographic sources, represent a museum-curatorial approach that corrects persistent errors in the literature—most notably the misidentification of the tortoise-shell ingot as a *jongkong* (boat) shape, which had subsequently misled Mrs A. Hingston Quiggin in her 1949 survey of primitive money [[2, p. 103]](#ref-2). Cowan's 1950 study of Bannerman's tin scheme drew on previously underutilised Penang manuscript records—the Council minutes, Letters & Orders in Council, and correspondence with Malay rulers—to reconstruct a short-lived but revealing episode in the intersection of colonial trade policy and European great-power diplomacy, and also incorporated the 1786 Dutch-Selangor treaty from the Landsarchief at Batavia, Anderson's 1824 pamphlet on the tin trade, and Braddell's trade statistics [1, Archival Notes]. Together, these works trace a trajectory from geological and commercial inquiry through political administration to cultural and archival scholarship, each generation of researchers building on and correcting the findings of its predecessors.
