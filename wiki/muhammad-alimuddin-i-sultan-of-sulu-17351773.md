# Muhammad Alimuddin I, Sultan of Sulu, 1735–1773

D. S. Johnson's "Land Crabs" (1965) is a comprehensive synthesis of the terrestrial decapod crustaceans of the Indo-West-Pacific region, published in a memorial volume honouring C. A. Gibson-Hill. Drawing on Gibson-Hill's field notes and collections from Christmas Island, Pulau Jarak, and the Cocos-Keeling group, the article surveys the taxonomy, ecology, physiology, and behaviour of all major groups of land-dwelling crabs in the region, arguing that these creatures represent a remarkable but still poorly understood radiation into terrestrial habitats.

## Summary

The article begins by establishing which decapod lineages have successfully colonized land, tracing the progression from marine ancestors through freshwater and brackish intermediates to fully terrestrial forms. It then proceeds group by group—land-hermit-crabs and the robber-crab, ghost-crabs, grapsids, and the "true" land-crabs of the family Gecarcinidae—examining their distributions, habitats, feeding behaviour, and reproductive strategies. A recurring theme is the tension between the apparent robustness of the crab body plan and the specific physiological challenges of terrestrial life: water conservation, aerial respiration, and the obligatory return to the sea for spawning by most species.

A substantial portion of the article is devoted to the physiological adaptations that make terrestrial life possible, including the transformation of gill-chambers into lung-like structures, osmoregulatory mechanisms, and the ventilatory systems that differ between anomuran and brachyuran lineages. Johnson also addresses the unsolved problem of why large land-crabs are absent from continental mainlands in the Indo-West-Pacific, suggesting that predation pressure from warm-blooded vertebrates—particularly the crab-eating macaque (*Macaca irus*)—and increased competition may be responsible. The article closes with observations on the remarkable behavioural complexity of terrestrial crabs, which Johnson considers the nearest approach to "intelligent" behaviour found among non-social invertebrates.

### Key Findings

- The robber-crab *Birgus latro* may reach an extreme span of 3 feet across the first pair of walking-legs, a carapace width of over 6 inches, and a weight of five to six pounds (p. 47).
- *Coenobita rugosa*, the most widespread land-hermit-crab in the region, is confined to a narrow coastal strip usually less than 100 yards in width and makes nightly excursions of up to 200 yards to moisten its gill-chamber (p. 46).
- *Sesarma gracillima* has been found alongside streams at heights of up to 2,500 ft and at distances of over 50 miles from the sea, while *S. foxi* ascends to nearly 4,000 ft (p. 51).
- Mass spawning migrations of *Gecarcoidea lalandei* at Christmas Island involve populations numbering in the thousands, with young crabs moving onshore to form a "moving, red carpet" that may completely blanket large patches of ground (p. 54).
- *Cardisoma carnifex* in the Andaman Islands constructs "castles" of termitarium-like form ranging from 30 to 50 cm in height, a behaviour not recorded elsewhere and possibly unique to that population (p. 54).
- The Atlantic species *Gecarcinus lateralis* survived 91 hours in air of 74% humidity without access to water; no land crab has been shown to survive indefinitely under such conditions (p. 56).

### Conclusion

Johnson's definitive takeaway is that terrestrial decapods, while morphologically conservative in their basic body plan, have achieved a degree of physiological and behavioural sophistication that makes them among the most interesting non-vertebrate terrestrial animals. The article simultaneously documents what is known and delineates the significant gaps—particularly in the reproduction of small grapsids, the sensory physiology of land crabs, and the ecological factors limiting large land-crabs to island environments—thereby setting an agenda for future field and laboratory research.

## Context

- The article is built substantially on the field notes and crab collections of C. A. Gibson-Hill from Christmas Island, Pulau Jarak, and the Cocos-Keeling group, published in the *Bulletin of the Raffles Museum*; it serves as both a memorial tribute and a synthesis of the carcinological literature available up to 1965.
- As a natural history review rather than a primary research paper, its contribution lies in integrating scattered observations from multiple expeditions and authors into a coherent framework for understanding terrestrial decapod ecology in the Indo-West-Pacific.
