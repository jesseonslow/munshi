# A journey into the interior of Borneo to visit the Kalabit tribes

R.S. Douglas, a colonial administrator in Sarawak, published this travel account in 1907 describing his seventeen-day expedition into the central Bornean tableland to visit the Kalabit tribes, a people who had only recently acknowledged allegiance to the Sarawak Government. The article serves as both a geographical and ethnographic record of a region that was, at the time of writing, still largely uncontacted by European administration, and it documents the practical mechanics of extending colonial authority into the interior.

## Summary

Douglas's account traces a route from the Government station at Claudetown up the Tutau River, over the Mulu Range, and into the Kalabit country on the central tableland from which the Baram, Tutau, Limbang, Trusan, Padas, and Bahau rivers all spring. The journey was necessitated by the recent migration of Kalabit groups into the headwaters of the Tutau, and Douglas undertook it to establish contact, collect the poll tax, and formally receive declarations of loyalty. His escort, the Kenyah chief Dyau Blawing, and a party of Punan guides who knew the hill routes, frame the narrative as a collaboration between colonial authority and indigenous intermediaries.

The ethnographic observations are the article's principal contribution. Douglas records the Kalabits as an irrigating agricultural people capable of two paddy crops annually, above average in stature, and possessed of extraordinary walking endurance but no familiarity with watercraft. He documents their domestic architecture (including a ground plan of Chief Ili Bawang's longhouse), their feast customs, women's historical song, rat-hunting practices, and the collection of poll tax in rubber. The Punans, encountered at the river mouth on the return journey, are described as a foraging people dependent on wild sago and the blowpipe.

The return journey through a narrow limestone gorge—five hours of unassisted descent between sheer cliffs—provides the account's most vivid passage and underscores the physical barriers that had kept the interior isolated. The practical problem of transporting the collected rubber downstream is solved by the indigenous method of winding it into a ball on a rattan rope and releasing it to the current, a detail that illustrates the gap between colonial administrative expectations and local logistical reality.

### Key Findings

- The Kalabit village at the mouth of the Seridan stream numbered approximately 200 souls and had recently relocated from near the headwaters of the Trusan River (p. 51).
- A grand feast to mark Douglas's arrival consumed one buffalo and nine pigs; the women's historical song procession eventually involved over a hundred performers (p. 52).
- The poll tax was collected in rubber at a rate of three katties per married man, as coins were unknown in the region (p. 53).
- The ascent of Bukit Sigerun Sigop reached an elevation of at least 5,000 feet; the return descent through the gorge took five hours of continuous rapid travel (pp. 50, 59).
- The rubber tax was dispatched downstream by winding it into a ball approximately 9 feet in diameter on a 50-yard rattan rope; one such ball was once detained in the gorge for six months before emerging at Long Iman (p. 61).
- The total absence from Claudetown was seventeen days, the return journey taking only four days compared to the longer outward route (p. 62).

### Conclusion

Douglas's account is fundamentally a record of administrative contact: the extension of the Sarawak Government's fiscal and political reach into a region that had been beyond its effective control. The Kalabits' genuine relief at living under Government protection—freed from "constant alarms of war and rumours of war"—is presented as the principal justification for the expedition, and the smooth collection of the rubber tax and the public declarations of loyalty confirm that the objective was achieved.

## Context

- Douglas was a colonial officer of the Sarawak Government; the article is framed as an official report on the establishment of administrative contact with a newly subject people, and the poll tax collection and loyalty oaths are its administrative core.
- The ethnographic descriptions of the Kalabits and Punans reflect the classificatory conventions of early twentieth-century colonial anthropology, with the Punans labelled "wild people" and the Kalabits "quite one of the most uncivilized in Borneo" (p. 49).
