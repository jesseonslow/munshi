# The Tawaran and Putatan rivers, North Borneo

S. Elphinstone Dalrymple's 1884 account in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* documents a field survey of the Tawaran and Putatan river systems in the north-western interior of North Borneo, traversed on foot and by native boat. The article's central thesis is that the Putatan valley represents the most intensively cultivated and prosperous agricultural district in the entire territory, a claim supported by direct observation of its irrigation infrastructure, land tenure, and export economy.

## Summary

Dalrymple's narrative follows his overland and riverine route from the coast at Gaya island, through the Karimbunai and Mengkabong basins, across a low dividing ridge to the Tawaran, and thence to the Putatan. The Tawaran section is comparatively brief, sketching the Timbalang country, the Latud Dusun settlements, the imposing presence of Kina Balu, and the destructive flood of January 1883. The Putatan section carries the weight of the article. Dalrymple describes a landscape of extraordinary agricultural intensity: a level plain studded with small wooded hills, every foot of soil tilled, irrigation channels neatly maintained, and substantial wooden and stone land-marks delineating individual proprietors' holdings. He contrasts this sharply with the sparser cultivation of the Papar and Kimanis valleys to the south, and notes the near-total absence of swamp attributable to systematic drainage and clearing.

The ethnographic and economic observations are woven throughout. Dalrymple records the Putatan Dusuns as the finest type he had encountered—tall, clean, and intelligent—and notes their use of the letter Z in speech, which he links to the Milanaus of Sarawak. He describes a healing ceremony involving gong-beating, a feathered fetish rod, and a sword, and documents the *tonkoonong*, a six-stringed bamboo guitar. Economically, the district produces gutta, tobacco, camphor, beeswax, and armadillo skins, with 80 to 100 Chinese settlers (mostly intermarried with Dusuns) operating as arrack distillers rather than agriculturists. The Brunei Government levies a per-head tax on the population, and the Orang Tagas, a hill Dusun group wearing the *chawat* loin-cloth, occupy the upper reaches where the Pagunan (the true Putatan) can be ascended for fifteen days.

### Key Findings

- Land in the Putatan valley commands a price of approximately $40 per acre; a single paddy plain is estimated at some 600 acres in extent (p. 269).
- The Brunei Government imposes a tax of $6 to $9 per head per annum, or roughly $200 per *pangkalan* (village landing-place) (p. 270).
- Approximately 80 to 100 Chinese are settled on the Putatan, predominantly arrack distillers who barter with the Dusun population; the bulk are descendants of earlier settlers intermarried with Dusuns (p. 270).
- Monthly exports from the district include some five piculs of gutta, together with tobacco, camphor, beeswax, and armadillo skins (p. 270).
- The January 1883 flood was "very destructive" in the Tawaran district (p. 264).
- Funeral expenses for an opulent Dusun chief can exceed $600, involving the killing of buffaloes, consumption of *tuak*, and *obat* ceremonies; burial jars of great age are valued in hundreds of dollars (p. 271–272).

### Conclusion

Dalrymple's definitive judgment is that the Putatan is "the show river of North Borneo" and its district "unequalled in the whole of North Borneo" for evidence of prosperity, industry, and exhaustive cultivation. He nonetheless notes a limitation: the lands along its banks are firmly held and highly valued by the Dusun proprietors, making it doubtful that any area could ever be brought into the colonial market.

## Context

- Dalrymple was a surveyor and administrator operating under the North Borneo Chartered Company; his account is framed as a reconnaissance of the district's commercial and strategic potential, including the feasibility of a canal through the Gantisan neck to open the Karimbunai basin to sea access (p. 262).
- The article draws entirely on first-hand field observation and native testimony; no archival or documentary sources are cited. Its historiographical value lies in providing a contemporaneous baseline for the agricultural and demographic state of the Putatan and Tawaran valleys prior to the major colonial land acquisitions of the 1890s.
