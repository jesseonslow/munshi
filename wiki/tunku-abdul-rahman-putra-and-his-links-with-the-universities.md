# Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra and his links with the universities of Cambridge and Malaya, 1960‒1962

Chandran Jeshurun's 2009 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the personal and institutional ties between Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Al-Haj and the universities of Cambridge and Malaya during the critical period of 1960–1962, arguing that the Tunku's indefatigable support was instrumental in establishing the University of Malaya at its Pantai Valley campus as a leading institution within the Association of Commonwealth Universities.

## Summary

The article reconstructs two landmark events through the Tunku's private correspondence: the conferral of an Honorary Doctor of Law by the University of Cambridge in June 1960, and his installation as the first Chancellor of the newly established University of Malaya at Pantai Valley in June 1962. Rather than offering a dry institutional history, Jeshurun uses the personal letters to reveal the Tunku's genuine emotional attachment to both institutions—his pride at being honoured by his alma mater, his initial reluctance to accept the Chancellorship for fear of appearing to "control" the university, and his warm friendship with Malcolm MacDonald, the last British Commissioner-General for South-East Asia and former Chancellor of the old Singapore-based university. The correspondence illuminates the human network—MacDonald, Sir Alexander Oppenheim, Edwin E. Rich, Rayson Huang, and others—that collectively built the Pantai Valley campus into a credible centre of higher learning in its first decade.

A significant historiographical thread runs through the article: the Tunku's first favourable indication to the British government of the possibility of a Federation of Malaysia came on 10 June 1960, just one day after his Cambridge degree ceremony. Jeshurun cautiously speculates on whether the euphoric context of that visit in London influenced the Tunku's willingness to engage with Whitehall's "Grand Design," a question that remains unresolved due to the absence of relevant Malaysian official documents. The author also draws attention to the ethnic composition of the first graduating class from Pantai Valley, which reveals the still-limited access of Malay students to professional and scientific degrees in the early 1960s.

### Key Findings

- The University of Cambridge conferred an Honorary LLD on the Tunku on 9 June 1960; the following day, 10 June, he told Lord Perth at the Colonial Office that he was "prepared to face the possibility" of the full "Grand Design" for a Federation of Malaysia (pp. 4–5).
- The Tunku was formally installed as first Chancellor of the University of Malaya at the Stadium Negara on 16 June 1962, an occasion attended by senior representatives from all forty-four member universities of the Association of Commonwealth Universities (p. 11).
- The Tunku served as Chancellor until just after his retirement as Prime Minister in September 1970, when he was succeeded by Sultanah Bahiyah, the first woman to hold the office (p. 12).
- The first batch of students enrolled at the Pantai Valley campus in May 1959; by 1962 the graduating class comprised 28 Malays, 21 Chinese, 16 Indians, and 1 Eurasian for BA (Hons.), but only 1 Chinese for BSc (Hons.) and all 17 BEng graduands were Chinese (p. 13).
- The University of Malaya Students' Union House, declared open by the Tunku in June 1962, cost approximately $200,000 and was described as "superior to anything of its type in South-East Asia" (p. 14).

### Conclusion

Jeshurun's definitive historical takeaway is that the University of Malaya's rise to prominence as a top institution in the Association of Commonwealth Universities during the 1960s and 1970s was inseparable from the Tunku's personal commitment and the dedicated work of a small circle of pioneers—above all Malcolm MacDonald and Sir Alexander Oppenheim—whose contributions have been largely forgotten in the national memory. The article serves as a plea for recognition of these founders, noting that even the modest Union House hall named after Oppenheim has been demolished without adequate commemoration.

## Context

- The article is drawn primarily from the Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Papers (TARPP) held at the Memorial Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Al-Haj in Kuala Lumpur, a private collection that had not previously been systematically exploited for the history of higher education or the diplomatic origins of the Malaysia federation.
- The study contributes to the historiography of the "Grand Design" by identifying the 10 June 1960 audience with Lord Perth as the first documented instance of Malayan acceptance of the federation concept, predating the Tunku's well-known public announcement in Singapore on 27 May 1961.
