# The great flood 1926

R. O. Winstedt, then a senior colonial administrator and scholar, compiled this account in 1927 from the official reports of civil servants, police, engineers, and other government officers across the Straits Settlements and Federated Malay States. The article documents the catastrophic floods of late December 1926 to early January 1927 that struck Perak, Pahang, Kelantan, and Trengganu, presenting a district-by-district administrative record of the disaster's physical destruction, human toll, and the colonial government's relief response.

## Summary

Winstedt's compilation is essentially a consolidated administrative report drawn from the district-level accounts submitted by the officers who managed the crisis on the ground. The narrative is organised by state and sub-district, tracing the rise and recession of the rivers, the destruction of infrastructure and property, the evacuation of populations, and the logistics of food distribution. The floods were caused by an extraordinary concentration of rainfall over the eastern half of the peninsula, with the Kemaman–Kuantan area receiving over 70 inches in the latter part of December, while the western coast received as little as 8 inches. This asymmetry meant that the East Coast states—Pahang, Kelantan, and Trengganu—suffered the most severe inundation, with river rises of 50 to 80 feet above normal levels.

The article reveals the colonial administration's dual concern with immediate humanitarian relief and the preservation of order and economic productivity. Winstedt records the evacuation of hospitals, the rationing of rice to thousands of refugees, the deployment of military platoons to prevent looting (which in the event did not materialise), and the careful management of food supplies to avoid both starvation and the demoralisation of able-bodied workers. A recurring theme is the tension between providing free relief and maintaining self-reliance: in Kuantan, philanthropic Chinese were distributing free rice to able-bodied Malays who consequently refused to work on road repairs, and this had to be stopped. The article also notes the long-term economic consequences—silted rice fields that would not be plantable for one or two seasons, destroyed rubber estates, and the likelihood of a "large programme of relief work" being required later in the year when reconstruction employment dried up.

Winstedt opens with a brief, wry observation about the folklore surrounding the floods—Malay tales of a crocodile king and a medicine-man's revenge—before dismissing them in favour of the meteorological and hydrological record. This framing signals the article's purpose: to provide a factual, evidence-based account that supersedes popular narrative, while still acknowledging the cultural context in which the disaster was experienced.

### Key Findings

- The Perak River at Kuala Kangsar reached 143.8 inches above mean sea level on 30 December 1926, exceeding the 1897 flood by 8 feet; approximately 800 kampong houses were destroyed and 10,000 acres of padi lost in that district alone (pp. 296–297).
- In Kelantan, at least 800 square miles of the main plain were deeply flooded, 50 people drowned, 2,900 cattle and buffaloes were lost, 1,700 houses were washed away, and the damage in Kuala Krai town alone was estimated at $300,000 (pp. 306–307).
- River rises recorded: Jelai 60 feet at Kuala Lipis, Pahang 53 feet at Temerloh, Tembeling at least 80 feet at Kuala Tahan, and Kelantan River 72 feet at Kuala Krai (p. 309).
- Rainfall in the Kemaman–Kuantan focus area exceeded 70 inches in late December; Trengganu recorded 42.64 inches in December and 42.07 inches in November, a two-month total of nearly 85 inches (pp. 308–309).
- The Perak Relief Fund closed at $100,000 on approximately 20 January 1927; the State Engineer's estimate of road and bridge damage in Perak was $225,000, of which $100,000 was in the Perak River Valley (p. 298).
- A post-flood fire in Kota Baharu on 13 January 1927 destroyed 250 houses and caused damage exceeding $500,000, compounding the flood losses (p. 308).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that while the immediate human toll was relatively modest—fewer than 100 deaths across all affected states—the economic devastation was immense and its full consequences would not be felt for months. The destruction of rice crops, the silting of fields, the loss of livestock, and the ruin of rubber estates meant that serious poverty and a large-scale relief programme were inevitable in the latter part of 1927, even after the water had receded and reconstruction work had provided temporary employment.

## Context

- Winstedt held the position of Director of Education in the Straits Settlements and was a prominent figure in colonial administration; the article is compiled from official government reports and reflects the administrative perspective of the colonial state as both rescuer and economic manager.
- The source material consists entirely of internal government communications—district officer reports, police accounts, engineering assessments, and medical officer observations—making this a primary administrative record of the disaster rather than an independent scholarly analysis.
