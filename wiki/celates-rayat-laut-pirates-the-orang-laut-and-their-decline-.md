# Celates, Rayat-Laut, pirates: the Orang Laut and their decline in history

Timothy P. Barnard's 2007 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the Orang Laut (sea peoples) of the southern Melaka Straits and their transition from indispensable maritime power brokers to a marginalized remnant over the course of the eighteenth century. Set against the rise and fall of Srivijaya, Melaka, and Johor, Barnard argues that the Orang Laut's decline was not primarily caused by European technological superiority but by the internal shattering of the patron–client bond between Malay rulers and their sea-based followers, a process initiated by the regicide that ended the direct Melaka–Johor line of sovereignty.

## Summary

The article surveys the role of the Orang Laut in supporting Malay rulers from the Srivijaya period through the Johor–Riau era, drawing on Portuguese sources (Pires, Barros, Eredia), Malay chronicles (*Sulalat us-Salatin*, *Hikayat Siak*, *Tuhfat al-Nafis*), and Dutch VOC records. Barnard establishes that the Orang Laut were not a homogeneous group but a constellation of subgroups (*suku*) who provided rowers, raiders, scouts, smiths, and navigators to their patrons. Their expertise in the complex hydrography of the Melaka Straits—its reefs, currents, and river bores—made them the essential link between river-based Malay courts and the maritime trade networks that sustained those courts. Without their assistance, a Malay state remained a closed entity tied to the coast and port.

The core argument concerns the mechanism of decline. Barnard contends that the murder of the childless Sultan Mahmud Syah II severed the chain of *daulat* (sovereignty) that had bound Orang Laut loyalty to the Melaka–Johor line since the founding of the state. Without a legitimate sovereign to whom they owed allegiance, Orang Laut groups became politically mobile, shifting their support to new patrons such as the Minangkabau adventurer Raja Kecik and later Siak princes. This capriciousness, in turn, allowed Bugis mercenaries to establish themselves as *Yamtuan Muda* of Riau and eventually enabled the Ilanun—armed with superior vessels, compasses, and boarding equipment—to overwhelm the Orang Laut militarily in the late eighteenth century. European technology and colonial anti-piracy campaigns were the final blow, not the cause.

Barnard draws on a mix of European archival reports, Malay literary chronicles, and oral traditions to reconstruct the Orang Laut's economic base and their political functions. He engages with the historiographical debate around piracy, following Joseph a Campo's distinction between predatory and parasitic raiding, to reframe Orang Laut raiding as a form of state service rather than criminality, and to show how the discourse shifted as European and Dutch colonial powers imposed their own definitions of maritime order.

### Key Findings

- The murder of Sultan Mahmud Syah II of Johor in 1699 severed the direct line of *daulat* stretching back through Melaka to Srivijaya, initiating the political realignment that undermined Orang Laut loyalty to a single sovereign (p. 41).
- The *terubuk* (*Alosa toli*) fishery in the Bengkalis Straits supported up to 2,000 vessels during spawning season (September–January), and the pickled roe was traded as a delicacy comparable to Russian caviar (pp. 39–40).
- Ilanun vessels carried up to eighty fighting men and one hundred rowers, and were equipped with compasses, telescopes, and boarding hooks—technological advantages that rendered traditional Orang Laut maritime expertise obsolete (p. 45).
- The sacking of the English ship *Nancy* by Raja Alam of Siak yielded specie worth over 50,000 *laksas*, illustrating the economic scale of Orang Laut–assisted raiding in the mid-eighteenth century (p. 44).
- The Ilanun, displaced from Mindanao by a volcanic eruption in 1765, began raiding in the Melaka Straits region by the 1780s; by 1787 they had been invited by the Johor sultan to attack Riau, after which they settled permanently in eastern Sumatra and Pulau Tujuh (pp. 45–46).
- In 1836, the British vessel *Andromache* destroyed Orang Laut communities on Galang and Karimun with little resistance, marking the final military elimination of Orang Laut power in the Riau Archipelago (p. 46).

### Conclusion

Barnard's definitive takeaway is that the Orang Laut's decline was an internally generated political process, not an externally imposed technological one. The rupture of the patron–client bond following the 1699 regicide created a vacuum of legitimate authority into which Bugis, Minangkabau, and Ilanun migrants poured, each displacing the Orang Laut from their traditional role as the maritime arm of Malay sovereignty. By the time European colonial powers arrived with superior technology and anti-piracy mandates, the Orang Laut had already been reduced to what Crawfurd called a "rude class"—a marginalized remnant whose former centrality to the political economy of the Straits was no longer visible to contemporary observers.

## Context

- Barnard synthesizes Portuguese sources (*Suma Oriental*, *Decadas da Asia*, Eredia), Malay chronicles (*Sulalat us-Salatin*, *Hikayat Siak*, *Tuhfat al-Nafis*), Dutch VOC archives, and nineteenth-century British and Dutch administrative accounts into a political history of maritime patronage.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malay state formation by centering the Orang Laut as active agents in the rise and fall of Srivijaya, Melaka, and Johor, rather than treating them as passive victims of European encroachment, and by challenging the standard narrative that attributes their decline primarily to technological obsolescence.
