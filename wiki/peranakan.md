# Peranakan

The Peranakan, from the Malay word meaning "born in the country," denote the local-born offspring of Chinese settlers who intermarried with Malay and Indonesian women in the ports and hinterlands of maritime Southeast Asia. Across the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, and the northern littoral of the region, Peranakan communities developed distinctive linguistic, cultural, and economic profiles that set them apart from both their Chinese-origin ancestors and their Malay neighbours. Their significance lies in the hybrid identities they forged—simultaneously Chinese and local, cosmopolitan and rooted—which made them pivotal intermediaries in the trade, print, and cultural networks of the colonial Indian Ocean world [[1, pp. 5–7]](#ref-1).

## Scope and Historical Definition

The term "Peranakan" is applied to communities across a wide subregion, and the records reveal considerable variation in how the label is used and understood. In Malacca and Singapore, the Peranakan are the Baba and Nyonya whose language, Baba Malay, is a Malay-based creole with substantial Hokkien lexical strata [[2, pp. 150–151]](#ref-2). In Penang, by contrast, the Peranakan speak a Hokkien-based dialect—Baba Hokkien—into which Malay lexical items have been superimposed, a profile the authors Lim Beng Soon and Boon Seong Teoh argue reflects the island's unique demographic history, in which the absence of a large pre-existing Malay population before British acquisition in 1786 meant the Peranakan were less influenced by Malays and more by the influx of Chinese immigrants from the early nineteenth century [[3, pp. 125–126]](#ref-3). In northeast Kelantan, Tan Chee Beng identifies an acculturated rural Chinese population—wearing Malay dress, speaking Kelantan Malay and often Thai, eating with their fingers—whom he terms "Peranakan" by analogy with the Baba of Malacca, while noting that the local Malay label *Cina Kampong* is spatially reductive and that "Baba" is inappropriate because these people do not use the term [[4, pp. 26–28]](#ref-4). In Phuket, Khoo Salma Nasution documents a Hokkien Peranakan community that emerged not from a single origin event but from multi-generational, bidirectional exchanges with Penang, distinguishing Phuket's Hokkien population from other Chinese communities in Siam [[5, pp. 81–83]](#ref-5).

The foundational distinction in the literature is between the Baba (local-born, pre-colonial merchant-class offspring) and the *Sinkheh* (new immigrants, particularly labour migrants from the mid-nineteenth century), a divide that marks a fundamental shift in the composition and linguistic profile of Chinese communities in the Straits ports [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). Loh Wei Leng further argues that the dominant concept of a singular "Chinese diasporic identity" is inadequate for describing Peranakan communities, whose open tradition of cultural adaptation produced hybrid and multiple identities instead, and introduces Tan Chee Beng's framework of "multiple identities" to capture the simultaneous bounded (national) and unbounded (*Huaren*) dimensions of Chinese-origin individuals in the region [[1, pp. 5–6]](#ref-1).

## Economic Networks and Commercial Infrastructure

Peranakan Chinese merchants were central to the intra-regional trade circuits that sustained the northern littoral of Southeast Asia. Loh Wei Leng demonstrates that Penang functioned as the premier commercial centre of this littoral throughout the colonial era, sustained by dense intra-regional trade and shipping networks rather than merely serving as a feeder port to Singapore. Straits Settlements Blue Book data show that Southeast Asian trade consistently accounted for the largest share of Penang's imports, peaking at 72.70 per cent in 1908, and for exports in most years, confirming the port's primary orientation toward its regional hinterland rather than the West [[6, p. 30]](#ref-6). The 1933–34 Straits Settlements Trade Commission formally described Penang as "the natural centre of an area including the East and North Coasts of Sumatra, the West coast as far south as Padang, the Northern Malay States and Southern Siam" [[6, p. 31]](#ref-6).

Two case studies illustrate the mechanisms through which Peranakan families sustained these networks. Thio Thiau Siat (Cheong Fatt Tse) operated a network spanning Penang, the Malay States, and Sumatra through opium syndicates, the Li Wang Company, and the Deli Bank, linking Hakka, Hokkien, and Peranakan Chinese actors across the region [[6, pp. 32–33]](#ref-6). The Khaw family's shipping firm Koe Guan grew from a few vessels in the 1870s to 16 steamers by 1904, operating regular services to Burma, Siam, Sumatra, Singapore, and China, and responded to European competition by founding the Eastern Shipping Company in collaboration with other Penang shipowners [[6, pp. 33–34]](#ref-6).

The Penang–Phuket axis provides a further case study. Khoo Salma Nasution shows that Penang functioned as far more than a commercial entrepôt for Phuket tin; it was a cultural reference point that supplied provisions, credit, religious intermediaries, educational institutions, architects, and marriage partners, all of which shaped the identity of the Hokkien Chinese in Phuket [[5, pp. 81–83]](#ref-5). The Tongkah Harbour Tin Dredging Company, floated in 1906 and operational by 1907, was the world's first tin dredge, the first offshore dredge, and the first dredge to operate in Asia [[5, p. 97]](#ref-5). Tan Mah Siang's Penang-style mansion at 98 Krabi Road, completed in 1907, cost 500,000 baht and incorporated materials imported from the Netherlands, Italy, France, Britain, Germany, and the United States [[5, p. 102]](#ref-5).

At the individual level, Liu and Sim re-examine the 1888–1889 travelogue of Li Qinghui, a Hokkien Peranakan born in Melaka, to argue that his journey across the treaty-port system was not a leisurely sightseeing tour but a deliberate exercise in cultivating and consolidating business networks for his family's shipping, trading, and financial enterprises spanning Singapore, Shanghai, and the broader Asian Mediterranean [[7, pp. 33–36]](#ref-7). The Li family navigated multiple, overlapping affiliations: as a traditional Chinese family bound by surname and succession; as a Creole, mixed-linguistic group comfortable in English, Hokkien, Han Chinese, and Malay; as business actors operating as compradors and financiers; and as political affiliates simultaneously aligned with the Qing government and the British colonial administration [[7, pp. 46–51]](#ref-7).

## Language, Religion, and Cultural Practice

The linguistic profiles of Peranakan communities are among the most thoroughly documented aspects of their cultural life. Tan Chee Beng's 1980 analysis of Baba Malay in Malacca demonstrates that the dialect is not a "corrupted" or degraded form of Malay but a legitimate variety with its own consistent phonological, lexical, and syntactic structure, developed through centuries of Chinese-Malay-Indonesian contact [[2, pp. 150–151]](#ref-2). Five regular phonological patterns differentiate Baba Malay from Standard Malay, including the realization of final /a/ as [a] rather than [ə], and the substitution of /ar/ for /er/ (e.g., *kasar* → *kaser*) [[2, pp. 159–162]](#ref-2). Chinese (Hokkien) loanwords are concentrated in the domains of kinship, ritual, and religion, where Malay lacks equivalent vocabulary; examples include *m-peh* (father's elder brother), *siki* (death anniversary), and *teyian* (religious donation) [[2, pp. 152–154]](#ref-2). The consistent use of *gua* and *lu* (rather than Malay *aku* and *engkau*) is identified as a primary marker of Baba identity [[2, p. 152]](#ref-2).

In Penang, Lim and Teoh document that Malay lexical items embedded in Baba Hokkien have undergone systematic phonological remodelling, including tone superimposition, syllable truncation, and consonant substitution, as well as semantic narrowing and broadening [[3, pp. 129–130]](#ref-3). Above the level of the individual lexicon, the authors identify fossilised formulaic expressions and loan translations—sayings that encode a Malay worldview but are rendered in Hokkien syntax, such as "The Batak is masquerading as a Nonya" [[3, p. 131]](#ref-3). Clammer's statistic illustrates the language-shift trajectory: in 1870 only 20 per cent of Malacca and Singapore Babas could speak Chinese, but by 1975 the figure was approximately 42 per cent in Malacca and higher in Penang [[3, p. 132]](#ref-3).

In northeast Kelantan, Tan Chee Beng argues that Chinese religion is the single most important institution preserving Chinese cultural identity among a community otherwise heavily acculturated by Malay and Thai neighbours [[4, pp. 26–27]](#ref-4). Each Peranakan settlement maintains a patron deity (Siong Tek Kong, the Ong Yahs, Mazu) whose birthday celebration is the community's most important communal event. Unlike Chinese elsewhere in Malaysia who stage Chinese operas for such occasions, the Peranakan hire *menora* troupes, *wayang kulit*, or *wayang gedek*—performances in Kelantan Malay and Thai that their community can understand [[4, pp. 44–45]](#ref-4). Tan's central argument is that because Malay culture is fundamentally Islamic, a Chinese person cannot become culturally Malay without abandoning Chinese religion; conversely, as long as Peranakan Chinese observe their religious practices, they remain Chinese [[4, p. 45]](#ref-4).

In the performing arts, Tan Sooi Beng documents the eclectic street performance traditions of Penang's Peranakan community from the early twentieth century, including *bangsawan*, *dondang sayang*, *kroncong*, *ronggeng*, and *wayang peranakan*, which fused Malay, Chinese, European, and Indian musical and theatrical elements [[8, pp. 157–160]](#ref-8). The *Penghiboran Hati* (1924) was a published collection of sixteen *kroncong* and *stambul* pieces with musical notation for violin, mandolin, and guitar, including *Stambol Satoe*—later adopted as Malaysia's national anthem [[8, p. 159]](#ref-8). The decline of these traditions is attributed to the 1930s economic recession, the Japanese Occupation, the post-war advent of film and television, and the formation of the Federation of Malaya in 1948, which forced diverse communities to identify with one of three racial categories, diminishing the space for mixed identities [[8, p. 161]](#ref-8).

In material culture, Cheah examines the needlework traditions of Peranakan women in Penang, arguing that metallic thread embroidery maintained a shared regional style that reinforced a common Baba identity, while silk thread embroidery and beadwork became vehicles for expressing distinct local character [[9, pp. 145–147]](#ref-9). A coherent "Penang style" in knot-stitch embroidery and beadwork emerged by the early 1900s, characterized by light pastel palettes, European-derived floral and figural motifs, and the distinctive use of counted thread canvas as a base material, facilitated by the presence of German trading firms [[9, pp. 150–151]](#ref-9).

## Institutional Life and Contemporary Reconstitution

The institutional history of Peranakan associations reveals a transformation from colonial-era political instruments into cultural preservation networks. Lee Su Kim traces the genealogy from the Straits Chinese British Association (SCBA), founded in Singapore on 17 August 1900 with over 800 members, through its Malacca branch (25 September 1900, approximately 200 enrollees) and Penang branch (1920), to their post-independence reconstitutions [[10, pp. 168–169]](#ref-10). The Persatuan Peranakan Cina Melaka, renamed from the SCBA in 1981, acquired its clubhouse at No. 149, Jalan Tun Tan Cheng Lock in 1984 for RM330,000 and currently maintains 48 life members, 202 ordinary members, and 59 associate members with a median age of 50–59 [[10, p. 171]](#ref-10). The Peranakan Association of Singapore's membership grew from 342 in 1993 to 1,882 by the end of December 2008, reflecting the post-1990s cultural resurgence [[10, p. 169]](#ref-10).

Kim distinguishes between the "old" associations—forged in the Straits Settlements and originally constituted to protect the political and economic interests of Straits-born Chinese against the encroachment of China-born compatriots—and the "new" associations in Kuala Lumpur, Phuket, and Melbourne, which were never political organisms and instead emerged from a desire to create tangible spaces for heritage connection among dispersed communities [[10, pp. 167–169]](#ref-10). The Persatuan Peranakan Baba Nyonya Kuala Lumpur and Selangor, officially registered on 28 July 2008, had reached 60 members and 2 associate members by the time of writing, with a notably younger demographic profile than its older counterparts [[10, pp. 173–175]](#ref-10).

The annual Baba Convention, initiated by Datuk Khoo Keat Siew in 1988, brought together Peranakans from Penang, Melaka, and Singapore; a Phuket delegation joined from 2002, and the 16th Convention (5–7 December 2003) in Penang included a Thai Baba meal and *Loi Loi Kratong* dancing [[8, p. 161]](#ref-8). The 15th Convention in Singapore (2002) produced a consensus that Baba culture could not be preserved as it was in the pre-war days; Baba Lee Liang Hai stressed that "the young Peranakan should know what their culture is so that they can adapt it in their own way in the 21st century" [[8, p. 163]](#ref-8).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's journal has documented Peranakan communities across a wide chronological and disciplinary arc, with shifting priorities reflecting broader historiographical currents. The earliest contributions in the records are linguistic: Shellabear's 1913 introductory sketch of Baba Malay, followed by Tan Chee Beng's systematic 1980 dialect analysis, which provided the first rigorous linguistic description of Baba Malay as a legitimate variety rather than a "broken" language [[2, pp. 150–151]](#ref-2). Tan's concurrent doctoral dissertation at Cornell University (1979) on Baba ethnic identity complemented this linguistic work [[2, p. 150]](#ref-2). The 1999 article by Lim and Teoh extended the comparative Peranakan linguistics programme to the under-studied Penang variety of Baba Hokkien, filling a gap left by the concentration of earlier scholarship on the Malacca and Singapore varieties [[3, pp. 125–127]](#ref-3).

The 1970s and 1980s saw the emergence of ethnographic and historical approaches. Tan Chee Beng's 1982 study of Peranakan Chinese in northeast Kelantan was one of the first dedicated monographic treatments of a rural Peranakan community, explicitly calling for further research on religion, kinship, marriage, and funeral customs [[4, pp. 26–27]](#ref-4). Ding Choo Ming's 1978 article introduced the Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia's collection of over 1,500 titles of Indonesian Peranakan literature, arguing for the integration of Peranakan Chinese fiction into the broader literary and social historical record of colonial Indonesia [[11, pp. 54–57]](#ref-11).

The 2009 volume *Peranakan Chinese in Penang and the Region: Evolving Identities and Networks*, funded by the Toyota Foundation's Asian Neighbors Network Program, represents a significant consolidation of the field, bringing together scholars from different disciplines to examine Peranakan networks across the northern littoral subregion [[1, pp. 1–7]](#ref-1). The volume's contributions span economic history (Loh Wei Leng on Penang's commercial networks; Khoo Salma Nasution on the Penang–Phuket axis), print culture and political consciousness (Su Lin Lewis on Penang and Rangoon), material culture (Cheah on Nyonya needlework), performing arts (Tan Sooi Beng on street culture), and institutional history (Lee Su Kim on Peranakan associations) [[1, pp. 1–7]](#ref-1). This multi-disciplinary approach reflects a broader shift from the earlier focus on language and ethnicity toward network analysis, material culture, and the politics of heritage in postcolonial Southeast Asia.

A persistent historiographical tension runs through the literature: the question of whether Peranakan identity is best understood as a fixed cultural type or as a fluid, context-dependent set of affiliations. Liu and Sim's concept of "tribal cosmopolitanism"—rooted in language, ethnicity, and association networks rather than the universalist detachment of Enlightenment thought—offers one resolution [[7, pp. 46–51]](#ref-7). Loh Wei Leng's critique of the "singular Chinese diasporic identity" and his adoption of Tan Chee Beng's "multiple identities" framework point in a similar direction [[1, pp. 5–6]](#ref-1). The records collectively suggest that the Peranakan are best understood not as a monolithic community but as a family of related yet distinct cultural formations, each shaped by its specific local ecology of trade, language, religion, and political circumstance.
