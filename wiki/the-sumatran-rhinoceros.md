# The Sumatran rhinoceros

H. N. Ridley, director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, published this account of the flora of Mount Ophir (Gunong Ledang) in 1901, providing the first comprehensive botanical survey of the highest accessible peak in the Malay Peninsula. The paper synthesises collections made over half a century by Griffith, Wallace, Maingay, Hullett, and others, and argues that the upper flora (3,000–4,000 feet) is composed of three distinct elements—Malayan, alpine, and notably Australian—whose distribution reflects the geological denudation of the peninsula's western highlands.

## Summary

Ridley opens with a historical survey of botanical work on Mount Ophir, noting that Griffith made the first extensive collection in 1845, followed by Cuming, Lobb, Wallace (whose notes were lost), Maingay, and Hullett. He justifies restricting his account to the upper flora above 3,000 feet, arguing that the lower woods differ little from similar altitudes elsewhere in the peninsula, and that the combined collections of so many botanists in such a limited district have likely captured most of the upper range's flora (pp. 105–106).

The core of the paper is a detailed floristic list covering the upper slopes of Gunong Ledang and Gunong Mering, with descriptions of several new species including *Garcinia montana*, *Microtropis ophirensis*, *Argostemma hirta*, *A. equifolia*, and *Hedyotis flexuosa*. Ridley also records animal observations—tiger tracks on Gunong Tunduk, deer footprints on the summit, and a notably sparse fauna above Padang Batu—before turning to the paper's most significant theoretical contribution (pp. 106–112).

Ridley divides the Ophir flora into three elements: a Malayan lowland component that has ascended the hills; an alpine element shared with other high ranges; and an Australian element represented by genera such as *Boeckia*, *Leptospermum*, *Tristania*, *Leucopogon*, *Dianella*, *Gahnia*, *Lepidosperma*, and *Cladium*. He demonstrates that this Australian element is not confined to Ophir but occurs along mountain ridges and sandy coasts throughout the Malay Archipelago, dying out westward, and is entirely absent from lowland forests. He speculates that these plants are relics of a flora that once grew on the sandy shores of a sea which formerly washed the foot of the mountain, before the enormous denudation of the peninsula's western highlands (pp. 112–114).

### Key Findings

- Griffith's 1845 collection was the first extensive botanical survey of the mountain; Wallace's 1854 expedition notes and Royal Geographical Society paper were lost, leaving a gap in the historical record (p. 105).
- *Linaria alpina*, collected by Hullett on the summit, is "a most remarkable" find, not recorded from any other part of tropical Asia nor from any other Malayan locality, representing the sole example of a "northern Alpine flora" on the mountain (p. 112).
- The Australian element includes at least six Cyperaceae species, four of which (*Gahnia* ×2, *Lepidosperma*, *Cladium*) belong to genera that are "typical Australian" or related to West Indian forms rather than anything else in the region (p. 112).
- The Australian genera are found on hills at 3,000–4,000 feet and on sandy sea coasts but are "quite absent from the forest country between the sea coast and the hill tops," suggesting a sand-loving or dry-country origin (pp. 113–114).
- Ridley describes *Garcinia montana* as having the smallest flowers known in the genus, with thick veinless leaves, and *Dacrydium elatum* var. on Ophir as morphologically distinct from the Penang form, possibly representing *D. beccarii* of Sarawak (pp. 108–109, 121).
- The flora of Gunong Mering, ascended by Ridley and Hervey in 1892 (the first recorded European ascent), is "to all intents and purposes the same as that of Ledang" but differs from the highlands of Perak and Selangor, more closely resembling Kedah Peak (pp. 111–112).

### Conclusion

Ridley's definitive takeaway is that the distinctive character of the Mount Ophir flora—particularly its Australian component—cannot be explained by present-day geography alone but must be understood in terms of the peninsula's geological history. The isolated position of the mountain, its proximity to the sea, and the "enormous" denudation of the western highlands have left behind a relict assemblage of sand-loving and dry-country plants that once thrived on a coastal plain now submerged or eroded away (p. 114).

## Context

- The paper was published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, the principal scholarly outlet for natural history in the Straits Settlements, and reflects the institutional role of the Singapore Botanic Gardens as the region's centre of botanical research.
- Ridley's own collections from two expeditions to Mount Ophir are preserved in the Herbarium of the Botanic Gardens, Singapore, alongside those of Hullett and Derry, forming a key reference collection for Peninsular Malayan botany (p. 106).
