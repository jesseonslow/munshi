# Court language and etiquette of the Malays

Dato' Muhammad Ghazzali, a senior courtier of Kelantan with thirty years of service to the royal family, published this practical manual on Malay court language and etiquette in 1933, addressing a European readership seeking to understand the unwritten but rigidly enforced code of conduct governing interaction with Malay royalty. The article's overarching thesis is that mastery of court language and manners constitutes a *sine qua non* of Malay social life, valued even above formal education, and that the entire system functions as a mechanism of social stratification preserved "from time immemorial."

## Summary

Ghazzali's article is structured as a comprehensive guide to the linguistic, material, and behavioural protocols of the Malay court, drawing on his direct experience as Dato' Bentara Luar of Kelantan. The opening sections establish the elaborate system of formal address, in which every individual—ruler, prince, minister, commoner, and even the Almighty—occupies a fixed position in a hierarchy of pronouns, titles, and self-designations. The author demonstrates through extensive Malay-language examples how a single sentence shifts in register depending on whether the speaker addresses the Sultan, a Crown Prince, or a Dato', and how the third-person pronoun *dia* is entirely replaced by the possessive suffix *-nya* when referring to royalty. This linguistic architecture is not merely decorative; Ghazzali notes that in an earlier era, a misstep in address could provoke a fatal fight with the kris, and that the phrase *anak kurang ajar* was itself a mortal insult.

The remaining sections extend this analysis into the material and ceremonial dimensions of court life: the colour-coded flags and umbrellas of state, the hierarchical spatial arrangement of the palace audience hall (*balai*) with its tiered platforms, the choreography of the royal feast (*jamuan raja*), the egalitarian exception of the mosque, the democratic character of Hari Raya, and the financial obligations of marriage festivities. Throughout, Ghazzali presents these customs not as relics but as living, strictly observed rules whose violation carries social consequences ranging from loss of face to permanent exclusion from court.

The article's evidential basis is entirely the author's own observation and participation in Kelantan court life, supplemented by his knowledge of broader Malay practice. He does not cite other sources or engage with prior scholarship; the text functions as an ethnographic record produced from within the system it describes.

### Key Findings

- The formal address of the Sultan is *Duli Yang Maha Mulia Sultan*; the Crown Prince is *Yang Teramat Mulia Raja Muda*; the Chief Minister (if non-royal) is *Yang Teramat Hormat Dato' Mentri*. The third-person pronoun *dia* is never applied to royalty, being replaced by the suffix *-nya* (p. 273–274).
- State colours are strictly assigned: white for the royal standard, yellow for royalty, green for the Bendahara (Chief Minister), black for the Temenggong (Keeper of the Peace), and red for the Laksamana (military commander); a royal officer's flag is bordered with yellow (p. 278).
- The palace audience hall (*balai*) is divided into four tiers: a dais (approximately 15 by 40 feet) for royalty, a first *tapakan* for ministers and Dato's, a second for gentry, and the ground floor for the peasantry (p. 279).
- At a royal marriage, the *Ikat Tangan* contribution is fixed by rank: the Crown Prince pays $100, the Chief Minister $70, other chiefs $50, and a rich merchant approximately $25; the money is counted aloud in silver dollars and thrown into a gold or silver tray (p. 284).
- The Sovereign is preceded by between seven and forty armed spearmen in Indian file; the foremost carries five spears, the second three, the third two, and the remainder one each. All other members of the royal family and Dato's are limited to five guards (p. 278–279).
- The expression *tidak tahu* ("I do not know") is never used before royalty; the correct formulations are *kurang preksa* (polite) or *kurang cherap* (reserved for conversation with royalty only) (p. 276).

### Conclusion

Ghazzali's definitive takeaway is that Malay court etiquette is not a collection of arbitrary customs but a coherent, internally consistent system of social ordering in which language, space, colour, and bodily conduct all encode the same hierarchical relationships. The system's power lies in its universality: it binds the humblest subject to the highest ruler through shared knowledge of protocol, and its violation—whether linguistic, spatial, or behavioural—constitutes a breach of the social contract itself. By the article's closing pages, the reader understands that to navigate a Malay court successfully, one must internalise a grammar of deference as rigorous as any formal language.

## Context

- The author was Dato' Bentara Luar of Kelantan, a senior court position, and explicitly identifies himself as a courtier and tutor to the Kelantan royal family for thirty years; the article is therefore an insider's account written for an external (predominantly European) audience during the colonial period.
- The text draws exclusively on the author's direct observation of Kelantan court practice, with occasional references to Trengganu variants (e.g., *kaki batil* vs. *tapak serja*); no archival collections or prior scholarly literature are cited.
