# A brief account of the Mah Meri

Iskandar Carey, writing in 1973 for the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 46, No. 2), presents a concise ethnographic account of the Mah Meri, a Senoi-speaking *Orang Asli* group of approximately 1,400 people in southwest Selangor, based on fieldwork conducted in August 1971. The article's central concern is the Mah Meri's striking anomaly: they speak a Mon-Khmer (Senoi) language yet maintain a social structure and culture that is typically Proto-Malay, a combination Carey attributes to a historical migration from the Endau region followed by adaptation to a settled, land-limited environment.

## Summary

The article opens with the long-standing ethnographic puzzle of the Mah Meri's tribal name and origin. Carey's fieldwork resolved the naming question: "Mah Meri" (literally "forest people") is a generic term for all aborigines, while the group's actual self-designation is *Besisi*, meaning "men" or "mankind" (p. 183). On origin, all informants unanimously claimed descent from an area near Endau in southern Pahang/northern Johore, where their ancestors were fishermen who migrated westward seeking better catches. Carey finds this plausible given the presence of the Semelai, another Senoi-speaking group with cultural similarities to the Mah Meri, in that same region today. He argues that the Mah Meri were originally a Senoi people who, upon migrating to Selangor, encountered limited land and a settled way of life that gradually produced a Proto-Malay-style social organisation (p. 184).

The economic section documents a fundamental transformation from fishing and shifting agriculture to a cash economy centred on rubber, coffee, and tapioca, all sold to Chinese middlemen. In Tanjong Sepat, of roughly forty families, only eight still fish, and even these confine themselves to coastal shellfish collection rather than open-sea fishing (p. 185). This shift has eroded the traditional emphasis on collective property in favour of individual family ownership, and has produced the beginnings of a three-tier social stratification: a small ruling class of *batin* and *jinang* and their relatives, a middle class of land-holding families, and a wage-earning proletariat employed on nearby rubber estates (pp. 186–191). The Department for *Orang Asli* Affairs has been a significant agent of change, providing schools, clinics, community halls, and employment through its "Pasokan Pembina" (Construction Corps), while also encouraging the Mah Meri's traditional wood-carving craft for commercial sale in Kuala Lumpur (p. 186).

The social and cultural sections describe a hierarchical but highly egalitarian leadership system in which every level of authority — from the village *penghulu* to the village-complex *batin* — is paired with a designated assistant (*penggawa*, *jukra*, *mangku* respectively), and in which the *batin* rules only with the consent of the village elders (pp. 188–191). The kinship terminology, comprising 17 terms spanning eleven generations, is distinctive among *Orang Asli* systems for its depth and its emphasis on generational rather than bilateral distinctions, and strongly suggests a patrilineal descent system (p. 192). Marriage is overwhelmingly monogamous, with a standard cash bride-price of M$44, and the household (not the village) is the strictly exogamous unit (p. 191). Religiously, the Mah Meri are animists who worship a high god (*tuhan*), revere their tribal ancestors (*moyang*), and consult *bomoh* medicine-men at life-cycle crises, though the latter's prestige has declined with the availability of Western medicine (pp. 193–194).

### Key Findings

- The Mah Meri number approximately 1,400 individuals across fifteen major settlements in southwest Selangor, four of which are coastal (p. 185).
- Average family cash income ranges from M$60 to M$100 per month, supplemented by subsistence crops and poultry; most families are heavily indebted to middlemen (p. 186).
- In Tanjong Sepat, only 8 of approximately 40 families still derive a livelihood from fishing, and these are limited to coastal shellfish collection (p. 185).
- The standard cash component of the bride-price is M$44, with higher amounts for brides whose fathers hold leadership positions (p. 191).
- The Mah Meri kinship terminology comprises 17 terms covering eleven generations, of which only two (*adik* and *moyang*) are borrowed from Malay (p. 192).
- The Selangor state government issues formal letters of authority (*surat kuasa*) to each *batin*, confirming rather than creating their leadership (p. 191).

### Conclusion

Carey's definitive historical takeaway is that the Mah Meri constitute a unique case in the ethnography of Western Malaysia: a linguistically Senoi people whose Proto-Malay cultural profile is best explained as an adaptive response to the environmental and economic constraints of their post-migration homeland. Their contemporary identity, he observes, is being further reconfigured by the cash economy, state employment, and the Department for *Orang Asli* Affairs, which has inadvertently fostered a broader pan-*Orang Asli* self-consciousness among the Mah Meri through inter-tribal contact (p. 194).

## Context

- Fieldwork was conducted in August 1971 at two villages: Tanjong Sepat (near Morib) and Bukit Mangkong, both accessible by car (p. 183).
- The article represents one of the few detailed ethnographic treatments of the Mah Meri available at the time and directly addresses the longstanding historiographical puzzle of their linguistic-cultural mismatch, a question Carey notes has remained unresolved in the existing literature (p. 183).
