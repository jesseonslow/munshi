# Through an unknown corner of Pahang with H. Clifford in 1897. F.W. Douglas

F.W. Douglas published this first-person travel narrative in 1922, recounting an April 1897 expedition through the uncharted interior of northern Pahang undertaken alongside Sir Hugh Charles Clifford, then British Resident of Pahang. The account documents a fourteen-day journey from Pekan up the Pahang River, across the Bukit Lada watershed, and down the Pertang–Tekal–Tembiling river system, a route whose survey records were lost and which remained blank on the map thereafter.

## Summary

Douglas's narrative is a vivid, day-by-day account of a small party of Europeans and Malay and Sakai (Senoi) guides penetrating a region of northern Pahang that had seen little European contact. The party departed Pekan on 8 April 1897, travelled upriver to the Pahang Corporation's mine at Jeram Batang, and then proceeded upstream in dug-out canoes before transitioning to overland travel up the Senandok valley. The crossing of the Bukit Lada divide (approximately 700 ft by aneroid) marked the transition from the Pahang side to the Trengganu side, after which the party descended to the Sungai Pertang and eventually to the Tekal River, where they constructed bamboo rafts and rafted downstream through a series of rapids (pp. 136–138).

The expedition was marked by considerable hardship and misfortune. Douglas describes leech infestations, hornet attacks, waist-deep river crossings in rain, and a series of rafting accidents at rapids including Jeram Jerami, Jeram Tahan Badak, and Jeram Mena, the last of which caused Clifford to lose his raft and all his equipment. The party's food supplies were progressively depleted, and their only remaining fowl was lost at a rapid. Most critically, all time and compass survey records were destroyed, meaning the route was never formally charted (p. 139).

The narrative also provides ethnographic observations, particularly on the Kuantan Sakai, whose blowpipe construction method Douglas compared to that of the Patagonians of South America. Two Sakai men, Chong and Bo'uk, were recruited as guides and later demonstrated their blowpipe skills at Douglas's house, with one blowpipe eventually entering the British Museum collection (p. 136).

### Key Findings

- The expedition ran from 8 to 23 April 1897, covering the route Pekan → Jeram Batang → Senandok valley → Bukit Lada (c. 700 ft) → Sungai Pertang → Tekal River → Tembiling River → Pekan (pp. 135–139).
- The Pahang Corporation's old mill at Jeram Batang was running for the last time before being moved to Sungai Lembing, where all stamps were being concentrated; Nicholson's and Willink's lodes were still being worked at the time of writing (p. 135).
- The party consisted of four dug-outs and twenty men for the initial upriver leg; after rafting began, most men were sent home, leaving a reduced party of six (p. 136).
- The Kuantan Sakai constructed blowpipes by splitting a piece of wood, boring out the half-sections, and binding the two pieces with rotan and gutta-percha—a technique Douglas noted was shared only with the Patagonians (p. 136).
- All time and compass survey records were lost during the journey, and the route was never formally recorded; Douglas stated that "no one has been through that way since" as of 1922 (p. 139).
- The expedition established that the Tekal and Tembiling Rivers rise from the same hill, with the Tembiling running north then bending west before running south parallel to the Tekal (p. 139).

### Conclusion

Douglas's account is primarily a personal travelogue rather than a formal survey report, and its principal historical significance lies in documenting a route through northern Pahang that was never subsequently charted. The loss of all survey records rendered the expedition's cartographic contribution null, leaving "that corner of the map" blank. The narrative nonetheless preserves valuable ethnographic detail on the Kuantan Sakai and provides a rare contemporary description of the Pahang Corporation's mining operations at Jeram Batang during the period of consolidation at Sungai Lembing.

## Context

- This is a contemporary colonial-era travel narrative written by a British official (Douglas) accompanying the British Resident of Pahang; the account reflects the administrative and exploratory priorities of the Straits Settlements colonial government in the 1890s.
- The publication appeared in JSBRAS Vol. 85 (1922), twenty-five years after the event, suggesting it was written from memory rather than from contemporaneous field notes.
