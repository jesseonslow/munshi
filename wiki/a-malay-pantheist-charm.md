# A Malay pantheist charm

R.O. Winstedt, a colonial-era scholar of Malay language and literature, published this article in 1922 in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society (Vol. 86, pp. 261–267). The piece presents a translation and analysis of a Malay pantheist charm called the *Kota Tawhid* ("Fortress of the Unity of God"), attributed to a practitioner named Maklab Setam, and situates it within the broader tradition of Sufi-derived mysticism that permeated Malay popular religion. Winstedt's overarching argument is that the Malay magician's esoteric vocabulary—its pantheist identifications of the self with the Absolute, its numerological symbolism, and its invocation of the Prophet as a cosmological key—represents a direct, if often garbled, inheritance from Persian Sufi metaphysics, particularly the school of al-Jili.

## Summary

Winstedt opens by establishing the intellectual genealogy of Malay mysticism: from animist roots through Hindu-Brahminical *mantra* to Sufi pantheism, with the Shi'ite figure of Ali as the traditional repository of esoteric knowledge in Malay charms. He draws heavily on Snouck Hurgronje's *Arabie en Oost-Indie* (1907) to demonstrate that the "mystic four"—the four elements, winds, Khalifs, and legal schools—functioned as a popular symbolic system for expressing the indivisibility of the Divine, and that this system was not confined to learned circles but formed the "woof and warp" of popular belief across the archipelago. A Kelantanese *pawang* charm invoking the four winds mapped onto the four layers of the body (skin, flesh, sinew, soul) is cited as a concrete illustration of this numerological mysticism in practice.

The core of the article is Winstedt's translation of the *Kota Tawhid* charm, which he copied from a manuscript he judged to be approximately a century old, itself transcribed from an even older source. The charm is structured as a protective incantation in which the practitioner constructs a metaphorical fortress: its roof is the *shahada*, its wall is the name of Muhammad, and its key is the power of God. Each architectural element is then unpacked in a series of "intentions" (*murad*) that map Islamic theological concepts onto the human body and its faculties—the "house" is the body, the "property" is the internal organs, the "possessions" are the ten senses (five outward, five inward), and the "sevenfold fortress" corresponds to the seven attributes of the perfect human being. The theological content is explicitly pantheist: the practitioner is instructed to recite until "I am lost in the universal and absolute Essence of God" and "my self becomes absolute and universal too," a formulation Winstedt links directly to al-Jili's doctrine of the Perfect Man.

Winstedt closes by connecting the charm to two historical episodes: the *Sejarah Melayu* account of Sultan Ahmad of Malacca taking his Sufi teacher into battle against the Portuguese, and a recorded case of heterodox pantheism in Perak roughly thirty years earlier, in which a preacher was imprisoned for teaching that "I am God" and for reducing Islamic ritual to sexual allegory. The contrast between the refined Sufi pantheism of the charm and the vulgar, obscene pantheism of the Perak case serves Winstedt's implicit argument that the same metaphysical source could produce either elevated mysticism or flagrant heresy depending on the practitioner's intellectual capacity.

### Key Findings

- The charm's colophon identifies it as "a chapter to explain the charm called the Fortress of the Unity of God, practised by Maklab Setam," and prescribes recitation four times nightly from Thursday to the following Thursday (p. 262).
- The "sevenfold fortress" corresponds to seven attributes of the perfect human being—life, knowledge, power, will, hearing, sight, and speech—and seven parts of the body used in prostration: forehead, palms, knees, and toes (pp. 263–264).
- The "ten senses" are divided into five outward (sight, hearing, taste, smell, touch) and five inward (consciousness, faith, insight, perception, judgment) (p. 264).
- The Perak heterodox case, recorded approximately thirty years before 1922, involved a preacher who declared "La ilaha illa'llah! Aku Allah!" and was sentenced to gaol; his teaching reduced the 44 members required for a mosque congregation to 40 (the combined fingers and toes of man and woman) and identified Hell with anger and heaven with sexual love (p. 265).
- The *Sejarah Melayu* (Ch. 20) records that Sultan Mansur Shah of Malacca offered a prize of gold and two female slaves to any Pasai theologian who could resolve the esoteric question of whether the inhabitants of heaven and hell remain in their respective states forever (p. 264).
- Winstedt identifies the manuscript source as "probably a century old" and transcribed from "a still older manuscript" according to its colophon (p. 262).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that Malay popular mysticism is not a degenerate or superstitious corruption of Islam but a direct, if often unmediated, transmission of Persian Sufi metaphysics—specifically the pantheist school of al-Jili—into the vernacular religious practice of the Malay world. The charm's sophisticated mapping of theological abstractions onto bodily and numerical categories demonstrates that the "unconscious inheritor" of Gnostic and Neo-Platonic doctrines in the Malay magician was working from a genuine, if popularized, philosophical tradition rather than from mere fantasy.

## Context

- Winstedt was a senior colonial administrator and scholar (D.Litt., Oxford) serving in the Straits Settlements; his access to private manuscripts in East coast Malay states was facilitated by his official position, and the charm's provenance is deliberately obscured ("a promise to its possessor forbids me to divulge its home exactly," p. 262).
- The article draws on the then-current Dutch and British scholarly apparatus on Malay and Javanese mysticism—Snouck Hurgronje, Schrieke, Kraemer, Rinkes, and Nicholson—positioning itself within the early twentieth-century European academic study of Islamic esotericism in Southeast Asia.
