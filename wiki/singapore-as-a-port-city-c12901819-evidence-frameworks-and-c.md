# Singapore as a port city, c.1290–1819: evidence, frameworks and challenges

## Abstract

Singapore was a major Asian port city from the late thirteenth to the beginning of the nineteenth century. After identifying and examining the different types of historical evidence relating to Singapore’s role in the region, this article explains four main analytical frameworks that have been used to organize and interpret this body of evidence: Polanyi’s port of trade and the idea of a port city in general, Bennet Bronson’s upstream–downstream economy, and approaches from the vantage point of strategic location and the longue duree. This article concludes by exploring the consequences the evidence and frameworks of Singapore’s past have on the reconstruction and understanding of Singapore and its historical trajectory.

## Summary

Borschberg and Khoo survey the full range of evidence bearing on Singapore's role as a port city between the late thirteenth and early nineteenth centuries, then evaluate four analytical frameworks used to organise that evidence. The evidentiary base is fourfold: archaeological (excavations at Fort Canning, Old Parliament House, and Empress Place), textual (Chinese court documents, the *Sejarah Melayu*, Javanese court poetry, and European travellers' accounts), cartographic (portolan charts, rutters, and the Eredia maps), and scientific (paleoclimate data on monsoon weakness and tsunami deposits). The authors are careful to stress that each source type carries inherent limitations—archaeological finds are geographically confined and chronologically thin after the fourteenth century; textual references are fragmentary and riddled with toponymic ambiguity; maps reflect what was visible to passing sailors rather than settled reality; and climate data, while promising, remains in its infancy as a historical tool (pp. 2–12).

The four frameworks assessed are Polanyi's "port of trade" model, Bronson's upstream–downstream riverine economy, the concept of strategic location, and the *longue durée*. Each is shown to be partially applicable but insufficient on its own. Polanyi's model fits Singapore's fourteenth-century profile but cannot be empirically sustained for later centuries; Bronson's riverine logic is seductive but lacks the data to confirm sustained upstream trade flows; strategic location risks retro-projecting nineteenth-century advantages onto an earlier period; and the *longue durée* reveals competing cyclical and discontinuous narratives that resist a single master explanation (pp. 12–20).

The article ultimately functions as a methodological roadmap, demonstrating that no single framework or source type can resolve the history of pre-1819 Singapore. The authors argue that the frameworks should serve as interrogative tools rather than prescriptive templates, and that the plurality of evidence demands a correspondingly plural historiography (pp. 19–20).

### Key Findings

- The 1984 Fort Canning excavation yielded approximately 30,000 artefacts, including close to 8,000 glass beads and over 1,000 glass fragments, while the 2015 Empress Place dig recovered roughly three metric tonnes of material dating from the thirteenth to sixteenth centuries—the largest single assemblage in Singapore's archaeological record (pp. 3–4).
- The Ottoman rutter *Muhit* (1558) and Eredia's *Declaracam de Malaca* (c. 1613) both place a functioning port at Singapore, the latter labelling a settlement "Xabandaria" after the shahbandar, corroborating eyewitness accounts by De Coutre and Matelieff of a working port of call in the early seventeenth century (pp. 6, 10).
- Cook et al. (2010) identified persistently weak monsoon winds across tropical South and Southeast Asia during 1351–1368, a finding that aligns with the Portuguese chronicler João de Barros' claim that shifting wind patterns contributed to the abandonment of Singapore and the founding of Melaka (pp. 11–12).
- Jankaew et al. (2008) documented tsunami sand-sheet deposits on Phra Thong Island dating to c. 1300–1450, suggesting a major seismic event in the region that could have disrupted maritime trading networks during the same period as Singapore's political decline (p. 11).
- Miksic concluded that Singapore fulfilled three of Polanyi's four conditions for a "port of trade" (autonomous specialised town, transhipment point, neutral buffer zone) but that the evidence for administered trade with institutional price control remains insufficient (p. 13).
- Zedler's *Universal-Lexicon* (1743) lists Singapore under five separate entries—as a strait, a promontory, an island, a port, and a city—illustrating the persistent ambiguity with which European knowledge systems categorised the place (p. 21).

### Conclusion

The authors' definitive historical takeaway is that the widely held perception of Singapore as a "sleepy backwater *kampung*" before Raffles' arrival in 1819 is a myth. The accumulated evidence—archaeological, textual, cartographic, and scientific—demonstrates a continuous, if fluctuating, history of port activity, settlement, and strategic significance stretching back at least to the thirteenth century. Singapore was "far more intimately connected to its historical predecessors as well as its locational context" than earlier historiography had acknowledged, and the challenge for future research is to hold open the multiple, sometimes contradictory, identities that the sources assign to the place (pp. 20–21).
