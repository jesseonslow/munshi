# Singapore as a port city, c.1290–1819: evidence, frameworks and challenges

## Abstract

The importance of Gua Kajang (Kajang Cave) as a prehistoric archaeological site in Peninsular Malaysia has been recognized since the early 1900s and was designated as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2012. Over the past 10 years, archaeological excavations and research in this cave have produced evidence of prehistoric occupation that broadens the understanding of prehistoric cave archaeology in the Lenggong Valley. This article presents the early lifeways of prehistoric people of Gua Kajang between 11,000 and 4,000 years ago based on two human burials and associated finds, including stone artefacts, fauna remains and earthenware pottery. The material indicates that the early humans of Peninsula Malaysia were cave-dwellers or hunter-gatherers who depended heavily on forest resources.

## Summary

This article reports on the 2007 excavation at Gua Kajang (Kajang Cave), a limestone tunnel in the Lenggong Valley, Perak, and presents the earliest evidence of human lifeways at the site. The study was motivated by a long-standing problem: despite investigations dating back to Evans's 1917 season, the cave's chronology had never been firmly established, and earlier work was poorly documented or methodologically questionable (pp. 4–5). The 2007 excavation targeted the only remaining undisturbed area in the front cave and recovered a 1.6-metre-thick stratified deposit spanning the Late Pleistocene to the Holocene, yielding two incomplete human burials and over 15,000 associated artefacts (pp. 6–7).

The core argument is that the early inhabitants of Peninsular Malaysia were hunter-gatherers who depended heavily on forest resources and practised flexed burial, a mortuary tradition characteristic of pre-Neolithic cultures across Mainland Southeast Asia. The stone tool assemblage—dominated by flake tools and 'Sumatralith' pebble tools made from locally available quartz, quartzite and limestone—showed little variability across stratigraphic layers, indicating continuity in technology from the Late Pleistocene into the Early Holocene. The fauna assemblage, comprising small- to middle-sized mammals (wild boar, deer, monkey, bat) and reptiles (monitor lizard, tortoise), was consistent with a foraging economy drawing on rainforest and riverine resources available within a few kilometres of the site (pp. 13–14).

The emergence of earthenware pottery in the upper layers, provisionally dated to 3,000–4,000 years ago, marks a technological shift associated with the transition to the Neolithic. The authors argue that Gua Kajang was not a permanent settlement but a repeatedly used short-term campsite and burial ground, a pattern supported by the relatively low density of cultural remains compared with primary long-term habitation sites such as the coastal shell midden at Guar Kepah (pp. 14–15).

### Key Findings

- The 2007 excavation recovered **15,329 cultural artefacts** from a 1.6-metre-thick deposit, comprising 700 stone artefacts, 14,592 fauna remains, 37 pottery sherds, and human skeletal material (p. 6).
- **GK 1**, a flexed burial at 120–140 cm depth, was identified as an adult female, 155–163 cm tall, and radiocarbon dated to **10,820 ± 60 BP** (calibrated 12,810–12,651 BP), placing it in the Late Pleistocene (pp. 8–10).
- **GK 2**, found at 60–80 cm depth, was dated to **7,890 ± 80 BP** (calibrated 8,991–8,549 BP), an Early Holocene date; it was buried with a haematite-stained limestone slab, a feature absent from GK 1 and associated with Holocene mortuary culture in the region (pp. 10–12).
- Four radiocarbon determinations (from shell and organic sediment samples) confirm intermittent occupation between approximately **11,000 and 7,000 years ago**, with the upper undated layers extending occupation to at least **3,000–4,000 years ago** (pp. 7–8).
- The stone tool assemblage is dominated by flake tools and oval unifacial/bifacial pebble tools ('Sumatralith' type), with no significant typological change across the Late Pleistocene–Early Holocene sequence, suggesting technological continuity (p. 13).
- Freshwater gastropod shells (*Brotia costula* and *Brotia spinosa*) were present in extremely high densities (1,411 per m² in the GK 1 burial area), sourced from the Sungai Temelong, 3–4 km from the site (pp. 9, 14).

### Conclusion

The 2007 excavation resolves the long-standing chronological uncertainty at Gua Kajang and demonstrates that early humans in the Malay Peninsula were hunter-gatherers dependent on forest and riverine resources who carried out structured mortuary practices involving flexed burial and the interment of used stone tools and fauna. The authors emphasise that there is no strong evidence for continuous or sequential occupation throughout the Holocene; rather, the cave was used intermittently as a short-term campsite and burial ground over a span of roughly 8,000 years or more (pp. 14–15).

## Context

- The study draws on the 2007 excavation data collected by the authors in collaboration with the Centre for Global Archaeological Research (CGAR), Universiti Sains Malaysia, and builds on a century of prior investigations by Evans (1917), Williams-Hunt (1945–52), Chia (1995) and Zuraina Majid (1998).
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the construction of a local and national prehistoric chronological sequence for the Lenggong Valley, a project Zuraina identified as a primary objective of regional research since 1987, and situates the Gua Kajang burials within broader Mainland Southeast Asian patterns of Late Pleistocene–Early Holocene mortuary practice (pp. 5, 13).
