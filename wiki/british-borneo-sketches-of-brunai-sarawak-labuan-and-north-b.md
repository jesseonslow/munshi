# British Borneo: sketches of Brunai, Sarawak, Labuan and North Borneo

W.H. Treacher, who served as Acting British Consul-General in Borneo and would shortly become the first Governor of British North Borneo, published this multi-part survey in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1890. Writing from direct administrative experience accumulated since 1871, Treacher provides a comprehensive political and ethnographic account of the British possessions and interests in Borneo—Brunei, Sarawak, Labuan, and the Company's territory—tracing the island's history from first European contact in 1518 to the final partition between Britain and the Netherlands in 1881. The overarching thesis is that the old monopolistic trading-company model has been superseded by territorial governance, and that Borneo is entering a new phase of development under non-monopolistic British administration.

## Summary

The article opens with a political history of Borneo, tracing European involvement from the Portuguese landing at Brunei in 1518 through the Spanish, Dutch, and English East India Company periods, the failed Balambangan settlement of the 1760s–1770s, the establishment of Sarawak under James Brooke in 1841, the Labuan cession of 1846–47, and the Royal Charter of 1881 creating the British North Borneo Company as a territorial power rather than a trading concern. Treacher frames this history as a narrative of mistaken monopolistic policy that damaged Borneo's trade and agriculture, now being corrected by the new territorial governments, which have deliberately abandoned the old practice of trade monopolies (pp. 14–20).

The bulk of this first instalment is devoted to an intimate portrait of the Brunei Sultanate—its city, governance, feudal system, economy, and the character of its people. Treacher contrasts the city's former grandeur, as described by Pigafetta in 1521, with its present squalid condition, and analyses the structural causes of the Sultanate's decline: the degeneration of the Pangeran aristocracy, the oppressive feudal taxation system, the progressive loss of territory to Sarawak and the Company, and the inability of the central government to maintain order in its remaining provinces. He draws on the *Telselah Besar*, a native chronicle, to trace the introduction of Islam and the historical Chinese connection, and provides detailed ethnographic observations on Malay customs, dress, weapons, boats, fishing methods, and the sago industry (pp. 23–74).

The article also addresses the racial and ethnic composition of Borneo, drawing on Professor Keane's theories of Malay and Indonesian migration to explain the presence of both the aboriginal Dyak tribes in the interior and the later-arriving Mahomedan Malays on the coast. Treacher distinguishes between the "Pagan" and "Mahomedan" aboriginal groups, noting their differing temperaments, and records a native creation myth of the Buludupih tribe. The instalment concludes with the dramatic account of the 1846 British naval expedition against Brunei, which resulted in the formal treaty confirming British influence over the Sultanate (pp. 20–23, 69–74).

### Key Findings

- Borneo's total area is estimated at 263,000 to 300,000 square miles; the British North Borneo Company's chartered territory comprises 31,000 square miles, and the Dutch hold nominal suzerainty over approximately two-thirds of the island, though probably not a tenth is under their direct administrative control (pp. 14, 18–19).
- The city of Brunei's population had declined from Pigafetta's 1521 estimate of 25,000 families to approximately 12,000–15,000 natives and about 80 Chinese by 1884, following a cholera epidemic; Sir Spencer St. John had recorded a low of 25,000 in 1863 (pp. 26–27, 51).
- Brunei's sea-board, which extended some 700 miles when Britain first intervened, had been reduced to 125–130 miles through cessions to Sarawak and the British North Borneo Company (p. 28).
- Annual territorial payments to the Brunei Sultanate: Sarawak contributes approximately $16,000; the British North Borneo Company pays $11,800 for its concessions plus a separate $5,000 annual payment to the Sultan (p. 44).
- The 1774 pepper crop at Brunei was reported at 4,000 pikuls (approximately 240 tons), valued at 17¼ Spanish dollars per pikul, with the Chinese as sole planters; the crop had been abandoned by 1809 owing to the rapacity of the native government (p. 67–68).
- The Muara coalfields, at the mouth of the Brunei River, contain seams 20, 25, and 26 feet thick of excellent quality bituminised coal, worked by private enterprise and of sufficient interest to have drawn a Russian man-of-war to the port in early 1887 (p. 44).

### Conclusion

Treacher's definitive historical takeaway is that the old era of European commercial monopoly in the Malay Archipelago has ended, and that the new model of territorial governance—exemplified by the British North Borneo Company's refusal to trade, Sarawak's Crown-Colony-style administration, and the Dutch abandonment of monopolies—offers genuine hope for Borneo's economic recovery and the welfare of its native peoples. The collapse of the Brunei Sultanate, which he documents in unflinching detail, serves as both a cautionary tale of what happens when European influence is exercised without genuine commitment to native regeneration, and a demonstration that the alternative—active territorial administration—can succeed where the old treaty-based approach failed.

## Context

- Treacher was serving as Acting British Consul-General in Borneo at the time of writing and had been resident in the country since 1871; the article reflects the administrative perspective of a senior colonial official who would become the first Governor of British North Borneo in 1888, and it is explicitly structured to inform a British readership about a territory they knew little of (p. 14).
- The text draws on the *Telselah Besar* (a native Brunei chronicle), Pigafetta's 1521 account, Sir James Brooke's published *Journal* (ed. Mundy, 1848), and the author's own consular and administrative experience, but is fundamentally a colonial-era document that frames native societies through the lens of European civilisational hierarchy, treating the "Pagan" aborigines as more "natural" and the Mahomedan Malays as more "civilised" while regarding both as objects of benevolent paternal governance (pp. 22–23).
