# Raffles, Acheh and the Order of the Golden Sword

C.A. Gibson-Hill's 1956 article investigates the circumstances under which Sir Stamford Raffles received the Order of the Golden Sword from the Sultan of Acheh and subsequently incorporated it into his personal coat of arms, a matter that none of Raffles's biographers adequately addressed. Set against the backdrop of British-Achenese political entanglements between 1810 and 1819, Gibson-Hill argues that the decoration was of trivial significance, that Raffles's elevation of it on his arms was an act of vanity, and that the precise date and reason for its conferral remain irrecoverable from the surviving record.

## Summary

The article begins with the warrant of 2 October 1817, by which Raffles was granted a coat of arms bearing, on a green chief, two oval medallions suspended from a chain representing the Order of the Golden Sword, described as "a personal Decoration . . . conferred upon the said SIR THOMAS-STAMFORD RAFFLES by the Chief or King of Atcheen . . . as a mark of the High Regard of the said King and in testimony of the good understanding which had been happily established between that Prince and the British Government" (p. 1). Gibson-Hill immediately notes the anachronism: Raffles had left Java for England in early 1816, yet the "good understanding" referenced in the warrant did not materialise until the treaty of 22 April 1819, a full two years after the arms were granted. The device is also aesthetically unsuitable for armorial display and bears no relation to Raffles's actual services in Java (p. 4).

Gibson-Hill then reconstructs the political landscape of Acheh during the 1810s. Sultan Jauhar al-Alam Shah, who had succeeded his father Ala'ud-din Mahomet Shah in 1802, was a weak and profligant ruler whose authority was steadily eroded by rebellious provincial chiefs and by the commercial ambitions of Syed Husain, a wealthy Arab-Penang trader of partial royal descent who had his trading privileges withdrawn in 1809 (pp. 7–12). By 1814–15, the three *panglima sagi* (provincial governors) had formally elected Syed Husain's son as a rival sultan, Saif al-Alam Shah, and the resulting civil war disrupted trade along the entire Achenese coast (pp. 13–15). The Penang government, influenced by merchants with stakes in the rebel faction, adopted a hostile posture toward Jauhar al-Alam, refusing him military assistance when he fled to Penang in 1816 (p. 14).

When Raffles arrived at Bencoolen in March 1818, he was already determined to support Jauhar al-Alam, having advocated intervention in Achenese affairs since 1811 (pp. 8–9, 19). Appointed senior commissioner alongside Captain Coombs, who favoured Saif al-Alam, Raffles used his casting vote to secure a treaty with Jauhar at Pedir on 22 April 1819 (p. 16). John Palmer, the Calcutta merchant who had long opposed Raffles and whose trading interests were tied to Syed Husain, reacted with characteristic venom, referring to Raffles as "the Golden Sword" and accusing him of "barefaced predilection" and "documentary Fabrications" (pp. 17–18). Gibson-Hill demonstrates that Palmer's nickname was a double pun on Raffles's knighthood and the Achenese decoration, and that the joke was current before the 1819 intervention (p. 2).

### Key Findings

- The Order of the Golden Sword (*Orang Kaya dari Pedang Mas*) was a minor Achenese honour, with no references prior to the late eighteenth century; only three British sea captains—Douglas Richardson, Robert Smart, and Thomas Forrest—were known to have received it before Raffles (p. 4).
- Captain Thomas Forrest's 1792 account provides the only detailed description of the investiture ceremony: a gold chain with two oval medallions bearing a waved dagger in relief and Arabic filigree, placed around the recipient's neck by a courtier in the presence of the sultan (pp. 4–5).
- Raffles's coat of arms was granted on 2 October 1817, yet the "good understanding" it references was not established until April 1819, creating an irreconcilable chronological gap that Gibson-Hill cannot resolve (pp. 1–2).
- The Penang government's hostility toward Sultan Jauhar al-Alam from 1812 onward was driven by commercial interests: merchants such as Syed Husain profited from illicit trade with rebellious chiefs who evaded the sultan's customs duties, and Husain's five vessels sailed from Penang under British colours to support his son's claim (pp. 11–14).
- Raffles's predilection for Jauhar al-Alam was consistent from 1811, when he first recommended British support for the legitimate ruler, and was not contingent on the Order of the Golden Sword; his 1818 letter from Bencoolen promising to restore Jauhar to the throne predates any plausible receipt of the decoration (pp. 8, 19).
- John Palmer's use of "the Golden Sword" as a nickname for Raffles in correspondence of 1819 confirms that the decoration was known to his political opponents and was treated as a mark of vanity rather than genuine distinction (pp. 2, 17).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive takeaway is that the Order of the Golden Sword was a trivial decoration that Raffles inflated into a symbol of personal importance, and that the circumstances of its conferral—whether in 1810–11 as a junior official, or later in defiance of instructions while Lieutenant-Governor of Java—cannot be established from the available evidence. The article ultimately functions as a demonstration of the limits of the archival record: the decoration's existence is confirmed by the armorial grant and by Palmer's correspondence, but its origin story remains, in Gibson-Hill's words, a problem that even Sir Thomas Browne might have struggled to solve (p. 19).

## Context

- The article draws on the letter-books of John Palmer held in the Bodleian Library, Oxford (English MSS section), quoted via C.E. Wurtzburg's *Raffles of the Eastern Seas* (1954), and on the unpublished correspondence of the Penang and Bengal governments as printed in the *Journal of the Indian Archipelago* (1856).
- The coat of arms warrant was supplied by the Hon'ble Sir George Bellew, Garter Principal King of Arms, underscoring the article's reliance on the College of Arms records as the primary documentary anchor for the entire inquiry.
