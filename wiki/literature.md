# Literature

### Scope and Historical Definition

The study of literature in the Malay world, as documented in the Society's journal, encompasses a broad field that extends well beyond the conventional boundaries of English-language fiction to include Malay-language novels, biographical writing, colonial administrative prose, and the literary representation of indigenous peoples in Western print culture. The records reveal a discipline that has evolved from the early bibliographic compilation of English-language works set in the region, through the critical examination of colonial literary production and its ideological functions, to the historiographical analysis of how biographical narratives are constructed and deployed for political purposes. The region's literary landscape is characterised by a persistent tension between the work of expatriate and metropolitan authors and the emerging body of indigenous writing in both English and local languages, a tension that has shaped scholarly attention from the 1890s to the present.

The earliest systematic attempt to document this field was W. R. Roff's 1982 check-list, which compiled approximately 368 titles of English-language fiction set in or relating to Malaysia, Singapore, and Brunei, spanning from the 1890s to the late 1970s [[1, pp. 62–77]](#ref-1). The list reveals that the majority of entries are by British or Western authors writing during the colonial period, with a significant concentration in the 1920s through 1950s, reflecting the era of greatest British literary production about the region [[1]](#ref-1). Joseph Conrad contributes the largest single-author corpus with eight titles, all set in the Malay Archipelago, establishing the region as a central setting in his literary output [[1, p. 65]](#ref-1). A substantial proportion of entries are adventure or thriller fiction, particularly those set in Singapore, including the "Chandu" series by Own Rutter (1913–1926) and numerous 1950s–1960s crime novels [[1, pp. 69]](#ref-1). The list also identifies multiple pseudonymous authors, revealing that several prolific writers of Malayan-set fiction published under pen names, including Gavin Black (Oswald Wynd), Mark Derby (Harry Wilcox), and Michael East (Morris L. West) [[1, pp. 63–64]](#ref-1).

### Colonial Fiction and the Imperialist Agenda

The most sustained critical engagement with the ideological dimensions of colonial literature in the region appears in Mohamed Rashidi Pakri's 2004 study of Sir Hugh Clifford (1866–1941), the British Resident of Pahang and prolific writer of Malayan subjects [[2]](#ref-2). Pakri traces a discernible evolution across Clifford's early works—from *In Court and Kampong* (1897) through *Studies in Brown Humanity* (1898), *In a Corner of Asia* (1899), to the novel *A Prince of Malaya* (1926)—in which the author's stance shifts from what Clifford himself called "archaeological studies" drawn from life toward an explicit advocacy of British civilizing mission [[2, pp. 107–108]](#ref-2). The central dilemma Pakri identifies is structural: Clifford loved the Malay peasants and their culture while simultaneously serving as an agent of the empire that was dismantling that very culture. His prefatory remarks across the collections reveal this contradiction in its rawest form—he describes his "brown friends" as "very real and very lovable" while declaring himself "a firm believer in the necessity for the intervention of Great Britain in Malaya" [[2, pp. 107–108]](#ref-2).

Pakri demonstrates how the imperialist agenda operates beneath the surface of seemingly sentimental narratives. In "A Tale of a Theft," the depiction of a slave-debtor's imprisonment under the Malay aristocracy functions as an indictment of native governance that implicitly demands British correction; Clifford's narrator explicitly contrasts the prison with Singapore's "churches, and its ballrooms, its societies for the prevention of cruelty" to demand immediate British action [[2, pp. 110–111]](#ref-2). In "Umat," the loyalty of a blind fisherman to his white employer encodes a message of Malay dependency on the colonial ruler [[2, pp. 110–113]](#ref-2). The longest section of the article is devoted to *A Prince of Malaya*, in which Pakri reads the character of Raja Saleh as a literary argument against Malay self-government. Saleh's denationalization in England, his failure to reintegrate into Malay society, and his ultimate death while seeking guidance from the British Resident Jack Norris collectively construct a narrative in which the Malay prince is "unfitted by training to be a Malay raja, unsuited by nature to be an Englishman" [[2, p. 120]](#ref-2). Pakri notes that Clifford's own friend Joseph Conrad criticized the novel for its "immaturity or rather superficialness in character," yet the work's political logic—that the Malays require perpetual British tutelage—remained intact and underpinned half a century of colonial rule [[2, pp. 120–121]](#ref-2).

Clifford's prefatory statement in *In Court and Kampong* (1897) frames British intervention as compressing "six long centuries" of European development into "twenty years of revolutions in facts and ideas" for the Malay race [[2, p. 109]](#ref-2). Pakri suggests that Clifford's return to Malaya in 1927 at age sixty-one to serve as Governor of the Straits Settlements, and his departure in 1929 citing his wife's health, was motivated by his recognition that the Malays he remembered no longer existed [[2, pp. 122–123]](#ref-2).

### Malay-Language Literature and Political Biography

The records also document the use of Malay-language literary sources in historical reconstruction. Laurent Metzger's 1996 article on Joseph Ducroux, a French Comintern agent arrested in Singapore in 1931, makes distinctive use of two Malay-language novels by Shamsuddin Saleh—*Siasat yang Dasyat* (1936) and *Pemimpin Sulit* (1937)—which incorporate a character named Lipan, clearly based on Lefranc/Ducroux, and which depict him as a leading figure in the communist organisation across Malaya and Indonesia [[3]](#ref-3). While the novels contain factual errors, including the wrong arrest date, they provide evidence of Ducroux's contacts with Malay and Indonesian communists, and possibly with a French liaison agent named Marie Arnaud, whose existence is confirmed in a single Hanoi dispatch but who appears nowhere else in the record [[3]](#ref-3).

The construction of biographical literature for political purposes is examined in Huang Jianli's 2009 study of Singapore entrepreneur Lee Kong Chian (1893–1967) [[4]](#ref-4). Huang demonstrates that the vast majority of substantive Chinese-language writings on Lee trace back to a single master account: the 28-page biographical sketch by his friend Tan Ee Leong, completed just before Lee's death in June 1967 and published in revised form in 1969 [[4, pp. 72–75]](#ref-4). This template was reprinted, adapted, and expanded across successive waves of publication interest—the 1980s "Asian values" discourse, China's post-1978 market reforms and renewed interest in overseas Chinese leaders, the 1993 centenary celebrations in Nan'an, and the 1997 book-length biographies by Zheng Bingshan and Li Yuanrong—each layer adding new materials while remaining structurally dependent on Tan's original narrative [[4]](#ref-4). Huang identifies three dominant portraits of Lee—Nanyang capitalist-philanthropist, diasporic patriot, and local "virtuous pioneer"—and argues that none are fixed historical truths but contingent products of changing circuits of power, each emerging in response to specific political and economic conjunctures in Singapore, China, and the broader Chinese diaspora [[4]](#ref-4).

### The Literary and Scholarly Infrastructure

The development of literary and scholarly infrastructure in the region is documented in J. M. Gullick's 1991 assessment of Sir William Maxwell (1846–1897), the Straits Settlements administrator who, in the absence of any professional academic establishment in Malaya, became the pioneering figure in the systematic study of Malay society, language, and history during the 1870s and 1880s [[5]](#ref-5). Gullick's central argument is that Maxwell's unique combination of legal training, deep immersion in Malay village life, multilingual competence, and voracious reading made him the first "first-hand observer with scholastic knowledge" in Malayan studies—a role that no university-trained scholar could fill until well after his death [[5]](#ref-5). Maxwell served as editor of the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (1883–1887), a period when the Society was struggling with declining membership, lack of premises, and a dearth of contributors [[5, p. 21]](#ref-5). He kept the Journal alive through his own prolific contributions, the introduction of *Notes and Queries*, the publication of Malay texts in Jawi, and the cultivation of contacts with French and Dutch scholars [[5]](#ref-5). His bequest to the Royal Asiatic Society in London comprised 105 items of Malay books and manuscripts, including the *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Hikayat Abdullah*, the *Taj al-Salatin*, and the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* in two volumes; in several cases he borrowed originals from Malay owners to have copies made, then returned the originals so they remained in Malaya [[5, p. 17]](#ref-5).

The literary representation of indigenous peoples in Western scientific writing is addressed in John van Wyhe's 2018 article on Alfred Russel Wallace's collecting expedition in the Malay Archipelago [[6]](#ref-6). Van Wyhe challenges the persistent hagiographic framing of Wallace as a lone genius, arguing instead that his celebrated collection of 125,660 natural history specimens was assembled with the indispensable labour of a vast network of local people [[6]](#ref-6). He estimates that at least 1,200 local individuals (possibly twice that number) aided Wallace in some capacity, in addition to more than 100 Europeans, bringing the minimum total to approximately 1,300 people [[6, p. 67]](#ref-6). The article also corrects anachronistic characterizations of Wallace, insisting that he was a Victorian commercial collector-naturalist rather than a "field biologist" or "scientist" in any modern professionalized sense [[6]](#ref-6). This reframing has implications for how the literary and scientific narratives of the period are read: the published accounts that have shaped Wallace's reputation are themselves products of a colonial publishing culture that systematically erased the contributions of local collaborators.

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on this subject reveals a clear chronological progression in scholarly priorities. The earliest phase, represented by Roff's 1982 check-list, was fundamentally bibliographic and descriptive, aiming to document the breadth of English-language fictional engagement with the region [[1]](#ref-1). This compilation drew on the Echols Collection at Cornell University and built on earlier partial bibliographies by Habibah binte Dato Salleh (1968), Donn V. Hart, and Robin Winks (c. 1976) [[1, p. 77]](#ref-1). The list notes juvenile fiction where identifiable and flags uncertain dates in square brackets, acknowledging the limits of the available evidence [[1]](#ref-1).

The second phase, emerging in the 1990s and 2000s, shifted toward critical and historiographical analysis. Pakri's 2004 article situates Clifford's fiction within the broader debate over the literary representation of colonial subjects in Southeast Asia, connecting Clifford's work to the wider tradition of "Asia in Western fiction" identified by Winks (1990) and to the specific Malayan context of the "noble savage" trope used to justify the protectorate system [[2]](#ref-2). Huang's 2009 study applies a similar analytical framework to biographical literature, reading the published literature as a body of cultural production and tracing how each portrait serves the interests of particular political actors at particular moments [[4]](#ref-4). Van Wyhe's 2018 article extends this critical impulse to the scientific and literary narratives of the Victorian period, demonstrating how the hagiographic framing of Wallace as a solitary genius obscures the collective labour that produced his work [[6]](#ref-6).

A recurring methodological concern across these studies is the relationship between literary text and historical fact. Metzger's use of Malay-language novels as historical evidence, while acknowledging their factual errors, illustrates the productive tension between literary and archival sources [[3]](#ref-3). Huang's treatment of Zheng Bingshan's discovery of a short autobiography reportedly written by Lee in December 1961, and Li Chengfeng's frank 1993 conference testimony that Lee had planned to evacuate his family from Singapore before the Japanese invasion, treats such materials as evidence of the gaps and silences in the dominant narrative rather than as the foundation of a new account [[4]](#ref-4). Gullick's assessment of Maxwell similarly demonstrates how the literary and scholarly output of a single individual can serve as a lens for understanding the institutional development of an entire field [[5]](#ref-5).

The records also reveal a persistent concern with attribution and erasure. Van Wyhe and Rookmaaker's 2012 study of Charles Allen, Wallace's largely forgotten assistant, documents how Allen collected over 28,438 specimens during his independent 1860–62 campaign, amounting to roughly a quarter of Wallace's total, yet all specimens preserved in British museums are credited to Wallace alone and no species bears Allen's name [[7, p. 17]](#ref-7). This pattern of erasure—whether of local collaborators in scientific narratives, of indigenous voices in colonial fiction, or of the political contingencies that shape biographical literature—constitutes a unifying thread through the Society's documentation of literature in the Malay world.
