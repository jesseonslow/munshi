# The early land administration and agricultural development of Penang

K.G. Tregonning's 1966 article reconstructs the evolution of land administration and agricultural development in Penang from its founding in 1786 through the 1840s, arguing that the island's land policy oscillated between liberal informality and bureaucratic overreach before ultimately reverting to the principles of its founder. The study draws primarily on the Straits Settlements Fort Road records and W.E. Phillips' 1884 land revenue report to trace how a succession of distant governments—Bengal, then London—imposed land regulations that were consistently out of touch with local conditions, while the agricultural economy shifted from rice subsistence to pepper dominance and finally to spice cultivation.

## Summary

Tregonning's central concern is the mismatch between the administrative apparatus available in Penang and the land policies imposed upon it. Light arrived in 1786 with no land regulations, no survey staff, and no forms; Bengal's instructions were limited to growing "greens, grain and fruit" and, absurdly, rearing sheep. The result was a system of verbal permissions reinforced by a 1788 public declaration of perpetual ownership, with written grants few because the labour of survey and registration simply did not exist. When Bengal finally intervened in 1794—ordering a five-year maximum lease term and the reservation of land for public buildings—the instructions were physically impossible to implement in an eight-year-old town with a crowded waterfront, and Light chose to ignore them. This pattern of distant, ill-informed directives followed by local non-implementation or quiet shelving recurs throughout the period: Bengal's 1796 restriction to twenty-five acres was shelved by MacDonald; London's 1805 order to halt all grants pending a survey stalled cultivation for three years; and Fullerton's 1827 leasing system was defeated by the mismatch between lease terms and the biological realities of spice trees.

The agricultural narrative runs parallel to the administrative one. Pepper, introduced around 1790 through Light's agent Che Kay purchasing seeds in Acheh, became the island's economic mainstay by 1805, with an export value of $400,000—nearly double the next most valuable commodity. Its collapse after the Napoleonic Wars, compounded by overproduction and shipping shortages, drove the price from $17 per pikul in 1804 to under $8 by 1807 and reduced production from four million pounds in 1810 to barely 200,000 by 1835. The spice experiment, initiated by Light's expensive seedlings from Mauritius and expanded through shipments from the Moluccas after 1796, initially failed catastrophically—the Company's Ayer Hitam estate was sold for a mere $9,000 in 1805—but was revived by private planters, particularly David Brown at Glugor, and by protective tariffs from 1819. By the 1830s, nutmeg and clove had displaced pepper as Penang's leading agricultural exports.

Tregonning uses Light's own 1790 agricultural survey, Major Kyd's 1795 report, and the travel accounts of Finlayson (1821) and Balestier (1848) to illustrate the physical transformation of the island from unbroken jungle to a cultivated landscape, while Phillips' exhaustive 1884 report provides the quantitative backbone for the land revenue figures. The article is structured as a thematic treatment of land tenure policy and crop development rather than a strict chronology, with the two threads interwoven to show how administrative failure and agricultural change reinforced each other.

### Key Findings

- Light issued only 28 written land grants during his nine years of office (1786–1794); by 1790, 600 orlongs had been cleared at Sungei Kluan alone, yielding 3,600 lbs of rice (pp. 35–37).
- Pepper production peaked at 2,000 tons by 1805 with an export value of $400,000 in 1804; the price collapsed from $17 per pikul (1804) to under $8 (1807), and by 1835 output had fallen to approximately 200,000 pounds from a 1810 high of 4 million (pp. 42–44).
- Land revenue collected between 1795 and 1822 totalled only $26,000, a negligible sum attributable to the absence of an adequate collection staff and a functional land registry (p. 42).
- By 1823, 760 Cutting Papers had been issued covering 10,842 orlongs; Fullerton's 1827 leasing system (20-year terms, 50 for spices, rent-free first quarter) was a complete failure, as summarised by Crawfurd in 1838 (pp. 40–47).
- The Company's spice plantation in Ayer Hitam Valley, which received over 70,000 nutmeg and 50,000 clove seeds by 1802, was sold in 1805 for $9,000; by 1825, private spice exports had reached 18,000 lbs of nutmegs ($11,500) and 7,000 lbs of cloves ($3,500) (pp. 44–46).
- The leasing system was abandoned in 1841 in favour of crown land sales by public auction, a policy recommended by Commissioner W.R. Young in 1838 and modelled on practice in Canada, Australia, and Ceylon (p. 48).

### Conclusion

Tregonning's definitive takeaway is that Penang's land administration was characterised by a persistent gap between the ambitions of distant governments and the administrative capacity of the island, and that the most successful agricultural outcomes—pepper in the 1790s–1800s, spices from the 1820s onward—emerged not from government planning but from private initiative operating within a permissive regulatory environment. The final return to crown land sales in 1841 represented, in Tregonning's framing, a belated vindication of Light's original liberal approach.

## Context

- The article draws heavily on the Straits Settlements Fort Road records (S.S.(F).R.) and W.E. Phillips' *Papers and Correspondence Relating to the Land Revenue Administration of the Straits Settlements, 1823–1837* (1884), the latter being the principal quantitative source for land revenue figures.
- Historiographically, the article fills a gap identified in its opening paragraph: while Light's character and Penang's commercial history had received attention, the agricultural and land-administrative dimensions of the early settlement had been "little studied." It predates the more systematic land-history work that would follow in Straits Settlements historiography.
