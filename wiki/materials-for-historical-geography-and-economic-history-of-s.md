# Materials for historical geography and economic history of Southeast Asia in nineteenth century Malayan newspapers

R.D. Hill published this bibliographic guide in 1971, cataloguing articles on historical geography and economic history of Southeast Asia that appeared in nineteenth-century Malayan newspapers spanning 1806 to 1900. The article's overarching purpose is to demonstrate that the English-language press of the Straits Settlements—particularly the *Singapore Free Press* and *Straits Times*—constitutes a substantial and underutilised repository of geographical and economic information for the entire region, drawing on material originally published in 123 newspapers, journals, and magazines from across Southeast Asia, Britain, and Continental Europe (p. 151).

## Summary

Hill's article is a systematic index of newspaper articles relevant to the historical geographer and economic historian, organised by region and then by thematic sub-category (general description and travels, economy and trade, agriculture and forestry, manufacturing and transport, minerals and mining, and physical geography). The work covers the full breadth of Southeast Asia as perceived from the Straits Settlements: the Malay Peninsula (both Straits Settlements and Protected States), the Dutch East Indies (Sumatra, Java, Outer Islands), British Borneo (Sarawak, North Borneo, Labuan), Burma, Siam, the Thai Malay States, the Philippines, and Indochina (Cambodia, Cochin-China, Annam, Tonkin).

The article addresses a practical research problem: where to find dispersed geographical and economic information about the region that was never collected into a single reference work. Hill explains that Malayan newspaper proprietors, while focused on local news, routinely reprinted and translated articles from overseas sources—Dutch East Indies papers such as the *Java Bode* and *Handelsblad*, Burmese papers like the *Rangoon Gazette* and *Maulmain Chronicle*, Siamese papers including the *Bangkok Recorder* and *Siam Observer*, and occasionally French and Spanish-language publications from Saigon and Manila (pp. 151–152). This practice created an accidental but valuable archive of regional knowledge accessible in a single location.

Hill's methodology was selective rather than exhaustive: because major articles were almost always reprinted by contemporaneous papers, he consulted only one set of issues per year and included articles exceeding three column-inches in length. He omitted material available elsewhere, such as Straits Branch Royal Asiatic Society reports and the Singapore Municipal Engineer's monthly reports (p. 152). The holdings consulted were microfilm copies held at the University of Singapore Library and the Singapore National Library, as most originals were in too poor a condition for consultation (p. 152).

### Key Findings

- The index draws on 123 distinct newspapers, journals, and magazines as original sources, with the *Singapore Free Press and Mercantile Advertiser* identified as the single most valuable nineteenth-century source, followed by the *Straits Times* (pp. 151–152, 153).
- The *Prince of Wales Island Gazette* (March 1806–July 1827) provides the earliest coverage, including daily weather and temperature reports for Penang and Malacca from July 1815, and an account of the eruption of Mount Tambora (Sumbawa) on 11 April 1815 (pp. 152, 167, 189).
- The *Straits Times* carried an extensive series on the Krakatoa eruption of 1883, with reports spanning from 27 August 1883 through February 1886, making it a key source for that event (p. 189).
- Dutch East Indies papers (*Daghblad*, *Handelsblad*, *Java Bode*, *Courant*, *Vaderland*, *Locomotief*) and Burmese papers (*Rangoon Gazette*, *Maulmain Chronicle*, *Rangoon Times*, *Maulmain Advertiser*) were the most frequently reprinted foreign sources, alongside Siamese papers (*Siam Times*, *Bangkok Recorder*, *Siam Weekly Advertiser*, *Siam Observer*, *Bangkok Summary*) (p. 151).
- The *Singapore Free Press* published annual retrospect articles covering the Straits Settlements from 1852 through the 1890s, providing serial demographic and trade statistics for the colony (p. 166).
- The *Pinang Gazette and Straits Chronicle* (1838–1900) served as a key conduit for Penang-specific agricultural and economic reporting, including detailed coverage of nutmeg disease outbreaks in 1852–1855 and the expansion of coffee-growing in the 1860s (pp. 153, 174).

### Conclusion

Hill's definitive takeaway is that nineteenth-century Malayan newspapers represent a rich, geographically wide-ranging, and largely untapped source for the historical geography and economic history of Southeast Asia. By indexing these materials systematically, he provides researchers with a practical tool for locating specific information—on trade, agriculture, mining, transport, and physical geography—across the entire region without needing to consult the original foreign-language publications scattered across multiple national archives.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: microfilm holdings of Malayan newspapers at the University of Singapore Library and the Singapore National Library, Singapore; some original *Straits Times* issues held by the Straits Times group of companies (p. 152).
- Historiographical contribution: the article functions as a research guide that redirects attention from the standard colonial administrative sources (blue books, gazetteers, official reports) toward the commercial and popular press as a parallel source base for economic and geographical history, a perspective that was relatively underdeveloped in Southeast Asian historiography at the time of publication.
