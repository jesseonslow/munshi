# The Perak Sultanate: ancient and modern

Khoo Kay Kim's 1986 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* traces the Perak Sultanate from its legendary founding in the early sixteenth century through to the accession of Sultan Azlan Shah in 1984, arguing that the sultanate's distinctive rotation-based succession system—rather than primogeniture—has been the defining structural feature of its political history, shaped and reshaped by external pressures from Aceh, the Bugis, Siam, and ultimately the British. The article synthesizes indigenous sources (the *Kedah Annals*, the *Perak Salasilah*, the "Legend of the White Semang") with colonial-era records to reconstruct a continuous narrative of dynastic legitimacy, internal rivalry, and foreign intervention.

## Summary

The article opens by examining three competing origin traditions for Perak. The *Hikayat Marong Mahawangsa* (Kedah Annals) presents Perak as founded by Prince Marong Maha Podisat, son of Kedah's first king, who named the kingdom after a silver arrow (*Indra Sakti*) he shot onto an island (p. 1). W. E. Maxwell's "Legend of the White Semang" offers an alternative: a Johor envoy, Nakhodah Kassim, married a Semang woman whose white blood marked her as extraordinary; their daughter Tan Puteh was found in river foam, and a Minangkabau-descended prince from Johor was installed as Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin Shah (pp. 1–2). The *Perak Salasilah*, which gained official recognition, traces the dynasty to Raja Muzaffar, son of Sultan Mahmud of Melaka, who fled to Klang and was brought to Perak by a Manjung trader (p. 3). Khoo notes that the "White Semang" tradition is not wholly rejected because it accounts for the Perak regalia and *nobat*, including the state sword *Chura Si Manja Kini*, believed to derive from Sanskrit *Churiga (Si) Mandakini* and linked to Alexander the Great (pp. 3–4).

The middle sections trace the sultanate's political evolution through three broad phases. In the early period (1528–1752), succession inclined toward brother-to-brother transfer and accepted matrilineal legitimacy, as when Sultan Muzaffar Shah II (of Siak origin) ascended through his wife's descent from the second Perak ruler (p. 5). Aceh's conquest in the late 1570s disrupted but did not destroy the dynasty; the Achinese installed rulers and married Perak princesses into their own court (p. 5). The Bugis, unlike the Achinese, did not establish hegemony but introduced new offices (Raja Di Hilir, Raja Di Hulu, etc.) and perpetuated political instability through the eighteenth century (p. 7). The "golden age" of Sultan Iskandar Zulkarnain (1752–1765) saw the capital established at Pulau Indra Sakti and the *Bendahara* title transferred from the Megat family to royalty (p. 9).

The nineteenth century brought the most severe challenges. Siam's expansion prompted Kedah to invade Perak in 1818, and the state became a pawn in the Selangor–Siam struggle until the Low Treaty of 1826 (p. 10). The tin boom of the 1840s–1860s intensified succession disputes by giving chieftains independent revenue from mining concessions (p. 11). The 1871–1874 crisis—triggered by Chinese disturbances in Larut and a contested succession between Raja Abdullah and Raja Ismail—culminated in the Pangkor Treaty of January 1874, which installed a British Resident and demoted Sultan Ismail to Sultan Muda (p. 14). The assassination of Resident J. W. W. Birch in 1875 led to the exile of Sultan Abdullah and Ngah Ibrahim to the Seychelles, and the British appointed Raja Yusuf as Regent in 1877 (pp. 14–15).

The final phase, from Sultan Idris Murshidul 'adzam Shah's accession in 1887 to the present, marks Perak's emergence as the preeminent Malay court. Kuala Kangsar hosted the first Conference of Malay Rulers in 1897 and the inaugural Federal Council meeting in 1909 (p. 16). Sultan Idris championed education, founding the Malay College Kuala Kangsar (1905), the Madrasah Mariah (1914), and the Madrasah Idrisiah (1922) (pp. 16–17). The British imposed a ban on descendants of the exiled Sultan Abdullah succeeding to the throne, a restriction modified in 1915 to allow those who could also claim descent from Sultan Idris (p. 19). By the 1960s, a new informal rotation among three families (Sultan Abdul Jalil, Sultan Iskandar Shah, and Sultan Abdul Aziz) had emerged, though Sultan Abdul Aziz deliberately refused to place his son Raja Musa in the succession line to avoid establishing a new tradition (p. 21).

### Key Findings

- Three distinct origin traditions exist for Perak: the *Kedah Annals* (founding by Prince Marong Maha Podisat), the "Legend of the White Semang" (Johor envoy and Semang princess), and the *Perak Salasilah* (Raja Muzaffar, son of Sultan Mahmud of Melaka); only the last received official recognition, yet the "White Semang" tradition uniquely accounts for the regalia and *nobat* (pp. 1–4).
- The Perak succession system evolved through three identifiable stages: an early open system favouring brother-to-brother succession with matrilineal legitimacy accepted; a rotation among three families instituted in the 1820s (descendants of Sultan Abdul Malek Mansur Shah, Raja Inu, and Raja Abdul Rahman); and a post-1874 British-mediated system that preserved the rotation formality while effectively concentrating power in the line of Sultan Abdul Malek Mansur Shah (pp. 5, 10–11, 21–22).
- The Bugis introduced nine new offices below the *Raja Muda* (Raja Di Hilir, Raja Di Hulu, Raja Di Barat, Raja Di Baroh, and five Raja Kechil positions), fundamentally altering the political hierarchy; by the nineteenth century, three of these posts had lapsed (p. 7).
- The Pangkor Treaty of 1874 was the product of competing Straits merchant interests lobbying the Colonial Office; Sir Andrew Clarke was instructed to investigate British intervention to protect investments, and Raja Abdullah's faction won the contest against Sultan Ismail's supporters (p. 14).
- Sultan Idris (1887–1916) transformed Kuala Kangsar into the preeminent royal centre in Malaya: the first Conference of Malay Rulers (1897) and the first Federal Council meeting (1909) were both held there, and the state became the centre of Malay educational development (pp. 16–17).
- The British ban on descendants of the exiled Sultan Abdullah succeeding to the throne was publicly disclosed only in 1915, when a modification allowed those who could also claim descent from Sultan Idris; the ban caused visible distress to Sultan Idris, two of whose daughters were married to Raja Mansur and Raja Chulan (p. 19).

### Conclusion

Khoo Kay Kim's definitive takeaway is that the Perak Sultanate's survival and distinctiveness rest on two foundational principles that have persisted from the earliest period to the present: the new ruler must be *elected*, and he must be a member of the royal lineage (*Waris Negeri*) either patrilineally or through the distaff side (p. 22). The rotation system, while important as a formal mechanism since the 1820s, was always subordinate to these deeper principles. British intervention, though it disrupted the system twice (1874 and 1877), ultimately preserved the rotation's formality while concentrating succession in a single patrilineal line. The sultanate today retains its reputation as one of the two oldest in Malaysia and the most noble, its lineage tracing directly from the ancient Melaka dynasty descended from Sang Sapurba of Bukit Si Guntang (p. 22).

## Context

- The article draws heavily on W. E. Maxwell's late-nineteenth-century research and the MBRAS reprint of *A History of Perak* (Winstedt & Wilkinson, 1974), as well as Barbara Watson Andaya's *Perak: The Abode of Grace* (1979), positioning itself within the MBRAS's long-standing commitment to documenting Malay state histories from indigenous and colonial sources.
- The genealogical charts (Genealogies I–V) and maps (Perak River Basin, Mid-Nineteenth Century Perak, Modern Perak) included in the article serve as reference tools for the MBRAS archival collection on Perak royal lineage.
