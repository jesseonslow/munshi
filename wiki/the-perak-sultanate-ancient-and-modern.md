# The Perak Sultanate: ancient and modern

J. B. Scrivenor, Geologist to the Federated Malay States Government, published this paper in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1911. Drawing on seven years of fieldwork conducted in the course of economic geological surveys, he attempts to reconstruct the deep geological history of the Malay Peninsula from the Carboniferous to the present. His overarching thesis is that the Peninsula's present topography — its three distinct types of mountain ranges and its coastal plains — is the product of a sequence of events beginning with deep-sea deposition off the ancient continent of Gondwana-land, followed by granite intrusion, folding, faulting, and ultimately denudation and recent submergence.

## Summary

The paper identifies three types of mountain ranges in the Peninsula and uses them as chronological markers. The first, composed of quartzite, clay slate, and conglomerate, represents the Gondwana Series (early Permian) deposited in shallow seas off the coast of Gondwana-land. The second, limestone and calcareous clay slates of Carboniferous or Permo-Carboniferous age, records a deeper, clearer sea environment punctuated by periodic volcanic eruptions. The third and youngest type, the great granite ranges, is probably Cretaceous in age, and Scrivenor argues that its intrusion was the principal agent in shaping the Peninsula's present configuration (pp. 1–4).

Scrivenor reconstructs the mechanism by which the granite came to occupy its present position. After the Gondwana and calcareous rocks were buried beneath younger sediments, lateral pressure folded them into a series of low arches trending approximately north-north-west to south-south-east. A vast molten granite magma beneath these arches, aided by entangled steam, forced its way upward through the weakest points — the apices — shattering the arches into blocks that dropped into the magma. The resulting complex of faulted, folded, and intruded rocks was subsequently exposed by denudation, the harder rocks (granite, quartzite, conglomerate, limestone) resisting erosion to form the mountain ranges and coastal islands, while the softer clay slates were worn away to form the flat coastal plains (pp. 5–7).

A particularly significant finding concerns the pre-Carboniferous history. At Gopeng in Perak, boulder clays overlying the limestone contain fragments of tourmaline-schists and quartz-tourmaline rocks that Scrivenor identifies as derived from an older, Palaeozoic granite — a mass that existed before the Carboniferous calcareous rocks were deposited. These clays bear a strong resemblance to glacial till, and Scrivenor correlates them with the Talchir Boulder Bed of India and equivalent glacial deposits in Australia and South Africa, suggesting that glaciers descended from the hills of Gondwana-land's coast during the early Permian, depositing tin-bearing boulder clays that are still being mined (pp. 11–12).

### Key Findings

- The Tahan Range, part in Pahang and part in Kelantan, contains the highest peak in the Peninsula and is composed of quartzite, clay slate, and conglomerate of the Gondwana Series, early Permian in age (p. 2).
- At Gopeng, a limestone cliff approximately 800 feet high marks the position of a major dislocation; the distance across the main granite range to the calcareous rocks in Pahang is about thirty miles, representing the minimum width of crust that sank into the granite magma (p. 7).
- The boulder clays at Gopeng contain common boulders of tourmaline-schists, quartz-tourmaline rocks, and quartz-tourmaline-mica rocks, all older than the Mesozoic granite, indicating a Palaeozoic tin-bearing granite that predates the Carboniferous (p. 11).
- Pulau Nanas, between Pulau Ubin and the Johore coast, is composed of Carboniferous volcanic lavas and ashes containing angular fragments of an older Palaeozoic granite, proving that a granitic mass existed on the sea floor before the volcanic eruptions (pp. 9–10).
- Recent corals and sea-shells at the base of Elephant's Hill at the mouth of the Kedah River, combined with the low-lying padi-fields of Alor Star, indicate that the Peninsula was an island in recent geological times and that the flat coastal areas were once shallow sea (pp. 8–9).
- The main dislocations (faults) trend roughly N.N.W.–S.S.E., with evidence of transverse dislocations at right angles, as demonstrated by a seven-mile-wide block of limestone and Gondwana rocks let down between two transverse faults at Gopeng (p. 8).

### Conclusion

Scrivenor's definitive takeaway is that the Malay Peninsula's present topography is the end-product of a long sequence: deep-sea Carboniferous deposition off Gondwana-land, shallow-water Permian Gondwana sedimentation with glacial tin-bearing boulder clays, burial and folding into arches, Cretaceous granite intrusion that shattered those arches, and finally prolonged denudation that carved the harder rocks into ranges and the softer rocks into plains, with a recent episode of submergence that turned the low coastal areas into sea and the hills into islands (pp. 12–13).

## Context

- Scrivenor held the appointment of Geologist to the Federated Malay States Government, and the paper explicitly states it was written to show how "the information gained during the economic work of the last seven years assists the science of geology" (p. 1). The study is thus framed by the colonial mining administration's interest in tin geology.
- The paper's most practically significant finding — that a Palaeozoic granite older than the Mesozoic granite gave rise to a separate, earlier tin-field — was of direct relevance to the tin mining industry at Gopeng and elsewhere in Perak (pp. 11–12).
