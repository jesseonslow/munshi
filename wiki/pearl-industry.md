# Pearl industry

The pearl industry in the Malay Archipelago, as documented in the Society's literature, is known almost exclusively through a single but remarkably detailed investigation into the alleged reproductive properties of certain pearls, conducted by the physician and naturalist N. B. Dennys in Singapore in 1878. The records do not attest to a broader commercial pearl trade, industrial infrastructure, or systematic economic analysis of pearl harvesting in the region; rather, they preserve a focused natural-historical inquiry into a phenomenon that occupied the attention of European residents across the Straits Settlements and the Malay Peninsula, and which drew upon testimony from Borneo, Java, and the wider Archipelago [[1, pp. 31–37]](#ref-1).

## Scope and Historical Definition

Dennys's paper, read before the Singapore branch of the Royal Asiatic Society on 28 February 1878, addresses what he terms "Breeding Pearls"—pearls alleged to possess the capacity to reproduce fresh specimens under specific conditions [[1, p. 31]](#ref-1). The pearls in question were reputed to come chiefly from Borneo and Java, although they were said to be found in nearly all islands of the Archipelago and even in Singapore itself [[1, p. 31]](#ref-1). No specific native name distinguished them from ordinary pearls [[1, p. 31]](#ref-1).

In terms of physical appearance, the specimens shown to Dennys resembled ordinary jeweller's pearls, though slightly more irregular in shape. The largest of regular shape he had seen measured something over three sixteenths of an inch in diameter, while an irregularly formed one exceeded one quarter inch in length by three sixteenths in width; the smallest was a mere pin-point of microscopic dimensions [[1, p. 31]](#ref-1). As regards substance, they were alleged to present exactly the same laminated section as the ordinary pearl when cut. Professor Huxley examined one specimen at the request of a lady resident in Singapore and subjected it to numerous tests, reporting the result to be that it was absolutely indistinguishable from the ordinary pearl used for jewellery [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1).

Mr. H. B. Woodford, who had himself bred a considerable number, provided further taxonomic detail. He reported that breeding pearls were found in several oyster and clam species, including those known as *Tridacnae* with a fan-shaped shell. The shells yielding them abounded chiefly on the coast of Borneo, but were also found throughout the Malayan Archipelago and even in Singapore [[1, p. 33]](#ref-1). Woodford had found one at Tanah Merah Kechil beach, and out of fifteen or twenty shells he picked up there, he came across only one bearing a breeding pearl [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1). Mr. P. Marcus told him he had extracted them from all descriptions of bivalve shells, the larger the shells the larger being the pearls; in one case he took a very large one from *Tridacna gigas*, the giant clam, of which a specimen may be seen at the foot of the stairs leading to the Raffles Library [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1).

The pearls when discovered were usually found embedded close to the valves of the shell, though in some cases found adhering to the fish [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1). They were almost invariably spherical when found, but when commencing to breed, changed their shape to a more or less irregular oval, with layers of scales visible to the naked eye; in some cases the scales were themselves spherical [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1).

## The Alleged Reproductive Process

The process by which reproduction was held to take place involved only very simple preparations. Four or five large-sized pearls—most people having begun with three—were placed in a small chip or other box with as many grains of uncooked white rice as the experimenter chose, from fifteen to thirty being usually employed [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1). Absolute freedom from disturbance was, by some, alleged to be necessary for the formation of the new pearls, while others denied that this made any difference if they were not unduly handled or shaken [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1).

If examined at the end of a certain period—about a year—objects resembling small seed pearls would be found strewn about the bottom of the box, while in many cases the original pearls themselves would be found to have increased in size. If again left untouched for a further period of six months or a year, some of the seeds would be found to have become larger, while fresh seeds would have formed [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1). Each grain of rice presented a curious appearance: a small circular bite seemed to have been taken from the end of each, the number of seed pearls agreeing with the number of grains thus affected [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1).

Dennys examined a collection belonging to a lady resident in the Colony at the end of December 1877. It consisted of about five large or medium-sized pearls and, as nearly as could be estimated, about 120 small-sized pearls, varying from the most minute speck to a size large enough for use in certain descriptions of jewellery. Every grain of rice was, so far as he could see, marked as described—looking in fact as if some beetle had gnawed away a portion of its end. The larger pearls had been in their present box for about twenty years; she had only put four or five into the box when it was just closed, and except to show to persons interested, the box had always been kept shut [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1).

Mrs. ——, wife of Dr. Rowell, the principal Medical Officer of Singapore, provided a written account of her own experience. She had three breeding pearls given to her in June or July 1874. On 17 July she shut these three up with a layer of cotton wool above and below them and some few grains of a very fine rice, called locally "Pulot" rice. On 14 July 1875, the box was opened in the company of two or three friends, and they discovered twelve pearls of various sizes—the three original ones standing out distinctly by their greater size, though some of the newly bred ones were by no means insignificant to look at. One or two were about the size of a pin's head and perfectly round. The rice looked crumbly and worm-eaten [[1, pp. 32–33]](#ref-1).

Woodford, however, named a considerably longer period than that specified by the other correspondents. He stated that it usually took eight years for a seed pearl to increase to four times its original diameter, that is, about one quarter of an inch, though he had seen one over one quarter inch in measurement produced in that period [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1). After a certain time, which appeared to be uncertain, breeding pearls died and changed their lustrous colour to a dirty flake white, the outer scales appearing to have peeled off [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1).

## Scientific Objections and Alternative Explanations

The evidence against the existence of breeding pearls could be classed under two heads: the results of positive experiment, and a scientific demonstration of the absolute impossibility of pearls breeding pearls [[1, p. 35]](#ref-1). Dr. Robertson, well known to members of the Society, having been informed that not only would pearls breed, but that a resident in Singapore had actually added to her income by selling the pearls thus produced, obtained four or five specimens which were carefully sealed up at the Singapore Dispensary in a box with grains of rice, as directed by the donor. This operation was performed in the presence of Dr. R. Little and Mr. Jamie, and the box was then put away. At the expiration of the period directed, the box was opened in the presence of those gentlemen, the seal being intact, and the result was nil—no trace appeared either of pearls or of anything which could form a nucleus around which a pearly growth might in time take place [[1, p. 35]](#ref-1). Mr. C. K. E. Woods, Solicitor, also reported that he had tried the experiment once but did not succeed in increasing the stock [[1, p. 35]](#ref-1).

Dennys acknowledged that negative evidence was always weaker than positive, illustrating his point with an incident in which he and friends imported a selection of English flower seeds: not one of thirty-two varieties in his own case came up, but one recipient was more fortunate, and all their negative evidence that the seeds would not grow was set aside by the simple fact that in one case they did [[1, p. 35]](#ref-1).

The scientific objection was straightforward: the oyster or mussel pearl was, as everybody knew, usually the result of a mucus secretion deposited by the animal on some foreign substance, though this foreign substance was not always detectable by analysis. Under no conceivable circumstances could mucus breed mucus when it had once hardened into the lustrous nacre of a pearly surface [[1, p. 36]](#ref-1).

Dennys's own tentative explanation was that the pearls produced resulted from the labours of some insect which had existed in the original oyster and, as a foreign irritant body, caused the deposition of a pearly secretion. It may be that this insect existed and bred in rice under certain circumstances, and that the original pearls had very little, or perhaps nothing, to do with the production of new ones [[1, p. 36]](#ref-1). Woodford had independently attributed their formation to insects, though this otherwise feasible theory was at variance with all received beliefs as to the formation of the pearl within the oyster [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1).

Dennys cited as a parallel the account by Mr. Frank Buckland in the second volume of *Curiosities of Natural History* of an old China dinner-plate, which had been in the possession of its owner's family for nearly three hundred years, that had broken out in an eruption of crystals resembling shrubs and flowers. Buckland concluded that this was a natural production—a mineral parasitic growth resulting from some chemical decomposition of the clay of which the plate was originally formed [[1, p. 36]](#ref-1). Dennys cited this simply as a parallel to the apparently spontaneous production of pearls, concluding that granting the truth of all that was alleged, the matter had not yet gone beyond Topsy's "'Spect they grew" [[1, p. 37]](#ref-1).

Dennys also noted that he had been informed that both Sir James Brooke and Admiral Keppel had made mention of breeding pearls in their published works, though a tolerably thorough search through the Raffles Library had not enabled him to find the notices in question, and the present Raja Brooke of Sarawak told him he did not know of them [[1, p. 37]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on the pearl industry is confined to a single paper, Dennys's 1878 investigation, which reflects the natural-historical preoccupations of the Straits Settlements in the 1870s. The paper is notable for its methodological rigour: Dennys collected testimony from multiple independent witnesses who had not communicated with one another on the subject, examined physical specimens directly, and reported both positive and negative experimental results with equal candour [[1, pp. 31–37]](#ref-1). His overarching thesis was one of cautious agnosticism—the positive testimony of multiple independent witnesses was compelling, yet no accepted scientific mechanism accounted for the phenomenon [[1, p. 31]](#ref-1).

The paper also illuminates the social context of natural-historical inquiry in colonial Singapore. The witnesses included a lady resident whose collection had been maintained for twenty years, the principal Medical Officer's wife, a schoolmistress, a solicitor, and a gentleman who had personally bred pearls [[1, pp. 32–35]](#ref-1). The involvement of Professor Huxley in examining a specimen underscores the connections between colonial naturalists and metropolitan scientific authority [[1, p. 32]](#ref-1). The reference to the Raffles Library as a repository for published accounts of the region's natural history, and to the giant clam specimen displayed there, situates the inquiry within the institutional infrastructure of colonial knowledge production [[1, pp. 34]](#ref-1).

No subsequent papers in the Society's journals are attested in the available records as addressing the pearl industry, breeding pearls, or pearl oyster harvesting in the Malay Archipelago. The topic, as far as the archival record shows, was investigated once, thoroughly, and left in a state of unresolved scientific ambiguity.
