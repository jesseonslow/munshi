# Stone sculptures from south-west Borneo (Kalimantan Barat

Tom Harrisson, former Curator of the Sarawak Museum (1947–66), published this article in 1973 to report the discovery of five stone sculptures from the middle Kapuas river region of Kalimantan Barat, found in the Capuchin Mission museum at Tilburg, Holland. The overarching argument is that these finds nearly double the known corpus of Bornean stone sculpture in the round and point to a once widespread, now largely lost tradition of stone working across the island.

## Summary

Harrisson begins by establishing the extreme rarity of stone carving in the round in Borneo. Prior to this discovery, the record was confined to four half-life-size figures in soft white schist found near Sarikei in south-west Sarawak, attributed to the recently extinct Seru people, and a single life-size figure seen at Long Po on the upper Kayan in 1945. The island was otherwise rich in megalithic and micro-megalithic traditions but had produced almost no true sculpture. The five new pieces from the Kapuas region—three larger sandstone figures and two small carved pebbles—therefore represent a significant expansion of the known record.

The three sandstone figures, collected by P. Cassenius in the 1930s from the debris of an abandoned village (Kampong Semendai near Nandang) and from the Christian village of Nazareth, are described as rough but purposeful works, likely serving an apotropaic function analogous to the well-known perishable wood carvings of interior Borneo. Harrisson argues they belong to a single school of thought and may share a remote common origin with the Seru figures, both deriving from a stock of Bornean animists who migrated along the classic Kapuas-to-Sarawak corridor during the early Iron Age. A hardwood carving (104 cm high) from the same Tilburg series shows striking formal similarities—particularly in the treatment of the head and ears—suggesting a continuous tradition from stone into wood.

The dating problem remains unresolved. Harrisson leans toward an ancient origin, noting that the rounded sculpting tradition in Borneo extends back to the early limits of the metal age, and that the pecking technique visible on one figure is characteristic of prehistoric megaliths dated to c. 1,000 A.D. in the Sarawak River delta. He acknowledges, however, that more recent manufacture cannot be excluded, and that the Hindu stone figures (nandi, ganesa, yoni, lingga, buddhas) known from the same Kapuas sector—where Indian influence was strongest between the 9th and late 15th centuries—may have provided a marginal stimulus. His definitive position is that the overall "feel" of the work is endemic and native Bornean, even if the idea of expressing form in stone was externally prompted.

### Key Findings

- The five Kapuas sculptures (three sandstone figures, Tilburg nos. A.VI. 437–439; two small black pebbles, A.VI. 423–424) were collected by P. Cassenius in the 1930s from Kampong Semendai near Nandang and from Nazareth (1935), in the middle Kapuas river region behind Pontianak (pp. 179–180).
- The largest sandstone figure (no. 439) clearly stood upright, partially buried in the ground, consistent with the placement of apotropaic figures just beyond the village edge (p. 180).
- A hardwood carving from the same series (A.VI. 436), 104 cm high, shows strikingly similar features—especially the asymmetrical slanting head and distinctive ear treatment—supporting a shared stylistic tradition (p. 181).
- No megalithic work in Borneo has been dated as early as the Stone Age; the earliest dated megalithic activity (pecked designs in the Sarawak River delta) is c. 1,000 A.D. (p. 181).
- Hindu/Indian stone figures (nandi, ganesa, yoni, lingga, buddhas) are known from the Kapuas region, with Indian influence strongest in the 9th to late 15th centuries, particularly during the final stages of the Majapahit Empire (1293–c. 1520 A.D.) (p. 182).
- The two carved pebbles are unique in the entire island; all other known pebble use in Borneo involves plain, natural stones, making these the only examples of "sculpture in miniature" (p. 182).

### Conclusion

Harrisson concludes that the Kapuas stone works—both large and small—are likely the surviving last echoes of a once abundant, island-wide interest in stone sculpture, now recovered only by chance, much as monumental megalithic activity was evidently once far more widespread and intense than can be recognised today.

## Context

- Harrisson's argument draws on his extensive unpublished field material held at the Sarawak Museum, accumulated during his curatorship (1947–66), and on direct examination of the Tilburg collection in February 1973 (p. 183).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Bornean prehistory by extending the known geographic range of stone sculpture from the Sarawak-Kalimantan border region southward into the Kapuas basin, and by proposing a possible common animist origin linking the Seru and Kapuas traditions.
