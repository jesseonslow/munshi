# Tin and its native land. Facsimile reprint

Xavier Brau de Saint Pol Lias, a French naturalist and explorer, published "Tin and Its Native Land" in *Popular Science Monthly* in June 1890 (translated from the *Revue Scientifique*), and it was reprinted as a facsimile in the *JMBRAS* in December 2020. Writing from direct observation of Chinese-operated tin mines in Perak and Pahang during the late 1880s, Lias argues that the Malay Peninsula is not merely the most ancient but also the most productive tin-producing region in the world, and that it may have been the original cradle of tin metallurgy from which knowledge diffused to Europe over millennia.

## Summary

The article is a popular-science survey that moves from the physical properties of tin to its global production, its ancient and medieval uses, and finally to the question of its ultimate geographic origin. Lias opens with a vivid first-person account of his visit to Taiping, Perak, in the late 1880s, describing the landscape of alluvial tin workings—the mounds of white earth, the Chinese coolies on cocoa-trunk ladders, the primitive wooden Archimedes screws lifting water from open pits, and the brick furnaces with bamboo bellows where ore was smelted into the familiar "pig" ingots. He notes that no European operator had yet been established in the country, and that the rudimentary methods caused significant metal loss, which the Chinese and Malays poetically called "young tin" returned to the earth to ripen.

The middle sections trace tin's history from Sumerian cuneiform references (the name *anaku* in a hymn to fire, some 5,000 years old) through Egyptian bronze statuettes of the pyramid age, Homer's *kassiteros*, and the Phoenician trade from the Cassiterides. Lias then devotes considerable space to medieval European uses—roofing, devotional objects, pewter plate, commemorative medals, and even the burial of tin effigies with the dead—before turning to the production statistics of his own era. He cites M. de Ramaix's figures for the Dutch East Indies (10,000 tons), Cornwall (8,000 tons), and Australia (7,000 tons), and contrasts these with his own estimate of 6,139 tons from Perak alone in 1881, arguing that the peninsula's total output exceeded 24,000 tons and thus surpassed all other sources combined.

The article's culminating argument, following the French geologist Germain Bapst, is that the Malay Peninsula was the original source of tin knowledge in the ancient world. Lias points to the etymological parallels between the Malay *tima* (and its compounds *tima poute* for tin and *tima itam* for lead) and the European *tin*, *zinn*, *tenn*, and Pliny's *plumbum candidum* / *plumbum nigrum*, to suggest that the word and the metallurgical knowledge travelled from Malacca to Europe by slow migration, "over the heads of the Assyrians and Greeks." He closes with a characteristically expansive flourish, imagining the peninsula in antiquity as a civilisation at the head of the world while Europe was still in its "period of orang-sakeys."

### Key Findings

- Lias estimates Perak's tin export to Penang in 1881 at 6,139 tons; the Malay states exported approximately 2,500 tons and the Siamese states 7,000 tons in 1877, totalling 9,500 tons for the peninsula that year (pp. 195–196).
- M. de La Croix's 1888 French government publication gives the peninsula's production from official documents as over 24,000 tons, "more than half that of the entire globe" (p. 199, n. 1).
- M. de Ramaix's figures for the Dutch East Indies (10,000 tons), Cornwall (8,000 tons), and Australia (7,000 tons) total 25,000 tons; Lias argues the world total was "not less than 45,000 tons" (p. 195).
- The price of tin before the copper crash had reached $800 per ton (p. 196).
- The oldest known textual reference to tin is the Sumerian *anaku* in a hymn to fire, some 5,000 years old; the next is an Egyptian bronze statuette of the pyramid age, c. 3600 B.C. (p. 194).
- The Chinese washing process reduced foreign matter to 25–35 per cent before smelting; the metal was cast into cubic ingots with flared "ears" for handling (p. 194).

### Conclusion

Lias's definitive historical takeaway is that the Malay Peninsula, then still largely covered in virgin forest and inhabited by tigers and orangutans, was in antiquity the most advanced tin-producing civilisation on Earth and the probable origin point from which both the metal and its name spread westward to Europe. The article is ultimately an argument for the primacy of Southeast Asian metallurgical history, framed in the popular-science idiom of the 1890s and underpinned by the author's own field observations of Chinese mining operations in British-administered Perak.

## Context

- The author was a French scientist and explorer writing for a general-audience journal; the piece is a popular-science dispatch rather than a peer-reviewed scholarly study, and its etymological and civilisational claims (particularly the Malacca-as-origin thesis) reflect the speculative diffusionism common in late-nineteenth-century French science.
- The article was reprinted in the *JMBRAS* as a facsimile with an editor's note clarifying that "Malacca" in nineteenth-century usage referred to the Malay Peninsula as a whole, and that the illustrations depict workings of the British Pahang Corporation (p. 199, n. 2).
