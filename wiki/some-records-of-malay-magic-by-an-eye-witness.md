# Some records of Malay magic by an eye-witness

W.W. Skeat, a senior British colonial administrator in the Straits Settlements, published this first-person ethnographic record in 1898, documenting Malay magical and ritual practices he personally witnessed in the Negri Sembilan region. The article's overarching thesis is that direct observation—rather than second-hand reportage—yields the "rigid and accurate knowledge" necessary to understand these ceremonies, and that their apparent irrationality dissolves once interpreted through the principles of sympathetic magic as articulated by Frazer.

## Summary

Skeat's account is organized around several distinct ceremonial complexes he observed or had described to him by local pawang (medicine men) and their assistants. The most extensively treated is the padi (rice) ceremony, in which the first seven ears of a selected sheaf are ritually extracted, swathed in cloth, placed in a basket-cradle with an egg, a jungle-nut, a shell, and an iron nail, and carried home as a "rice-child" to be treated with the same taboos as a newborn for three days. Skeat records the full sequence of prayers, the "Ten Prayers" (doa sapuloh), the omens observed during the cutting, and the subsequent pounding and storage of the grain. He then moves to a healing ceremony in which a male pawang, after being "shampooed" in incense smoke, enters a trance state, is possessed by a tiger spirit, and licks the patient's body from head to foot before letting his own blood over the prostrate form.

The article also documents the Lanchang (spirit-boat) rite for expelling evil spirits, the elaborate kelong (fishing-stake) offering ceremony involving three sacrificial trays suspended from mangrove trees and wooden tripods, and the invocation of water-spirits (mambang tali harus) with their named hierarchy of sea-chiefs and angels. Throughout, Skeat provides the Malay text of charms and invocations alongside his own translations, and includes a vocabulary table of the Bhasa Hantu (spirit language) used by the pawang during possession. His interpretive framework, drawn explicitly from Frazer's *Golden Bough*, identifies the rice-child as an instance of sympathetic magic in which the padi is induced to bear by pretending it has already borne a child, the sugar-cane as a fertility pole analogous to the European May-pole, and the pawang's eye-rolling and throat-clicking as homoeopathic imitations of rice-whitening and slow eating.

The article closes with a brief digression into the matrilineal land-tenure customs of the Negri Sembilan, linking them to the original Sakai (aboriginal) inhabitants and the intermarriage of the first Malay settlers with Sakai women—a passage that reveals Skeat's broader administrative interest in native customary law.

### Key Findings

- The padi ceremony's "cradle" measured approximately fourteen inches in length and contained, at the moment of Skeat's inspection, a strip of white cloth, a piece of partly coloured thread (benang pancharona), an egg, a hard jungle-nut (buah kras), a shell called "krang," a long iron nail, and five hasta of red cloth for slinging (p. 3).
- The "Ten Prayers" (doa sapuloh) recited by the pawang without taking breath invoke, in sequence: Allah, Muhammad, the five daily prayers, the Five Indras, the opening of the door of sustenance, seven ranks of exaltation, the gate of Rengkiang, the gate of Paradise, the child in the mother's womb, and the child made by Allah, concluding with the names Isa, Karun, and Daud (p. 6).
- The healing ceremony's spirit-language (Bhasa Hantu) vocabulary table records 17 Malay-to-spirit-language correspondences, including *anak* (child) = *demit*, *mati* (dead) = *merat*, *ayam* (fowl) = *mendong*, and *ayam* (water) = *jam jam* (pp. 23–24).
- The kelong offering ceremony lasted a full hour and a half, required exactly fourteen participants (seven males and seven females), and the central tray contained the head of a black goat, five portions of cooked fowl, five portions of raw fowl, seven kinds of coloured rice, five portions of cakes, twenty-eight ornamental rice receptacles (fourteen katupat and fourteen lepat), five receptacles of sugar-cane-juice, one receptacle of goat's blood, five lighted tapers, five chews of betel-leaf, five cigarettes, and five silver coins (pp. 30–32).
- The Lanchang invocation (Lagu Pemanggil) comprises 61 lines describing a golden-plated ship with a mast named "Raja Mendelu," a rudder named "The Hanging Bees' Nest," and oars named "The Centipede's Feet," crewed by fourteen oarsmen (twice seven) (pp. 16–19).
- The 1891 census showed that in Rembau the native women outnumbered the native men, and not one per cent of native customary land holdings were registered as the property of men, a condition Skeat attributed to the matrilineal inheritance from Sakai wives of the first Malay settlers (p. 42).

### Conclusion

Skeat's definitive takeaway is that Malay padi and healing ceremonies are not "mere childish folly" but intelligible expressions of sympathetic magic whose logic becomes transparent once the observer possesses the key of comparative ethnography. He argues that the distinction between the rice-child as a literal child and as a mannikin-soul (*semangat*) is one that the practitioners themselves would not draw, and that the entire ceremonial apparatus—cradle, umbrella, lamp, taboos—functions as a coherent system of homoeopathic imitation designed to ensure agricultural fertility and human health.

## Context

- Skeat held senior administrative posts in the Straits Settlements and the Malay States (he later served as Secretary to the Governor and as a member of the Federal Council); this article was composed in the course of his official duties and reflects the colonial administrator's dual interest in native customary law and in the ethnographic "curiosity" of indigenous belief systems.
- The primary evidential basis is Skeat's own direct observation supplemented by information volunteered by the pawang and household members present; no archival collections are consulted. The article's historiographical contribution lies in providing one of the earliest verbatim records of Malay ritual language (charms, invocations, spirit-language vocabulary) transcribed by a literate outsider, predating most subsequent ethnographic collections in the region.
