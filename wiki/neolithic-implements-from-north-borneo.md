# Neolithic implements from North Borneo

C. A. Gibson-Hill published this systematic typological study of fishing boats operating from Singapore Island in 1950, drawing on Fisheries Department licence returns and direct observation of vessels in local builder's yards. The article provides a detailed morphological and constructional analysis of the principal craft types employed in Singapore's sheltered, silt-heavy coastal waters, arguing that environmental conditions—calm seas, extensive mangroves, and the absence of broad sandy beaches or accessible deep-water grounds—dictated a fleet dominated by small, lightly built inshore craft rather than large sailing vessels.

## Summary

The article is structured around the Fisheries Department's licensing categories for 1947–49, which Gibson-Hill reorganizes into three principal groups: powered boats, keeled small boats (kolek), and Chinese keelless boats, with a brief note on the Perahu Payang. The environmental setting is central to the analysis: Singapore's waters are calm, heavily impregnated with silt, fringed by mangroves, and lack the rich blue-water areas close to the island that would support large sailing boats for extended periods. The kelong (stake-net platform) is identified as the most important and productive fishing unit, with nearly all other fishing conducted inshore with light gear, making launches and small canoes the typical craft of the region (p. 146).

The keeled small boats section is the most detailed, distinguishing three types by stem and stern post form and construction method: the Johore Kolek (Malay-built, wood-peg fastening, single-piece ribs), the Kolek Chiau (Chinese-built, diagonal nail fastening, named for the long-shafted oar), and the Kolek Selat (Chinese-built, three-piece ribs, tendency toward flat bottom). Gibson-Hill traces constructional differences—particularly the Malay wood-peg technique versus Chinese diagonal nailing, and the survival of dug-out-based construction in the Singapore-Johore area longer than elsewhere in Malaya—as evidence of distinct cultural traditions in boatbuilding that are converging in this particular locale (pp. 150–158).

The Chinese keelless boats section covers the Sampan Kotak (with its distinctive bow transom and stern wings, and the oculus feature traced to the China coast), the Lepap, the Kwa-Tow, the Pomehai, and the Chinese Seine-boat. Gibson-Hill notes the influence of southern Chinese designs and the Rhio-Lingga Archipelago, and discusses the functional logic of each type's form—flat bottoms for stability in casting nets, shallow draughts for mangrove work, and the practical difficulties of beaching heavier vessels in Singapore's limited shoreline (pp. 158–168).

### Key Findings

- Total licensed fishing boats: 1,530 (1947), 1,653 (1948), 1,722 (1949); the three kolek types alone comprised approximately three-quarters of the fleet (pp. 148, 149).
- On 31 December 1949, 213 power-driven vessels held valid licences; 206 had inboard motors (162 diesel, 37 converted car, 3 petrol/paraffin) and 7 outboard; only 43 were registered for work inside the 30-mile limit, 117 for coastal operations, and 44 for deep sea (p. 148).
- In 1949, 4,387 registered fishermen worked from Singapore Island: 3,073 Chinese, 1,301 Malay, 10 Indian, 3 other nationalities (p. 170).
- Kelong licences: 234 in 1948 (126 in depths >3 fathoms, 108 in depths <3 fathoms) rising to 247 in 1949 (139 + 108) (p. 170).
- An 18-foot Kolek Selat could be built by two men in 6–7 days at a cost of approximately $100; a 20-foot example (beam 54–55 inches) cost roughly $120 (p. 157).
- The Johore Kolek ranges from 15 to 24 feet; a 15-foot example has a maximum beam of about 31 inches and depth amidships of 16½ inches, while a 20-foot example has a beam of 42 inches and depth of 19 inches (p. 151).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive historical takeaway is that Singapore's fishing fleet is a product of its specific coastal environment—sheltered, silt-heavy, mangrove-fringed, and lacking suitable deep-water grounds—which favours small, lightly built inshore craft over large sailing vessels. The article demonstrates that the fleet is a mosaic of Malay and Chinese building traditions, with the kelong as the single most productive unit, and that constructional details (fastening methods, rib formation, post finish) serve as reliable markers of cultural origin even where general hull forms converge. The paper is explicitly framed as a preliminary study for the official survey of the Singapore Fisheries and Fishing Industry (p. 169).

## Context

- Primary sources: Fisheries Department, Singapore licence returns (1947–49); direct observation of boats in builder's yards (Beach Road area) and at fishing localities including Siglap, Bedok, Tanah Merah, Pulau Sudong, Pasir Panjang, and Tanjong Keling.
- Historiographical contribution: Provides one of the first systematic typological accounts of Singapore's fishing fleet and documents constructional details—particularly the survival of dug-out-based building and the adoption of Chinese nailing techniques by Malay builders in the Singapore-Johore area—before these features disappeared entirely from the region.
