# The contents of a Dyak medicine chest

C. Hose, writing in 1903, documents a first-hand ethnographic encounter with a Sea-Dyak *Manang* (spiritual healer) named Dasu in the upper Saribas River, cataloguing the contents and ritual uses of his *Lupong* (medicine chest) as a means of comparing indigenous Bornean spiritual beliefs with Malay *Pawang* and *Bomom* practices. The article is a compact field note arising from a single evening's interview in the longhouse of *Tuai* Brok at Kundong village, conducted with the assistance of Rev. William Howell, a noted authority on Sea-Dyak language and custom (pp. 65–66).

## Summary

Hose frames the encounter within a broader comparative-religious project: he wished to understand how the original spiritual beliefs of Bornean peoples related to the animist underpinnings of Malay Islam, and how the *Manang*'s curative methods compared with those of his Malay counterparts (p. 65). The narrative is shaped by the social dynamics of the interview itself. Dasu, a *Manang* with a large local practice, was initially reticent—partly because Howell's Christian teaching had undermined the profession's authority in the area, and partly because Dasu suspected Hose intended to seize his *materia medica* (p. 66). Only after repeated assurances and the intervention of the longhouse elders did Dasu consent to display his *Lupong*, an open-mouthed bamboo basket hung by a strip of bark (p. 67).

The core of the article is a sequential description of six principal charms, each identified by its Dyak name, physical description, and stated therapeutic or diagnostic function. The items range from a transparent water-worn stone (*Batu bintang*) regarded as the *Manang*'s badge of authority, to a six-sided crystal (*Batu ilau*) used as a scrying stone to locate a sick person's wandering soul or the demon causing illness (pp. 67–69). Hose notes the absence of one highly prized item—the *Batu burung endan* (Pelican stone), which would have secured safe passage to the spirit world (*Selayan*)—and records Dasu's expressed wish to obtain it (pp. 69–70). The article closes with Hose's candid observation that Dasu appeared not to fully believe his own account of the tortoise-spirit vision that had initiated his career, and that he seemed relieved to recover his possessions (p. 70).

### Key Findings

- The *Lupong* was an open-mouthed basket of thin bamboo shavings, hung by a strip of bark; its contents were tied in a cloth bag, with the most prized items further enclosed in secondary containers (a second cloth, a small bamboo box with lid, or a match-box) (p. 67).
- *Batu bintang* (Star-stone): a small transparent, water-rounded stone, almost spherical with a rough surface; Dasu claimed it was given to him by his guardian-spirit (appearing as a tortoise) in a dream while he was working as a cooly in Upper Sarawak, and that it marked his initiation into the *Manang* office (p. 68).
- *Batu krat ikan sembilan* (Petrified section of the Sembilan fish): an oblong stone approximately 2 inches long, 1 inch broad, and ½ inch thick in the middle, tapering to ⅛ inch at the edges, with a large oval perforation through the thick part; used by soaking in water, which is then drunk or rubbed on the affected part (p. 68).
- *Batu lintar* (Thunder-bolt): a small dark stone about 1½ inches long, ¼ inch thick at the base, tapering to 1/16 inch at the point, curved and highly polished, resembling a very small rhinoceros horn; pressed firmly against the body wherever pain is felt. Hose identifies it as the same stone type used for stone implements found in the Malay Peninsula (pp. 68–69).
- *Batu nitar* (another name for Thunder-bolt): a minute four-sided crystal, half an inch long and about two lines thick; a charm reserved for extreme cases—dipped in water and shaken over the patient, whose start at the falling drops was taken as a sign of recovery, absence of start as a sign of death (p. 69).
- *Batu ilau* (Sparkling stone / *Batu kras*): a six-sided crystal, 2 inches long and ¾ inch thick, one end covered with *malau* (lac) where it had formerly been fitted into a handle; the indispensable scrying stone for viewing the future, the distant, or the invisible, and specifically for locating a sick person's errant soul or the causative demon (p. 69).

### Conclusion

Hose's definitive takeaway is ethnographic rather than evaluative: the value of the account lies in having a set of charms "actually in use exhibited and explained by their owner," as opposed to the several similar collections previously given to him by *Manangs* who had converted to Christianity (p. 70). His closing observation—that Dasu seemed not to believe his own origin story and was visibly relieved to recover his possessions—introduces a note of scepticism about the sincerity of the practitioner's own spiritual claims, while the article as a whole serves as a comparative datum for understanding the structural parallels between Bornean animist healing and Malay *Pawang* practice.

## Context

- Hose was Bishop of Sarawak and a long-standing colonial administrator and ethnographer; the article is written from the perspective of a European observer with established access to indigenous communities, and his comparative framework (Bornean animism vs. Malay Islam) reflects the intellectual preoccupations of late-Victorian colonial scholarship (p. 65).
- The primary source is Hose's own field observation, supplemented by the linguistic and cultural expertise of Rev. William Howell, co-author of *A Dictionary of the Sea-Dyak Language* (p. 65).
