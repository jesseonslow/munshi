# The Singapore Chronicle, 1824–37

C.A. Gibson-Hill's 1953 article provides a meticulous institutional and prosopographical history of Singapore's first newspaper, the *Singapore Chronicle*, from its founding in January 1824 to its final cessation in September 1837. Drawing on East India Company letter-books, surviving copies of the paper, and local registers, Gibson-Hill demonstrates that the *Chronicle* was conceived not as an independent press but as a controlled commercial instrument of the Resident, and that its thirteen-year life was shaped by the tensions between editorial ambition and colonial administrative oversight, culminating in its commercial destruction by the rival *Singapore Free Press*.

## Summary

The article reconstructs the full editorial succession of the *Chronicle*—Bernard, Campbell, Loch, George, Moor, Lorrain, and Carnegie—using company correspondence, baptismal and death records, land registers, and the paper's own surviving issues. Gibson-Hill's central argument is that the *Chronicle* was from the outset a creature of Crawfurd's administrative imagination: the 1823 application to Fort William explicitly pledged the paper to be "entirely under the control of the local authority," and Crawfurd functioned as editor-in-fact throughout his residency. The paper's early years under Bernard and Campbell were unremarkable, but the arrival of James Loch in January 1827 introduced a critical editorial voice that provoked a cascade of censorship disputes between Singapore, Penang, and Fort William. Gibson-Hill carefully untangles the jurisdictional confusion: because Singapore had been placed directly under the Supreme Government (bypassing Penang) from 1823, the *Chronicle* technically operated under Bengal's post-1818 press regulations rather than Penang's stricter proof-submission practice, yet Crawfurd's personal assurances meant proofs were in fact censored locally. When Loch's attacks on Bengal's press policy alarmed Governor Fullerton, direct censorship was hastily reimposed in March 1827.

The middle period under W.R. George and J.H. Moor saw the paper grow in size and ambition, with the *Commercial Register* amalgamated into a weekly *New Series* from January 1831. Moor, arriving from Malacca after his own censorship troubles over the Naning tythe dispute, brought editorial vigour and expanded the paper to five or six pages. The censorship was formally lifted on 28 March 1833, and for nearly five years the *Chronicle* enjoyed a near-monopoly in the Straits Settlements. The paper's decline began in September 1835 when George sold it to James Fairlie Carnegie, a Penang merchant whose arrival was commercially unwelcome. The local business community responded by founding the *Singapore Free Press* on 8 October 1835, backed by Boustead, Napier, and Maclaine Fraser. Despite Carnegie's price cuts and a "unanimous" subscriber vote to restore the original subscription rate, the *Chronicle* ceased publication on 30 September 1837, and its press was shipped to Penang where it was used to launch the *Pinang Gazette & Straits Chronicle* in April 1838.

### Key Findings

- The *Singapore Chronicle* published its first issue on 1 January 1824 and its last on 30 September 1837, a total run of approximately thirteen years and nine months (pp. 176, 198).
- The editor received a fixed government subsidy of $60 per month for publishing official notifications, withdrawn in 1829 on grounds of economy; the *Malacca Observer* received a comparable subsidy of Sicca Rupees 50 per month, also withdrawn in June 1829 (pp. 179, 185).
- The *New Series* (from 6 January 1831) was printed on four pages measuring 12½" × 19½", with four columns of type per page on the first three sheets and a price-current table on the fourth; subscription was $18 per year, reduced to $12 per year in 1835 to compete with the *Free Press* (pp. 191, 194).
- Direct press censorship was formally lifted at Singapore on 28 March 1833, after having been reimposed in March 1827 following Loch's attacks on Bengal's press policy (pp. 181, 192).
- Advertising rates in the *Chronicle* were $4 per advertisement (not exceeding eight lines) from September 1831, reduced to $2.50 per half-column by January 1836; the *Free Press* charged $2.50 for 15 lines or less and inserted advertisements four times per payment versus the *Chronicle*'s three (pp. 191, 194).
- Carnegie sailed for Penang on 23 October 1837 aboard the British brig *Harriet*; the *Chronicle*'s press was subsequently used to produce the first issue of the *Pinang Gazette & Straits Chronicle* on 7 April 1838 (pp. 197–198).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive takeaway is that the *Singapore Chronicle* was never a genuinely independent press: it was born as Crawfurd's administrative tool, survived through a series of editors who oscillated between compliance and mild dissent, and was ultimately killed not by government suppression but by commercial competition from a rival paper backed by the settlement's leading mercantile interests. The paper's demise illustrates how, in a small colonial settlement, the boundaries between press, commerce, and government were porous to the point of indistinguishability.

## Context

- Primary archival sources: East India Company letter-books (Straits Settlements series) held in the Raffles Museum Archives; surviving copies of the *Singapore Chronicle* in the India Office Library and Raffles Museum Archives; the *Singapore Free Press* (Raffles Museum Archives, Vols. 1–33).
- The article contributes to the historiography of colonial press freedom in the Straits Settlements by demonstrating that the formal lifting of censorship in 1833 was less significant than the commercial and personal networks that actually determined what was published, and by correcting several errors in Buckley's *Anecdotal History* and Makepeace's *One Hundred Years of Singapore*.
