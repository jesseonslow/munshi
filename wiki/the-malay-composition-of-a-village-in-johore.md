# The Malay composition of a village in Johore

Kenelm O. L. Burridge's 1956 monograph presents a detailed kinship and landholding analysis of a long-established Malay village on the west coast of Johore, researched in 1955 under a University of Malaya Social Research Unit fellowship. The article argues that village social order is structured by the interplay of patrilineal agnation, the bilateral kinship category *saudara*, land tenure, and political authority, with a dominant Bugis agnatic line—descended from a prince who settled in the early nineteenth century—functioning as the gravitational centre around which all other households orient themselves.

## Summary

Burridge's central concern is to explain how a village of some forty-one Malay households (approximately two hundred and fifty persons) maintains internal cohesion despite significant variation in wealth, origin, and degree of integration. He identifies four broad household groupings: the dominant Bugis agnates descended from a prince P who arrived as conqueror and settler in the early nineteenth century; the descendants of S, a pre-P figure of local renown whose origins are variously placed in Malacca, Negri Sembilan, or Selangor; a cluster of households tied to a long-established agnatic line from the Singapore islands whose members held penghuluships in the district; and a residual group of immigrant households with minimal local kin connections. The first two groups are the most analytically developed. The Bugis agnates own land, hold political office (ten penghulus among their agnatic descendants), and practice reciprocal cousin marriages across a river estuary. The descendants of S, by contrast, are uniformly poor, live on land owned by others (including the Bugis agnates, an Indian chettiar, and a Sumatran immigrant's widow), and maintain their position through service roles—midwifery, traditional medicine, Quranic teaching, and caretaking—performed for the dominant group.

The article's theoretical contribution lies in its treatment of *saudara* as the operative social category that contains and mediates between unilineal agnatic groups. Burridge demonstrates that *saudara* is not a corporate group but a relational field defined from the perspective of a particular individual or sibling pair, extending through both male and female lines and, by courtesy, to neighbours who behave as kin. Marriage within the *saudara* range is a conservative device that concentrates wealth (especially land) within a bounded set of households; marriage outside it disperses wealth but may provide a point of entry into a cycle of rising prosperity for the poor. The poor and landless are not in opposition to the rich and landowning but are structurally dependent on them, and this dependence is reinforced by the fact that all parties are *saudara* or behave as such.

Burridge also examines household composition, tabulating forty-one households into eight structural types. He notes that while the basic nuclear pattern (husband, wife, children) accounts for nineteen households, a further nine (22 per cent) contain matrilineal descent groups counted from female to female, and a further three contain female-to-male lines. This means that within the household itself, women form significant linear groups even though political descent and the transmission of wealth and power are patrilineal. The article thus presents a picture in which patrilineal agnation provides the axis of political and economic authority, while the bilateral *saudara* and the internal structure of households provide the social fabric that binds the village into a single community.

### Key Findings

- The village contained 41 Malay households (approximately 250 persons), 4 Indians, and 119 Chinese distributed in 25 homesteads and shophouses; the Chinese were regarded as transients by both themselves and their Malay neighbours (p. 60).
- The dominant Bugis agnatic line, descended from prince P who arrived in the early nineteenth century, produced no fewer than ten penghulus among its agnatic descendants, and its members owned land in the village and across the river estuary (pp. 61–63).
- All nine households descended from S (the pre-P local notable) were poor; one old lady was reduced to selling sand from outside her front door to a building contractor (p. 65).
- Of 41 households, 19 conformed to the basic nuclear type (husband, wife, children); 9 (22 per cent) contained matrilineal descent groups (female-to-female), and a further 3 contained female-to-male lines, giving a combined 29 per cent with significant female-line descent groups within the household (pp. 73–75).
- Of 50 couples counted, 24 husbands were from outside the village while only 16 wives were; yet only 3 households lived on land belonging to the wife, compared with 12 on the husband's land and 11 on the land of more distant relatives or non-relatives (pp. 75–76).
- The Javanese immigrant household (p, q, r) illustrates an agnatic descent group "in the making": the father arrived at around forty, established a plantation, his son q became village headmaster and bought land from a Bugis agnate, and the younger son r became school caretaker and street-light contractor (pp. 69–70).

### Conclusion

Burridge's definitive takeaway is that the village's political unity—largely an arrangement among males in relation to one another—is underpinned by the bilateral *saudara*, which extends kin obligations beyond the unilineal group through female siblings and their children. Descent, understood as a political notion involving the transmission of wealth, influence, and power, runs through males; but it is balanced by an equivalent recognition of kinship through females, which forms the *saudara* and provides the necessary links between the agnatic holders of power and the whole community. The most frequent comment in the event of a dispute—"By quarrelling thus he is splitting the village"—confirms that this solidarity, hierarchically based and taking its departure from agnation with P, is the operative ideal (p. 76).

## Context

- Fieldwork was conducted in 1955 under a Research Fellowship with the Social Research Unit, University of Malaya (p. 60, n. 1).
- The article contributes to the early post-war literature on Malay kinship and village organisation by demonstrating that the *saudara*—rather than a corporate patrilineal descent group—is the primary social category through which political and economic relations are mediated in a multi-origin Malay community.
