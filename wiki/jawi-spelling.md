# Jawi spelling

Zainal Abidin bin Ahmad's 1928 article "Jawi Spelling," published in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, provides a systematic phonological analysis of the Malayo-Arabic script as it stood in the late colonial period. Arguing against the prevailing view that Jawi spelling is chaotic and ruleless, Ahmad demonstrates that the irregularities of the script are the predictable product of a structural mismatch between the Arabic diacritical system and the richer vowel inventory of Malay, and that general principles do govern the spelling of the majority of words once they are properly classified.

## Summary

The article addresses a fundamental problem in Malay orthography: the Jawi script, inherited from Arabic, was never designed to represent the full range of Malay vowel sounds. Ahmad traces the historical development from the early *baris* (diacritical stroke) system—evident in the 14th-century Trengganu inscription and in fully vocalised manuscripts of the 16th–17th centuries—to the later adoption of *huruf saksi* (vowel-letters) as a practical compromise. Scribes found the three Arabic diacritics insufficient for Malay's six or more distinct vowel sounds, but introducing new diacritics would have been cumbersome; wholesale omission of diacritics would have rendered the text unintelligible given Malay's grammar. The solution was to repurpose *alif*, *ya*, and *waw* as vowel-letters, while retaining the *baris* system for many words already established in that form. The result is a hybrid orthography in which the two systems coexist without a clear boundary rule.

Ahmad's analytical framework classifies Malay vowel sounds into three primary divisions—*Atas* (high), *Bawah* (low), and *Hadapan* (front)—each subdivided into *halus* (narrow), *sedang* (medium), and *kasar* (broad), yielding nine sounds in total, of which six are practically sufficient. He then cross-references these with the distinction between open and closed syllables, producing twelve syllabic types that constitute all purely Malay words. From this matrix he derives two broad spelling rules: (a) *Atas Halus* sounds and all closed *Atas Kasar* sounds are written without a *huruf saksi*; (b) all open *Atas Kasar*, all *Bawah*, and all *Hadapan* sounds, whether open or closed, are written with the appropriate *huruf saksi*. The article further examines the triple function of *alif*, *ya*, and *waw* as consonants, vowel-letters, and (in the case of *alif*) glottal plosives, and closes with a detailed treatment of the variable pronunciation and inconsistent spelling of final open *a* syllables.

The work is grounded in close observation of contemporary usage, comparison with old manuscript spellings, and reference to the Trengganu inscription. Ahmad acknowledges the transitional state of many words, noting that fixed spelling will only be achieved through the standardising influence of novels, dictionaries, and spelling books.

### Key Findings

- Malay possesses at least six distinct vowel sounds (practically) or nine (in finer phonetic analysis), compared to only three in Arabic, making the Arabic diacritical system structurally inadequate for Malay (pp. 82–85).
- The 14th-century Trengganu inscription makes "a lavish use of *huruf saksi*, especially *alif*," while 16th–17th century standardised spelling used them "very sparingly," indicating that scribes of that period still had the *baris* system in mind (p. 82).
- The three letters *alif*, *ya*, and *waw* each serve dual or triple functions: as consonants (*huruf benar*), as vowel-letters (*huruf saksi*), and in the case of *alif*, as a glottal plosive equivalent to *hamzah* (pp. 93–98).
- Ahmad identifies two broad governing rules for modern Jawi spelling: (a) *Atas Halus* and closed *Atas Kasar* syllables are written without *huruf saksi*; (b) open *Atas Kasar*, all *Bawah*, and all *Hadapan* syllables require the appropriate *huruf saksi* (p. 88).
- The late Dato' Bentara Dalam of Johore (Muhammad Ibrahim Munshi, son of Munshi Abdullah) proposed in his pamphlet *Pemimpin Johore* a distinction between *ya halus* and *ya kasar* and between *waw halus* and *waw kasar*, but after thirty years his scheme "has not come into general use" (p. 94).
- Final open *a* syllables (e.g., in *gula*, *mata*, *kira*) vary in pronunciation between dialects—the Riau-Johor pronunciation (somewhat like *atas halus*) is recognised as standard, while Penang and Province Wellesley dialects pronounce them as *atas kasar* (p. 102).

### Conclusion

Ahmad's definitive takeaway is that Jawi spelling, far from being arbitrary, operates according to discernible general principles once the structural causes of its irregularities are understood. The irregularities are concentrated in the most common everyday words and are the inevitable consequence of forcing a three-vowel diacritical system onto a language with six or more vowel sounds, compounded by the dual consonant-vowel role of three letters. The path to regularisation, Ahmad implies, lies not in abandoning Jawi but in codifying the principles already observable in popular practice through dictionaries, spelling books, and uniform literary usage.

## Context

- The article draws on old Malay manuscripts (fully vocalised with *baris*), the Trengganu inscription, and contemporary popular usage as its primary evidential base; it also references the *Pemimpin Johore* pamphlet by Muhammad Ibrahim Munshi.
- Historiographically, the article represents an early attempt at a systematic, phonologically grounded description of Jawi orthography from a native Malay scholar, predating most Western-language treatments of the subject and offering a framework that remains broadly relevant to discussions of Jawi standardisation.
