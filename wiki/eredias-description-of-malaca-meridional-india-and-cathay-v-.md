# Eredia’s description of Malaca, Meridional India, and Cathay; .V. Mills

Chandran Jeshurun's 2009 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 82, Part 1) reconstructs the personal and institutional ties between Malaysia's first Prime Minister, Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Al-Haj, and two universities—Cambridge and the University of Malaya—during the critical period of 1960–1962. Drawing primarily on the Tunku's private papers held at the Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Al-Haj Memorial in Kuala Lumpur, Jeshurun argues that the Tunku's indefatigable support was instrumental in elevating the newly established Pantai Valley campus to prominence within the Association of Commonwealth Universities during the 1960s and 1970s.

## Summary

The article opens with the Tunku's receipt of an Honorary Doctor of Law (LLD) from the University of Cambridge in June 1960, a distinction that the Tunku himself later described as one of the most eventful moments of his life, particularly poignant given that he had originally been "sent down" for lateness at his undergraduate degree examination (p. 4). The correspondence between the Tunku, Vice Chancellor Herbert Butterfield, and Dr E. E. Rich, Master of St Catherine's College, reveals a warm and personal relationship that extended to the College offering him an Honorary Fellowship—the highest honour a Cambridge college could bestow (pp. 2–3). Jeshurun situates this academic honour within a broader political context: just one day after the Cambridge ceremony, on 10 June 1960, the Tunku privately informed Lord Perth at the Colonial Office that he was "prepared to face the possibility" of a Federation encompassing Malaya, Singapore, North Borneo, Sarawak, and Brunei—the so-called "Grand Design"—a disclosure that predates the Tunku's well-known public announcement in Singapore in May 1961 (pp. 5–6).

The second major thread concerns the Tunku's installation as the first Chancellor of the University of Malaya at its new Pantai Valley campus in Kuala Lumpur in 1962. Jeshurun documents the Tunku's initial reluctance, his comparison of the role to Harold Macmillan's chancellorship of Oxford, and the persuasion of the Malay Rulers that ultimately secured his acceptance (p. 6). The installation ceremony at Stadium Negara on 16 June 1962 was attended by representatives of forty-four member institutions of the Association of Commonwealth Universities and twenty-one others from the United States, Europe, and Asia, underscoring the international stature the young university was already achieving (p. 11). Jeshurun also profiles key figures in the university's early development, including Vice Chancellor Sir Alexander Oppenheim, Registrar Foo Yeow Yoke, and the former Chancellor Malcolm MacDonald, whose 1960 convocation address in Singapore articulated a vision of multi-racial harmony that anticipated the political challenges of the new Federation (pp. 7–8).

Finally, Jeshurun provides a detailed ethnic and academic breakdown of the 1962 graduating class—the first batch to enrol at Pantai Valley in 1959—revealing a predominantly Chinese cohort in the sciences and engineering, with Malays concentrated in the humanities (p. 13). He traces the subsequent careers of notable alumni, including Tan Sri Razali Ismail, Krishen Jit, and John Lim Kok Min, to demonstrate the long-term significance of this founding generation.

### Key Findings

- The Tunku's private papers at the Memorial contain previously unexamined correspondence that reveals the personal dynamics behind his Cambridge honorary degree, including a humorous exchange with Dr Rich about the "slight alarm" caused by the death of the Tunku's namesake, the first Yang di-Pertuan Agong (pp. 2–3).
- The Tunku's 10 June 1960 private communication to Lord Perth regarding the "full Grand Design" for Malaysia predates his public announcement by nearly a year, suggesting a deliberate strategy to pre-empt British decolonization planning in the wake of the PAP's 1959 electoral victory in Singapore (pp. 5–6).
- The 1962 graduating class at Pantai Valley comprised 28 Malays, 21 Chinese, 16 Indians, and 1 Eurasian in BA (Hons.), with Chinese students overwhelmingly dominant in BSc and BEng programmes, reflecting the ethnic stratification of higher education in early independent Malaysia (p. 13).
- Malcolm MacDonald's 1960 convocation address at the Victoria Theatre, Singapore, explicitly framed multi-racial coexistence in Malaya and Singapore as "a practical working model for all other peoples to follow," a statement of remarkable political boldness for a British colonial official (p. 8).
- The Tunku pledged at his 1962 installation that the University would "increasingly use the Malay language in its teaching and research activities," a commitment that would shape the institution's linguistic policy for decades (p. 12).
- The Tunku served as Chancellor until just after his retirement as Prime Minister in September 1970, after which he was succeeded by Sultanah Bahiyah, the first woman to hold the office in Malaysian academia (p. 12).

### Conclusion

Jeshurun's definitive takeaway is that the Tunku's personal commitment to higher education—rooted in his own Cambridge experience and sustained through his political career—was a decisive factor in the University of Malaya's rapid ascent to international standing. The article positions the Tunku not merely as a ceremonial figurehead but as an active patron whose political capital, personal relationships with British academic and diplomatic elites, and institutional advocacy collectively shaped the university's trajectory during its formative decade.

## Context

- The article is based on the Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Papers (TARPP) held at the Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Al-Haj Memorial in Kuala Lumpur, with citations made by courtesy of the National Archives of Malaysia (p. 13, fn. 1).
- The Tunku Memorial complex stands on the grounds of the nineteenth-century British Residency in Kuala Lumpur, a site that Jeshurun notes adds a layer of historical continuity to the Tunku's personal and institutional legacy (p. 13).
