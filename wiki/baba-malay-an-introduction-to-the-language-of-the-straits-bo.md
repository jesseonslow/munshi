# Baba Malay: an introduction to the language of the Straits-born Chinese

W. G. Shellabear, a missionary and linguist working in the Straits Settlements, published this study in 1913 to document and analyse Baba Malay—the colloquial variety of Malay spoken by the Straits-born Chinese (Babas)—as a distinct dialect worthy of systematic description. Writing at a moment when the British colonial administration was expanding vernacular education, Shellabear argues that Baba Malay is not a degenerate corruption of Malay but a living, self-sustaining language with its own grammar, vocabulary, and literary potential, and that it merits recognition as the mother tongue of the most influential section of the Chinese community in the British possessions.

## Summary

Shellabear opens by untangling the Dutch-derived terms "High Malay" and "Low Malay," demonstrating that the former refers to the literary language of the Malacca–Acheen axis (which has remained remarkably stable for three centuries) while the latter is a Javanese-heavy pidgin imposed by the Dutch in Java. He then establishes a crucial distinction for the British sphere: the pure Malay of the villages, which the Malays themselves speak with little change, and the "colloquial Malay of the Settlements," which he names Baba Malay. This is the business language of Singapore, Penang, and the Federated Malay States, and the mother tongue of the majority of Chinese women and children in the Straits Settlements. Shellabear insists that the Babas will never accept "Classical Malay" for their children's education—they call it *Malayu hutan*, "the language of the jungle"—and that any practical programme of Chinese education in the Straits must work through Baba Malay rather than against it.

The bulk of the article traces the evolution of this dialect from the earliest Chinese immigration to Malacca (pre-dating European arrival by at least 300 years, with Hokkien as the source dialect) through the process of intermarriage with Malay women, the gradual separation of the Baba community from the Malay population, and the consequent fixation of their idiom. Shellabear identifies four principal divergences from pure Malay: the incorporation of Chinese vocabulary (pronounced in a Malay phonological system, without the tonal distinctions of Hokkien); the absence from the Baba lexicon of a large body of Malay synonyms and derived forms; systematic mispronunciation of Malay words (final *ai* → *e*, final *h* dropped, final *a* → *ak*); and, most significantly, the adoption of Chinese syntactic patterns, particularly the overuse of the possessive particle *punya* in the manner of the Hokkien *e*, the placement of demonstrative pronouns before rather than after the noun, and the avoidance of the Malay passive *di-* construction.

Shellabear's evidence is drawn from direct observation of Baba speech in Malacca and Singapore, from the writings of Baba authors (including a translation of *Black Beauty* by Goh Hood Keng), and from his own reconstruction of typical Baba sentences into their Malay equivalents to illustrate the structural differences. He closes with a reference list of words peculiar to the Babas, though the full vocabulary tables (pp. 60–62) are largely illegible in the surviving copy.

### Key Findings

- The first Chinese immigrants to Malacca were almost certainly Hokkien speakers from Amoy, and the burial ground *Bukit China* predates the *Sjarah Melayu* by at least 300 years, placing Chinese presence in the Straits well before European arrival (p. 52).
- The most frequently used Chinese-origin words in Baba Malay are the first- and second-person pronouns *goa* ("I") and *lu* ("you"), while the third-person *dia* and first-person plural *kina* remain Malay; kinship terms, however, are overwhelmingly Chinese with a corrupted prefix (e.g., *'ntia* for father, *'rgkong* for grandfather, *tachi* for elder sister) (p. 54).
- The Babas use only *tergok* and *nampak* (occasionally *lihat* and *pandang*) where the Malays deploy at least eight synonyms for "to look/see"; similarly one word *taroh* covers what the Malays express with *taroh*, *buboh*, and *letak*, and *angkat* subsumes *pikul*, *kelek*, *tatang*, *kandang*, *kendong*, *junjung*, *dokong* (p. 56).
- The possessive particle *punya* is used by the Babas with the frequency and syntactic position of the Hokkien *e*, producing constructions such as "tiga bulan punya lama" and "seperti macham itu punya kreta" that Shellabear judges "ludicrous to a Malay" (p. 58).
- Final *ai* is uniformly reduced to *e* (*surge* for *syurga*, *pulo* for *pulau*), final *h* is dropped (*ruma*, *bodo*, *bole*), and final *a* is often hardened to *ak* (*bapak*, *bawak*, *pulak*), creating homophonic confusion (e.g., *charik* "to tear" vs. *chari* "to buy") (p. 58).
- The Babas never use the Malay polite register—*bonda*, *adinda*, *kakanda*, *firman*, *titah*, *sabda*—nor the full system of pronominal hierarchy; the entire apparatus of Malay honorific phraseology is "practically unknown" to them (p. 57).

### Conclusion

Shellabear's definitive argument is that Baba Malay is a natural, stable, and expanding creole-variety that will endure as the business and domestic language of the Straits Settlements for an indefinite period, and that its survival is guaranteed by the social position of the Baba community. He calls for the development of a written standard and a literature in Baba Malay so that it may become "an adequate medium for conveying thought and for imparting instruction," positioning the dialect not as a problem to be corrected but as a legitimate educational tool for the Chinese community under British rule.

## Context

- Shellabear was an ordained missionary (Rev., D.D.) and the author of the well-known *Malay-English Dictionary* (1905); his framing of Baba Malay as an educational medium reflects the colonial administration's interest in vernacular schooling for the Chinese population, and his tone—while respectful of the dialect's vitality—retains the assumption that a "pure" literary Malay remains the scholarly ideal against which all other varieties are measured.
- The study draws on direct field observation in Malacca and Singapore, the published writings of Baba authors, and the missionary press; it represents one of the earliest systematic attempts to describe a Chinese-influenced Malay variety as a distinct linguistic entity rather than a mere corruption.
