# Early Indo-Chinese influence in the Malay Peninsula, as illustrated by some of the dialects of the aboriginal tribes

C. Otto Blagden, a colonial administrator based in Malacca, published this comparative linguistic study in 1894, arguing that the dialects of the Malay Peninsula's aboriginal tribes preserve a substantial layer of Mon-Annam (Indo-Chinese) vocabulary that testifies to a former period of political dominion by a Mon-Annam race over the Peninsula before the Malay migration. The article revives and extends a line of inquiry first opened by J.R. Logan in the 1850s, which Blagden contends had been neglected or misinterpreted by subsequent scholars.

## Summary

Blagden's central argument rests on a comparative vocabulary drawing on the dialects of at least a dozen aboriginal groups across the Peninsula—Negrito Semang, the fairer Sakai, and mixed Malayan tribes of the south—set against Mon (Peguan), Khmer (Cambojan), and a range of "uncivilized" dialects of the Mekong valley and southern Siam. He demonstrates that a considerable number of basic vocabulary items (body parts, natural features, numerals, verbs) in these mutually unintelligible Peninsular dialects correspond closely to their Mon-Annam equivalents, a coincidence he considers too extensive to be accidental (pp. 28–40).

The article then moves from lexical evidence to structural and historical inference. Blagden notes that the Negrito dialects differ syntactically from Mon-Annam (following Terrien de Lacouperie's ideological formula), whereas the Sakai dialects share the Mon-Annam word order, suggesting the latter may carry a genetic as well as a contact-based Indo-Chinese element (pp. 43–44). He argues that the presence of the same Mon-Annam imprint across ethnologically distinct groups—Negritos, fairer jungle-men, and mixed southern tribes—points not to casual intrusion but to the sustained pressure of a politically dominant, culturally superior race (p. 45).

To corroborate the linguistic evidence, Blagden turns to Chinese chronicles. He cites the Liang dynasty entry (A.D. 502–556) describing "Tun-Sun" as a peninsula more than a thousand *li* in extent, with five kings acknowledging the supremacy of Fu-nan, and Moura's account that by c. 650 A.D. the "King of Chon-lap" (Camboja) had united Fu-nan and extended suzerainty over states "situated towards the isthmus of the Malay Peninsula" (pp. 50–52). He also references Logan's claim of a Peguan colony in Kedah attested by inscriptions in the Mon character found in Province Wellesley (p. 51).

### Key Findings

- The comparative vocabulary spans approximately 100 lexical items across eight semantic categories (body, animals, plants, inanimate objects, qualities, actions, numerals), with correspondences drawn from Mon, Khmer, and up to fourteen "uncivilized" Indo-Chinese dialects (pp. 28–40).
- The Johor dialect recorded by Miklucho-Maclay possesses a numeral series extending to seven that matches the Peguan (Mon) system rather than either old or modern Khmer, suggesting the Peninsular Mon-Annam element predates the divergence between the two branches (pp. 48–49).
- The Liang dynasty chronicle (A.D. 502–556) records "Tun-Sun" as a peninsula over a thousand *li* in extent, with five kings acknowledging Fu-nan's supremacy (p. 50).
- Moura's account states that in 627 A.D. the King of Chon-lap (Camboja) united Fu-nan under his authority, and by c. 650 A.D. had incorporated states "towards the isthmus of the Malay Peninsula" (p. 52).
- The Sakai dialects share the Mon-Annam syntactical structure (subject–verb–object), while the Negrito Semang dialects follow a different order (genitive before noun; object before verb), indicating the two groups experienced the Indo-Chinese influence through different mechanisms (pp. 43–44).
- Blagden identifies the lower Menam valley as the most probable source region for the Peninsular Mon-Annam element, given its geographical position between the Mon and Khmer language fields and its ease of access to the Peninsula (p. 50).

### Conclusion

Blagden concludes that a Mon-Annam race—whether Peguan, Cambojan, or a form intermediate between the two—held political sway over at least part of the Malay Peninsula in the early centuries of the Christian era, and that this dominion was eventually broken by the growing pressure of the Siamese from the north rather than by the Malay migration from Sumatra. The linguistic traces embedded in the dialects of isolated jungle tribes from Patani to Johor constitute, in his view, "clear indications of a dominant Indo-Chinese influence" and "the former presence of a ruling race that has long since passed away from the land" (p. 54).

## Context

- Blagden was a district officer in the Straits Settlements (Malacca) and conducted his research largely from published vocabularies and a short field collection of Besisi and Mentra words; he explicitly acknowledges his limitations as a non-specialist philologist and frames the paper as a preliminary outline intended to stimulate further investigation (pp. 55–56).
- The article draws on archival and published sources including Miklucho-Maclay's vocabularies (JSBRAS No. 1), Newbold's *British Settlements in the Straits of Malacca*, Clifford's dialect studies (JSBRAS No. 24), Haswell's Peguan grammar, Moura's *Vocabulaire Cambodgien* and *Le Royaume du Cambodge*, and Garnier's *Voyage d'Exploration en Indo-Chine*, as well as Chinese dynastic chronicles as reported by Moura and Forbes.
