# Baba Malay dialect

Tan Chee-Beng's 1980 article "Baba Malay Dialect," published in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 53, pp. 150–166), presents a systematic linguistic analysis of the Malay dialect spoken by the Peranakan Chinese community in Malacca. Drawing on fieldwork conducted throughout 1977, the article argues that Baba Malay is not a "corrupted" or degraded form of Malay but a legitimate dialect with its own consistent phonological, lexical, and syntactic structure, developed through centuries of Chinese-Malay-Indonesian contact.

## Summary

The article addresses a persistent misconception among both Malays and non-Baba Chinese that Baba Malay is merely a broken or mixed language. Tan situates the dialect's origins in the historical process by which early Chinese male settlers in Malacca (permanent by the seventeenth century) were compelled to acquire local colloquial Malay for commerce and domestic life, particularly after intermarrying with Malay and Indonesian women in the absence of Chinese female emigration. This process of linguistic acculturation, combined with the retention of Hokkien vocabulary for culturally Chinese domains and the influence of a pre-existing "commercial Malay" *lingua franca*, produced a dialect that diverges systematically from both Standard Malay and the regional Malacca Malay spoken by the local Malay population.

The core of the article is a five-part typology of divergence: loanwords (Chinese, Indonesian, and English), phonological patterns, lexical and semantic differences, and syntactic structure. Tan demonstrates that each category exhibits regular, rule-governed variation rather than random corruption. For instance, phonological changes follow predictable patterns tied to final syllable structure, and Chinese loanwords are concentrated in semantically coherent domains (kinship, ritual, religion) where Malay vocabulary is insufficient. The syntactic analysis reveals that Baba Malay sentence construction mirrors Hokkien grammar, particularly in the pre-nominal use of *punya* for possession and the deployment of *sama* to replace both *dengan* and *kepada*.

The article also documents the dialect's ongoing vitality and its role as a marker of ethnic identity. The consistent use of *gua* and *lu* (rather than Malay *aku* and *engkau*) is identified as a key in-group boundary marker, and the terms of address *encek* and *bibi* serve to distinguish Baba speakers from their Malay neighbours who use *pacik* and *macik*. Tan notes that the younger generation is showing some convergence toward Standard Malay pronunciation, particularly in the third phonological pattern, suggesting the dialect is under pressure from the broader national linguistic environment.

### Key Findings

- The most distinctive Baba Malay pronouns are *gua* (I) and *lu* (you), derived from Hokkien, which replace the Malay *aku* and *engkau*; the consistent use of this pair is a primary marker of Baba identity (p. 152).
- Five regular phonological patterns differentiate Baba Malay from Standard Malay: final /a/ realised as [a] (not [ə]); /ar/ → /er/ (e.g., *kasar* → *kaser*); /as/ → /e/ (e.g., *keras* → *kere*); /au/ → /o/ (e.g., *pisau* → *piso*); and /ai/ → /ei/ (e.g., *sampai* → *sampei*) (pp. 159–162).
- Chinese (Hokkien) loanwords are concentrated in the domains of kinship, ritual, and religion, where Malay lacks equivalent vocabulary; examples include *m-peh* (father's elder brother), *siki* (death anniversary), and *teyian* (religious donation) (pp. 152–154).
- Baba Malay contains Indonesian-origin words absent from Malaysian Malay, such as *cangkil* (cup, vs. Malay *cawan*) and the terms of address *bibi* and *wak*, reflecting historical contact with Indonesian immigrants (p. 156).
- Syntactically, Baba Malay follows Chinese word order: *punya* precedes the noun in possessive constructions (e.g., *dia punya buku* for "his book"), and *sama* replaces both *dengan* ("with") and *kepada* ("to"), mirroring Hokkien grammar (pp. 164–165).
- Baba Malay shares specific phonological features with Jakarta (Batavia) Malay, such as the pronunciation of *pakai* as *pakei* and *sampai* as *sampei*, supporting the hypothesis of a common "commercial Malay" substrate in the Malay Archipelago (p. 151).

### Conclusion

Tan's definitive takeaway is that Baba Malay constitutes a fully systematic dialect in its own right, not a degenerate hybrid. The dialect emerged through a specific historical process of linguistic acculturation in which a Chinese-speaking population adopted Malay as its primary language while retaining structural and lexical elements from Hokkien and Indonesian. The resulting system exhibits internal consistency and regularity that disqualify any characterisation of it as "corrupted." Tan further argues that the dialect functions as a crucial symbol of Peranakan ethnic identity and that judging it by the standards of Standard Malay is both linguistically unsound and culturally reductive.

## Context

- Fieldwork was conducted by the author among Baba communities in Malacca's Central District throughout 1977, sponsored by the Culture Learning Institute of the East West Center (p. 150, n. 1).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Peranakan studies by providing the first systematic linguistic description of Baba Malay as a dialect, complementing Tan's concurrent doctoral dissertation on Baba ethnic identity (Cornell University, 1979) and building on Shellabear's earlier 1913 introductory sketch of the language.
