# The growth of Kuala Lumpur and of the Malay community of Selangor before 1880

J.M. Gullick's 1990 article traces the physical and demographic origins of Kuala Lumpur from a small river transhipment point in the late 1850s to its establishment as the administrative capital of Selangor in 1880. Writing as a contribution to the preservation of the city's remaining nineteenth-century buildings, Gullick reconstructs how topographical constraints, tin mining economics, and the decisions of key actors—Yap Ah Loy, Bloomfield Douglas, and F.A. Swettenham—determined the town's layout, its Chinese and Malay quarters, and the infrastructure that made the transfer of the state capital from Klang possible.

## Summary

The article addresses a gap in the historiography of Kuala Lumpur by covering the "pre-colonial" period before 1880, complementing Gullick's earlier study of 1880–1895. The central problem is one of reconstruction: the original village was completely destroyed during the Selangor Civil War (1867–73), and only one brief contemporary description survives from before that destruction. Gullick therefore pieces together the town's physical form from Swettenham's 1875 sketch map, post-war visitor accounts (Hornaday 1878, Isabella Bird 1879, Douglas's diary), and logical inference from the topographical factors—river flooding, the need for higher ground, and the orientation of the track to the mines—that would have governed settlement in any case. The resulting picture is a compact rectangular grid bounded by the river, Market Street, Cross Street, and High Street, with a market square at its centre.

A second thread concerns the Malay community. Gullick establishes that Sumatran Malays (principally Rawa-Mandiling and Minangkabau groups) had settled along the upper Klang River from at least the early nineteenth century, numbering no more than 2,000–3,000 scattered in small mining settlements in the 1870s. Their relationship to the Chinese mining economy was complex: they were not displaced by the Chinese influx of 1857, and during the civil war Malay and Chinese contingents fought as allies on both sides. The Malay quarter at Kuala Lumpur itself was a later development, with newcomers after 1874 building on piles at the river bank before relocating to higher ground (the area later known as Kampong Rawa) following the devastating floods of 1881.

The article's most original argument concerns the Damansara Road. This 15-mile overland route, surveyed by D.D. Daly in 1875 and partially completed by 1878, was intended to replace the slow and tortuous river journey between Kuala Lumpur and its port at Klang. It ultimately failed as a commercial route—drainage problems made it impassable for wheeled traffic in the wet season, and the volume of goods traffic during the post-1879 tin boom exceeded its capacity anyway. Yet it served two decisive purposes: it made Kuala Lumpur accessible to European officials (Douglas used it for his monthly visits from 1876), and it created a point of arrival on the town's south-west side (Mile 15, later Brickfields) that pulled the centre of gravity westward and necessitated the Market Street bridge. These factors, combined with Klang's visible commercial decline, made the transfer of the state capital in 1880 inevitable despite Douglas's initial resistance.

### Key Findings

- In 1857 Raja Abdullah despatched 87 Chinese miners from Lukut to open mines at Ampang, investing approximately $70,000 (much borrowed from Melaka towkays); the first trading post at the Gombak-Klang confluence was established by two Hakkas, Hiu Siew and Ah Sze, in 1859 (pp. 15–16).
- By March 1875 the rebuilt town had a population of approximately 1,000 Chinese and 700 Malays, with a working mining population of 7,000; the London tin price had fallen from £98/ton in 1874 to £61/ton by early 1879, bringing Yap Ah Loy to the verge of bankruptcy before a sudden recovery to £95/ton in October 1879 (pp. 18, 26).
- The Damansara Road was 15 miles long and 30 feet wide, with an estimated construction cost of $13,000; 12 miles were completed as an unmetalled earth road by 1878, and Yap Ah Loy undertook to complete the remaining 3½ miles in liquidation of a debt to the Selangor Government at a cost of $5,429 (pp. 23–24, 37).
- The first Selangor Government building in Kuala Lumpur—a combined courthouse and resthouse—was erected in 1879, with Sultan Abdul Samad placing the first pole on 9 May 1879; it was sited at the north end of what later became Klyne Street (pp. 26–27).
- State revenue in 1877 was only $226,853 (the sole year above $200,000 before 1887), while the accumulated civil war debt stood at $400,000 in 1877, reduced to $300,000 by 1880; revenue subsequently rose to $300,000 (1883), $566,000 (1885), and $1,154,000 (1887) (pp. 28, 35).
- Yap Ah Loy's tapioca plantation on a 12,000-acre concession along the Damansara Road lost $40,000 over several years; his brickfield and kiln, established in 1879, was probably the first of its kind in the area later known as Brickfields (pp. 22–23).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive historical takeaway is that Kuala Lumpur's transformation from a Chinese mining village to a state capital was not the product of a single decision but of a convergence of economic, topographical, and infrastructural factors over two decades. The tin price crash of the mid-1870s nearly destroyed the town, but Yap Ah Loy's cost-reduction strategies and the eventual price recovery saved it; the Damansara Road, though commercially futile, made the town accessible enough to attract administrative attention; and the visible decay of Klang by 1879 made the transfer of the capital in 1880 a foregone conclusion. The article thus positions 1880 as the decisive threshold after which the town's growth accelerated beyond the reach of any single actor.

## Context

- Primary archival sources include Swettenham's *Malayan Journals* (1874–76), the unpublished diary of Bloomfield Douglas (held at Rhodes House Library, Oxford), Selangor Secretariat files, and the *Selangor Journal*; Gullick also draws on his own earlier publications and on Middlebrook's study of the civil war.
- The article is explicitly framed as a contribution to the heritage preservation movement for Kuala Lumpur's surviving nineteenth-century buildings, and it is the first instalment of a planned series (the author notes the study will be "continued, it is hoped, in some later issue" covering development to 1900).
