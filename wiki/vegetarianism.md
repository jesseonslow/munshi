# Vegetarianism

## Vegetarianism

Vegetarianism in the context of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo is most extensively documented through the institution of the Chinese women's vegetarian house (*su shi tang*, lit. "vegetarian hall"), a religious and social organisation that provided board, lodging, and old-age security for unattached women who worshipped Buddha. These houses existed in all towns in Malaya where large numbers of such women were concentrated, and their prevalence was shaped by the failure of the Chinese joint family system to provide for women who were unmarried, widowed, or otherwise severed from male kin support in colonial Malaya [[1, pp. 51–52]](#ref-1).

## Origins and Social Function

The vegetarian house emerged from a specific demographic and structural condition: the large-scale emigration of Chinese women to Malaya, particularly between 1934 and 1938, when a migrational gain of 190,000 female Chinese deck passengers was recorded, the majority being peasant women from the Sun Tak and Dun Kwan districts of Kwangtung [[1, p. 55]](#ref-1). The Aliens Ordinance of 1933 had established a monthly quota for alien deck passengers, but women were outside this quota until May 1938, when a monthly quota of 500 was specifically introduced for them [[1, p. 54]](#ref-1). Many of these women had already been disinclined to marry in China, or had been unhappily married, and saw emigration as a means of escaping relatives or in-laws [[1, pp. 54–55]](#ref-1).

Topley argues that these institutions functioned primarily as pragmatic old-age security arrangements rather than as strictly religious communities [[1, p. 51]](#ref-1). The houses offered "care while alive and a funeral at death" in return for work in the house or a sum of money paid in instalments by working women, or a lump sum from those entering permanently [[1, p. 56]](#ref-1). Permanent boarding fees ranged from $300 to $700 depending on the standing of the house; working women could alternatively pay monthly rates of up to $20 in advance of their retirement [[1, p. 61]](#ref-1).

## Religious Framework and Daily Life

Nearly all vegetarian houses were Buddhist, with the Pure Land Sect of the "Greater Vehicle" being the most popular among ordinary people in China and in Malaya [[1, p. 58]](#ref-1). The major tenet of this sect is belief in a Western Paradise ruled by Amida Buddha, to which all who trust in his power and repeat his name are admitted after death [[1, p. 58]](#ref-1). Kuan Yin (Avalokiteśvara), known in the West as the "Goddess of Mercy," was another major deity, and her worship was extremely popular among all Chinese women, not only vegetarians [[1, pp. 58–59]](#ref-1).

Members were required to lead a pious life, learn the sutras, and follow a vegetarian diet while living in the house [[1, p. 57]](#ref-1). The diet consisted of vegetables, salted and fresh, mushrooms of all kinds, peanut oil, rice, and *mee* (noodles), with food prepared in such a way that it resembled various kinds of meat and fish dishes [[1, p. 58]](#ref-1). Women were free to come and go as they pleased, provided they completed their allotted work and attended religious observances [[1, p. 57]](#ref-1). Some women who initially became vegetarians later became nuns, shaving their heads and taking binding vows, but the majority preferred not to, considering it an unnecessary step and an insult to their families [[1, p. 57]](#ref-1).

## Economic Activities and Death Benefit Societies

Vegetarian houses engaged in a range of economic activities. Many owned plots of land where they grew fruit trees and vegetables, achieving a degree of self-sufficiency [[1, p. 58]](#ref-1). Members could engage in business ventures, and some houses held shares in vegetarian restaurants [[1, p. 58]](#ref-1). A number of the more highly organised and wealthier houses ran death benefit societies, which arranged funerals, supplied nuns or vegetarians for funeral ceremonies, provided mourners, and secured burial plots [[1, p. 65]](#ref-1). A documented society operated on a monthly subscription of $3.00 for ten years, after which only an annual "oil and incense" fee of $3.00 was required; a death benefit of $500 was payable after four months' continuous subscription [[1, p. 65]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The primary scholarly treatment of vegetarianism in the Malayan context is Marjorie Topley's 1954 article, based on a year and a half of fieldwork in Singapore during which she visited between thirty and forty vegetarian houses, each on several occasions, conducting interviews in Cantonese or with the aid of dialect interpreters [[1, p. 51]](#ref-1). The article represents one of the first systematic studies of this institution in the Malayan context and relies entirely on original ethnographic observation rather than archival or administrative records [[1, p. 51]](#ref-1). Topley included a brief comparative note with vegetarian houses in Hong Kong, observing that they appeared fewer in number and more limited in scope, which she attributed to the closer geographical proximity of Hong Kong to the Chinese mainland and the easier possibility of return for elderly women [[1, p. 67]](#ref-1). She concluded that the future of vegetarian houses in Malaya depended on the continuance of Buddhist belief and women's prejudice against marriage, and that while the number of spinsters seeking such a life might decrease, there would always be women without support in old age [[1, p. 67]](#ref-1).
