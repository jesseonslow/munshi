# Ethnographic profiles of the Dusun-speaking peoples of Sabah, Malaysia. With the collaboration of R. Harrison

G.N. Appell, with the collaboration of Robert Harrison, published this ethnographic classification of the Dusun-speaking peoples of Sabah in 1968, working within the framework of George Peter Murdock's *Ethnographic Atlas* to correct inaccuracies and complete the cultural trait profile for these groups. The article's central thesis is that the colonial label "Dusun" masks profound cultural heterogeneity, and that the most analytically productive division is between swidden-based (SDS) and irrigation-based (IDS) societies, each of which entails a distinct constellation of sociocultural traits across domains from land tenure to residence patterns.

## Summary

Appell's primary intervention is twofold: first, a critique of the taxonomic category "Dusun" itself, which he argues is a historical accident originating with Malay and then English voyagers who applied the term to all peoples between the coast and the mountainous interior, ignoring the self-conscious ethnic distinctions recognized by the peoples themselves. The British colonial administration perpetuated this label as a demographic census category, and it has since leaked into ethnological literature. Appell restricts his own use of the term to linguistic reference only, noting that even the degree of linguistic relatedness among these groups is not fully resolved—the Banggi Islanders, for instance, may be more closely related to Indonesian languages than to the Dusun languages proper (pp. 132–133).

The substantive core of the article is a comparative trait list organized according to Murdock's Atlas categories, contrasting SDS societies (drawn primarily from Appell's fieldwork among the Rungus of the Kudat District) with IDS societies (drawn primarily from Harrison's work among the Ranau Dusun). The authors identify the introduction of wet rice agriculture—likely introduced by Chinese or Coastal Muslim traditions in the remote past—as the single most consequential external factor, because it triggered a cascade of changes: settlement shifted from the long-house to clustered family dwellings, land ownership moved from village-held rights to individual rights, and residence patterns began to shift from uxorilocal toward virilocal or utrolocal arrangements (pp. 133, 143–144). They also address the contested question of descent groups, reviewing Williams' claims of cognatic descent among the Tambunan Dusun and Glyn-Jones' reference to the *bongkawan*, and conclude that no functioning descent groups exist in the Dusun-speaking societies studied, though descent constructs are used for property rights (pp. 137–139).

A significant portion of the article is devoted to the problem of premarital sexual norms and the value premise that sexual relations are potentially deleterious unless properly entered into through marriage institutions. Appell argues this premise is a major shared cultural trait across all Dusun-speaking groups and provides the rationale for bride-price, but acknowledges contradictory evidence from Harrison regarding the Ranau and from Evans regarding the Tempasuk Dusun-speakers (pp. 141–142).

### Key Findings

- The Dusun demographic category comprised approximately 138,000 individuals as of the 1960 census (Jones 1962), of whom roughly 95% spoke closely related communilects in the geographically contiguous West Coast and Interior Residencies (p. 133).
- Mean size of local communities: IDS 400–1,000 persons; SDS 200–399 persons (p. 139).
- The Banggi Islanders and the Rungus share less than 28% of cognates in a list of basic vocabulary items, suggesting the Banggi language may be more closely related to Indonesian than to Dusun languages (p. 132).
- The British outlawed the previously existing incipient, nonhereditary form of slavery after assuming administrative control of North Borneo in 1882 (p. 141).
- The curved bow plow used in IDS societies for defining bunds and weeding was invented by the Chinese in the Tang Dynasty (p. 140).
- Subsistence economy estimates: agriculture accounts for 66–75% of subsistence in both IDS and SDS; animal husbandry 26–35%; gathering 0–5% (IDS) vs. 6–15% (SDS); fishing 6–15% (IDS) vs. 0–5% (SDS) (p. 136).

### Conclusion

Appell's definitive takeaway is that the introduction of wet rice agriculture affected only two major areas of the indigenous cultural system—land tenure and residence patterns—while leaving most other cultural domains largely undisturbed. He further argues against straightforward economic determinism in explaining residence change: the shift toward virilocal residence among the Rungus is not a direct response to productive efficiency but is mediated through the society's pre-existing value system, in which land devolves to the contributor (typically males) and land ownership is seized as a culturally legitimate resolution to the male's dissatisfaction with uxorilocal residence. The ethnographer's proper question, Appell concludes, is not how productive efficiency determines residence but how factors of production influence the value systems that ultimately govern all decisions (pp. 143–146).

## Context

- The article draws on Appell's own fieldwork among the Rungus (Kudat District) and Harrison's fieldwork among the Ranau Dusun, supplemented by secondary sources from Woolley, Glyn-Jones, Evans, and Rutter—primarily colonial-era Native Affairs Bulletins and monographs on North Borneo customary law.
- Historiographically, the article is a direct corrective to Murdock's 1967 *Ethnographic Atlas* and to T.R. Williams' *The Dusun: A North Borneo Society*, challenging both the homogenizing "Dusun" category and Williams' identification of cognatic descent groups.
