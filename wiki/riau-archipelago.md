# Riau Archipelago

The Riau Archipelago, comprising approximately 3,214 islands in the waterways separating Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore, has served as a political, economic, and cultural nexus for successive Malay kingdoms since at least the late seventeenth century [[1, p. 127]](#ref-1). Its thousands of small islands, sparse populations, and inherent centrifugal tendencies meant that political centralization could never become strongly institutionalized; the state "merely floated on top of the local currents and eddies of an open sea" [[1, p. 127]](#ref-1). The archipelago's strategic position at the confluence of the Straits of Melaka, Singapore, and Riau made it a focal point for maritime trade, dynastic rivalry, and colonial intervention, and its indigenous populations—land- or sea-oriented, sedentary or nomadic—form part of an *Alam Melayu* (Malay world) that transcends the modern national boundaries drawn through its waters [[1, p. 124]](#ref-1).

## Political History and the Bugis-Malay Compact

The political history of Riau is inseparable from the arrival of five Bugis princes from Luwu' in South Celebes, who, as adventurers and free-lances, established dynastic rule across the Malay world between the early eighteenth and mid-nineteenth centuries [[2, p. 339]](#ref-2). The *Silsilah Melayu dan Bugis dan Sakalian Raja-raja-nya*, composed by 'Ali bin Raja Haji Ahmad Raja Haji and printed in Singapore circa 1900, traces their genealogy to La Maddusalat, the first Bugis convert to Islam, and their father Upu Daeng Rilaka, who had served as garrison commander at Mengkasar (Makassar) before sailing west [[2, pp. 341]](#ref-2).

The core political drama of the period centres on the rivalry between the Bugis and Malay factions for control of the throne. Following the murder of Sultan Mahmud at Kota Tinggi in 1699, the royal family relocated to Riau, and the succession crisis that followed was resolved through a formal compact between the Bugis princes and the Malay royal house [[3, p. 287]](#ref-3). The Bugis-Malay covenant established a distinctive constitutional arrangement: the Yamtuan Besar (the Malay sultan) was "like the wife," passive and receiving sustenance, while the Yamtuan Muda (the Bugis vice-regent) was "the husband," whose decisions governed all matters of state [[2, p. 353]](#ref-2). This arrangement was sworn on the Koran and proved durable, though it was repeatedly tested by the ambitions of Raja Kechi' of Siak, who swore a formal oath in a mosque not to commit treachery against Johore and Riau, only to break it within months and be defeated at least four times [[2, pp. 364–366]](#ref-2).

The *Tuhfat al-Nafis*, a chronicle authored by Raja 'Ali bin Raja Haji and edited by R. O. Winstedt in 1932, documents the escalating conflict between the Malay-Bugis alliance and the Dutch East India Company, culminating in the siege of Penyengat and the death of the warrior Raja Haji at Teluk Ketapang in 1784 [[3, p. 292]](#ref-3). Despite this military defeat, the chronicle records a period of economic prosperity driven by the trade of gambir and tin [[3, p. 291]](#ref-3). The political landscape shifted again with the British takeover of Malacca and Singapore, leading to the division of the Johor Empire, where the Sultanate of Singapore emerged under the Temenggong while Riau remained under Dutch influence [[3, p. 295]](#ref-3). The 1818 treaty between the Riau-Lingga rulers and the Dutch granted the latter veto power over the selection of a new sultan, the right to bring warships into the kingdom, and the establishment of a Resident in Tanjung Pinang [[4, p. 21]](#ref-4).

Winstedt's 1933 "Outline of a Malay history of Riau," drawn from the Malay manuscript *Sadjarah Radja-Radja Riouw I*, provides a condensed narrative of this turbulent period, covering the reigns from Raja Kechil through the Bugis-Malay succession crises to the consolidation of Marhum Janggut's rule, extending to approximately 1166 A.H. (c. 1753 CE) [[5, pp. 157–160]](#ref-5). The Dutch claimed $77,754 for the Linggi campaign, a debt that Marhum Janggut agreed to defray in exchange for the Bendahara Muda handing over Riau to him [[5, p. 158]](#ref-5). The manuscript also records that the Bendahara of Pahang sent letters annually to the Dutch, Bengal, Madras, and Batavia requesting help against the Bugis at Riau, indicating the international dimensions of what was ostensibly a local succession dispute [[5, p. 159]](#ref-5).

## Economic Life and Social Structure

During the reign of the Yamtuan Muda Upu Daeng Challa, Riau experienced a period of remarkable commercial flourishing. Hundreds of vessels came from Bengal, Java, the further East, China, and Siam; the people of Riau became rich, especially the Bugis, many of whom were exempted from paying custom-duty and harbour-dues [[2, p. 366]](#ref-2). Thousands of Chinese came to work as coolies in the gambier-plantations, and according to one tradition it was a Bugis, the Penggawa of Tarum, who first planted gambier by order of the Yamtuan Muda [[2, p. 366]](#ref-2). The revenues amounted to hundreds of thousands in every season, and a large fleet of heavily armed vessels was maintained [[2, p. 366]](#ref-2). Economic data from 1735 recorded in the *Tuhfat al-Nafis* indicates that a *pikul* of gambir could be bought for one and sold for 8 or 10 in Java, while Javanese rice fetched 3 Bengal *rupiah* a *pikul* [[3, p. 291]](#ref-3).

The social structure of the Riau Archipelago is characterized by a typology of indigenous habitation modes—sedentary or nomadic, land- or sea-oriented—that V. Wee has shown to be central to understanding local conceptions of civilizational status [[1, p. 124]](#ref-1). The civilizing process in Riau is fundamentally a process of increasing sedentism, whereby permanent settlement becomes a marker of social superiority and nomadic origins become a source of stigma requiring genealogical amnesia [[1, p. 129]](#ref-1). Communities with known Orang Laut origins develop marriage taboos and genealogical amnesia to expunge memory of nomadic ancestors, a mechanism through which they claim civilizational superiority over those with visible nomadic lineages [[1, p. 129]](#ref-1). The people of Karas Island, of known Orang Laut origin, describe themselves as Orang Berniaga (People who Trade) and maintain active trading routes from Singapore to Jambi via Sungai Jodoh in Batam [[1, p. 130]](#ref-1).

The spatial cognition of Riau inhabitants reflects a sea-centred logic that predates modern national boundaries. The Riau colloquial term *jemberang* (a local pronunciation of *menyeberang*, to cross a body of water) is used for crossing any of the three straits by sea and is not applied to air travel, indicating a sea-specific spatial logic [[1, p. 125]](#ref-1). By contrast, the term *menyeludup* (to sneak across without a passport) carries no colloquial pronunciation, marking it as belonging to a formal legal rather than vernacular framework [[1, p. 125]](#ref-1). The Malay word *pulau* in indigenous Riau usage refers only to small, directly experienceable patches of land; larger landmasses are called *tanah* or *bahagian*, a distinction confirmed by Wilkinson's dictionary [[1, p. 128]](#ref-1). Inhabitants describe the archipelago's extent as "a measure of black peppercorns" (*segantang lada hitam*) [[1, p. 127]](#ref-1).

## Cultural Life and Elite Formation

The cultural life of the Riau elite in the late nineteenth century reveals a pragmatic openness to Western influence that contrasted sharply with the virulently anti-European posture of earlier generations. Timothy Barnard's analysis of the Rusydiah Club, a literary and cultural organization based on Pulau Penyengat, demonstrates that the club's members—descendants of Bugis mercenaries who had shifted their political legitimacy from military to religious grounds—displayed a willingness to adopt Western cultural forms so long as they did not threaten their continued pre-eminence in the region [[4, pp. 17–18]](#ref-4).

The primary evidence for this shift is the pamphlet *Taman Penghiburan* ("The Garden of Leisure"), produced for an Aidil Fitri celebration in 1896, which lists fourteen specific games including a running race while lighting a cigarette, a sack race, a three-legged race, "blind horse and deaf jockey" (*kuda buta sais bisu*), and a Malay adaptation of pin-the-tail-on-the-donkey [[4, p. 33]](#ref-4). These activities bear no resemblance to traditional Malay holiday entertainments such as cock-fighting or communal dancing, and instead reflect Western-origin recreational forms [[4, pp. 33–34]](#ref-4). The *Mathba'at Al Riauwiyah* printing press was established on Penyengat in 1895 and operated until 1905, when Dutch pressure forced its transfer to Singapore, where it became the *Al Ahmadiyah* press [[4, pp. 26]](#ref-4).

The religious and spiritual dimensions of Riau's elite culture are also well documented. The *Tuhfat al-Nafis* records the study of the *tarikat* Khalwatiah and the performance of the Hajj, with R. Ahmad travelling to Mecca in 1828 [[3, pp. 295]](#ref-3). Southeast Asian hajj pilgrims rose from approximately 2,000 per year in the mid-1800s to over 5,000 by 1890, a trend facilitated by steamship travel and the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 [4, p. 21 n. 23]. Dutch restrictions on the hajj pilgrimage—including financial solvency tests and post-pilgrimage examinations—were not fully lifted until 1905 [4, p. 24 n. 36]. The suppression of Ilanun pirates in 1832, where leaders were beheaded after being captured with the aid of the Dutch and the Yamtuan Muda of Indragiri, illustrates the intersection of religious authority and military power in the archipelago [[3, p. 299]](#ref-3).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on the Riau Archipelago spans nearly a century and reveals shifting priorities in how the region has been investigated. The earliest engagements were fundamentally philological: Overbeck's 1926 translation of the *Silsilah Melayu dan Bugis* was undertaken at the request of Prof. Otley Beyer of the University of the Philippines, who sought Malay literature illuminating connections between the Malay kingdoms of the British and Dutch East and those of the Southern Philippines [[2, p. 339]](#ref-2). Winstedt's 1932 edition of the *Tuhfat al-Nafis* and his 1933 outline of the *Sadjarah Radja-Radja Riouw I* represent the early twentieth-century colonial scholarly practice of rendering Malay manuscripts accessible to the English-speaking academic community [[5, p. 157]](#ref-5), [[3, p. 285]](#ref-3). The *Tuhfat al-Nafis* edition relies on two manuscript copies: one from the Royal Asiatic Society (Maxwell MS, copied in 1890 from an original dated 1865) and another from the Johore royal family [[3, p. 285]](#ref-3).

A significant methodological shift occurred in the 1990s with Barnard's 1994 article on the Rusydiah Club, which moved beyond political and economic history to examine cultural attitudes and elite formation through a single surviving pamphlet [[4, pp. 17–18]](#ref-4). This approach—treating a minor documentary source as the evidentiary anchor for a broader argument about cultural change—filled a historiographical gap between Roff's and Andaya's studies of post-club nationalist activities and the earlier work of Muchtar Lutfi on the organization's internal structure [[4, pp. 17]](#ref-4). Barnard's 2016 article on the historiography of Sultan Mahmud Syah's death extended this approach further, applying Nancy Florida's "inscribing the future" framework to demonstrate that accounts of the regicide have been continually reshaped to serve the ideological needs of their authors, from contemporary European travelogues through nineteenth-century *hikayat* to twentieth-century film and comics [[6, pp. 1–23]](#ref-6).

The most recent contribution, V. Wee's 2018 article, represents a further methodological advance by grounding long-term ethnographic fieldwork in the Riau Archipelago (conducted since 1979) in a theoretical framework that links Geertz's *pasisir* typology with indigenous spatial cognition [[1, p. 124]](#ref-1). Wee's central argument—that the Riau colloquial term *jemberang* reflects a pre-national, sea-centred spatial logic that predates and transcends the modern nation-state boundaries drawn by the 1824 Anglo-Dutch Treaty—reframes the civilizing process as one of increasing sedentism rather than a linear progression toward fixed territoriality [[1, pp. 125]](#ref-1). This work, supplemented by her 1985 PhD thesis at the Australian National University on Malay hierarchies in Riau, contributes to the study of pre-modern maritime political formations by demonstrating how indigenous populations conceptualize movement across the straits in terms that remain fundamentally at odds with the bounded nation-state framework imposed by colonialism [[1, p. 124]](#ref-1).
