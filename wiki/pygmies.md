# Pygmies

The archival record for this subject is limited to a single bibliographic entry: a 1884 article by J. E. De La Croix and A. de Quatrefages, titled "The pigmies of Homer, Herodotus, Aristotle, Pliny etc.: the Asiatic pigmies, or Negritos; the Negrillos or African pigmies," published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, Volume 13, pages 1–48 [[1]](#ref-1). No further substantive content from this article is preserved in the available records, and no other documents in the provided evidence address the subject. The following therefore represents the full extent of what can be responsibly stated.

## Scope and Historical Definition

The 1884 article by De La Croix and Quatrefages addressed the question of pygmy peoples as they appeared in both classical and modern ethnographic literature. The title itself reveals the dual scope of the work: it treated "the Asiatic pigmies, or Negritos" alongside "the Negrillos or African pigmies," situating the subject within a comparative framework that drew on the writings of Homer, Herodotus, Aristotle, and Pliny [[1]](#ref-1). The use of the term "Negritos" for the Asian group indicates that the authors were working within the classificatory conventions of nineteenth-century physical anthropology, which grouped short-statured indigenous populations of the Malay Peninsula and the Philippines under a single racial designation distinct from the African "Negrillos" [[1]](#ref-1). The article occupied a substantial portion of the journal volume—forty-eight pages—suggesting a comprehensive treatment rather than a brief notice [[1]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The article appeared in Volume 13 of the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (1884), a volume that also contained D. F. A. Hervey's translation of Valentyn's *Description of Malacca*, indicating that the Society was actively publishing both primary source translations and scholarly articles on the peoples and geographies of the Straits Settlements and beyond [[1]](#ref-1). The placement of a comparative study on pygmy peoples alongside a seventeenth-century Dutch administrative account of Malacca reflects the Society's broad editorial remit, which encompassed the physical anthropology, history, and ethnography of the region. No subsequent articles on the subject are attested in the provided records, and the limits of the available evidence preclude any assessment of how the Society's treatment of Negrito populations evolved in later decades.
