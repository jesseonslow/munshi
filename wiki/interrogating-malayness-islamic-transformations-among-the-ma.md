# Interrogating ‘Malayness’: Islamic transformations among the Malay College Kuala Kangsar (MCKK) cohort

Huang Jianli's 2014 article chronicles the eruption of civil society activism in Singapore following the government's September 2011 announcement of an eight-lane highway through the Bukit Brown Chinese cemetery, a site of over 100,000 graves that had fallen into decades of public amnesia. The central thesis is that the Bukit Brown saga marks a genuine turning point in Singapore's state-society relations: after four decades of systematic depoliticization under the People's Action Party, the limits of the dominant party-state's developmental hegemony were reached, and heritage, history, and identity surged to the forefront of citizenry consciousness in ways that interrogated the fundamentals of governance (pp. 21–22).

## Summary

The article situates Bukit Brown within the long arc of Singapore's state-society relations, tracing how civil society space expanded under early British light-touch rule, contracted progressively from the 1920s regulatory tightening through post-independence PAP dominance, and remained locked in what the author calls "old politics" through the Goh Chok Tong and early Lee Hsien Loong eras despite rhetorical gestures toward openness (pp. 23–24). The author argues that previous scholarship on Chinese burial grounds in Singapore—principally the work of Brenda Yeoh and Tan Boon Hui—had centred the contestation between Western and Chinese burial practices and between parochial sub-communal interests and modernist developmental regimes, without bringing heritage concerns directly into the microphysics of power. The Bukit Brown narrative, by contrast, reveals a new dimension: citizens asserting the intrinsic worth of history and heritage against the state's cost-benefit calculus (pp. 24–25).

The article then chronicles the activism itself in its multiple registers. Cyberactivism via Facebook groups and blogs provided an exceptional space beyond state policing; established civil society organizations (the Nature Society, Singapore Heritage Society) issued position papers and organized tours; and an unexpected spark came from the Asia Paranormal Investigators, whose ghost-tour business led brothers Raymond and Charles Goh to become the movement's most visible figures (pp. 25–27). The author documents the government's responses—the $250,000 documentation project, the vehicular bridge compromise, the refusal to share the biodiversity impact assessment—and the escalating civil society counter-responses, including a mock National Day Parade, a UN Human Rights letter, and ultimately the World Monuments Fund's 2014 Watch listing (pp. 28–35).

A distinctive analytical move is the author's emphasis on the temporal interplay unique to deathscape heritage: the buried bodies constitute a "heavy and haunting baggage of the past," traceable kinship ties anchor the present, and the presumed presence of ancestral spirits weighs on the future in ways that inanimate heritage objects cannot (pp. 38–39). Youth engagement—students from Raffles Girls' School, Pioneer Junior College, and polytechnics producing research booklets—demonstrates this interplay most vividly, with the doyen of Chinese Overseas studies Wang Gungwu praising the younger generation's work as fusing past, present, and future into a single act of historical meaning-making (pp. 39–40).

### Key Findings

- The government allocated only S$250,000 (inclusive of equipment and researchers) for the documentation of highway-affected graves, a figure activists contrasted with the hundreds of millions spent on other research grants and with the high public cost of maintaining the Sun Yat-sen Nanyang Memorial Hall at Wanqingyuan (p. 28).
- The Nature Society recorded 91 species of resident and migratory birds at Bukit Brown, of which 13 were nationally threatened species (equivalent to 23 per cent of the 56 species in the Singapore Red Data Book); the site was listed in its 1990 Master Plan for the Conservation of Nature (p. 31).
- By October 2013, free heritage tours organized by All Things Bukit Brown, the Singapore Heritage Society, and the Nature Society had reached more than 7,000 participants, including 450 students from Raffles Girls' School in a single outing (p. 27).
- The government's March 2012 compromise reduced projected grave exhumation from 5,000 to 3,746 by substituting a 600-metre vehicular bridge for a ground-level road segment; the construction contract was awarded in August 2013 with the highway expected operational by end-2017 (pp. 22, 30).
- In July 2013, the government created a new Impact Assessment and Mitigation Division within the National Heritage Board and renamed the Preservation of Monument Board as "Preservation of Sites and Monuments"; Prime Minister Lee Hsien Loong announced a S$5 million grant over four years for citizenry heritage projects (p. 36).
- The PAP's vote share in the May 2011 general election fell to 60.1 per cent, its lowest since 1965, and its endorsed presidential candidate captured only 35.2 per cent in August 2011, providing the political context in which the Bukit Brown activism gained traction (p. 38).

### Conclusion

Huang Jianli concludes that the Bukit Brown saga is a turning point in two interlocking senses: it demonstrates that the limits of the dominant party-state have been reached, with citizens now demanding genuine social space for expression and policy input, and it signals a shift in national priority away from the long-held fixation on modernist economic development toward the intrinsic value of heritage, history, and identity. The old binary of "spaces of the dead giving way to the living" is no longer tenable; the cost-benefit equations of development planning are henceforth more complex, and the government itself—through institutional restructuring and the Prime Minister's acknowledgment that "the government does not own the Singapore heritage"—has been forced to adapt (pp. 36–37).

## Context

- The article draws primarily on contemporary media reports, organizational position papers, social media archives, and interviews rather than traditional archival collections; its evidential base is the public record of the 2011–2013 controversy as documented in Singaporean English and Chinese press, NGO publications, and online platforms.
- Historiographically, the study extends the Yeoh–Tan framework on the politics of Chinese burial grounds in Singapore by introducing heritage activism and citizenry consciousness as an analytical category, arguing that the Bukit Brown episode moves beyond the earlier literature's focus on state regulatory power and developmental planning to reveal a new axis of contestation centred on the intrinsic cultural value of the past (pp. 24–25).
