# Interrogating ‘Malayness’: Islamic transformations among the Malay College Kuala Kangsar (MCKK) cohort

Patricia Sloane-White's 2014 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines a generational rupture among graduates of Malay College Kuala Kangsar (MCKK), the elite "school for sultans' sons" founded in 1905. Drawing on two decades of anthropological fieldwork (1993–1998 and 2007–2012), Sloane-White traces how a cohort of self-described "outlier" old boys—mostly in their early forties—have rejected the ethnic, UMNO-aligned, and NEP-era identity that defined their senior counterparts, replacing it with a shariah-based Islamic corporate identity that interrogates Malayness itself (pp. 21–22).

## Summary

Sloane-White employs Louis Chauvel's concept of the "social generation" to distinguish two MCKK cohorts shaped by different historical moments. The first, studied in the mid-1990s, was formed by the 13 May 1969 racial crisis and the subsequent New Economic Policy; these men—many of them "Halim's boys," the inner circle around entrepreneur Halim Saad and Finance Minister Daim Zainuddin—built interlocking corporate networks (Renong, Proton, UEM, TV3) and presented themselves as self-made "New Malays" who had earned their capitalist success through merit and hardship (pp. 23–26). They maintained a tight old-boy solidarity, symbolized by the "Big Tree" on the MCKK campus and the MCOBA association, which journalist Rehman Rashid likened to "the nearest thing to a Masonic lodge this country's ever known" (p. 25).

The second generation, encountered between 2007 and 2012, had undergone what they described as religious "awakenings" at university, forging affiliations with Islamist groups such as JIM (Jamaah Islah Malaysia) and Ikram, the latter consciously modelled on the Muslim Brotherhood (p. 27). These men—prosperous directors of Islamic banks, takaful companies, IT firms, and corporate training providers—openly mocked MCKK's ethnic and aristocratic culture, deriding their senior old boys as "Malay-puteras," "UMNO-puteras," and "MCKK-puteras" (pp. 28–29). They rejected the school's hierarchical *adik/abang* system, its royal associations, and what they perceived as a culture of dishonesty and racial competition with the Chinese (p. 29).

Yet Sloane-White demonstrates that this rejection is incomplete. The outlier old boys remain embedded in privileged networks, now reconstituted under the banner of *al-ukhuwah al-Islamiyyah* (Islamic brotherhood) rather than MCKK blood ties. They continue to do business with UMNO-aligned entities—Rozali counted Khazanah, run by old boy Azman Mokhtar, among his best clients—and employ shariah-approved stratagems (*hiyal*) to render *haram* dealings *halal*, including channeling profits from such engagements to PAS or *zakat* as a form of spiritual counterbalancing (pp. 32–33).

### Key Findings

- The outlier cohort explicitly sought to "sever or amputate (memotong) MCKK from my life," a sentiment repeated across the group, yet their business networks remained structurally dependent on the same elite access channels their seniors had built (pp. 27–28, 32).
- These men ran companies significantly smaller in scale than the 1990s-era NEP conglomerates but positioned themselves as agents of a "global Islamic economy" in which Malaysia would become the "centre of gravity" (pp. 27, 30).
- Despite rejecting racialism, the outlier old boys maintained strict ethnic boundaries in hiring: one insisted his company was "100 per cent Muslim," and they cited MCKK old boy Rameli Musa (CEO of Ingress) as a model for engaging Chinese only through contracts, never as employees (p. 31).
- The group articulated a vision of an "Islamic NEP" that would produce a generation of Muslim economic leaders whose shariah-based success would eventually subsume Chinese economic hegemony, with the Chinese required to "live within the terms of Malaysian Islam or leave" (p. 32).
- Their critique of MCKK extended to the institution's royal symbolism—the four dormitories named after sultans, the sultan's palace nearby, and ceremonial visits by royalty—which they reframed as "a demonstration of the wastefulness and error in honouring a man over God" (p. 29).
- The outlier old boys' material lifestyle (company cars, gated homes, overseas university tuitions) remained indistinguishable from their senior counterparts; what shifted was the *moral justification* for wealth, recast as *barakah* (divine blessing) earned through *halal* practice rather than ethnic privilege (p. 31).

### Conclusion

Sloane-White's definitive takeaway is that the outlier old boys represent not a break from Malay elite capitalism but its Islamic refashioning: the objective, practice, and intent of their capitalist striving have shifted, but the pursuit of capitalist success itself has not (p. 30). Their identity is now sourced in Islam rather than *Melayu*, yet the structural privileges of their MCKK origin persist beneath the new moral vocabulary, revealing that "interrogating Malayness" functions less as a genuine abrogation of elite status than as a new legitimating framework for it.

## Context

- The article draws on Sloane-White's long-term ethnographic access to MCKK old-boy networks, supported by the Fulbright Commission, the International Islamic University of Malaysia, and Universiti Malaya (p. 21, n. 1).
- The piece situates itself within a broader scholarly conversation on Malay Islamization, engaging directly with the work of Anthony Milner on *bangsa*-claims, Judith Nagata on *umma* networks, and Yao Souchou on Bumiputera anxiety (pp. 30, 32).
