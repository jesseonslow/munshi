# Horsing around Melayu: Kuda kepang, Islamic piety, and identity politics at play in Singapore’s Malay community

Barbara Watson Andaya (2014) examines the letters of John Adolphus Pope, a fourteen-year-old British country trader who sailed through the Bay of Bengal between 1785 and 1788, to illuminate how knowledge was gathered, filtered, and transmitted within the expanding British "information order" of the late eighteenth century. The article argues that Pope's youthful correspondence reveals the complex interplay between textual learning, personal observation, local informants, and material collection that underpinned British commercial and intellectual engagement with Southeast Asia at a time when imperial dominance was still aspirational rather than achieved.

## Summary

The article situates Pope's letters within Christopher Bayly's framework of the "information order" and George Miller's work on country traders as key accumulators of regional knowledge. Pope, recruited by the Orientalist Sir William Jones as an "assiduous auxiliary," was tasked with collecting written descriptions and physical specimens from ports across the Bay of Bengal. The letters, published by Anne Bulley in 1992, cover a three-year voyage from Madras through the Strait of Melaka to China, and they reveal how a young, intellectually curious mind navigated between the limited books available to him aboard ship, the information supplied by fellow traders, and the knowledge provided by local informants (pp. 1–3).

Andaya traces the multiple channels through which Pope acquired and transmitted knowledge: his modest library aboard the *Princess Royal* (Chambers's *Cyclopaedia*, Pluche's *Spectacle de la Nature*, Boyle's chemical works); his exchanges with fellow country captains; his socializing with Dutch traders in Melaka; and above all, his cultivation of local informants through language acquisition, gift exchange, and personal friendship. The article highlights how Pope's youthful perspective—neither fully adult nor child—produces a distinctive voice that sometimes reveals European racial prejudice but more often conveys genuine cross-cultural empathy and respect for indigenous knowledge systems (pp. 6–17).

The article also addresses the limitations and inaccuracies inherent in Pope's reporting, noting how information passed through trader networks could rest on longstanding errors, and how Pope's own observations were sometimes unreliable. Yet the letters remain valuable for their detailed descriptions of local economies, defences, social structures, and material culture in ports like Kedah, Pedir, Melaka, and Yangon, much of which is not available elsewhere (pp. 8–13).

### Key Findings

- Pope was appointed Third Officer on the 300-ton Malabar-built country ship *Princess Royal* in December 1785 at age fourteen and did not return to Kolkata until October 1788 (p. 4).
- He dispatched to India a deal box with four partitions containing shells, rice and pepper, wood specimens, and a Batak bow with spear and three arrows (p. 12).
- From China he sent 57 pictures of fish varieties, 94 of fruit, and 113 paintings of different trades (p. 12).
- His description of Kedah in April 1786 notes a brick fort with cannon "nothing larger than a six pounder," and that vessels of 200–300 tons could navigate the river at spring tide (p. 10).
- He reported that not less than 1,000 Chinese had settled in Penang by December 1787, and predicted the new settlement would eclipse Melaka (p. 4).
- His detailed account of betelnut production distinguishes white, red, and "Chicanee" varieties by their market destinations (China, Pegu, Coromandel) and notes that red betel in Pedir was only ready in April (p. 11).

### Conclusion

Andaya concludes that Pope's letters, though sometimes marked by the racial attitudes of his era, ultimately project an appealing honesty and cross-cultural openness that distinguished him from many of his compatriots. He functioned as a small but genuine cog in the expanding information order that supported British economic and political power in the Bay of Bengal, and his later career as a country captain, Sheriff of Mumbai, and editor of the *Mumbai Gazette* confirms the credentials his youthful work earned him. The letters offer a rare window into the lived experience of knowledge exchange in a period when British ascendancy was still a project rather than a fait accompli (pp. 17–18).

## Context

- The primary source is the unpublished correspondence of John Adolphus Pope, held by independent researcher Anne Bulley and first published in her 1992 monograph *Free Mariner* (p. 2, n.6).
- The article extends Bayly's "information order" framework and Miller's work on country traders to a younger, less prominent figure, foregrounding the role of personal relationships and gift exchange in enabling cross-cultural information flow (pp. 1–3).
