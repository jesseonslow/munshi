# Chinese names: notes on the use of surnames and personal names by the Chinese in Malaya

The document provided is an 1899 obituary for Sir William E. Maxwell, K.C.M.G., written by C. W. S. K. and published in the Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society. It is a commemorative sketch of Maxwell's career in the Straits Settlements and the Federated Malay States, his scholarly contributions to Malay studies, and his role in sustaining the Society during its early decades. (Note: the metadata references a 1959 article by R. Jones on Chinese names, but the source text supplied is this 1899 in memoriam piece; the summary below reflects the text actually provided.)

## Summary

The obituary traces Maxwell's career from his early employment in the Supreme Court (1855–1869) through his service as Assistant Government Agent in Province Wellesley (1874), District Commissioner in the Perak War (1875), Assistant Resident of Perak (1878), Commissioner of Land Titles, Resident Councillor of Penang (1884–1889), British Resident of Selangor (1889), Colonial Secretary of the Straits Settlements (1892), and finally Governor of the Gold Coast, where he died in the prime of life. The author frames Maxwell as a figure of exceptional dual achievement: a capable administrator whose legislative work on land tenure and district administration proved enduring, and a scholar whose output on Malay proverbs, folklore, and language was substantial and influential.

The second half of the piece turns to Maxwell's relationship with the Society itself. Elected Honorary Secretary in 1883 and Vice President in 1893, Maxwell is credited with sustaining the Society's journal during a period of scanty membership support. The author enumerates his contributions — papers on Malay proverbs, the Sakais, shamanism in Perak, land tenure, the Dutch in Perak, and a journey to the Patani frontier — as well as his edition of three Malay fairy tales and his *Manual of the Malay Language*. The obituary closes with a reflection on the fragility of the Society's institutional life, noting that its early survival depended heavily on one man's energy and that maintaining the standard Maxwell set would require increased effort from members.

### Key Findings

- Maxwell served in the Supreme Court from 1855 to 1869, his father Sir P. Benson Maxwell being Chief Justice of the Colony; he qualified as an advocate in 1867 (p. 2).
- He served as District Commissioner with the Larut field force during the Perak War of 1875, was specially mentioned in despatches, and received the Perak Medal (p. 2).
- He was created a C.M.G. for services connected with the Nisero affair, and later a K.C.M.G. for distinguished services in the Ashanti Expedition (p. 3).
- He was elected Honorary Secretary of the Society in 1883 and held the office until 1887; elected Vice President in 1893 (p. 3).
- He bequeathed a fine library of Malay manuscripts to the Royal Asiatic Society (p. 4).
- His *Manual of the Malay Language* is identified as the work by which he is likely to have exercised his widest influence on learners of the language (p. 4).

### Conclusion

The author's definitive takeaway is that Maxwell's loss is the most severe the Society has sustained since its foundation in 1877, and that the institution's early viability was in large measure a function of one man's sustained effort. The obituary issues an implicit warning: without increased cooperation from members, the standard of the journal that Maxwell set may not be maintained, echoing the fate of Logan's *Straits Asiatic Society* journal, which perished for want of sufficient contributors.

## Context

- The author signs only as "C. W. S. K." and writes from the position of a contemporary Society member; the tone is that of an official tribute rather than a critical assessment.
- The piece reflects the colonial administrative framing of the period, presenting Maxwell's career as a linear progression of imperial appointments and crediting him with institutional achievements (district administration, land legislation) that the author treats as self-evidently beneficial.
