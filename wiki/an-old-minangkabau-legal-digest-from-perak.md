# An old Minangkabau legal digest from Perak

C.A. Gibson-Hill, W.W. Skeat, and F.F. Laidlaw published this comprehensive account of the Cambridge University Expedition to the Siamese-controlled Malay states of the east coast peninsula in 1953, drawing on Skeat's personal diaries and field notes from 1899–1900. The volume presents a detailed ethnographic, zoological, and botanical survey of Patani, Kelantan, Trengganu, Kedah, Perlis, and Upper Perak, produced at a moment when European contact was rapidly transforming the social and administrative fabric of these states.

## Summary

The expedition was conceived by W.W. Skeat, a Selangor District Magistrate and scholar of Malay magic and custom, who feared that contact with outside cultures was disintegrating the social life of both Malays and aborigines. Funded largely by the Worts Fund and modelled on the Haddon Expedition to New Guinea, the party comprised Skeat (leader), T.N. Annandale (zoologist), Richard Evans (morphologist), D.T. Gwynne-Vaughan (botanist), F.F. Laidlaw (medical practitioner and naturalist), and R.H. Yapp (botanist), accompanied by nine Malays and a Javanese servant. The main party departed England in January 1899, reached Bangkok in March, and proceeded south through Singgora, Patani, Jalor, Raman, Trengganu, and the upper Kelantan and Pergau rivers before turning back to Kedah, Perlis, and Setul in late 1999 and early 1900 (pp. 5–8).

Skeat's narrative, which forms the bulk of the volume, is organised as a day-by-day journal and covers an extraordinary range of subjects: the full cycle of Malay ceremonial life (weddings, circumcisions, funerals, spirit-boat launches, tolak bala rites), the administrative machinery of Siamese governance in the Malay states, local economies (salt-pans, rice cultivation, opium farming, the bunga mas tribute system), and the beliefs and practices of both Malays and Negritos. His observations on the Phram (Brahmin) community at Lampam, the Samsam (Malayo-Siamese) population, and the elaborate spirit-hall (balai hantu) architecture of Patani and Kelantan represent some of the last systematic recordings of these traditions before their rapid disappearance (pp. 15–19, 64–66, 133–134). The account of the failed attempt on Gunong Tahan in August–September 1899 is a dramatic narrative of survival in uncharted highland jungle, where the party ran out of rice, lost all their matches to flooding, and were forced to descend on raw rice and jungle fruit (pp. 99–111).

Laidlaw's supplementary narrative covers the party's stay at Kuala Aring and their subsequent expedition to Gunong Inas in Upper Perak, where they established a camp at 3,000–4,000 feet and collected numerous new species, including a rhododendron, two rats, a keelback snake, a pit-viper, and a millipede (pp. 152–164). The volume closes with a list of over thirty scientific papers published from the expedition's collections, spanning zoology, botany, and ethnography, the most significant being Skeat and Bladen's *The Pagan Races of the Malay Peninsula* (1906) (pp. 171–174).

### Key Findings

- The expedition documented the bunga mas tribute system in detail: at Patani the gold tree was valued at over $5,000 and the silver tree at $5,000; at Jering the combined value was estimated at $7,000; at Perlis the total expenditure was approximately $2,000 (pp. 67, 77–78, 137).
- The Sleeping Buddha at Gua Gambar (Biserat) measured approximately 96 feet in total length, with a left arm of 42 feet and a mouth 1.5 feet wide; the figure was carved in solid limestone and flanked by colossal cobra figures, one with seven heads in a single hood (pp. 39, 44–45).
- Setul's government farm revenue (Pajak Besar) rose from $10,000 to $26,000 in two years, and import/export duties (Pajak Chukai) from $900–$1,000 to $3,180 in the same period, reflecting the rapid economic opening of the port (p. 131).
- The Kelantan gaol at Kota Bharu contained approximately forty prisoners in cells measuring six feet by six feet by twenty-two inches; the gaoler was ignorant of the prisoners' sentences and crimes, and the Sultan supplied an average ration of six gantang of rice per day (p. 112).
- During the smallpox epidemic of 1899–1900, up-river Malays in Kelantan and Jering set sick children adrift on rafts with a plateful of rice and a small vessel of water; several children were found washed ashore at Pasir Mas, Pasir Kankong, and Bukit Pandai (pp. 114–115).
- The expedition's Gunong Inas camp at 3,000–4,000 feet yielded sixteen bird species, four mammal species (including two new rat species, *Rattus alticola inas* and *R. edwardsi ciliatus*), a new keelback (*Natrix inas*), a new pit-viper (*Trimeresurus popeorum*), and a new millipede (*Platyrrhachus humberti*) (pp. 161–164).

### Conclusion

Skeat's account stands as an irreplaceable contemporary record of the Siamese Malay states at the precise moment of transition from traditional to colonial administration, capturing ceremonial practices, economic structures, and administrative arrangements that were already in visible decline by the time of his writing. The volume's enduring value lies not in any single discovery but in the density and precision of its ethnographic detail, recorded by a scholar who combined civil service experience with rigorous academic training and an unusual fluency in both Malay and Siamese.

## Context

- The primary archival source is Skeat's personal manuscript, originally prepared in 1935–40 from diaries and field notes, revised in 1952 shortly before his death, and abridged by Sir R.O. Winstedt with further revision by Gibson-Hill for publication (p. 8).
- The expedition's scientific collections are held at the University of Cambridge (Zoology Department, Herbarium, and Museum of Ethnology and Anthropology), and the volume's appendix lists over thirty resulting publications, the most significant being Skeat and Bladen's *The Pagan Races of the Malay Peninsula* (1906), which remains the only authoritative general survey of the subject (pp. 171–174).
