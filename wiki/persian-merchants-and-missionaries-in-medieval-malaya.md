# Persian merchants and missionaries in medieval Malaya

Roland Braddell's 1969 article responds to a 1948 challenge by Han Wai Toon and H.D. Collings, who had argued that the ancient Chinese toponyms Tan-ma-hsi and Lung-ya-men referred to the Johore area rather than Singapore, and that no significant settlement had ever existed on Singapore Island. Braddell systematically defends the traditional identification of Tan-ma-hsi with Singapore and argues that Lung-ya-men (Dragon Teeth Gate) most probably designates the western entrance to Keppel Harbour, drawing on Chinese navigational texts, Portuguese accounts, and early British eyewitness descriptions of the ancient Malay settlement.

## Summary

The article opens by confronting Collings' claim that the absence of archaeological remains on Singapore Island disproves the existence of ancient Singapura. Braddell counters that the settlement's reality is attested by the Javanese *Pararaton* and *Nagarakretagama*, the *Sejarah Melayu*, and—critically—by the eyewitness accounts of Stamford Raffles, Major Farquhar, and John Crawfurd in 1819–1822, who described a substantial walled enclosure, a hilltop temple complex, Malay royal graves, and Chinese coins on what was then still a largely undeveloped island. He argues that the subsequent destruction of the site during British construction rendered the absence of later archaeological evidence uninformative, and that the settlement's modest size is consistent with its strategic position on the China–India sea route rather than any claim to urban grandeur.

The core of the article is a philological and geographical analysis of the two Chinese toponyms. Braddell demonstrates that Tan-ma-hsi is an Amoy Hokkien transcription of the Malay *Temasek* (rendered *Tam-ma-sak* in the Amoy dialect), making the identification with Singapore Island virtually certain. For Lung-ya-men, he distinguishes it from the earlier Ling-ya-men (the "Strait of Lingga" of the 13th-century *Chu fan chi*) and argues that the "Dragon Teeth Gate" description—two hills resembling the cable pegs in the bows of a Chinese junk—fits the western entrance to Keppel Harbour far better than the Singapore main strait or any passage in the Lingga archipelago. He supports this with Linschoten's 1598 description of the harbour entrance, the 1618 *Tung hsi yang k'ao* sailing directions, and the Malay name *Selat Panikam* ("pointed-weapon strait") recorded by Crawfurd.

A complicating factor is the *Wu pei chih* charts, whose sailing directions Mr. J. V. Mills has worked out as running through the Singapore main strait rather than Keppel Harbour. Braddell acknowledges this but suggests the Chinese may have retained the old name after switching to the main strait, paralleling the Portuguese shift from the Keppel passage (called *Estreito Velho* on Eredia's 1604 map) to the main strait (*Estreito Novo*) by 1615. He notes that the main strait's dangerous tidal swirls would have made it unattractive to 14th-century junks, lending further weight to the Keppel Harbour identification for the earlier period.

### Key Findings

- Dr. W. Linehan's dating places Singapura's existence at 1299–1391 A.D., with its sack by the Javanese in 1376 A.D.; the *Nagarakretagama* is dated 1365 A.D. (p. 11).
- Crawfurd's 1822 description records the ancient wall as approximately 16 feet broad at its base and 8–9 feet high, running nearly a mile from the sea to the base of a 150-foot hill; Chinese brass coins of the 10th and 11th centuries (Emperors Ching chung d. 967, Jin-chung d. 1067, Shin-chung d. 1085) were found among the ruins (pp. 22–23).
- The *Tao i chih lio* (1349 A.D.) records that Siamese raiders arrived with seventy junks, attacked the city moat of Tan-ma-hsi, and were repelled after a month's resistance when an Imperial envoy passed through (p. 17).
- Linschoten (1598) describes the Keppel Harbour entrance as "about a stone's throw across between two high mountains" with a least depth of four and a half fathoms; the *Tung hsi yang k'ao* (1618) gives depths of thirty *t'ō* in the middle and twenty *t'ō* in the south (pp. 19, 18).
- Eredia's 1604 map labels the Keppel passage "Estreito Velho" and the main strait "Estreito Novo"; the Portuguese adopted the main strait (Governor's Strait) by 1615 A.D. (pp. 20–21).
- The *Wu pei chih* charts give a course from Lung-ya-men to Pedra Branca of 75°–90° (per J. V. Mills) or 82.5° (per Hsu Yun-ts'iao), which Mills interprets as running through the Singapore main strait rather than Keppel Harbour (pp. 20–21).

### Conclusion

Braddell concludes that Tan-ma-hsi is beyond reasonable doubt Singapore Island, and that Lung-ya-men most probably refers to the western entrance of Keppel Harbour in its pre-British topographical form, when the hills at either end were higher and the passage narrower. He concedes that the *Wu pei chih* sailing directions point to the main strait, but attributes this to a later change in navigational practice with retention of the older name, and maintains that the physical description of "Dragon Teeth" fits Keppel Harbour's entrance far more naturally than any other candidate passage.

## Context

- The article engages directly with the 1948 *Journal of the South Seas Society* debate (Han Wai Toon, Collings, Hsu Yun-ts'iao) and builds on the earlier work of J. V. Mills on the *Wu pei chih* charts and Eredia's map (1930).
- Braddell draws on primary Chinese sources (*Chu fan chi*, *Tao i chih lio*, *Hsing ch'a sheng lan*, *Tung hsi yang k'ao*, *Wu pei chih* charts), Portuguese accounts (Linschoten, de Barros, Eredia), and the unpublished Crawfurd Journal (1822), the latter reprinted in full as an appendix to the article.
