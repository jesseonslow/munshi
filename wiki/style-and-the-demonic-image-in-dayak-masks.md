# Style and the demonic image in Dayak masks

Sarah Gill's 1967 article "Style and the Demonic Image in Dayak Masks" (JMBRAS 40.1, pp. 78–92) presents a pilot stylistic survey of Dayak masks held in Western museum collections, arguing that the extraordinary formal assurance of central Kalimantan and Sarawak mask traditions derives from a deeply rooted, ancient Southeast Asian protective demonic imagery that predates and is distinct from Indianizing influence.

## Summary

Gill organises her analysis around four broadly defined style areas—west Kalimantan, south Kalimantan, central Kalimantan, and Sarawak—using documented type-specimens from museum collections to establish stylistic parameters, then filling in gaps with masks of unknown provenance whose characteristics align with those parameters. The west and south Kalimantan styles are shown to be heavily indebted to Indianized tradition, exhibiting what Gill terms an "esthetic timidity" and "self-conscious particularism" characteristic of folk arts that imitate alien civilisational models. The west Kalimantan masks, in particular, are linked to Javanese *wayang topeng* prototypes and ultimately to the sculptural vocabulary of Pallava and Chalukya India, while south Kalimantan masks display a range from delicate Sampit faces to the more powerful Dusun *sabuka* forms that begin to break free of Indianized constraints (pp. 75–79).

The article's central argument shifts to the masks of central Kalimantan and Sarawak, which Gill identifies as the most potent Dayak style areas. These masks—characterised by huge round eyes, fanged mouths, bulbous noses, and extravagant earpieces—functioned in agricultural rites as protective demonic images representing *hantu* spirits whose terrible countenances warded off evil and retrieved the rice-soul. Gill demonstrates that this demonic iconography is not an Indian import but a widespread Southeast Asian phenomenon, tracing parallels to the *kala* heads of Javanese architecture (which she argues predate Indian *kirtimukha* influence), the *singa* faces of Batak Sumatra, the *Rangda* of Balinese drama, and ultimately to a wooden figure from a 4th–3rd century B.C. funerary chamber at Ch'ang-sha in south China that reproduces the full vocabulary of the Southeast Asian demonic image (pp. 87–92).

The methodological basis is primarily museum collections in Leiden, Amsterdam, London, Copenhagen, New York, Philadelphia, Cambridge, and Chicago, supplemented by ethnographic accounts from Nieuwenhuis, Elshout, Tillema, Hose, Roth, and others. Gill is explicit that her style areas represent only a "coarse approximation" of actual production zones on an island of Borneo's vast size, and that the study is intended to open avenues for further iconographical and stylistic research rather than to provide a definitive map (p. 75).

### Key Findings

- Of 43 documented masks from central Kalimantan and Sarawak examined for the study, 30 possessed demonic features, making the demonic image the dominant iconographical theme in these style areas (p. 87).
- The earliest documented appearance of a Southeast Asian-type demonic image occurs in south China during the later Chou dynasty (4th–3rd centuries B.C.), in a wooden figure from a funerary chamber at Ch'ang-sha, Honan, which exhibits huge bulging eyes, a snubbed nose, a wide fanged mouth, a tongue hanging to the waist, horns, taloned paws, and a scaly body pattern interpretable as shaggy hair (pp. 91–92).
- The Javanese *kala* head appears on the earliest *tjandi* buildings of the 8th-century Dieng Plateau, and the *tjandi* form itself has no Indian precedent, supporting the argument that the Indonesian demonic image predates Indian influence; diverging *makara* and demonic heads of the Javanese type do not appear in India until the 10th century (p. 90).
- The west Kalimantan masks were collected by Dutch officials at Ngabang and donated in 1893 to the Rijksmuseum voor Volkenkunde in Leiden; they were used in court ceremonies celebrating ritual immersions, circumcisions, ear-piercings, Koran readings, and marriages of the local rajah's children (pp. 75–76).
- Central Kalimantan masks from the Mendalam river were used in agricultural rites extending over three periods of nine days each, with masked dances on the first day of the second and third periods; similar rites on the Mahakam occurred every two or three years (p. 80).
- Gill extends the argument to Oceanic art, noting that middle Sepik River masks in New Guinea share the same demonic predication (huge round eyes, wide mouths, protruding tongues) and protective function, suggesting the ancient Southeast Asian demonic concept may have stimulated the development of these styles (p. 92).

### Conclusion

Gill's definitive historical takeaway is that the protective demonic image constitutes an ancient, coherent iconographical tradition in Southeast Asia—evident from at least the 4th–3rd centuries B.C. in south China and the 8th century in Java—that was largely submerged by the overwhelming cultural pressures of China and India along coastal regions but survived and flourished in the vast, isolated interior of Borneo. The dazzling formal assurance of central Kalimantan and Sarawak Dayak mask carving is thus not an isolated primitive achievement but the inheritance of a tradition whose roots lie in an anciently codified vocabulary of demonic features, a tradition that may also have seeded the development of vigorous Oceanic style areas to the east.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the collections of the Rijksmuseum voor Volkenkunde (Leiden), the Koninklijk Instituut voor de Tropen (Amsterdam), the British Museum (London), the Nationalmuseet (Copenhagen), the American Museum of Natural History (New York), the University Museum (Philadelphia), the Peabody Museum (Cambridge, Massachusetts), the Chicago Museum of Natural History, and the Sarawak Museum (Kuching), supplemented by ethnographic publications by Nieuwenhuis, Elshout, Tillema, Hose, Roth, and Low.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the then-emerging field of comparative primitive-art history by proposing Borneo as a critical "back door" through which Asian influence may have passed into Oceania, and by challenging the assumption that all demonic imagery in Southeast Asian art derives from Indian sources.
