# The freedom of labour under Raffles’ administration in Java, 1811–16

H.R.C. Wright's 1953 article examines the tension between Raffles' stated commitment to abolishing feudal labour services in Java and the practical demands of colonial administration during 1811–16. Drawing on Public Consultations and the Raffles Collection in the India Office, Wright argues that Raffles' enthusiasm for the freedom of labour grew over time but was consistently qualified by exceptions for public works, coffee cultivation, and colonisation schemes.

## Summary

Wright situates Raffles' labour policy within the broader problem of governing a Javanese society structured around centuries of feudal service (corvée). Lord Minto's foundational directive called for the creation of private property in land with free disposal of its produce, which implied the abolition of feudal services but did not explicitly guarantee the free disposal of labour. The practical difficulty was immediate: without compulsory service, the government would struggle to obtain labour for public works, and export crop production (particularly coffee) would suffer, since Javanese peasants preferred rice cultivation.

Wright traces the evolution of Raffles' approach through three phases. The first, in January 1813, saw the sale of large tracts of land to Europeans on conditions that abolished feudal services but transferred administrative control of minor chiefs and headmen to the new proprietors — a compromise that in practice allowed landowners to command their tenants' labour. The second phase, the general land reform of mid-1813, extended the abolition to leased lands in village units, with paid labour for public works, though Raffles explicitly acknowledged that services would not be voluntary and that exceptions would be necessary. The third phase, from 1814 onward, saw Raffles attempting to enforce his police regulations on the private estates, limiting landowners' control over tenants and requiring registration of any contracts below the government rate of hire.

Throughout, Wright highlights the exceptions that qualified the principle: Preanger was excluded from reform to preserve coffee cultivation as feudal service; the Ceylon recruitment of 1813 was handled by appealing to the Sultan of Madura rather than by conscription; and the colonisation schemes at Carimon Java and Banjarmasin involved the forced transportation of "less criminal persons" and convicts. Wright treats these not as contradictions but as evidence of a policy in which Raffles recognised the need for exceptions to general principles while his personal commitment to emancipation strengthened over time.

### Key Findings

- The January 1813 land sales abolished feudal services on alienated estates but transferred control of minor chiefs and headmen to the proprietors, effectively preserving a mechanism for commanding tenant labour (pp. 3–4).
- The general land reform of 14 June 1813 leased lands in village units to minor chiefs and headmen, with obligations to furnish paid labourers for public service; Raffles noted the payments would serve as a record to trace "unnecessary or unauthorised employment" (p. 4).
- Preanger was entirely excluded from the 1813 reforms to maintain coffee cultivation as a feudal service, on the advice of Superintendent T. McQuoid (p. 4).
- In June 1813, Raffles refused Captain de Busche's request to conscript 500 Javanese for Ceylon military service, instead appealing to the Sultan of Madura; by October 1813 only 90 men had been recruited (pp. 5–6).
- A 1815 regulation required the registration of all contracts between landowners and tenants providing services below the government rate of hire, with unregistered arrangements deemed a deviation from the terms of sale (p. 9).
- In September 1815, after Cranssen's protest in Council, Raffles suspended further enforcement of police regulations on Dutch-era estates and referred the matter to the Supreme Government in Bengal, effectively conceding the point (p. 10).

### Conclusion

Wright's definitive takeaway is that Raffles' freedom-of-labour policy was neither a consistent ideological programme nor a mere rhetorical gesture, but a pragmatic and evolving position in which the principle of emancipation was progressively strengthened as Raffles warmed to his role as land reformer, yet was always tempered by the administrative and economic necessities of colonial rule. The 1815 suspension of enforcement on the older Dutch estates marked the limit of what Raffles could achieve within the constraints of his authority and the opposition of the European landowning class.

## Context

- Primary sources: Public Consultations (various dates 1812–1815), India Office Raffles Collection, and India Office Java despatches (Java 69, 70).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Raffles' administrative record by demonstrating that his labour policy was more coherent and principled than earlier accounts suggested, while also revealing the structural limits imposed by the colonial landowning interest.
