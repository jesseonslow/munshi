# Report on the exploration of the caves of Borneo. A.H. Everett, Introductory remarks J. Evans, and notes on bones collected G. Busk

A.H. Everett's 1880 report, supplemented by introductory remarks from John Evans and osteological notes from George Busk, documents a systematic 1878–79 exploration of thirty-two limestone caves in Upper Sarawak and Niah, north-west Borneo. The central finding is that while the caves yielded ossiferous deposits and human remains, all proved geologically recent—post-dating the last marine transgression of the region—rendering them useless for resolving questions about the deep antiquity of human occupation or faunal evolution in the Indo-Malayan subregion.

## Summary

Everett's exploration was motivated by the hope of discovering cave deposits of sufficient antiquity to illuminate the origins of the human race and the developmental history of Borneo's fauna. He examined thirty-two caves, carrying out partial excavations in twelve, and identified a remarkably consistent stratigraphic sequence across sites separated by considerable distances (Jambusan and Niah). The series comprises, from top to bottom: a surface layer of Dyak camp debris (charcoal, pottery, glass beads); a talus of loam and limestone blocks crowded with rodent bones and land-shell tests; a stratum of river mud mixed with bat-guano and containing broken, water-worn mammalian bones of extant genera; and a basal yellow clay with pig bones and land-shell casts, resting directly on the limestone floor (pp. 275–277).

The critical argument concerns the age of these deposits. Everett reasons that the cave contents must have accumulated since the last submergence of north-west Borneo, which he estimates at a minimum of 500 feet based on marine denudation features on inland limestone hills and the transport of cinnabar pebbles to the Busan Hills' summit. Supporting evidence includes the absence of heavy crystalline stalagmite floors, the absence of large mammals (elephant, rhinoceros, tapir, wild ox) from the north-west districts, the rapid present-day seaward advance of the Sarawak coast, and the slight differentiation of Borneo's fauna since its last connection with continental Asia (pp. 277–278).

Human remains were found in eleven caves, always near entrances on steep cliff faces, associated with glazed and painted pottery, dark-blue glass beads, iron, and manufactured gold. A single Neolithic stone celt, examined by Sir Charles Lyell, was found in river gravel on the Siniawan—the only evidence of stone-tool manufacture yet discovered in Borneo. Busk's osteological analysis of the human bones (at least five individuals) noted an "exaggerated Malay aspect" in one facial specimen but concluded the remains could equally be of Chinese origin, pointing to the bulging of the sphenoidal fossa as a distinguishing feature (pp. 285–287).

### Key Findings

- Thirty-two caves examined; twelve partially excavated; the consistent four-layer deposit series found in caves Nos. V, XIII, XXI, and XXXII at floors approximately 40 feet above the surrounding flat land (pp. 275–277).
- Last marine submergence of north-west Borneo estimated at a minimum of 500 feet, evidenced by marine denudation on inland hills and cinnabar pebbles on the Busan Hills' summit (pp. 278, 282).
- Mean annual rainfall at Kuching over three years: 165 inches; limestone hills in Sarawak typically 300–800 feet in height, maximum 1,800 feet (pp. 274–275).
- Cape Sirik advancing seawards at approximately three fathoms per year, with one elder Paloh Malanau identifying nearly two miles of new land within his memory (p. 283).
- Busk's cranial measurements: calvaria circumference 20 inches, longitudinal diameter 7 inches, width 5.25 inches, height 5.7 inches; orbital index 0.83; nasal index 0.61 (pp. 285–286).
- A single Neolithic-type stone celt, found in Siniawan river gravel and examined by Sir C. Lyell, constitutes the only known evidence of stone-tool manufacture in Borneo (p. 279).

### Conclusion

Everett's definitive takeaway is one of measured disappointment: the exploration proved the existence of ossiferous caves and confirmed the co-occurrence of man with Borneo's fauna, but the recent geological age of all deposits and the advanced civilisation of the associated human remains meant that no light was thrown on the origin of the human race, the deep history of the Indo-Malayan fauna, or the pre-Malayan peopling of the island. He recommended that further cave research in north-west Borneo be left to private enterprise until the higher, unexamined regions of the north-east could be investigated (p. 281).

## Context

- The exploration was conducted under the patronage of the White Rajah administration in Sarawak; Everett's map and plans were offered for deposit in the Society's archives, and specimens were distributed between the British Museum and the Royal College of Surgeons (p. 274).
- The report reflects the Victorian-era scientific agenda of using cave archaeology to resolve questions of human origins and racial classification, a framework that shaped both the selection of sites and the interpretive language employed throughout.
