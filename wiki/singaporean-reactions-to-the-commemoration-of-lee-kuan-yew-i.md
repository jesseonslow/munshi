# Singaporean reactions to the commemoration of Lee Kuan Yew in Tamil Nadu

## Abstract

When Lee Kuan Yew passed away, many of the national and even some international rituals of mourning were expected. However, what took Singapore by surprise was an outpouring of grief and memorializing in the Indian state of Tamil Nadu. Singaporean mass media reported that in the village of Ullikkottai and the surrounding area, families mourned the death of a leader to whom they attributed their region’s prosperity and economic development. This article uses Arnold van Gennep’s observations on rites of passage and Victor Turner’s notion of liminality to examine the public reaction to media coverage of mourning rituals in Tamil Nadu. These reports reflexively shed light on the complex relationships and histories of race, immigration, statehood, and national identity in Singapore, all brought into focus between March and September 2015.

## Summary

This article examines how Singaporean mass media and social media users reacted to reports of grassroots mourning rituals for Lee Kuan Yew in the Tamil Nadu villages of Ullikkottai and Mannargudi following his death. Cherian frames the analysis through Arnold van Gennep's rites of passage and Victor Turner's concept of liminality, arguing that the week of national mourning, the SG50 celebrations, and the 2015 general election together created an extended liminal period in which Singapore as a nation-state had to reconstruct its identity without its founding father. The Tamil Nadu mourning rituals functioned as "sacra" within this liminal space—symbolically charged elements that catalysed reflection on race, immigration, statehood, and national identity (pp. 138–139).

The core argument is that Singaporean media coverage and social media responses to the Tamil Nadu rituals reflexively exposed fault lines in Singaporean society that state-sponsored rites of passage typically keep below the surface. By analysing official news sources, alternative outlets, and social media responses across Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, and the HardwareZone forum, Cherian demonstrates how the mourning became a site for contesting narratives about foreign workers, immigration policy, racial hierarchy, and PAP governance. The reactions ranged from genuine gratitude toward foreign workers to condescending mockery and outright racist stereotyping, revealing the gap between Singapore's official multicultural discourse and lived experience (pp. 146–150).

The article draws on Spivak's argument that colonial archives construct the colonizer rather than the colonized, applying this logic to show that Singaporean media narratives about Tamil Nadu mourning ultimately constructed Singaporean self-understanding rather than accurately representing the Tamil Nadu community. The hyperbolic and disproportionate framing of the rituals in mainstream outlets—described as a "Singapore craze"—served as a mode of abstraction that highlighted Singapore's own anxieties about transnational belonging and the status of its South Asian minority (pp. 142–145).

### Key Findings

- Lee Kuan Yew died on 23 March 2015; the 2015 general election was moved forward from its expected 2016 date to September 2015, extending the liminal period that also encompassed SG50 on 9 August (pp. 138–139).
- In Ullikkottai (approximately 3,000 households) and Mannargudi, residents planned a large-scale brass statue, a memorial hall, and a museum dedicated to Lee; a local man named his newborn son after Lee (pp. 137, 143).
- As of 2014, Chinese made up approximately 74 per cent of Singapore's citizen and permanent resident population, while citizens and PRs constituted less than two-thirds of the total resident population (pp. 140–141).
- The 2013 Little India riot, sparked by the death of construction worker Sakthivel Kumarvelu, was the first riot Singapore had experienced in over 40 years; the government subsequently re-examined foreign worker policies on income, housing, and social status (p. 142).
- In the 2011 general election, the PAP received 60.1 per cent of the vote—a figure explicitly referenced in social media responses linking the Tamil Nadu mourning to political cynicism (p. 147).
- The 2015 election campaign shifted foreign worker experiences to the periphery and re-centred mainstream Singaporean perspectives, with workers' rights and human rights issues discussed after the 2013 riot largely absent from both PAP and opposition rhetoric (p. 150).

### Conclusion

Cherian concludes that the Tamil Nadu mourning rituals introduced a new dimension into Singapore's national consciousness by de-centring the Singaporean nation-state and foregrounding the structural subalternity of foreign workers. The post-liminal identities that emerged were multifaceted and more reflective of actual pre- and post-liminal society than the cohesive citizenry the state-sponsored rites aimed to produce. The author argues that how Singapore engages with these transnational dialogues and negotiates its social contract with foreign workers will be key to shaping the country's future (p. 151).

## Context

- **Primary sources:** Singaporean mainstream and alternative news articles (*Straits Times*, *Today*, Channel News Asia, Asia One, Mothership.sg, Must Share News, All Singapore Stuff) and social media posts from Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, and HardwareZone, all accessed August–September 2015 (p. 140).
- **Historiographical contribution:** Applies ritual theory (van Gennep, Turner) and postcolonial theory (Spivak) to contemporary Singaporean media discourse, addressing a noted gap in scholarly research on anti-Indian racism and the politics of foreign worker representation in Singapore (p. 141).
