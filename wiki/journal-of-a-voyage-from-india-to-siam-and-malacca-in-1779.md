# Journal of a voyage from India to Siam and Malacca in 1779

Dr. Johann Georg Koenig's 1779 voyage journal, translated from his British Museum manuscripts and published in 1894, documents a naturalist's systematic botanical, zoological, and mineralogical survey of the Malay coast from Junk Ceylon (Penang) through Malacca to Quedar (Kedah), providing one of the earliest European scientific accounts of the region's biodiversity and indigenous practices.

## Summary

Koenig, a German naturalist serving aboard a Dutch East India Company vessel, undertook this voyage as part of a broader scientific mission to catalogue the flora and fauna of the Malay Archipelago. The journal covers his time ashore at Junk Ceylon (Penang), where he conducted intensive botanical and entomological fieldwork across multiple islands including Pullu Salang, Pullu Jambu, and the mainland, before falling ill with a violent bilious fever that forced him to transfer to Captain Scott's ship "Prince" bound for Malacca. The Malacca section records his recovery, continued botanical work in the town's gardens and surrounding swamps, visits to the Dutch governor's estate, and observations of the leper hospital, Chinese funerary customs, and the gambier-boiling industry at Pringi. The final section covers his return voyage south to Quedar, where he observed the king's audience, local agriculture, and coastal ecology.

The journal is notable for its methodological rigor within the Linnaean system. Koenig describes numerous species in detail—particularly orchids (Epidendra), gingers (Monandria/Scitamina), palms, and butterflies—often noting their medicinal uses among the Siamese, Malay, and Chinese populations. He records practical ethnographic information on the preparation of dragon's blood, the manufacture of agar-agar from seaweed, the collection of damar resin, the processing of gambier, and the smelting of tin. His observations on the leper hospital at Malacca, including clinical examination of patients and critique of the attending physician's methods, represent an early attempt at systematic medical observation in the region.

The text also captures the political and commercial context of the 1770s Malay coast: the presence of multiple English and Dutch ships, tensions between English captains and the Siamese king, the slave trade conducted by Malay islanders, and the commercial networks linking tin, gambier, damar, and dragon's blood to the broader Asian trade. Koenig's account of the Chinese funeral ceremony at Malacca and the king of Quedar's audience provide rare contemporary descriptions of local political and religious life.

### Key Findings

- Koenig describes the gambier-boiling establishment at Pringi in detail: three large holes three feet in diameter and two feet deep, surrounded by bark circlets about 1¼ feet high, with wooden gutters three feet wide and four feet long on a slanting stand; the whole complex measured fourteen paces long and eight paces wide (p. 65).
- The leper hospital at Malacca, founded by the Dutch East India Company and maintained by alms, contained twelve inmates at the time of Koenig's visit (all native Portuguese, Malays, and Chinese); a gate dated 1697 remained from an earlier, larger building plan, but the current enclosure was merely a fence of thin poles measuring about 150 steps square (pp. 60–61).
- Koenig records that the tin from Sallangor (Selangor) was considered superior to that of Junk Ceylon, while tin from the kingdom of Rhombo (Ranong) was considered the best and commanded the highest price (p. 33).
- A Chinese merchant at Tarnah (Teluk Anson) described the primitive tin-collecting method: violent rain washed earth away to uncover tin, which old women collected and brought to the smelter, who rendered them one-third of what they brought, since the prevailing custom was to give the smelter one-quarter of whatever he smelts (p. 25).
- Koenig's account of the Argus pheasant (Juno bird) describes a male of peacock size with tail feathers two of which were twice as long as the whole bird, and wing feathers more than two feet in length with kidney-shaped eyes showing iridescent bands of white, black, dark-brown, greenish, yellow, and blue-white (pp. 59–60).
- The journal records that the Damar resin from Quedah, when fresh, was greenish-white and quite transparent like Venetian glass, but turned increasingly yellow over time; a piece of five ounces' weight was shown to Koenig, and the clear fine variety could be sold in Bengal at a much higher price than common damar (p. 22).

### Conclusion

Koenig's journal stands as a foundational document of 18th-century natural history in the Malay world, combining meticulous Linnaean taxonomy with practical ethnography and commercial observation. Its value lies not in any single discovery but in the density and specificity of its field notes, which preserve details of species, techniques, and social arrangements that would otherwise have been lost. The account also illuminates the conditions under which European naturalists operated in the region—dependent on local guides, constrained by monsoon weather and tropical disease, and embedded in the commercial and political structures of the colonial trading world.

## Context

- The primary source is Koenig's own manuscript held in the British Museum, translated for publication by the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society in 1894; the text is marked "END OF VOLUME 3" and "END OF PART 2" and "END OF VOLUME XV," indicating it is an excerpt from a larger multi-volume work.
- Koenig was a German naturalist in the service of the Dutch East India Company (VOC), and his journal reflects the scientific priorities of the Linnaean era, with extensive use of Linnaean sexual classification (Monandria, Didynamia, Polyadelphia, etc.) and frequent reference to Rumphius's *Herbarium Amboinense* and Burmann's *Flora Zeylanica* as comparative authorities.
