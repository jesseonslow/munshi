# A cenotaph for Singapore: contestation and community at the Straits Settlements war memorial

## Abstract

The Singapore Cenotaph is a First World War memorial built when the island-city was a British colony. The design of the monument and its use in Remembrance Day celebrations by the British colonial administration departed from the less overtly patriotic commemorative practices common in Europe in the 1920s. Regardless of the social significance the British administration hoped to vest in the monument when it was commissioned, contemporary English- and Chinese-language sources indicate that British authorities had little control over this ‘sacred’ space at the heart of their administrative capital, and the Asian populations of Singapore freely interpreted it as they wished, sometimes reworking the official meaning to suit their own ends.

## Summary

Clay Eaton examines the Singapore Cenotaph, erected in 1922 as the Straits Settlements War Memorial to commemorate the 124 local residents who died fighting in Europe during the First World War. The article's central argument is that the monument functioned as a contested site where the boundaries of colonial community were delineated, transgressed, and renegotiated. Although the British colonial administration commissioned the Cenotaph to reaffirm the centrality of the European community in Singapore, Eaton demonstrates that they had neither a monopoly on its interpretation nor effective control over the public space it occupied.

Eaton draws on both English- and Chinese-language press sources—particularly *The Straits Times* and the *Nanyang Siang Pau*—alongside colonial administrative records to trace how different social groups engaged with the monument. Poorer Asian residents, excluded from the colonial community the Cenotaph was built for, used the site as a recreational space, sitting on its steps and hanging clotheslines from it, despite repeated British protests. Anglophone Asians, by contrast, actively claimed membership in an elite imperial community defined by class and respectability rather than race, pressing for a role in war commemoration and protesting when Indian police guards blocked their access to the monument.

The article also situates the Singapore Cenotaph within broader debates in memorial studies, contrasting it with Lutyens' austere Whitehall original. Eaton argues that the colonial version, designed by Denis Santry of Swan & Maclaren, was deliberately more overtly patriotic than its metropolitan model—incorporating a bronze crown, a laurel wreath of victory, and a granite sarcophagus—because in a colony far from the war, the monument served less as a site of individual mourning and more as a tool of British community-building.

### Key Findings

- The Executive Council resolved to erect a public war memorial in December 1918 and set aside $100,000 for its construction; the final tender from Swan & Maclaren was $125,000, approved by the Legislative Council in October 1921 (pp. 32, 37–38).
- The monument was unveiled by the Prince of Wales on 31 March 1922, with former French Premier Georges Clemenceau in attendance at the foundation-stone laying on 15 November 1920 (pp. 36–38).
- By the 1921 census, only 36.6 per cent of Singaporeans were literate in any language and a mere 8.3 per cent in English, which severely limited the effectiveness of multilingual signs posted at the Cenotaph to deter 'improper' use (p. 43).
- In 1927, Governor Hugh Clifford, a Catholic, removed Anglican services from Remembrance Day ceremonies to make them 'non-religious'; an Anglican-led prayer was reintroduced in 1928, with Clifford notably absent (pp. 40–41).
- The Cenotaph was reworked in 1951–52 to commemorate Second World War dead, but was subsequently overshadowed by the Kranji War Memorial (1957) and the Civilian War Memorial (1967) (pp. 46–47).
- In 2012, a fitness group called Journey Fitness incorporated the Cenotaph into a workout routine, triggering a media controversy that Eaton uses to draw parallels with the 1920s contestation over the site (p. 47).

### Conclusion

Eaton's definitive takeaway is that the Singapore Cenotaph was never simply an instrument of British imperial power but a dynamic site where multiple communities—European colonizers, Anglophone Asian elites, and the broader Asian population—negotiated their respective claims to belonging and respect. The monument's ceremonial practice expanded over the 1920s to encompass a multiracial conception of imperial community based on class and respectability, even as white racism persisted elsewhere in colonial society. This history of contestation continues into the present, where the Cenotaph remains a site for negotiating the boundaries of Singaporean national community.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on English-language newspapers (*The Straits Times*, *The Singapore Free Press and Mercantile Advertiser*, *Malaya Tribune*), Chinese-language newspapers (*Nanyang Siang Pau*), and unpublished colonial administrative papers held in the CO275 series (The National Archives, Kew).
- Historiographically, the study contributes to the growing body of work on the 'instabilities and vulnerabilities of colonial regimes' by shifting focus from European actors to Anglophone Asian elites as self-interested agents who used the language of class and respectability to claim privileged positions within the imperial system, building on the work of Chua Ai Lin, Mark Ravinder Frost, and Brenda Yeoh.
