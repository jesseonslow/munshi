# Sir Arthur Young and political control of the Chinese in Malaya and the Straits Settlements

C.F. Yong and R.B. McKenna (1984) examine how Sir Arthur Henderson Young, Governor of the Straits Settlements and High Commissioner of the Federated Malay States from 1911 to 1919, exercised political control over a Chinese community that constituted 34% of Malaya's and 72% of Singapore's population (p. 1). Their central thesis is that Young was a shrewd but reactive administrator who combined personal consultation with Chinese community leaders, targeted legislative amendments, and wartime emergency powers to manage rising Chinese nationalism—yet his failure to recognise the political threat posed by vernacular education left him unprepared for the May Fourth disturbances of 1919.

## Summary

Young inherited a multi-racial colony in which continuous Chinese immigration from Guangdong and Fujian had created a large, dialect-divided population increasingly drawn into Chinese nationalism following the 1911 Revolution. The fall of the Manchu regime and the rise of the Kuomintang (KMT) as successor to the Tung Meng Hui oriented Chinese political loyalty toward China, a development the British perceived as a threat to colonial stability (p. 1). Young's first major test came with the 1912 Kuala Lumpur New Year riots, in which anti-Manchu revolutionaries forcibly removed queues from pro-Manchu reformists; police fired on a brawling crowd, killing ten. Young's official response deliberately downplayed the nationalist dimension, emphasising inter-pang factionalism and the cooperation of "leading Chinese who were always willing to assist the Government" (p. 7). Behind this cautious demeanour, however, he began examining loopholes in the Banishment Ordinance and immigration procedures that allowed deported political activists to re-enter illegally (p. 8).

Young's legislative programme between 1913 and 1919 operated on two tracks: control of individuals and control of organisations. On the individual track, he pushed through amendments to the Banishment Ordinance providing life sentences for illegal re-entry, and successfully argued that banishment cases should remain within the jurisdiction of the Executive Council rather than the courts—a shrewd move that defused accusations of racial bias with the support of Chinese unofficial members such as Eu Tong Sen (p. 8). The 1913 amendments to the Societies Ordinance added three new controls: mandatory disclosure of constitution and membership documents, prohibition of unauthorised fund collection by persons of the Chinese race on behalf of any society, and power to search registered societies without notice (p. 10). Between 1912 and 1919, six of some twenty-seven KMT branches in the SS and FMS were deregistered, though the policy was not consistently applied (p. 10). The declaration of Martial Law in February 1915, following the Singapore mutiny, gave Young sweeping powers over the China-born as "technical aliens," restricting movement, communications, and fund collection (p. 9).

Young's most distinctive instrument of control was his personal relationship with Chinese community leaders. He maintained a policy of consultation that earned him the description from Lim Boon Keng as "the best Governor we have ever had" (p. 22). This rapport facilitated the passage of the War Tax Bill in January 1917, supported by unofficial members including Lim Boon Keng and Tan Jiak Kim and backed by 1,200 petitioners (p. 18). He also used selective naturalisation as a political reward, maintaining high fees (S$500 for imperial naturalisation) to prevent the honour from being "cheapened" in the eyes of the community (p. 13). Yet the article identifies a critical blind spot: the British had no policy toward Chinese vernacular education, which between 1911 and 1919 created over fifty schools teaching Chinese geography, history, and literature in Mandarin, with the theme of Malaya as *terra irredenta* (p. 18). This "quiet revolution" produced a generation of politically conscious young Chinese that Young never identified as a threat until the May Fourth movement erupted in 1919 (p. 20).

### Key Findings

- The 1912 Kuala Lumpur riots killed ten people when police opened fire; Young's official memorandum to London in October 1912 deliberately minimised the nationalist dimension, attributing the violence to inter-pang factionalism (pp. 7–8).
- The 1913 Societies Ordinance amendments prohibited unauthorised fund collection "by persons of the Chinese race... on behalf of any Society," directly targeting the KMT's core activity of generating financial support for Sun Yat-sen (p. 10).
- Martial Law declared in February 1915 gave Young power to restrict individual movement, private communications, and public fund collection by the China-born as "technical aliens," effectively overriding the Legislative Council's rejection of the Banishment amendments in November 1914 (p. 9).
- Malayan Chinese provided 53 aeroplanes for the British war effort, and leading figures such as Eu Tong Sen and Lim Peng Siang individually funded tanks and aeroplanes, demonstrating the political dividend of Young's consultation strategy (p. 14).
- The May Fourth disturbances of 1919, triggered by the Versailles decision to allot German concessions in Shantung to Japan, prompted Young to banish eleven teachers and recommend special legislation to control vernacular schools and their teachers, who "had formed one of the main channels through which anti-Japanese agitation had made itself felt" (pp. 20–21).
- Young's recommendation for supervision of vernacular education became the Schools Ordinances of 1920 in both the FMS and SS, a year after his retirement, establishing a template for British control of Chinese education that persisted into the next decade (p. 21).

### Conclusion

Yong and McKenna conclude that Young was "far more than a quiet governor who did not rock the boat" (p. 23). His skillful fostering of relationships with Chinese community leaders secured wartime loyalty and the passage of contentious legislation, while his legislative amendments to the Banishment and Societies Ordinances laid the groundwork for the eventual banning of the KMT in 1925. However, his essential character as a reactor rather than a forward planner meant that his most significant recommendation—supervision of vernacular schools—was only made after the 1919 disturbances exposed the limits of his understanding. He "paved the way; his successors had to do the building" (p. 23).

## Context

- The article draws extensively on Colonial Office despatches (CO 273 series) and Chinese Protectorate annual reports held in the Public Record Office, London, as well as the *Malayan Review of Chinese Affairs* and *Straits Times* reporting.
- The biographical appendix (pp. 27–30) profiles fourteen prominent Chinese community leaders under Young, documenting their political affiliations (KMT, Straits Chinese British Association), professional roles, and British honours received, providing a reference framework for the article's analysis of consultation and control.
