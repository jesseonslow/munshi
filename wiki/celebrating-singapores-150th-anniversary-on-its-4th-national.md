# Celebrating Singapore’s 150th anniversary on its 4th national day (9 August 1969

## Abstract

The commemoration of Singapore’s bicentenary in 2019 attracted scholarly and public attention that included questions about whether the start of colonial rule was a cause for celebration. The 150th anniversary of the arrival of Stamford Raffle, celebrated as part of the country’s National Day in 1969, received much less attention. This article explores how that anniversary was marked as part of the country’s fourth National Day celebration. Two years earlier, the Ministry of Culture had decided not to mark the event on a large scale, but a Cabinet meeting in February 1968 reversed that decision. This article argues that the new approach was driven both by an effort to use the event to build a national identity and bolster national confidence, and a wish to express goodwill towards the British and signal openness to them and the world. The article argues that differences in the way the two anniversaries were marked are emblematic of how Singapore’s self-conception as a nation has changed.

## Summary

Ng Paul Seen examines how Singapore marked the 150th anniversary of Stamford Raffles' arrival in 1819, an event that coincided with the country's fourth National Day on 9 August 1969. The central historical problem is one of apparent contradiction: the People's Action Party government, which had built its pre-independence identity on anti-colonialism and had actively removed colonial symbols from public life, chose to celebrate the sesquicentennial on a large scale and with conspicuous British participation. Ng argues that this was not a reversal of principle but a pragmatic deployment of history for two simultaneous purposes—domestically, to forge a shared national past and instil confidence in a young republic facing the imminent British military withdrawal; internationally, to signal goodwill to Britain and demonstrate to potential investors that Singapore had transcended anti-colonial resentments.

The article draws heavily on British Foreign and Commonwealth Office despatches, particularly those of High Commissioner Sir Arthur de la Mare, which reveal the "insider" deliberations behind the anniversary's design, alongside Singapore Ministry of Culture memoranda, official speeches by Lee Kuan Yew and S. Rajaratnam, and contemporary newspaper reporting. Ng situates the sesquicentennial within the broader context of the Five Power Defence Arrangement negotiations and the accelerated British military pullout, showing how the anniversary served as a diplomatic instrument as much as a national one. He also contrasts the 1969 marking with the 2019 bicentennial, arguing that the differences between the two are emblematic of Singapore's evolving self-conception as a nation.

### Key Findings

- A Cabinet meeting on 7 February 1968 reversed the Ministry of Culture's December 1967 decision not to mark the anniversary on a national scale, opting instead for "celebrations on a large scale" (pp. 60–61).
- The British military withdrawal was brought forward from the mid-1970s to 1971; the bases accounted for more than 20 per cent of Singapore's GDP and 25,000 base workers faced job losses (pp. 70–71).
- Princess Alexandra attended the National Day parade as the Queen's "Special Ambassador"; the British national anthem *God Save the Queen* was played at the parade for the first time since 1963 (pp. 63–64).
- Rajaratnam confirmed to the Australian High Commission that the parade invitations to the other Four Powers were deliberately aimed at the Five Power Defence Arrangement, stating "this was precisely what had been in Lee Kuan Yew's mind" (p. 72).
- A Chinese Chamber of Commerce essay-writing competition linked to the anniversary attracted more than 4,000 submissions (p. 73).
- In 1972, a new Raffles statue was erected bearing the inscription "with genius and perception [Raffles] changed the destiny of Singapore," marking the apotheosis of the hagiographic treatment begun in 1969 (p. 73).

### Conclusion

Ng concludes that the sesquicentennial was a one-off, state-directed exercise in which history was instrumentally curated to serve contemporary political needs—building a common national past, projecting confidence, and maintaining strategic and economic ties with Britain. The episode reveals that the Singapore leadership's "anti-colonialism" was directed at the mode of governance rather than at the British themselves, and that their positive sentiment towards Britain was a foundational assumption underlying the anniversary's design. The contrast with the 2019 bicentennial, which extended Singapore's narrative back to 1299 and deliberately downplayed Raffles, demonstrates how the country's self-conception has shifted from one of dependence on a colonial origin story to one of confident, multi-layered historical depth.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: British National Archives (FCO 24/117, 24/118, 24/379, 24/543, 24/583, 24/586, 57/143) and National Archives of Singapore (Ministry of Culture file MC 0229/64 Pt I Vol. I), supplemented by speeches, newspaper archives (*The Straits Times*, *Nanyang Siang Pau*, *Sin Chew Jit Poh*), and oral interviews.
- Historiographical contribution: The article fills a specific gap in Singapore historiography by studying the sesquicentennial as a discrete event, challenging the conventional scholarly narrative (Hong & Huang, Loh, Lau) that the 1960s represented a period of historical "downplaying" by showing that the government actively and publicly deployed history for nation-building purposes during this decade.
