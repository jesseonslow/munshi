# Foreword (J.M. Gullick Festschrift

J.L. Humphreys, a serving officer of the Malayan Civil Service, published "A Naning Recital" in 1921, presenting a restored and translated Malay customary poem (*teromba*) recited by an elder named Ungkai Lisut at a Naning wedding in the early 1900s. The article documents the remarkable survival of Menangkabau customary law (*Adat*) in the Naning settlement of Malacca despite the political annihilation of its tribal constitution following the Naning War of 1831–1832.

## Summary

The article centres on the recovery, restoration, and English translation of a lengthy Malay recital that encodes the mythological origins and legal principles of Menangkabau customary law. Humphreys first heard a shorter version at a wedding feast in Alor Gajah in 1908, after which Ungkai Lisut provided a tattered manuscript of the longer text. The restoration proved difficult because the old man's oral memory proved more accurate than his document; additional passages were recovered during a 1914 visit to Singapore following a period of prayer and fasting (p. 1).

The recital is structured in three parts: a prelude addressing the bride's clan elder (lines 1–51), a "Song of Origins" narrating the myth of Maharaja Di Raja and the division of his realm between two chieftains who established the two great Malay legal traditions (lines 52–276), and a peroration stating the speaker's purpose in paying the bride-price (lines 277–330) (p. 3). Humphreys provides the Malay text alongside his English translation, extensive footnotes elucidating obscure passages with reference to Negri Sembilan chiefs and earlier journal publications, and a final section analysing the accentual prosody of the verse (pp. 20–29).

The broader historical framing situates Naning within the Negri Sembilan: founded by Sumatran immigrants in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries who brought the *Adat Menangkabau* of exogamous tribes, matrilineal property descent, and mild criminal procedure of compromise and reparation. After passing under Portuguese and then Dutch nominal sovereignty, Naning remained in effect an autonomous semi-democratic state until the East India Company's attempts to levy full tribute triggered the 1831–1832 war. The tribal constitution was abolished and Naning became a Malacca district divided into Mukims under territorial Penghulus, yet the Custom persisted with notable vitality in all matters affecting property, marriage, and inheritance (p. 1).

### Key Findings

- The Naning War of 1831–1832 ended with Dato' Said's defeat; the tribal constitution was abolished and even the use of the terms *Dattoo* and *Sookoo* was formally forbidden (p. 1).
- The two mythic law-givers, Dato' Temenggong Bendahara Kaya and Dato' Perpatch Pinang Sabatang, were assigned separate realms at Bandar Rokan: the seaward Kampar Kiri received the law of talion ("the slayer shall be slain"), the landward Kampar Kanan the law of reparation ("whoso slays shall replace") (pp. 10–14).
- Only four of the original twelve Menangkabau tribes remain identifiable in Naning: Seri Melenggang, Tiga Batu, Mungkal, and Anak Melaka (p. 21, note 7).
- The Attorney General J.R. Innes cited the Naning Malays in the Legislative Council of Malacca, quoting Virgil's *O fortunatos nimium* in support of an amendment to the Malacca Lands Ordinance designed to secure their ancestral holdings (p. 1, note 1).
- The prosody is accentual rather than metrical, with two key accented words per line, originally performed with a beat of the *rebana* drum; Humphreys compares the system to Old English alliterative verse (p. 28).
- Ten years of the rubber industry had already left a measurable mark on the Custom's hold over economic life, though the union of ancient *Adat* with lenient British rule had produced a stable peasant society (p. 2).

### Conclusion

Humphreys' definitive historical takeaway is that the Naning peasantry's comfort derives from the conjunction of ancient customary law and a lenient colonial administration, producing "so comfortable a lot with so few regrets for the past." The survival of the *Adat* is attributed partly to the proximity of Rembau, where the fuller custom still operates, and partly to the natural fitness of exogamous clan regulation for a peasant community—though changing economic conditions are steadily eroding its foundations (p. 2).

## Context

- Humphreys was a serving Malayan Civil Service officer stationed at Alor Gajah; the article reflects the contemporary colonial administrative interest in documenting indigenous legal systems for governance and legislative purposes.
- The study draws on direct fieldwork with Ungkai Lisut and consultations with J.E. Nathan (District Officer, Kuala Pilah) and Negri Sembilan chiefs, and builds on earlier publications in the same journal series (*Naning Proverbs*, No. 67; *A Naning Wedding-speech*, No. 72).
