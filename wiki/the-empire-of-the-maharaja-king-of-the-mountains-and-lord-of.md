# The empire of the Maharaja, King of the Mountains and Lord of the Isles

C. O. Blagden's 1920 article reinterprets the political geography of pre-14th-century maritime Southeast Asia by arguing that Palembang was not a single localized kingdom in Southern Sumatra but a maritime empire that controlled the Straits of Malacca through outstations on both sides for roughly six centuries. Drawing primarily on M. G. Coedes' 1918 synthesis of epigraphic and textual evidence, Blagden reconstructs the rise, extent, and collapse of this "Maharaja" polity and its implications for the early colonization of the Malay Peninsula.

## Summary

Blagden's central argument is that the Palembang dynasty, styled "Maharaja" and claiming descent from "the king of the mountains," maintained a network of dependencies spanning both sides of the Straits from at least the 7th century until its fragmentation in the late 13th century. The empire's power rested not on geographic position—Palembang lay off the direct trade route—but on the coercive ability to compel merchant vessels to call at its ports and pay toll, a form of what Blagden terms piracy. The evidence for this imperial reach comes from multiple independent source traditions: the Chinese pilgrim I Tsing's 7th-century accounts, the Sanskrit inscription at Vieng Sa in Lower Siam (775), the Kota Kapur inscription on Bangka, a series of Arab geographers from the 9th century onward, Chola royal inscriptions from the 11th century, and the Chinese commercial treatise of Chao Ju Kua (c. 1225).

The article traces the empire's trajectory from its early annexation of Malayu in Central Sumatra, through its 11th-century conflict with the Chola dynasty of South India—which resulted in the temporary capture of Kedah and other outstations—through its gradual disintegration as dependencies like Kompai broke away, Siamese forces from Sukhodaya absorbed the northern Peninsula stations, and new Islamic polities in Northern Sumatra (Perlak, Pasai, Samudra) carved out independent trading roles. Blagden argues that the loss of these outstations destroyed the empire's toll-collecting monopoly, and that Singapore's rise as an independent entrepôt was a direct consequence of the old Palembang control structure collapsing.

A significant interpretive contribution is Blagden's identification of the dynastic epithet "king of the mountains" (attested in both the Vieng Sa inscription and the Chola records) as the source of the Malay legend of the three princes descending from Mount Siguntang Mahameru in the *Sejarah Melayu*. This links the Palembang royal house directly to the legendary ancestry of the Singapore dynasty and reframes that myth as a dynastic memory rather than a purely national Malay tradition.

### Key Findings

- The Vieng Sa inscription (775 A.D.), set up in what is now Lower Siam, records the erection of Buddhist buildings by order of the King of Srivijaya, demonstrating Palembang's political reach into the northern Peninsula as early as the 8th century (p. 24).
- The Chola grant of 1005 to a Buddhist temple at Negapatam names two Palembang kings, the second styled "king of Kataha and king of Srivishaya," confirming the empire's control of Kedah and its identity as Srivijaya (p. 24).
- Chola inscriptions of c. 1025 record the capture of the king of Kadaram and the seizure of the Nicobars, Lambri, Kadaram, Langkasuka, and Srivishaya itself; a further Chola claim in 1068 records the capture and subsequent restoration of Kedah (pp. 24–25).
- Chao Ju Kua (c. 1225) lists fifteen Palembang dependencies including Pahang, Trengganu, Kelantan, Langkasuka, Palembang, Sunda, Kompai, Lambri, and Ceylon, the last sending yearly tribute; he also notes that Kompai had already broken away and installed its own king (p. 25).
- Krom's 1919 findings (reported in Blagden's postscript) show that Javanese inscriptions dating to 778 A.D. place the "king of the mountains" dynasty ruling Central Java, indicating that the Sumatran house was the more powerful of two rival archipelagic powers until the 13th century (pp. 27–28).
- The Majapahit conquest of Palembang and its dependencies is dated to approximately 1377, marking the final end of the empire (p. 23).

### Conclusion

Blagden's definitive takeaway is that the Palembang "Maharaja" held a string of Straits Settlements for several centuries, making it the dominant political force in the region before the 14th century, and that the early Malay colonization of the Peninsula—beginning most plausibly at Kedah—was a product of this Sumatran imperial presence rather than an independent Malay migration. The article also leaves open the unresolved question of whether Mon-Khmer linguistic traces among the Peninsula's "aborigines" predate the 7th–8th century Sumatran influence or require a separate explanation.

## Context

- The article is a secondary synthesis built almost entirely on Coedes' 1918 paper "Le Royaume de Srivijaya" (*Bulletin de l'École Française d'Extrême-Orient*, XVIII, No. 6), supplemented by Krom's 1919 inaugural address at Leyden; Blagden's own contribution lies in the interpretive framing, the connection to Malay literary tradition, and the postscript on Javanese rivalry.
- The primary epigraphic sources underpinning the argument are the Vieng Sa inscription (published by Finot, 1910), the Kota Kapur inscription (previously published in JSBRAS No. 64), the Chola inscriptions at Negapatam, and the Javanese inscriptions cited by Krom.
