# Emily Innes, 1843–1927

J.M. Gullick's 1982 article in *JMBRAS* 55(2) is a biographical reassessment of Emily Innes (1843–1927), author of *The Chersonese with the Gilding Off* (1885), using the recently discovered private diary of Bloomfield Douglas, Resident of Selangor, alongside publishing records and Colonial Office files to separate the factual content of Innes' account from the distorting effects of her personal grievances.

## Summary

Gullick traces Innes' life from her birth in Kent as the daughter of Canon James Robertson, a leading church historian, through her marriage at nearly 32 to James Innes, a Scottish man of dubious financial reliability. The reconstruction of the Innes family background reveals that James had earlier financial failures in China, evidenced by trust restrictions in his father Cosmo Innes' 1871 will, and that his incompetence at the Sarawak Treasury—where a clerk absconded with government revenue—led to his forced resignation in March 1876. The couple then moved to Selangor, where James served as District Officer at Kuala Langat and later at Durian Sabatang in Perak, before resigning from the Selangor service in 1882.

The central argument concerns the reliability of Innes' published account. Gullick demonstrates that the Douglas diary reveals a far more cordial early relationship than Innes' book suggests: Douglas took Emily shooting, walked with her along the river, and recorded "very pleasant evenings" at Kuala Langat in 1876. The estrangement developed gradually over 1877–1880, driven by Emily's social snobbery toward the Douglas women, disputes over the government launch, and the transfer of James Innes to Durian Sabatang. By the final two years at Kuala Langat, the relationship had deteriorated into what Innes herself titles "Boycotting," during which she received only five European visitors in nearly two years. Gullick argues that this sustained social isolation, combined with physical discomfort, chronic boredom, and the absence of children, progressively distorted Innes' observations in the second half of her book.

The article also examines the aftermath: James Innes' failed attempts to obtain compensation from the Colonial Office, Emily's own intervention at the Office in 1884, and the publication process at Richard Bentley & Son, whose records show that the publisher's legal advisers flagged approximately thirty passages for potential libel, resulting in a considerably modified published text.

### Key Findings

- James Innes was ordered to resign as Sarawak Treasurer on 31 March 1876 after a clerk named Lewis absconded with "a few hundred dollars of Sarawak revenue" and the 1875 revenue fell short of expenditure by approximately 7 per cent (p. 96).
- Cosmo Innes' will of 22 May 1871 placed James Innes' share (approximately £5,000) in trust so that neither income nor capital should be "affectable for his debts or deeds nor subject to the diligence of his creditors"; a codicil of 21 December 1872 removed these restrictions, indicating his insolvency had been resolved (p. 92).
- The Bentley publishing records contain a memorandum of approximately thirty topics flagged for potential libel between 29 May and 4 June 1885, with instructions such as "alter name" against a passage identifying a forger; the book was published on 19 October 1885 at a production cost of about £200, with a print run of 500 bound copies (pp. 110–111).
- Emily Innes' share of the book's profits over the ensuing years was £41-9-0; by 1887 only 30 bound copies remained unsold (p. 111).
- During the "Boycotting" period at Kuala Langat (1880–1882), Emily Innes received only five European visitors in nearly two years (p. 107).
- James Innes' 1884 claim to the Colonial Office for three months' leave pay plus one month's pay per year of service totalled £337-14-0; the Office refused to reopen the matter (p. 109).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that Innes' book is a valuable but uneven source: her descriptive observations of Malay personalities and village life constitute first-rate reporting, but the second half of the volume is significantly distorted by the cumulative effect of social ostracism, physical hardship, and personal resentment. The Douglas diary serves as the key corrective, demonstrating that the antagonism Innes portrays as inherent in the Resident's character was in fact a gradual deterioration from genuine friendship, driven substantially by Innes' own social attitudes and the petty administrative disputes that accumulated over six years in the Malay States.

## Context

- Primary archival collections explored: the Bloomfield Douglas private diary (held by Mr. E. Potter), the Richard Bentley & Son publishing records (microfilmed by Chadwyck-Healey), the John Murray archives (Robertson and Innes correspondence), Colonial Office files at the Public Record Office, and Sarawak records at Kuching.
- Historiographical contribution: the article provides the first systematic cross-verification of Innes' published account against an independent contemporary source, establishing a framework for assessing the reliability of her observations chapter by chapter and correcting the earlier critical consensus (represented by Khoo Kay Kim and Heussler) that her account is uniformly unreliable.
