# The Gesneraceae of the Malay Peninsula

H.N. Ridley published this comprehensive floristic treatment of the Gesneraceae (Cyrtandreae) of the Malay Peninsula in 1905, documenting 121 species across 18 genera and establishing the Peninsula as a major centre of endemism for the family. The work represents a dramatic expansion over the 22 species recorded in the *Flora of British India* (1884) and the 72 listed in Ridley's own 1895 Linnean Society paper, drawing heavily on his own collections from Perak, Pahang, and Selangor, as well as material from Curtis, Scortechini, Kunstler, and Wray (p. 1).

## Summary

Ridley organises the treatment around 18 genera, providing a dichotomous key to the order, generic diagnoses, and species descriptions with locality data. The largest genera are *Didymocarpus* (44 species, 40 endemic), *Didissandra* (13 species, all endemic), *Paraboea* (12 species, all endemic), and *Boea* (9 species, all endemic). He revises Clarke's generic boundaries, in particular separating *Didissandra* from *Didymocarpus* on the basis of stamen number and corolla tube length, and retaining *Paraboea* and *Loxocarpus* as distinct from *Didymocarpus* (pp. 2–3). The section *Speciosæ* of *Didissandra*, comprising 71 species of small shrublets with showy blue, white, or yellow flowers, is identified as a very distinct group apparently confined to the Peninsula, with no representatives even from Sumatra or Borneo (p. 2).

Habitat analysis reveals that the majority of species are hill plants occurring between 1 and 6,000 feet elevation, with limestone rocks proving exceptionally prolific and supporting several entirely lithophytic genera (*Boea*, *Monophyllaea*, *Epithema*, *Phyllobœa*). Lowland species are few and are replaced by a distinct series at higher elevations (p. 7). The Lankawi limestone islands, explored extensively by Curtis, emerge as a particularly rich locality, yielding numerous endemic species across multiple genera (pp. 1, 54, 62, 72, 76–77).

Ridley describes numerous new species throughout the paper, including *Didissandra violacea*, *D. glabrescens*, *D. Wrayi*, *D. hirta*, *D. serratifolia*, *D. atrocyanea*, *D. filicina*, *Didymocarpus sulphurea*, *D. parviflora*, *D. ramosa*, *D. flavescens*, *D. hirta*, *D. viscidula*, *D. albina*, *D. alternans*, *D. glabrata*, *D. ophirensis*, *D. pulchella*, *D. crocea*, *D. rugosa*, *D. hirsuta*, *D. bombycina*, *D. fasciata*, *D. venusta*, *D. densifolia*, *D. serrata*, *D. pumila*, *Chirita sericea*, *C. rupestris*, *C. Glasgowii*, *C. Forbesii*, *Paraboea campanulata*, *P. Scortechinii*, *P. caerulea*, *P. capitata*, *P. polita*, *P. vulpina*, *P. laxa*, *P. Curtisii*, *P. obovata*, *Boea caerulescens*, *B. Havilandi*, *B. parviflora*, *Boeica brachyandra*, *Orchadocarpa lilacina*, *Monophyllaea patens*, *M. glabra*, and *Cyrtandromœa grandis* (pp. 12–92).

### Key Findings

- Of 121 species recorded, 95 (78.5%) are endemic to the Malay Peninsula, with only 7 extending to Burma and Siam, 17 to Sumatra, and 9 to Borneo (p. 7).
- The genus *Didissandra* section *Speciosæ* (71 species worldwide) is entirely Peninsular, with no species found in Sumatra or Borneo, making it the most striking example of regional endemism in the family (p. 2).
- Limestone rocks support an entire suite of specialised genera (*Boea*, *Monophyllaea*, *Epithema*, *Phyllobœa*) that are absent from granite or sandstone habitats, with all 12 *Paraboea* and all 9 *Boea* species in the Peninsula being endemic (pp. 4–5, 7).
- The Lankawi limestone islands, explored by Curtis, yielded a disproportionate number of new endemic species across at least eight genera, confirming Ridley's earlier observation that the district was "very rich" (pp. 1, 54, 62, 72, 76–77).
- *Didymocarpus* species are "as local in their distribution as those of *Didissandra*"; of 44 species, only four have been met with outside the Peninsula, and most endemics are known from single localities of small area (p. 3).
- The epiphytic genus *Aeschynanthus* is the most widely diffused, with 11 species in the Peninsula (only 3 endemic), reflecting its broader Indo-Malayan and island distribution compared to the terrestrial genera (pp. 2, 7–9).

### Conclusion

Ridley's definitive historical takeaway is that the Malay Peninsula constitutes a primary centre of origin and diversification for the Gesneraceae, with an endemism rate of nearly 80% that far exceeds that of neighbouring Sumatra or Borneo. The extreme locality of individual species—many known from a single hill or rock face—means that the true species count is likely to double as further mountain ridges are explored, and that the Peninsula's limestone and granite hill forests harbour a level of cryptic diversity that had been almost entirely unrecognised before the 1890s collecting campaigns (pp. 1, 3, 7).

## Context

- The paper draws on collections loaned by Dr. Prain of the Calcutta Gardens (Scortechini, Kunstler, Wray material from the Perak Hills) and on Curtis's series from the Lankawi islands, illustrating the collaborative infrastructure of late-Victorian botanical collecting in the Straits Settlements and Federated Malay States (p. 1).
- Ridley's 1895 paper in the *Journal of the Linnean Society* (vol. XXXII, p. 497) had listed 72 species; this 1905 work more than doubles that figure, reflecting both his own intensified fieldwork and the processing of previously unexamined herbarium material (p. 1).
