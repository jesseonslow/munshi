# The Malaysia plan and the first Brunei elections, 1962

Mohamad Yusop's 1998 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines how Tunku Abdul Rahman's Malaysia Plan, announced in May 1961, became inextricably entangled with Brunei's first general elections of August 1962, and how the Partai Rakyat Brunei's decisive electoral victory demonstrated popular rejection of the federation, ultimately contributing to Brunei's exclusion from Malaysia in 1963.

## Summary

The article traces the political crisis in Brunei between 1961 and 1962, in which the Sultan's alignment with the Federation of Malaya collided with the PRB's vision of an independent Borneo Federation. Yusop argues that the Tunku's tactless public advocacy of the Malaysia Plan—particularly his dismissal of Brunei as "too small" for independence and his threat to withdraw Malayan officers—backfired spectacularly, galvanising PRB support and alienating the Sultan. The British colonial administration, caught between the Tunku's pressure and the Sultan's ambivalence, attempted to manage the process through the Brunei-Malaysia Commission (January–February 1962) and the Cobbold Commission (February–April 1962), both of which revealed overwhelming local opposition to the federation.

The core argument is that the 1962 elections functioned as a de facto referendum on Malaysia, and that the governments of Brunei, Malaya, and Britain fatally misread the result. Despite the PRB's mandate, it was excluded from all subsequent negotiations on Brunei's terms of association. Yusop contends that this exclusion, combined with the Sultan's own wavering, created the conditions for the December 1962 armed rebellion and, ultimately, Brunei's decision to remain outside the Malaysian Federation. The narrative is driven by British official correspondence (CO 1030, DO 35 series), which reveals the internal deliberations of the Colonial Office, the High Commissioner's assessments, and the Tunku's private communications with the Sultan.

Yusop also highlights the role of the MSCC (Malaysia Solidarity Consultative Committee) in converting Borneo leaders to the Malaysia cause, and the significance of the Sultan's conditional acceptance of the plan in principle on 18 July 1962—a decision that was reversed after the election results and the subsequent rebellion.

### Key Findings

- PRB membership surged from 19,000 to 26,000 within one month of the Tunku's June 1961 "ultimatum" to withdraw Malayan officers from Brunei (p. 9).
- The 30 August 1962 elections comprised 55 District Councillor seats; the PRB won 32 uncontested seats on Nomination Day (21 June 1962) and all but one of the remaining 23 contested seats, with over 90% turnout from an electorate of approximately 6,000 (pp. 17–18).
- The pro-Malaysia candidate Haji Hasbollah received only 25 votes against his PRB opponent's 266, illustrating the depth of anti-Malaysia sentiment (p. 18).
- The Brunei-Malaysia Commission (chaired by Dato Marsal, with Azahari as Vice Chairman) held public hearings from mid-January to 3 February 1962; its findings of "almost unanimous opposition" were suppressed at the direction of Dato Abdul Aziz, the Malayan Attorney-General on secondment (pp. 13–14).
- The Legislative Council passed a resolution endorsing the Sultan's acceptance of the Malaysia Plan in principle by 24 votes to 4, with one abstention, on 18 July 1962 (p. 16).
- Brunei had extended two loans to the Federation of Malaya—$40 million in 1956 and $100 million in 1958—which underpinned the financial dimension of the Brunei–Malaya alignment (p. 52, n. 2).

### Conclusion

Yusop's definitive takeaway is that the failure of the three governments to recognise the electoral mandate of the PRB—by excluding the Party from all negotiations on Brunei's future—was the critical policy error that precipitated the December 1962 rebellion and, by extension, Brunei's permanent exclusion from Malaysia. The episode, he argues, "stifled the constitutional and political development in the Sultanate," leaving Brunei under British protection until full independence in 1984.

## Context

- Primary archival sources include Public Record Office files CO 1030 and DO 35, supplemented by the private papers of Dato' Paduka Haji Marsal bin Maun and contemporary newspapers (*The Borneo Bulletin*, *Straits Times*, *North Borneo and Sabah Times*).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malaysia's formation by centring Brunei's internal political dynamics—rather than the well-trodden Malaya–Singapore axis—and by drawing on oral history interviews (with Dato Marsal and Yassin Affandi) to illuminate the suppression of the Brunei-Malaysia Commission report.
