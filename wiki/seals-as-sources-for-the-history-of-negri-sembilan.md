# Seals as sources for the history of Negri Sembilan

Shuichi Nagata's 2013 note in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* documents the *tageh* sing ritual of the Kensiw (Kensiw) Orang Asli of Kedah, a musical performance tradition distinct from the shamanistic *pano'* ceremonies better known among western Negrito groups. Drawing on fieldwork conducted in the early 1970s near Baling, Kedah, Nagata argues that *tageh* represents a unique genre of Orang Asli religious performance with no parallel in other ethnolinguistic groups, constituting one polar type in the spectrum of ritual singing practices.

## Summary

The article addresses a gap in the ethnographic literature on Orang Asli religious practice. Geoffrey Benjamin's influential framework distinguished between "seasonal-fruit shamanism" in the west and "tiger shamanism" in the east, but Nagata identifies a third category among the Kensiw: a communal singing ritual that does not involve shamanistic possession or trance. The Kensiw themselves draw a sharp distinction between *penlogn pano'* (the shamanistic ceremony conducted in a conical *pano'* structure) and *penlogn tageh* (the communal sing), each with its own distinct songs and protocols.

The *tageh* ritual is performed on the seventh night after a death (*hay tujoh*), in total darkness, as part of a *kenduri* feast that marks the end of the mourning period. A *cao neges* (sing leader) directs the assembled participants in singing, accompanied by a horizontally held bamboo drum (*lebeh* or *gendang*) struck with a woven *palas* palm beater. The ritual's purpose is to invite *cenoy* (sky spirits) to join the singing for the well-being of all present. Smoke from the plant *heli kasay* (identified as *Dianella ensifolia*) is inhaled and blown in four directions to invoke *tageh* from the heavens, a practice Nagata compares to the Temiar use of fragrance in incantation. The author also records brief healing episodes during the ritual, in which the leader inhales a patient's breath and exhales it outward to expel illness.

Nagata situates the *tageh* within the broader comparative literature on Orang Asli music and religion, noting that while healing and dream songs are widely disseminated and subject to cross-group borrowing (as Roseman demonstrated for the Temiar), the *tageh* sing ritual appears to have no equivalent in any other group. He also notes the ritual's decline, partly due to complaints from surrounding Malay peasants about the noise of singing and bamboo drums.

### Key Findings

- The *tageh* ritual is performed on the seventh day after a death (*hay tujoh*), at night and in total darkness, to avoid frightening the *cenoy* sky spirits who join the singing (p. 79).
- The accompanying instrument is a 5-inch-diameter bamboo tube, 5–6 feet long, held horizontally and struck with a sandal-shaped beater of woven *palas* palm; no woman plays it (p. 81).
- The plant used for the ritual embers is identified as *Dianella ensifolia* (Family: Liliaceae), locally called *heli kasay* or *heli lægin* (p. 80).
- The *cao neges* is described as *sedikit hala'* ("a little bit shaman") because he can play the drum and sing, but he is not a full shaman (p. 80).
- Additional *kenduri* feasts are reported at 16, 20, 40, and 50 days after death, but the seventh-day feast with *tageh* is mandatory (p. 79, n. 25).
- The ritual's decline is attributed in part to complaints from Malay peasants about the noise of singing and bamboo drums (p. 79, n. 29).

### Conclusion

Nagata's definitive takeaway is that the *tageh* sing ritual is unique to the Kensiw and has no parallel in any other Orang Asli group, making it a potential polar type in the genre of ritual performance. While healing songs and dream songs circulate and are adapted across groups, the *tageh* as a structured communal singing ceremony with its specific instrument, smoke invocation, and nocturnal protocol appears to be a singular cultural achievement of the Kensiw.

## Context

- Primary source base: Nagata's own field notes from 1971–2001 (University of Toronto), supplemented by earlier ethnographic accounts from Schebesta (1954, 1957), Evans (1929, 1937), van der Sluys (1992), and Roseman (1991, 1995).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Orang Asli religious practice by challenging the binary east/west shamanism framework established by Benjamin and by documenting a performance genre previously unrecorded in the comparative literature.
