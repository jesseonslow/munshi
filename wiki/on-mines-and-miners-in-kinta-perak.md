# On mines and miners in Kinta, Perak

A. Hale, Inspector of Mines for Kinta, published this ethnographic and technical survey of Malay tin-mining practices in the Kinta Valley, Perak, in 1885. The article argues that the Kinta district constitutes a uniquely continuous mining landscape in which pre-Muhammadan traditions, Malay customary law, and indigenous metallurgical knowledge persist alongside and in tension with Chinese commercial mining. Its central contribution is a systematic record of the *pawang*'s regulatory role on Malay mines and a comprehensive glossary of technical Malay mining terminology that Hale judged at risk of being lost.

## Summary

Hale opens by establishing the Kinta Valley as a mining country of long antiquity, noting that local tradition attributes the earliest extensive workings to Siamese miners and that physical evidence—deep well-like pits on Lahat Hill averaging eight feet in diameter and twenty feet deep, a large pit of fifty feet diameter in virgin forest, and old tin slabs of unfamiliar shape—corroborates centuries of continuous extraction. He situates the Malay *kelian* (private mine) within a broader landscape of nearly five hundred registered mines, of which only three were worked by European companies, the remainder being either inherited Malay claims or new concessions granted to Malays and Chinese alike.

The substantive core of the article is devoted to the *pawang*, the medicine-man who functions as both spiritual intermediary and quasi-judicial authority on Malay mines. Hale documents the *bahasa pantang*—a taboo vocabulary in which ordinary words for animals, objects, and even tin itself are replaced by euphemisms to avoid offending the *hantu* (spirits) believed to inhabit the mine. He records seventeen specific rules enforced by the *pawang*, each carrying a fine (most commonly $12.50, which Hale notes equals half the maximum fine a Malay chief could impose for minor offences under customary law). The *pawang*'s professional duties include erecting the *genggulang* altar at a mine's opening, invoking tutelary spirits, and adjudicating disputes among miners.

The article's final and most extensive section is a technical vocabulary of Malay mining terminology, covering everything from the stratigraphy of tin-bearing drift (*kulit akar*, *tanah padi*, *tangloh*) to the specific implements and processes of hill mining (*leris*), ground sluicing (*lampan*), excavation (*tebok*, *ludang*), ore cleaning (*memalong*, *memandei*), and smelting (*mengumbus* in the Malay fashion versus *memuput* in the Chinese fashion). Hale explicitly states his purpose in compiling this glossary: the language is "so susceptible of change" that without a record the terms "may, perhaps as the method of working alter, be entirely lost."

### Key Findings

- The Kinta district contained nearly 500 registered mines in 1885, of which approximately 350 were private Malay *kelian*; only three were operated by European companies (p. 303).
- The *pawang*'s standard fine for most offences was $12.50, which Hale identifies as exactly half the maximum fine a Malay chief could impose on a *ra'iyat* for minor offences under customary law, and also the customary dowry for marriage to a slave or to the widow/divorced wife of a *ra'iyat* (p. 309).
- The customary furnace rent (*chukei senduk*) was two *kati* of tin per *karong* of tin sand smelted when the furnace owner was also the mine owner, and one *kati* per *karong* when they were different persons (p. 313).
- The *hasil kelian* (mine-owner's duty) was one-sixth of output for excavations (*tebok* and *ludang*) but one-third for hill mining (*leris*), reflecting the greater capital and risk of hill work (p. 314).
- Physical evidence of pre-Muhammadan mining includes at least fifty deep pits on Lahat Hill (avg. 8 ft diameter, ~20 ft deep) and a single large pit of 50 ft diameter and over 20 ft depth in virgin forest, identified by natives as a Siamese mine (p. 304).
- The *pawang*'s fee for erecting a *genggulang* at the opening of a mine was one *karong* of tin sand; for the cheaper *kapala nasi* alternative, one *gantang* of tin sand (p. 306).

### Conclusion

Hale's definitive takeaway is that Malay mining in Kinta represents a coherent, self-regulating system in which spiritual belief, customary law, and practical metallurgy are inseparable. The *pawang* is not merely a religious figure but the institutional mechanism through which the community enforces behavioural norms, adjudicates disputes, and mediates between human labour and the perceived agency of the tin ore itself. By recording the *bahasa pantang*, the fine schedule, and the technical vocabulary, Hale presents this system as a vanishing corpus of indigenous knowledge whose loss would be irreversible once Chinese commercial methods displaced Malay practice.

## Context

- The author was the sitting Inspector of Mines for Kinta at the time of writing, giving him direct administrative access to mine registers, concession records, and the miners themselves; the article reads as an official's ethnographic survey rather than an independent scholarly study.
- The article draws on no named archival collection; its evidential basis is Hale's own field observation and direct interview with Malay miners and *pawang* in the Kinta district during the early 1880s.
