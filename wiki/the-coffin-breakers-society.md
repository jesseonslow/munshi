# The Coffin Breakers Society

W.G. Stirling's 1926 article reconstructs the history of the Shiu Lok Peng On Society, a criminal organization that operated across the Straits Settlements and Hong Kong from 1885 to approximately 1892, specializing in the robbery of Chinese passengers aboard steamers. Writing from what appears to be direct administrative knowledge, Stirling documents the Society's structure, methods, and eventual suppression by the Chinese Protectorate.

## Summary

The Shiu Lok Peng On Society exploited the gap between its innocuous-sounding name—literally "Peace on Land and Water," a phrase familiar to Chinese readers from decorative red paper pasted over doorways—and its actual purpose: organized robbery, blackmail, and fraud targeting Chinese travellers on steamers between the Straits Settlements and Hong Kong. Founded by a Cantonese named Kwan Fan in 1885, the Society established a network of Headmen across Singapore, Penang, and Hong Kong, maintaining a register of members, a common fund, and a system of internal discipline. Its operations expanded from initial steamer thieving to include extortion of brothel keepers, confidence tricks, and rigged gambling, amassing considerable wealth in its early years.

Stirling devotes considerable attention to the Society's methods of steamer robbery, known in its internal slang as "breaking open coffins" (a reference to the coffin-shaped pillow boxes carried by Chinese passengers). The elaborate precautions taken to avoid detection—strict rules against taking incriminating clothing, the requirement to dispose of stolen goods in a different port from where the robbery occurred, the complicity of ship's cooks and hands, and the timing of robberies during monsoon seasickness—made prosecution virtually impossible. Victims, fearing retaliation, rarely came forward, and the crimes committed on the High Seas fell outside the jurisdiction of colonial police. The Society's power rested not on ritual or paraphernalia but on organizational discipline, mutual protection, and the economic leverage it held over the very communities it preyed upon.

The Society's decline began with the arrest of its Penang Headmen by the Chinese Protectorate in 1890 and the flight of its founder Kwan Fan to French Indo-China. The remaining members consolidated in Singapore, operating with greater secrecy, but the patient accumulation of evidence by G.T. Hare and the eventual issuance of a government proclamation naming wanted members brought the organization to its end. Stirling notes that the Society remained well remembered in Singapore at the time of writing, a testament to the terror it had inspired.

### Key Findings

- The Society was founded in 1885 (10th moon, 11th year of Kwong Su) by a Cantonese named Kwan Fan, who served as its "master brain" until his flight to French Indo-China in 1890 (p. 129).
- Total membership was estimated at roughly 400 people, including resident members and those who worked the steamers; entrance fees were $5 in Singapore and $6.20 in Penang, of which $2 was remitted to Hong Kong as commission (pp. 130–131).
- Members were required to pay 10% of their takings to the Society's common fund, which was used to engage lawyers, fund illegal gaming, and buy evidence; Headmen received a salary of $10 per month (p. 130).
- A silver coin was cast in Hong Kong bearing the characters "Peng On" and the member's registration number, serving as a membership token; a seal with the characters "Wa Ki" was used on correspondence (pp. 129–130).
- The Society observed two feast days (19th day of the 1st and 5th moons) and paid $10 as a gift on the marriage or funeral of any member (p. 131).
- Stirling appends a glossary of the Society's internal slang, including terms such as *lau pin* (to go on board a steamer), *loh fah* (to steal money), *si lok tai thai* (to throw overboard), and *a po a seng* (snatching ear-rings from a woman) (p. 132).

### Conclusion

Stirling's definitive takeaway is that the Shiu Lok Peng On Society represented a uniquely dangerous form of organized crime in the Straits Settlements precisely because it operated at the intersection of maritime jurisdiction, communal fear, and economic coercion. Its suppression required not a single dramatic intervention but the sustained, patient work of the Chinese Protectorate—exemplified by G.T. Hare's methodical collection of evidence—combined with the political will to issue a public proclamation naming wanted members, thereby breaking the culture of silence that had protected the Society for six years.

## Context

- Stirling was almost certainly a colonial official with direct involvement in or close observation of the Society's suppression, likely connected to the Chinese Protectorate or police in Singapore. The article reads as an insider's administrative account rather than a detached scholarly study.
- The piece draws on police records, Chinese Protectorate files, and personal knowledge of the Cantonese community in Singapore; its historiographical value lies in preserving operational details (slang, fee structures, internal rules) that would otherwise have been lost.
