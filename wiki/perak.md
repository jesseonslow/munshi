# Perak

Perak is a state on the north-western coast of the Malay Peninsula whose interior, mountain ranges, and river systems attracted sustained European scientific and administrative attention from the 1870s onward. Its tin-bearing alluvial deposits, extensive limestone formations, and navigable river network made it a focal point of colonial economic development, while its unexplored highlands and frontier territories provided a field for geographical survey, botanical collection, and ethnographical observation. The state's political history—marked by the Larut wars, the Pangkor Treaty of 1874, and the Anglo-Siamese boundary settlements of 1909—shaped the conditions under which much of this documentation was produced, and the Society's literature reflects the transition from a fragmented, semi-autonomous polity to a consolidated colonial administration.

### Physical Geography and Mineral Wealth

The physical geography of Perak was first systematically described by J. E. Tenison-Woods, a British geologist and colonial official, in a letter to *Nature* published in December 1884 and summarising ten months of fieldwork in the state. Tenison-Woods identified three principal geological formations: an immense granite mass rising into sharp, nearly meridional ridges; a Palaeozoic formation of slates, mottled sandstones, and clays found most abundantly at the foot of the ranges; and limestone in detached outliers of precipitous character, probably also of Palaeozoic age [[1, pp. 137]](#ref-1). The mountain system of the western peninsula, he observed, consists of detached groups of parallel ridges, each roughly twenty-five miles long and fourteen to sixteen miles broad, separated by narrow passes such as Gapis Pass at approximately 400 feet elevation [[1, pp. 139–140]](#ref-1). He catalogued these groups from south to north: the Dindings Islands, Gunong Bubu (5,600 feet), Mount Ijau (4,400 feet), the Kurau Group, Mount Inas (over 4,000 feet), Kedah Peak (over 4,000 feet), and the Kinta Ranges (3,750 feet), before turning to the Main Range, where he identified a peak over 8,000 feet and Gunong Robinson at over 7,000 feet, and noted unconfirmed reports of a summit exceeding 12,000 feet [[1, pp. 141–142]](#ref-1).

The tin deposits, which constituted the economic foundation of the state, were described by Tenison-Woods as exclusively stream tin in the form of small abraded cassiterite crystals of a distinctive blackish-gray or brown aspect, found in one or two strata from one to five feet thick in the lowest levels of the drift, generally covered by ten to thirty feet of tin-free material [[1, p. 138]](#ref-1). The matrix lay in the upper part of the granite at its junction with the Palaeozoic clays, and the workings were concentrated on the western slopes where denudation and wash had been greater due to prevailing rainfall patterns [[1, pp. 138]](#ref-1). H. W. C. Leech, writing in 1879, provided complementary detail on the Kinta district, noting that tin ore was found at depths varying from one to eight or nine fathoms below the surface soil, and that the limestone cliffs between the Kinta and Sungei Raya rose vertically 500 to 2,000 feet, were highly crystalline, contained no fossils or stratification marks, and were associated with some of the richest tin deposits in the district [[2, pp. 23–25]](#ref-2). Leech also recorded that Gopeng, the most important mining centre on the Kampar side, employed 700 to 800 Chinese miners [[2, p. 28]](#ref-2).

The Perak River itself flows southward for over 180 miles, collecting the Plus and Kinta from the east, and the entire system prevents any drainage from the central range reaching the west coast directly [[1, p. 141]](#ref-1). H. S. Deane's survey reports of 1879 documented the navigability of the Plus, noting that approximately 50 miles upriver the river remained broad and deep [[3, p. 137]](#ref-3), while Leech recorded that the coasting steamer *Pyah Pekhet* called weekly at Durian Sebatang, 40 miles up the Perak River, from where Kota Bahru was reachable in two to three days by native boat, and the Kinta was navigable a further two days to Musjid Lama [[2, p. 25]](#ref-2).

### Economic Life and Colonial Administration

The economic history of Perak in the mid-1870s has been reconstructed by John Gullick, who argued that this period represents the final phase of an autonomous, decentralised economy before British administrative control fundamentally restructured the state [[4, p. 27]](#ref-4). Gullick's central finding is that Perak's economy was fundamentally decentralised: an aggregate of local economies linked by the Perak River and coastal trade routes to Penang, rather than an integrated system. Tin mining, increasingly dominated by Penang Chinese financiers who imported their own labour, was the engine driving the economy, but the value of exports was dispersed through a complex web of royalties, taxes, and local trade [[4, p. 33]](#ref-4). The political fragmentation—rival Sultans, semi-independent chiefs, and the absence of effective central authority—meant that economic activity was shaped as much by local power dynamics as by market forces [[4, p. 33]](#ref-4).

The scale of the tin industry is illustrated by the figures Gullick provides: in 1876, Larut produced 30,576 piculs of tin out of a state total of 37,924, representing 80 per cent of Perak's output [5, p. 27, n. 1]. Rice imports in 1876 were valued at $180,621, more than a fifth of total Perak imports, indicating the state's net food deficit [[4, p. 34]](#ref-4). By 1879, annual "political allowances" to the Perak aristocracy totalled $54,456, estimated to equal the traditional revenues of the entire Perak River valley in pre-colonial times [[4, p. 33]](#ref-4). The tax rate on tin at Larut in 1874 was 27 per cent, compared with only 7 per cent in Lower Perak, reflecting the different administrative regimes in operation [[4, p. 32]](#ref-4).

The consolidation of British control following the Pangkor Treaty of January 1874 is documented in Gullick's 1953 monograph on Captain Speedy of Larut, which reconstructs the career of Tristram Charles Sawyer Speedy, the first European to administer a Chinese mining district in Malaya [[5, pp. 1–11]](#ref-5). Under Speedy's administration, Larut's Chinese mining population rose from 4,000 in January 1874 to 27,000 in December, and tin production reached approximately 3,000 piculs per month by 1876, making Larut the mainstay of Perak's revenues under the new Residential system [[5, pp. 36]](#ref-5). Total Larut government revenue in 1874 was $101,554, rising to $226,233 in 1875, with tin export duty constituting the largest single source at $70,227 in 1874 [[5, p. 48]](#ref-5). Speedy also established a police force, suppressed attempts to form new secret society lodges, laid out the new towns of Taiping and Kamunting, and constructed roads and government buildings [[5, pp. 36–52]](#ref-5). The opium farm controversy of April 1875, in which Resident Birch imposed a Singapore-based tax farmer on Larut, caused an estimated 3,000 to 5,000 Chinese miners to leave the district, a loss that was irreparable [[5, p. 45]](#ref-5). Taiping's population fell from 26,000 in 1874 to 9,000 in 1877 following the tin price slump [[4, p. 43]](#ref-4).

The territorial expansion of Perak to the north is documented in E. W. Birch's 1910 account of the physical handover of the Reman (Rahman) district from Siamese suzerainty to British protection in July 1909, conducted under the Anglo-Siamese Treaty of that year [[6, pp. 147–155]](#ref-6). Birch narrated his ten-day elephant-borne journey from Grit to Klian Intan, the formal ceremony at which the Ampur of Betong surrendered the district to British authority, and the subsequent demarcation of the boundary ridge at Betong [[6, pp. 147–155]](#ref-6). The Siamese census at the end of the Siamese year recorded a total population of 2,624: 1,295 Malays, 975 Chinese, 346 Siamese, and 8 Europeans, along with 423 buffaloes, 441 cattle, 5 elephants, and 148 guns [[6, pp. 150–151]](#ref-6). Birch, drawing on the check-rolls of the Rahman Tin Company and the Rahman Hydraulic Tin Mining Company, estimated the actual Chinese population at approximately 1,500 and the total district population at no fewer than 3,300 [[6, p. 152]](#ref-6).

### Exploration, Botany, and Ethnography

The unexplored interior of Perak provided a rich field for scientific expedition. J. E. Tenison Woods published in 1884 the first documented ascent of Gunong Bubu, the highest peak of Perak's coast range, undertaken at the request of Sir Hugh Low, the British Resident of Perak [[7, pp. 275–285]](#ref-7). The expedition, conducted in May 1884 with the botanist Revd. B. Scortechini and Mr. C. F. Bozzolo managing a Malay porter party, combined mountaineering with botanical and geographical survey in a landscape previously unexplored by Europeans [[7, pp. 275–276]](#ref-7). The ascent was rendered extraordinarily difficult by precipices of bare granite, close jungle that obscured all visibility, and persistent heavy rain [[7, pp. 278–279]](#ref-7). The lowest temperature recorded on the summit during the night of 27–28 May was 58°F, accompanied by heavy rain and strong easterly wind from 4 A.M. [[7, p. 283]](#ref-7). Signal fires lit at both ends of the crest were answered by fires from Sengang, Kwala Kangsa, Kota, and Matang, confirming visibility across the entire Perak valley for a distance of fifty to sixty miles [[7, p. 282]](#ref-7).

The botanical observations from this and other expeditions yielded significant taxonomic results. Tenison Woods and Scortechini recorded *Matonia pectinata* as new to Perak, previously known only from Java and Mount Ophir near Malacca, along with *Fagraea auriculata*, a second proteaceous species for the state, and summit vegetation including *Leptospermum flavescens* and *Leucopogon*—both of Australian affinity—alongside *Nepenthes*, *Rhododendron*, and a coniferous tree tentatively identified as *Dacrydium elatum* [[7, pp. 276]](#ref-7). Tenison-Woods, in his 1884 letter, noted the presence of Australian genera (*Melaleuca*, *Leptospermum*, *Podocarpus*, *Leucopogon*) on the highest granite ridges above 5,000 feet, a phenomenon he had not previously seen recorded in the Malay peninsula and which he speculated may represent relics of an ancient flora once extending across the Eastern Archipelago [[1, p. 138]](#ref-1).

The most comprehensive single-district flora was produced by H. N. Ridley, Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, who co-authored with H. C. Robinson an extensive expedition report on the Temengoh district in Upper Perak, published in 1911 [[8, pp. 5–122]](#ref-8). The expedition, conducted in July 1909, yielded over 40 new species formally described, including *Stichoneuron caudatum* (Roxburghiaceae), *Columbia integrifolia* (Tiliaceae), *Stephanotis parviflora* (Asclepiadaceae), *Hoya perakensis*, *Quercus minor* (Juglandaceae), *Ficus cordata*, *Costus velutinus*, *Saccolabium Hobsonii*, and *Cephalostachyum malayense* [[8, pp. 25–118]](#ref-8). Two new genera, *Campimia* and *Phaulanthus*, were erected within the Melastomaceae to resolve longstanding confusion in the section *Oxysporeae* [[8, pp. 40–41]](#ref-8). A notable zoological finding was that several bird species normally restricted to altitudes above 3,000 feet in Batang Padang and Selangor occurred below 1,000 feet at Temengoh, possibly due to the proximity of the main range [[8, p. 15]](#ref-8).

Ethnographical documentation of the aboriginal hill peoples of Perak appears in Leech's 1879 account of the Kinta interior, where he provided a detailed sketch of the Sakei covering their settlement patterns, subsistence strategies, weaponry (notably the blow-pipe and spring traps), marriage customs, burial practices, and physical characteristics [[2, pp. 28–30]](#ref-2). Leech recorded that the Sakei were confined to an elevation zone of 500 to 1,500 (possibly 2,000) feet, lived in groups of 8 to 30 individuals, and that approximately 90 per cent of those observed were suffering from *Kurap*, a skin disease [[2, pp. 28]](#ref-2). His treatment, while characteristically colonial in its framing, explicitly argued against the assumption that the Sakei were "incapable of improvement," pointing to groups near the Bidor who had already adopted Malay customs as evidence of their assimilability [[2, pp. 28–30]](#ref-2). Ridley's Temengoh expedition also recorded encounters with Semang (Negrito) communities, noting their apparent absence of deity concepts, their monogamous social structure, and their use of a travelling ration of pounded tapioca in bamboo cylinders [[8, pp. 8–9]](#ref-8).

The frontier regions of northern Perak were the subject of military and administrative expeditions that doubled as geographical surveys. W. E. Maxwell's 1882 journal of his March–April 1876 overland expedition through the uncharted interior of Ulu Perak and the Patani frontier, undertaken to capture Datoh Maharaja Lela, the chief suspect in the murder of British Resident J. W. Birch, produced the first European account of the route from the Krian river to the Patani border and contributed directly to the eventual identification, trial, and execution of the Birch murderers [[9, pp. 1–67]](#ref-9). The expedition covered approximately 200 miles of previously unexplored terrain and provided the first European geographical account of the route that later informed a 1877 government survey and Stanford's map of the Peninsula [[9, pp. 66–67]](#ref-9). Maxwell recorded in detail the primitive tin-smelting technology at Salama and Intan, the truck-system economics binding Chinese miners to Malay landlords, and the complex boundary history between Perak and Patani centred on the Intan and Endah mines [[9, pp. 8–11]](#ref-9). Arthur T. Dew, then Magistrate and Collector of Selama, published in 1887 an official journal of a mountain exploring expedition conducted in November 1883 under the direction of Resident Sir Hugh Low, with the purpose of determining whether a practicable route existed from Selama to Pong in Patani, with a view to constructing a road through the disputed territory should Anglo-Siamese negotiations restore it to Perak [[10, pp. 105–120]](#ref-10).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Perak spans nearly four decades of publication, from the earliest geographical surveys of the 1870s to the comprehensive botanical and administrative accounts of the early twentieth century, and reflects the shifting priorities of colonial science and governance. The earliest contributions—Leech's surveys of the Kinta, Slim, and Bernam valleys (1879), Deane's trigonometrical reports on Ulu Perak (1879), and Maxwell's frontier journal (1882)—were produced in the immediate aftermath of the Pangkor Treaty and the Larut wars, when the colonial administration required rapid geographical and economic intelligence to consolidate its hold over a newly subordinated state [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3), [[9]](#ref-9). These accounts are characterised by their dual function as both scientific report and commercial prospectus: Leech repeatedly signals openings for European enterprise in limestone, coffee cultivation, and tin mining, while Maxwell's journal serves simultaneously as a military dispatch and a geographical survey [[2, pp. 22–26]](#ref-2), [[9, pp. 66–67]](#ref-9).

The 1880s saw a shift toward more systematic scientific documentation. Tenison Woods's ascent of Gunong Bubu (1884) and Tenison-Woods's letter to *Nature* (1884) represent the integration of mountaineering, botany, and geology into a coherent physical geography of the state [[7]](#ref-7), [[1]](#ref-1). Dew's expedition journal (1887) and the supplementary 1874 account by W. E. M. extend this work to the Patani frontier, providing a comparative baseline showing how little the routes had changed in the intervening nine years [[10, pp. 120–123]](#ref-10). The 1910s brought two landmark contributions: Birch's official account of the Reman handover (1910), which preserves the boundary protocol in bilingual form and the population inventory of a newly acquired territory [[6]](#ref-6), and Ridley and Robinson's Temengoh expedition report (1911), which represents one of the most comprehensive single-district floras published for the Malay Peninsula in the early twentieth century [[8]](#ref-8).

The mid-twentieth century saw a turn toward historiographical reconstruction. Gullick's 1953 monograph on Captain Speedy of Larut drew heavily on Colonial Office records at the Public Records Office in London, the files of the *London Illustrated News*, and Sir Hugh Low's unpublished Perak Journal, to reconstruct the pioneer difficulties of governing a turbulent Chinese tin-mining community and to subject the character of Speedy to scrutiny in light of the bitter accusations levelled against him by Governor Sir William Jervois [10, p. i]. Gullick's 2010 article on the economy of Perak in the mid-1870s addressed a historiographical gap, arguing that this period had been overshadowed by the dramatic political events of the Larut wars and the "Perak War" of 1875–6, yet represented a phase when ordinary economic activity continued across the state [[4, p. 27]](#ref-4). He was candid that these were "colonial records history" but insisted they provided factual information about the local economy that is otherwise unavailable [[4, p. 27]](#ref-4). The reprint of Tenison-Woods's 1884 letter in *JMBRAS* Vol. 93 (2020) situates this text within the Society's ongoing project to recover and contextualise colonial-era scientific contributions to the natural history of the region [[1]](#ref-1).
