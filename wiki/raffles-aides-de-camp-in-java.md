# Raffles’ aides-de-camp in Java

John Bastin's 1992 article traces the careers of the five young East India Company officers who served as aides-de-camp to Sir Stamford Raffles during the British occupation of Java (1811–1816), arguing that this intimate "family" at Government House, Buitenzorg, was the crucible in which both Raffles' greatest loyalties and his most bitter betrayals were forged. Drawing heavily on Travers' manuscript Journal and British Library India Office records, Bastin reconstructs how three of the five men remained faithful to Raffles to the end while the other two — Watson and Methven — became his most active enemies in his final years.

## Summary

The article opens with Lady Raffles' 1830 tribute to Raffles' "little band of friends" in the Introduction to her *Memoir*, a passage Bastin notes is "strictly accurate with respect to Java" but deliberately omits the later enmity of Watson and Methven. The piece then proceeds as a series of biographical sketches, each anchored to the Java period but extending well beyond it to show how the relationships formed in Buitenzorg and Batavia shaped the men's subsequent careers and their ultimate relationship with Raffles. Travers, the oldest and most intimate of the group, is treated at greatest length; his Journal (seven surviving volumes, 1813–1843) provides the backbone of the narrative, and Bastin uses it to correct a long-standing misidentification of a portrait — the pastel drawing reproduced in Wurtzburg's *Raffles of the Eastern Isles* is almost certainly of Lieutenant Thomas Clode, who died in 1812, rather than Travers himself.

The remaining four officers receive proportionately shorter treatments, but each illustrates a distinct trajectory. Dalgairns remained loyal throughout, keeping Raffles informed of Watson and Methven's machinations in Calcutta during 1822–23. Garnham, the most professionally accomplished of the group, held successive residencies at Semarang, Besuki, and Yogyakarta before accompanying Raffles to England in 1816; he died at sea in 1827. Methven and Watson, by contrast, both fell spectacularly from grace. Methven's "abominable love of Money" (Raffles' own words) led to his expulsion from Singapore and his death in 1823, which Raffles dryly observed "has probably saved him from a shameful exposure & public disgrace." Watson's quarrel with Raffles at Fort Marlborough culminated in his being sent to Calcutta under arrest in 1822, and he died of cholera at Dhaka in 1834.

Bastin's method is essentially prosopographical: he assembles scattered references from the *Java Government Gazette*, the *Java Annual Directory*, Hodson's *List of the Officers of the Bengal Army*, De Haan's *Personalia*, and the Travers Journal to build coherent life narratives. The article also serves a minor art-historical function, reproducing three portraits (a Chinnery miniature of Watson, a Lane oil of Garnham, and a miniature of Travers dated to his wedding day in October 1817) and using them to challenge earlier identifications.

### Key Findings

- The five aides-de-camp were all in their twenties during the Java period: Travers (b. 1785), Garnham (b. 1782), Dalgairns (b. 1787), Watson (b. 1787), and Methven (b. 1787); Travers first met Raffles in Penang in 1806, establishing a twenty-year friendship (pp. 1, 3).
- Travers' manuscript Journal survives in seven volumes (1813–14, 1815–16, 1817–21, 1821–28, 1829–31, 1832–35, 1836–43); the first volume, begun in 1805 and covering his Penang years, was lent to a member of the Flint family in January 1891 and never confirmed returned (p. 12, n. 31).
- The pastel portrait long identified as Travers in Wurtzburg's *Raffles of the Eastern Isles* (1954) is almost certainly of Lieutenant Thomas Clode (b. 1786), another aide-de-camp who died of fever on P. Bangka on 28 June 1812 during the Palembang expedition (p. 1, n. 5).
- Garnham held the residencies of Semarang (appointed 10 October 1812), Besuki and Prabalingga (early 1814), and Yogyakarta (20 September 1814 – 31 August 1815); Sultan Hamengkubuwana IV presented him with a jewel-encrusted *keris* on his departure, depicted in his portrait (pp. 7–8).
- Methven died on 27 November 1823 after being thrown from his horse on parade in Calcutta, just as a commission of enquiry into his conduct was about to sit; Raffles wrote that his "violent death has probably saved him from a shameful exposure & public disgrace" (pp. 9–10).
- Watson was sent to Calcutta under arrest in June 1822 after a series of acts of disobedience at Fort Marlborough, including a campaign of persecution against Raffles' brother-in-law, Lieutenant Lawrence Nilson Hull (pp. 11–12).

### Conclusion

Bastin's definitive takeaway is that the Java period created a bond of extraordinary intimacy among Raffles and his young staff — a bond that made the subsequent betrayals by Watson and Methven all the more painful and that explains why Lady Raffles' 1830 tribute, while accurate for Java, could not honestly extend to the last years of Raffles' life. The article implicitly argues that understanding Raffles' character and his historical reputation requires attention to this personal network, not merely to his administrative or intellectual achievements.

## Context

- Primary archival sources: Travers' manuscript Journal (Wurtzburg Collection, Cambridge University Library); British Library, Oriental and India Office Collections (MSS. Eur. D.742/15, D.742/17, B.322); *Java Government Gazette* (1812–16); *Java Annual Directory and Almanac* (1814, 1815).
- Historiographical contribution: Corrects the misidentification of Travers' portrait in Wurtzburg (1954) and Bastin's own 1960 edition of the Journal; provides the most comprehensive prosopographical treatment of Raffles' Java staff to date, drawing on De Haan's *Personalia* (1935) and Hodson's officer lists.
