# Raffles’ aides-de-camp in Java

J.C. Moulton, Curator of the Sarawak Museum, published this account in 1913 of a brief joint collecting expedition to Mt. Poi (Gunong Poe), the highest and only granite mountain in Sarawak Proper, undertaken in April 1913 with Dr. Hanitsch, Director of the Raffles Museum, Singapore. The article documents the mountain's previously unexamined fauna—particularly its birds and insects—and offers ethnological observations on the Slakau Dayaks who inhabit its slopes, arguing that the granitic peak's biota shares more affinities with the distant Kina Balu than with intervening mountains of younger geological formation.

## Summary

Moulton frames the expedition as a response to a specific gap in natural history: since Beccari's 1866 ascent (itself undertaken to prospect for a coffee plantation), no European had attempted a serious zoological survey of Mt. Poi, leaving it a *terra incognita* for fauna. The practical constraints of the trip—only six days on the mountain, adverse weather with persistent cloud cover, and the inherent difficulty of the granitic terrain—meant the collections were necessarily partial. Moulton is candid about these limitations, noting that the fauna "had the best of us" and that 19 traps set by Dayak assistants yielded not a single capture.

The core argument of the article is biogeographical. Moulton identifies multiple species on Mt. Poi that were previously known only from Kina Balu (in North Borneo) or from the Penrissen-Dilit range, and which are absent from the younger volcanic mountains lying between the two granitic peaks. He concludes that the fauna of Kina Balu has more in common with that of Poi than with any of the intervening mountains, suggesting a deep historical continuity between these two ancient granitic formations. This is a modest but significant contribution to the biogeography of Borneo's mountain faunas at a time when such questions were being actively debated.

The ethnological section, while secondary to the natural history, is notable for its attention to the Slakau Dayaks, a group whose classification was uncertain even in 1913. Moulton records their own testimony that they are not related to the Land-Dayaks of Sarawak, that they migrated recently from the Sambas district of Dutch Borneo, and that their nearest relations are the Lara Dayaks. He observes cultural distinctions (absence of a head-house, prohibition on eating crocodile, different house architecture) and suggests they may represent an intermediate race linking the two major Dayak groupings—a hypothesis he commends to Dutch ethnographers for further investigation.

### Key Findings

- **Altitude and climate**: Moulton's aneroid recorded the camp at 3,300 ft and the summit (Gunong Rumput) at 4,350 ft, both approximately 1,000 ft lower than the figures recorded by Beccari (1866) and Foxworthy (1908); shade temperature varied only between 60° and 70° Fahrenheit throughout the six-day stay (pp. 3, 9).
- **New species described**: Two earthworms (*Pheretima poiana* and *Pheretima moultoni*), one beetle (*Anomala morio*, a Rutelid), and one Hispid beetle (*Botryonopa moultoni*) were all new to science as a result of this trip (pp. 8–9, 12).
- **Western range extensions**: *Funambulus everetti* (a tree shrew), *Pitta arcuata* (a ground-thrush), *Odontolabis waterstradti* (a Lucanid beetle), and *Chrysochroa lacordairei* (a Buprestid) were all previously known only from Kina Balu or the Penrissen-Dilit range; Mt. Poi represents their westernmost recorded occurrence (pp. 4, 6, 8).
- **Collection totals**: The joint expedition yielded approximately 8 mammal species, 40 bird species, 3 snakes, 1 lizard, a few frogs, Myriapoda, land Mollusca, and a small number of insects across various orders; 19 traps set by Dayak assistants produced zero captures (p. 4).
- **Slakau Dayak ethnography**: The Slakau are recorded as having migrated from the Sambas district of Dutch Borneo; they possess no head-house (*pangga*), prohibit eating crocodile, burn the dead only rarely, and use Malay as a lingua franca when communicating with Land-Dayaks, whom they cannot understand directly (pp. 10–11).
- **Historical continuity of the site**: The Court Writer Junan at Simatan, a Sambas Chinese who arrived in Kuching as a boy of 8 or 9 after the 1857 Chinese rebellion and had served at Simatan for 33 years, provided local knowledge and represented a living link to the earliest colonial period (p. 2).

### Conclusion

Moulton's definitive takeaway is that Mt. Poi's granitic geology has preserved a fauna of considerable antiquity, one that aligns more closely with the ancient Kina Balu massif than with the younger volcanic peaks separating them. The article thus contributes a single but well-observed data point to the emerging understanding of Borneo's biogeographical history, while its ethnological observations on the Slakau Dayaks flag a classificatory problem that the author explicitly hands to Dutch colleagues for resolution.

## Context

- Moulton wrote as Curator of the Sarawak Museum, a colonial scientific institution under the White Rajahs' administration; the expedition was a joint venture with the Raffles Museum, Singapore, reflecting the collaborative (if hierarchical) network of colonial natural history in the Straits Settlements and Sarawak.
- The article draws on the Sarawak Museum's own collections for comparative identification and on the published accounts of Beccari (1904) and Foxworthy (1908) for prior knowledge of the mountain; its historiographical contribution lies in extending the known ranges of several Bornean mountain endemics and in recording the Slakau Dayaks at a moment when their ethnological classification was still unsettled.
