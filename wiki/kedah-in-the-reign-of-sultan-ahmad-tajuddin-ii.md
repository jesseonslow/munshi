# Kedah in the reign of Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin II

J.M. Gullick's 1985 article reconstructs the history of Kedah during the reign of Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin II (1854–1879), a period of nearly thirty years marked by near-total silence in the contemporary records of the Straits Settlements government. The overarching thesis is that the Sultan transformed Kedah from a depopulated wasteland left by the Siamese occupation (1821–1842) into one of the most flourishing Malay states by the early 1870s, achieving this through personal energy, a new style of centralised governance, and major infrastructure projects — yet the regime was underpinned by structural weaknesses that would prove fatal to his successors.

## Summary

Gullick's article is explicitly a reconstruction from fragmentary sources, acknowledging that the Straits government's archives contain almost nothing about Kedah between 1850 and 1880. The narrative is built from the Kedah royal history compiled by Muhammad Hassan in the 1920s, Straits Settlements despatches, and a handful of contemporary European accounts. The article is a continuation of Gullick's earlier study of Kedah 1821–1855, and it picks up at the moment the young Sultan, in his early twenties, secured the throne in 1854 through the support of the powerful Wan family — particularly Wan Ismail and Wan Ibrahim — who had backed his claim against a rival kinsman. This alliance established a new pattern of government in which the traditional hereditary offices (Laxamana, Bendahara, Temenggong) were allowed to lapse and replaced by personal appointments of a *menteri besar* (chief minister) and revenue grants (*ampun kurnia*), shifting the basis of power-sharing from traditional claims to royal favour.

The economic recovery of Kedah is the article's central narrative thread. Gullick traces how the population was restored from Logan's 1850 estimate of 8,000 to roughly 50,000 by the 1870s, driven by migration from Patani, Province Wellesley, and Perak, and by the availability of previously cultivated but abandoned rice land that required only light clearing. The Sultan's most significant infrastructure achievement was the construction of a road of approximately 70 miles running due north from Alor Star to the Siamese border at Singgora, begun in late 1863 or early 1864 and completed within about a year and a half. This was an unprecedented feat among independent Malay states and was carried out through the *kerah* system of compulsory labour, which exacted a heavy human cost in fever deaths and border crossings into Province Wellesley. The road was part of a broader communications strategy that included shorter local roads, the Sultan's personal investment in shipping (from a British barque lost off China in 1857 to a shipyard established at Kuala Sungai Raja in 1862), and the development of new townships at Kuala Muda and Baling.

Yet Gullick is careful to show that the surface of firm government concealed deep structural vulnerabilities. The *kerah* system, which had already been resented in 1850, deteriorated as private revenue grantees sold the right to demand labour to third parties, approaching serfdom. The absence of a central treasury or budget system meant the Sultan maintained financial equilibrium only through personal vigilance — a defect that would have "disastrous results" under his extravagant successor. The Sultan's obsessive anxiety about Siamese suzerainty — inherited from his father's exile — constrained his diplomatic manoeuvring and made him reluctant to sign the treaty that Governor Ord put before him in 1868, an episode that reveals both his "stolid pertinacity" and the genuine limits of his freedom of action as a vassal ruler. The article closes with the Sultan's death in June 1879 at the age of forty-five, which immediately triggered the bitter intra-royal power struggle he had spent his reign trying to prevent.

### Key Findings

- Kedah's population was estimated at 8,000 by Logan in 1850 (excluding Setul and Perlis) and had recovered to approximately 50,000 by the 1870s, with Swettenham estimating 70,000 Malays plus a few thousand Chinese by 1889 (pp. 111, 122 n. 41).
- The main road from Alor Star to the Siamese border at Singgora was approximately 70 miles long, begun in late 1863 or early 1864, and ran "in an absolutely straight line due north" with a surface of hard laterite in dry weather (pp. 112–114).
- The 1868 Ord treaty proposed Kedah export duties of $8 per *koyan* (40 *pikuls*) of rice, $4 per *koyan* (800 *gantangs*) of padi, $1 per head of cattle, and $1 per 100 ducks or fowls (p. 118).
- The Sultan purchased his first British barque, the *Gratitude*, in 1856; she was lost off the coast of China in 1857, and the agent Lawrence Nairne subsequently sued the Sultan for $2,780.99, who pleaded sovereign immunity (p. 115, n. 61).
- In 1873 the Sultan raised a force of 1,000 men at the Siamese government's request to suppress a Chinese miners' revolt at Tongka in southern Siam (p. 122).
- A Malay school for the sons of rajas and notables was established in 1861 — probably the first in an independent Malay state — and a Dutch military instructor was appointed in 1862 to train a local force of about 100 men (p. 116).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin II was a ruler of exceptional personal energy and administrative ability who achieved a remarkable recovery for Kedah with minimal external assistance, yet whose regime rested on institutional foundations — the *kerah* system, the absence of a central treasury, and the unresolved question of hereditary succession under Siamese suzerainty — that were inherently unstable. The Sultan's death in 1879 at the age of forty-five exposed these vulnerabilities immediately, triggering the very intra-royal conflict he had worked to prevent and setting the stage for the financial mismanagement of his long-reigning but incapacitated son, Sultan Abdul Hamid.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the Kedah royal archives as mediated through Muhammad Hassan's *Al-Tarikh Negeri Kedah* (1928, republished 1968), Straits Settlements despatches (SSD), and a small number of contemporary European accounts (Logan, Swettenham, Anson, Maxwell). It is the second in a two-part study, following Gullick's "Kedah 1821–1855 — Years of Exile and Return" (JMBRAS 56(2), 1983).
- Historiographically, the article fills a significant gap in the English-language literature on Kedah and contributes to the broader understanding of how independent Malay states managed economic recovery and state-building in the mid-nineteenth century under the constraints of Siamese suzerainty.
