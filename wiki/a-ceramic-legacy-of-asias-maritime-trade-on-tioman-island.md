# A ceramic legacy of Asia’s maritime trade on Tioman Island

Jean Martin, writing in 1985 for the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, presents the ceramic assemblage recovered from Tioman Island as material evidence of the island's sustained role as a node in the maritime trade network linking China, Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean, and the Middle East from the 11th through the 19th century. The article was published to accompany a Southeast Asian Ceramic Society exhibition at Muzium Seni Asia, University of Malaya, and serves as both a curatorial essay and a preliminary synthesis of the island's trading history.

## Summary

Martin's central argument is that ceramics, unlike the organic and mineral commodities that formed the bulk of the regional trade, possess the durability to survive tropical conditions and thereby provide a reliable chronological record of habitation and commercial activity. The article weaves together literary evidence—Arabic navigational texts, Chinese shipping charts, Portuguese and Dutch accounts—with the physical ceramic record to reconstruct a picture of Tioman as a water-stop, shelter, and seasonal trading post whose fortunes rose and fell with broader shifts in the control of maritime routes.

A key interpretive move is the correlation between the distribution of wares on the island and the well-documented shift in Chinese export geography. The concentration of 11th- to 12th-century Guangdong stonewares at the western settlement of Nipah mirrors the period when Canton was the principal port for South Seas trade, while the later assemblage at the eastern settlement of Juara—comprising Fujian whitewares, Zhejiang celadons, and Jiangsu wares—corresponds to the 12th- to 14th-century dominance of Quanzhou. Martin further situates Tioman within a wider network by drawing parallels with the Sri Lankan site of Allai Piddy and with contemporaneous trading centres at Kedah and Kota Cina, arguing that the island was one link in a chain of small ports that facilitated the movement of goods between the Chinese and Middle Eastern markets.

The article also addresses a notable lacuna: the near-absence of 18th-century material, which Martin attributes to the combined effects of Dutch spice-trade monopolies, endemic piracy, and the diversion of trade to the Trengganu coast. The recovery of 17th-century "Kraak" design wares and Sawankhaloke storage jars, matched to the cargo of a VOC shipwreck, confirms the island's continued use during the Dutch period, while 19th-century European and Chinese "kitchen Ch'ing" wares signal a return of trade under British influence.

### Key Findings

- Of the 1,600 sherds and complete vessels in the exhibition, over 800 are Northern Song Guangdong wares dated to the 11th–12th century, all recovered from Teluk Nipah on the island's western coast (p. 86).
- The 1984 Muzium Negara excavations at both Nipah and Juara, led by Encik Adi Haji Taha, recovered approximately 2,000 additional sherds, including local earthenware found in direct association with imported ceramics at the lowest stratigraphic levels at Nipah (p. 86).
- 17th-century "Kraak" design blue-and-white and polychrome enamel fragments, together with Sawankhaloke storage jar sherds found at Tekek and Nipah, match wares from the VOC ship *Witte Leeuw* (1613) wrecked in St Helena harbour (p. 89).
- The earliest literary reference to the island appears in the Arabic *Akhbar as Sin wa'l Hind* (composed before 1000 AD), which names it *Betumah* and notes its fresh water; the Chinese *Wubeizhi* charts identify it as *Zhuma shan*, the Mountain of Rami (pp. 81–82).
- 19th-century European pottery recovered from the island includes wares from the J & M P Bell factory (Glasgow), the Petrus Regout factory (Maastricht), Staffordshire, and a plate fragment from Luneville, France, dating from the second half of the 19th century into the 20th (p. 89).
- John Turnbull Thomson's 1849 account records the island's population at only 200, noting it had been "deserted to within these last ten years" due to Illanun pirate attacks (p. 82).

### Conclusion

Martin concludes that the ceramic record, despite gaps—most notably the 18th century—demonstrates a continuous pattern of trade at Tioman spanning roughly eight centuries, from the early Northern Song period to the close of the 19th century. The island functioned as a small but persistent port in the South China Sea, its significance waxing and waning with the broader political and commercial currents of the region, and its material legacy offers a tangible, if fragmentary, record of that long engagement with maritime Asia.

## Context

- The article draws on a combination of literary sources (Arabic, Chinese, Portuguese, Dutch, and British), kiln-site archaeology from China as synthesised in Hughes and Kerr's *Kiln Sites of Ancient China* (1980), and the 1984 Muzium Negara excavations, which had not yet been formally published at the time of writing.
- Historiographically, the piece is significant for its early attempt to correlate a Southeast Asian island assemblage with dated Chinese kiln-site material and to position Tioman within a comparative framework that includes Allai Piddy (Sri Lanka), Kedah, and Kota Cina, thereby contributing to the emerging archaeological understanding of the pre-modern maritime trade network.
