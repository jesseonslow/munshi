# Producing Malaya : the photography of Carl A. Gibson-Hill

## Abstract

Much has been written on the subject of colonial photography but, despite the importance of British Malaya to the British Empire, it has received relatively little scholarly attention. In this article I present an analysis of *Malaya*, a book of photographs taken by Carl A. Gibson-Hill with an accompanying text written by Gerald Hawkins. Gibson-Hill, a former director of the Raffles (now National) Museum of Singapore photographed the region in the late 1940s and early 1950s, a time of crisis and the beginning of the end of direct colonial control of what became the independent Federation of Malaya (later Malaysia) and Republic of Singapore. His book reflects the socio-political condition of its times as well as the individual circumstances of the artist.

The author is an Associate Professor with the Wee Kim Wee School of Communication and Information at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore.

E-mail: brendan@ntu.edu.sg

## Summary

Brendan Luyt's article provides a detailed analysis of *Malaya* (1952), a book of photographs by Carl A. Gibson-Hill with accompanying text by Gerald Hawkins, published by the Singapore government during the Malayan Emergency. Drawing on Gibson-Hill's surviving correspondence and government archives, Luyt reconstructs the book's publication history: initially proposed to the ornithological publisher A. F. and G. Witherby in late 1948, the project was rejected on cost grounds until Acting-Governor Sir Patrick McKerron intervened, with the Government Printer producing a first run of 5,120 copies at a price of eight shillings. By the end of 1955, 19,000 copies had been sold (pp. 4–6). Luyt situates the book within the broader literature on colonial photography while arguing that British Malaya's photographic heritage has been comparatively neglected by scholars.

The article's central argument is that *Malaya* functioned as an exercise in state propaganda, produced at a moment when the colonial government needed to cultivate public opinion in the United Kingdom supportive of its counter-insurgency policies. The photographs, dominated by empty landscapes, traditional sailing boats, and villages, project an idyllic, conflict-free Malaya, while Hawkins' text supplies a narrative of development and modernization that justifies continued British presence (pp. 7–8, 15–17). Luyt identifies a clear division of labour between the two components: the images are picturesque and traditional, the text is developmental and justificatory.

Luyt also examines the material conditions of Gibson-Hill's photographic practice, showing how the Emergency curtailed his movement, how film shortages and tropical humidity degraded his negatives, and how his government position at the Raffles Museum enabled the work in the first place (pp. 10–11). The photographer's personal motivations—preservation of work lost during the Japanese occupation, desire for a metropolitan audience, and a genuine belief in the benefits of British rule—interacted with the state's propaganda needs to produce a book that served both individual and imperial purposes (pp. 8–10).

### Key Findings

- The book's photographic content is heavily skewed toward peaceful, traditional subjects: 34 general landscapes, 15 boats (all traditional sailing craft, no motorized vessels), 14 rivers, and 9 villages, while only 2 photographs depict tin mining and 4 show rubber cultivation, despite these being Malaya's principal exports (p. 13, Table 1).
- Conspicuous absences include the rainforest and its indigenous inhabitants, scenes of conflict or political unrest, schools and hospitals, and urban street scenes of Malacca or Penang; the only photographic hint of the Emergency is a single smiling portrait of a special Malay constable (pp. 12–14).
- Hawkins' text constructs a hierarchy of development among the Malay states, placing Perak at the top ("the best-balanced state in the peninsula" with "a modern mentality") and Trengganu at the bottom ("the least developed of all the Malay states"), while framing even the least developed states as showing promise of future industrialization (pp. 15–16).
- The text employs what Mary Louise Pratt terms "anti-conquest discourse": it presents British intervention as a response to local rulers' requests for help with "ruinous dynastic struggles," omitting the role of British merchants in exacerbating those conflicts and pressuring the Straits Settlements government to intervene (pp. 16–17).
- Gibson-Hill received no royalties from the book; his stated motivations were preservation of his work (having lost at least three manuscripts during the Japanese occupation), reaching readers in the UK, and ensuring his photographs were not attached to "maliciously inaccurate" texts about Malaya (pp. 8–9).
- The Emergency directly constrained Gibson-Hill's photography: he cancelled a planned trip to the Federation when told a police escort would be required, was forced to use "the tail end of film from a newsreel camera" upon his return to Singapore, and had to wash prints in buckets of water for lack of a darkroom (pp. 10–11).

### Conclusion

Luyt concludes that *Malaya* was, for the colonial state, a deliberate instrument of soft power: published under the tightened financial circumstances of the Emergency, it was designed to create a sympathetic public opinion in the metropole by depicting Malaya as peaceful, idyllic, and under effective British stewardship, thereby counterbalancing news coverage of the conflict and reinforcing the traditional colonial stereotype of the Malay as a man of nature and tradition who required protection from modernity (pp. 17–18). The book thus exemplifies how photography in the late colonial period served as a technology of imperial representation, mediating between the photographer's personal artistic and preservationist aims and the state's strategic communication needs.

## Context

- The Gibson-Hill correspondence is held at the Asian Civilisations Museum Library in Singapore, while government documents (including the Colonial Secretary's files on the book's publication) are in the National Archives of Singapore (PRO 7, 174/54) (pp. 4–6).
- Gibson-Hill served as editor of the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* from 1948 to 1961, publishing extensively on the history and ethnography of the colony during that period (p. 3).
