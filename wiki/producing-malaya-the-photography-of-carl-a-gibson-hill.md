# Producing Malaya : the photography of Carl A. Gibson-Hill

## Abstract

Historians working on Singapore and the Malay Peninsula during the early modern period have often relied on the works of the Eurasian writer and cartographer Manuel Godinho de Eredia (1563–1623). While some of his works have drawn attention, such as his *Description of Melaka* (c.1613), one of the lesser-known cartographic works ascribed to him, the *Atlas Miscelanea* (Miscellany Atlas, 1616–22), still requires closer and authoritative study. At the dawn of the seventeenth century, the escalation of Portuguese expeditionary activities in and around the region led to the uncovering and creation of new knowledge. The *Miscellany Atlas* contains three maps of the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra that offer a glimpse into a local trading world that was interconnected by riverine systems and overland trails. With the help of different analytical frameworks employed for understanding Malay polities of the pre-modern era, this paper carefully peels back the layers of information that have been sketched out and visually represented in these maps.

## Summary

This article by Peter Borschberg examines three virtually unknown maps by the Eurasian cartographer and adventurer Manuel Godinho de Eredia (1563–1623), found in his *Miscellany Atlas* (c. 1616–22), a private collection whose current whereabouts is unknown and which survives only in grainy black-and-white photographs reproduced in the *Portugaliae Monumenta Cartographica* (pp. 2–3). The three maps depict the Malay Peninsula, the Johor River and Singapore Straits, and the island of Sumatra, and Borschberg argues that they constitute the most significant historic resource and trade maps of the Straits region for the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries (p. 2).

The central argument is that these maps reveal an intermodal trading world in which riverine systems, overland trails, and seaports were integrated into a complex network of economic activity. Borschberg deploys analytical frameworks from Malay political geography—particularly the upstream–downstream economy model of Bronson and the gateway community concept of Hirth—to interpret how Singapore functioned as a strategic gatekeeper to the Johor River estuary and as an intermodal node connecting maritime, riverine, and overland trade (pp. 15–16). The maps are read in conjunction with Eredia's own written treatises, especially the *Informacao da Aurea Quersoneso* and the *Description of Melaka*, which provide textual corroboration for the mineral deposits, trade volumes, and extraction methods visualized on the maps (pp. 8–11).

A fourth map, the Selden map in the Bodleian Library at Oxford, is brought into dialogue with the three Eredia specimens to demonstrate how maritime routes from the South China Sea complemented rather than overlapped the overland and riverine networks depicted by Eredia, with key convergence points near the southern tip of the peninsula and off the coast of central-east Sumatra (pp. 23–24).

### Key Findings

- Perak yielded more than 300 *bahar* of tin annually (approximately 54–60 metric tons), with a further 100 *bahar* (approximately 18–20 metric tons) extracted along the Linggi River at the frontier of Portuguese Melaka (pp. 9–10).
- Eredia's map of the Malay Peninsula accurately marks the area now known in geological literature as the Central Gold Belt, with gold deposits concentrated in Pahang and Patani; the finest gold, however, came from the Minangkabau highlands of Sumatra (pp. 7–8, 20).
- The map reveals a denser network of overland trails along the eastern coast of the peninsula than the western coast, and identifies a significant north–south overland trading axis running from the Johor River region to Phattalung on the Isthmus of Kra, a finding that challenges the assumption that economic activity was concentrated on the west coast (pp. 4–5).
- Singapore is depicted as an island within the greater Johor River estuary, with the settlement of Shahbandaria marked on its east coast; this is one of only a handful of pre-1780 maps to name the island "Sincapura" (pp. 12–13).
- The Sumatra map shows a dense trail network in the northern half of the island under Acehnese control, with three north–south routes and one trans-insular route linking the gold-producing area of Salida on the west coast to the ports of Bengkalis, Siak, and Indragiri on the east coast (pp. 21–22).
- *Minyak tanah* (ground oil) was extracted at Perlak on the east coast of Sumatra, where the land was described as so oil-bearing that "many clay pots or vessels are filled every day" (p. 22).

### Conclusion

Borschberg concludes that Singapore was neither forgotten nor insignificant in the early seventeenth century: it was a recognized, charted island with a functioning port and shahbandar, serving as a strategic gatekeeper to the Johor River and an intermodal base for goods arriving or being redistributed by sea, river, and overland. The maps collectively demonstrate that Singapore and the Johor River region sat at the intersection of multiple trading networks—maritime, riverine, and overland—across both the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra, a finding that should stimulate further research into the interconnection of these routes (pp. 24–25).

## Context

- The primary archival source is the *Miscellany Atlas*, a private collection last recorded in Lisbon in the 1950s, now of unknown whereabouts; the maps are known only through black-and-white photographs in volume IV of the *Portugaliae Monumenta Cartographica* (Cortesão and Teixeira da Mota, 1987) (pp. 2–3).
- The article contributes to the historiography of early modern Southeast Asian trade by demonstrating that Eredia's maps function as economic documents encoding resource extraction, commodity flows, and intermodal connectivity, and by integrating them with the Selden map to produce a composite picture of trans-regional trade networks for the first two decades of the seventeenth century (pp. 23–25).
