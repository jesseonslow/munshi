# A brief moment of time: Kedah-Siam relations revisited

Kobkua Suwannathat-Pian (1999) re-examines Kedah–Siam tributary relations during the critical years 1809–1811 by translating and analysing five official dispatches from the Bangkok court, all dated to the year of the Goat, Trisok (J.S. 1173/1811). Her overarching thesis challenges the dominant scholarly narrative—established by R. Bonney and rooted in British archival sources—that Siamese overlordship of Kedah was characterised by abuse, excessive demands, and direct interference in internal affairs.

## Summary

The article is built around five dispatches from the Minister of Kalahom to Chao Phraya Zaiburi (Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin Halim Shah of Kedah) and to other southern governors, all issued in 1811. These documents concern the security of the western seaboard against anticipated Burmese attacks, the reorganisation of administrative jurisdictions in the south, and the internal redivision of Kedah's districts among rival royal brothers. Suwannathat-Pian uses them to demonstrate that, far from being a vassal under duress, Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin exercised considerable autonomy over his state's internal affairs, and that Bangkok's posture toward Kedah during this period was one of cooperation and even generous reward rather than coercion.

The historical problem she addresses is the accepted account that Kedah's political instability after 1803 was caused by Siamese interference and that the Sultan was progressively squeezed by excessive demands until he was compelled to act as Siam's instrument in the 1816 invasion of Perak. Suwannathat-Pian argues instead that the instability was self-generated: it originated in Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin's own decision in 1803 to invoke Siamese military power to unseat his uncle, Sultan Dhiauddin, thereby establishing a precedent that his numerous brothers followed in seeking outside patronage. The Songkhla–Nakhon rivalry among Siamese governors then provided the structural channel through which these internal disputes became entangled with Bangkok's strategic concerns. The five dispatches reveal a Bangkok court preoccupied with Burmese security threats, willing to overlook the Sultan's earlier indiscretion of secret contact with Burma, and actively praising and rewarding Kedah for its military contribution to the Thalang war of 1809–10.

Regarding the Perak invasion of 1816, Suwannathat-Pian contends that the Sultan acted on his own initiative after his diplomatic mission to persuade Perak to accept Siamese tributary status was rebuffed in May 1815. Bangkok, she shows, was still expecting a peaceful *bunga mas dan perak* mission from Perak as late as December 1816 and had no intention of sending an army. The Sultan's claim to the Penang governor that he was "forced" to invade Perak "before a Siamese army comes" was, in her reading, a diplomatic manoeuvre to gain British sympathy and forestall Penang's objection, rather than an accurate description of events.

### Key Findings

- All five dispatches are dated to 1811 (J.S. 1173), issued by the Minister of Kalahom, Chao Phraya Akkramahasenathibodi, and concern the security of the western seaboard following the Burmese attacks on Thalang in 1809–10 (pp. 65–75).
- Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin was elevated to the rank of *chao phraya*—the highest honour available to a Siamese tributary lord—after Kedah's contribution to the Thalang war, making him a peer of his immediate supervisor, Phraya Nakhon (Noi) (pp. 81, 85).
- In 1811, Tunku Bisnu (Phra Aphainuratraja) formally surrendered the districts of Setul and Lu-ngu to Bangkok and received in exchange the three districts of Parapok (Merbok), Kuala Muda, and Kerian, a transaction initiated and agreed upon by the two brothers before Bangkok's approval was sought (pp. 71–72, 81).
- The annual lease income from Penang and Province Wellesley was $10,000, and disputes over its distribution among the Sultan and his brothers were a persistent source of conflict (p. 77).
- Bangkok ordered Songkhla to temporarily assume defence of Setul and Lu-ngu after Tunku Bisnu's relocation, explicitly instructing the Sultan not to view this as a "power contest" with Songkhla (p. 70).
- The Kedah invasion of Perak occurred in November 1816, decided unilaterally by Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin after Perak's Sultan Mansur rejected tributary status in May 1815; Bangkok was still awaiting a *bunga mas dan perak* mission from Perak in December 1816 and had dispatched only a single official, not an army (pp. 86–88).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian concludes that the period 1809–1811 represents a "brief moment" in which Kedah–Siam relations operated entirely within the norms of traditional tributary diplomacy, and that the accepted narrative of Siamese oppression is an artefact of relying on sources produced by or for the Kedah court (particularly the Burney Papers and Anderson's 1824 account) without corroboration from contemporary Thai administrative records. The Sultan's political difficulties were the product of his own dynastic choices and the structural rivalries of the southern Siamese administration, not of external coercion.

## Context

- The primary archival collection explored is the *Chotmaihet Reign II J.S. 1173* (Documents Reign II's Files), held in the Manuscripts Section of the National Library, Bangkok, with translations drawn from the 1971 published edition by the Rama II Memorial Foundation (p. 76, fn. 1).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its systematic use of contemporaneous Thai administrative dispatches to challenge the British-source-based narrative of Kedah's subjugation, offering a corrective that repositions Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin as an active agent rather than a passive victim of Siamese expansion.
