# To’ Engku Klang. {Appendix of his History of Selangor

R.O. Winstedt's "A History of Selangor," published in the Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society in 1934, is a comprehensive narrative tracing the political development of Selangor from pre-Bugis antiquity through the establishment of British protection in 1874. Winstedt, then the leading authority on Malay historical sources, synthesizes Malay, Chinese, Dutch, and British archival materials to reconstruct a history of dynastic rivalry, Bugis military dominance, and the gradual erosion of Malay sovereignty under the pressures of tin mining and colonial commerce.

## Summary

Winstedt's account opens by establishing that Selangor's earliest traces of civilisation are meagre—neoliths, socketted iron tools, and a bronze bell—before moving through the successive layers of suzerainty: Sri Vijaya, Majapahit, Malacca, and Johor. The central narrative pivot is the arrival of the Bugis in the late seventeenth century, whose five brothers from Luwu in south Celebes transformed Selangor from a minor river state into a military power base for their campaigns across the Riau archipelago, Kedah, and Perak. Winstedt argues that Selangor's strategic position made it indispensable to Bugis strategy, and that the creation of a separate Selangor sultanate (Raja Lumu as Salahu'd-din Shah, c. 1765–1770) was a pragmatic recognition by the Sultans of Perak of Bugis de facto control, rather than a formal grant by the nominal overlord at Riau.

The second major phase covers the long reign of Sultan Ibrahim (d. 1826) and his son Sultan Muhammad (d. 1857), during which Selangor oscillated between Dutch and British commercial influence, waged intermittent war with Perak, and suffered the social disintegration that Newbold attributed to the extortions of the Sultan's numerous offspring. Winstedt draws heavily on the correspondence between Selangor rulers and the Penang Governor to show how the 1825 treaty with Siam and Britain fixed the Bernam boundary and how the 1824 London Treaty effectively removed the Dutch from the Peninsula, redirecting Bugis commercial aggression toward Perak and Penang.

The final and most dramatic section narrates the civil war of 1866–1874, in which the Viceroy Tengku Zia'u'd-din (brother of the Sultan of Kedah) fought to consolidate authority against Raja Mahdi, Sayid Mashhor, and the Sultan's own sons. Winstedt presents this as a war of attrition in which the Viceroy's blockade strategy, combined with Pahang military intervention and the massacre of the Kuala Lumpur garrison (119 men killed, including two European officers), ultimately broke the resistance. The war's conclusion was inseparable from the piracy crisis of 1873–74, which forced the British to intervene directly and led to the appointment of J.G. Davidson as the first Resident in 1874.

### Key Findings

- The 1823 treaty between Selangor and Perak fixed the tin duty at $12 per bahar, to be equally divided between the two states, and set a ransom of $10 per head for fugitive subjects (p. 12).
- Anderson's 1824 estimate placed Selangor's total population at roughly 5,000–6,000, with annual tin exports of 2,000 pikuls from the Selangor and Klang rivers combined; by the 1830s Newbold estimated 3,600 pikuls from Lukut, Klang, and Langat alone (p. 16).
- The massacre at Kuala Lumpur in 1872 killed 2 European officers (van Hagen and Cavalieri), 8 European or Eurasian non-commissioned officers, and 56 native sepoys; counting the earlier losses at Kuala Selangor, the total was 119 men (p. 28).
- The Viceroy's concession of 8 March 1873 to Davidson and de Seloes granted a ten-year licence to mine tin in Selangor, Klang, and Bernam on payment of 5 per cent of gross produce and $3 per bahar (312 katis) of tin exported, with a minimum capital of £100,000 (p. 30).
- The piracy trial of February 1874 at Kuala Jugra condemned seven of eight prisoners to death by spear and creese; the Sultan paid $5,000 in tin as compensation for the murder of British subjects (p. 31).
- The earliest budget of the Residency included a debt of $300,000 owed by the Viceroy to a Malacca Chinese merchant for munitions of war (p. 32).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that Selangor's transition from a Bugis military fief to a British-protected state was driven not by any single act of imperial will but by the convergence of internal dynastic fragmentation, the social disruption caused by large-scale Chinese immigration into the tin mines, and the inability of the Malay ruling class to maintain law and order in the face of piracy and civil war. The British intervention of 1874 was, in Winstedt's framing, a response to a crisis that the local actors had made unavoidable.

## Context

- The primary archival collections underpinning this study are the Straits Settlements Records (Colonial Secretariat, Singapore), the Dutch *Dagh-Register* of Batavia, and the Malay texts *Tuhfat al-Nafis*, *Hikayat Negeri Johor*, and *Misa Melayu*, all of which Winstedt himself edited or published.
- The article represents the culmination of Winstedt's programme of source-critical Malay historiography; its historiographical contribution lies in the systematic cross-referencing of Malay, Dutch, and British documents to resolve long-standing uncertainties about dates of succession and the precise terms of Bugis-Malay power-sharing arrangements.
