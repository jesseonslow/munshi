# A short history of Ulu Langat to 1900

J.M. Gullick's 2007 article traces the development of Ulu Langat district in Selangor from the early nineteenth century to 1900, arguing that despite its geographic isolation and comparatively modest tin deposits, the district transformed from a sparsely settled, hard-to-reach interior into a "flourishing planting district" by the century's end. The narrative is driven by the interplay of tin mining, colonial administrative consolidation, and—most decisively—the arrival of the railway and rubber cultivation in the 1890s.

## Summary

Gullick's account begins with the physical and logistical constraints that defined Ulu Langat's early history. The Langat River, the only route into the district, was navigable only to small ships for the lower reaches and became increasingly difficult upstream, with Swettenham recording a five-day journey over 57 miles in 1875. Until lateral roads were built in the 1880s, the river was the sole means of exporting tin, and in the dry season a heavily laden boat could take 13 days to travel downstream (pp. 1–2). This isolation shaped the character of settlement: early Malay communities owed vague fealty to the Toh Engku Klang, a Bugis-era office of diminishing consequence, while Sumatran migrants (Menangkabau and Mandailing) and Chinese miners arrived in the mid-nineteenth century drawn by tin and rattan (pp. 3–5).

The middle section of the article examines the turbulent post-civil-war period, centred on the figure of Sutan Puasa, the Mandailing leader whose suspected 1875 uprising at Kajang prompted a pre-emptive colonial strike and his eventual arrest. Gullick handles this episode with characteristic scepticism, noting that Davidson's intelligence was "the most scandalous exaggerations" and that Sutan Puasa himself wished to avoid open confrontation (pp. 5–7). The subsequent tenure of Raja Kahar as Malay Magistrate (1875–82) is portrayed as a period of gradual, imperfect order, marred by Kahar's personal failings and the catastrophic tin price collapse of the late 1870s (pp. 7–9). The 1878 boundary treaty with Sungei Ujong, which resolved a long-standing territorial dispute over the Labu River valley, is presented as a significant administrative milestone, marked by a ceremonial proclamation at Rekoh (pp. 9–10).

The final phase, 1894–1900, marks the district's "take off." The extension of the railway to Kajang in 1897, combined with the first European estate land applications in 1894 and the Kindersley brothers' experimental rubber planting in 1896, fundamentally altered the district's economic trajectory. Gullick closes with Ethel Hume's 1899 transit through Semenyih—a vivid, if brief, snapshot of a district now connected to the wider colonial economy (pp. 14–16).

### Key Findings

- The 1884 rough census recorded a district population of 3,695 (1,808 Malays, 1,675 Chinese); by the 1891 systematic census this had risen to 8,015 (3,166 Malays, 4,664 Chinese), reflecting significant immigration during the tin boom (pp. 11–12).
- The 1878 boundary treaty with Sungei Ujong ceded a coastal strip including Lukut to Selangor in exchange for the Labu River valley upstream; the ceremony at Rekoh was attended by some 200 locals and marked by a gun salute, flag-hoisting, and a buffalo feast (pp. 9–10).
- By the mid-1880s, 1,500 miners (almost all Chinese) were at work in the district following the 1879 tin price boom; the leading towkay Chan Ah Chan employed 400 Chinese and 200 Malays at one time (pp. 10–11).
- The railway reached Kajang in 1897; in 1894 the Resident approved the first two European estate land applications (500 acres each) outside the Kuala Lumpur and Klang districts, and in 1896 the Kindersley brothers planted an experimental 5 acres of rubber at Inch Kenneth estate (pp. 14–15).
- A 1891 tin ore discovery near Kajang generated 44 land applications covering 915 acres within ten days, illustrating the speculative intensity of the mining economy (p. 14).
- The men-to-women ratio in the district improved from approximately 10:1 in 1876 to 10:7 by 1891, indicating gradual settlement and family formation among the Malay population (p. 12).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that Ulu Langat's transformation was not driven by any single resource windfall but by the convergence of improved communications—above all the railway—with the global rubber boom of the 1890s. The district, long dismissed as a "misleading" and "struggling" backwater, became a "flourishing planting district" within a decade, a turning point that Gullick marks with the arrival of the Kindersley estates and the first European planters (pp. 14–15).

## Context

- The article draws extensively on primary sources including Swettenham's 1875 reports and journals, the Douglas diary (1876–82), Selangor Government Gazette district reports, Selangor Journal articles, census returns, and the 1878 boundary treaty documents, providing a granular district-level narrative that complements broader state histories of Selangor.
- Gullick's approach is characteristically sceptical of official intelligence and local magnate self-presentation, a methodological stance consistent with his wider body of work on colonial Selangor (e.g., *Rulers and Residents*, 1992).
