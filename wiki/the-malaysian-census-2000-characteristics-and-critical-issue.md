# The Malaysian census 2000: characteristics and critical issues

Usman Haji Yaakob's 2006 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* provides a comprehensive demographic analysis of Malaysia's 2000 population census, situating the findings within the broader trajectory of the country's demographic transition from 1970. The overarching thesis is that Malaysia has undergone a profound demographic transformation—characterized by rapid population growth, declining fertility, accelerating urbanization, and rising life expectancy—that will fundamentally reshape the nation's demands for education, employment, health services, and social infrastructure in the coming decades.

## Summary

The article systematically examines the principal demographic indicators recorded in the 2000 census—population size and growth, age structure, sex ratio, literacy, age at first marriage, urbanization, labour force distribution, fertility, and mortality—comparing them against earlier censuses from 1970, 1980, and 1991. Yaakob frames these data within the context of Malaysia's demographic transition, arguing that the country has moved from a high-fertility, high-mortality regime to one of low mortality and declining fertility, though fertility levels remain above replacement. The analysis draws heavily on official Department of Statistics of Malaysia (DOS) publications, including the *Basic Demographic Characteristics*, *Economic Characteristics of the Population*, *Education and Social Characteristics of the Population*, and *Vital Statistics* time series, supplemented by United Nations population projections and a small body of secondary literature on urbanization and fertility differentials.

A central analytical thread is the interplay between ethnic composition and demographic behaviour. Yaakob highlights how differential fertility rates among Malays, Chinese, and Indians have shaped the national age structure, with the East Coast states of Kelantan and Terengganu retaining the youngest populations and highest dependency ratios, while urban centres such as Kuala Lumpur and Pulau Pinang exhibit the oldest median ages and lowest dependency ratios. The article also traces the structural shift in the labour force from agriculture to manufacturing and services, linking this to the spatial concentration of investment under the Eighth Malaysia Plan and the resulting pressures on urban infrastructure.

The author treats the census not merely as a statistical exercise but as a planning instrument, emphasizing that the demographic picture emerging from 2000 data carries direct implications for government policy on education capacity, pension provision for an ageing population, urban planning, and employment creation. The discussion of the de jure enumeration method adopted in 2000 (a departure from the de facto method used in 1970, 1980, and 1991) underscores the methodological evolution of Malaysian census practice.

### Key Findings

- Malaysia's population grew from 10,439,430 in 1970 to 23,274,690 in 2000 (a 123 per cent increase), with an average annual growth rate of 2.60 per cent during 1991–2000; the United Nations projected the population would reach 31.3 million by 2025 and 37.8 million by 2050 (pp. 28–29).
- The total fertility rate declined from 6.83 per woman in 1950 to 2.90 in 2000, with ethnic differentials persisting: Bumiputera at 3.6, Chinese at 2.7, and Indian at 2.7 births per woman in 1998; the infant mortality rate fell from 55.5 per 1,000 live births in 1963 to 8.5 in 1998 (pp. 38–39).
- Urbanization accelerated sharply: 62.0 per cent of the population resided in urban areas in 2000, compared with 27.0 per cent in 1970; the number of urban centres grew from 49 in 1970 to 170 in 2000, and 23 urban centres exceeded 150,000 inhabitants (pp. 35–36).
- The agricultural share of the labour force collapsed from 52.1 per cent in 1970 to 15.2 per cent in 2000, while manufacturing rose from 8.4 to 21.7 per cent and community/social/personnel services reached 21.6 per cent (pp. 37–38).
- The national literacy rate rose from 58.0 per cent in 1970 to 91.0 per cent in 2000, though Kelantan (85.8 per cent), Sarawak (81.6 per cent), and Sabah (84.6 per cent) remained below the national average; the median age increased from 17.2 to 23.6 years over the same period (pp. 32–33).
- Dependency ratios varied dramatically by state: Kelantan recorded the highest overall dependency ratio at 86.2 (with a young dependency of 77.3), while Wilayah Persekutuan Kuala Lumpur recorded the lowest at 40.4 (with a young dependency of 35.9) (p. 31).

### Conclusion

Yaakob concludes that Malaysia's demographic transition, while achieving remarkable gains in life expectancy and literacy, has created a dual challenge: a swelling young working-age population requiring employment in manufacturing and services, and a gradually ageing cohort that will strain health and pension systems. The author frames these dynamics as inseparable from the country's broader development trajectory, arguing that the census data provide the evidentiary basis for anticipating and managing the social, political, and economic consequences of continued demographic change.

## Context

- The article draws exclusively on official Department of Statistics of Malaysia publications and United Nations projections; no independent archival research or original fieldwork is reported.
- The study contributes to the historiography of Malaysian demography by providing a consolidated, state-by-state comparative analysis of the 2000 census that was not available in the DOS publications themselves, and by explicitly linking demographic indicators to the policy framework of the Eighth Malaysia Plan.
