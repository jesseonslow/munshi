# The word kongsi: a note

Wang Tai-peng (Wong Tai Peng) published this concise etymological and political note in 1979, arguing that the term *kongsi*—long reduced by colonial administrators to a synonym for "secret society"—denoted in fact a form of open, representative self-government rooted in Chinese partnership and sworn-brotherhood traditions. The article situates this claim within a broader historiographical correction of how Southeast Asian Chinese political organisation has been understood.

## Summary

Wang begins by surveying the semantic range of *kongsi* as it appears across the archipelago, drawing on Bezemer's *Beknopte Encyclopaedie van Nederlandsch Oost-Indie* as the most comprehensive existing account. The term, derived from Hokkien (Hakka: *Kung-sze*), was applied to firms, clan societies, provincial clubs, temple associations, and what colonial authorities labelled "secret societies." This conflation caused persistent administrative confusion: the Straits Settlements government did not distinguish *hui-kuan* (clan or provincial associations) from *hui* (brotherhoods) until 1892, and even then the distinction was meaningful only to the administration, not to the Chinese community itself (pp. 102–103).

The core of Wang's argument is a redefinition. Rejecting Blythe's broad "any partnership or group with a common interest," Barbara Ward's vague "large political groups in the mining districts," and Jackson's narrower "alliances of mining unions," Wang defines *kongsi* as "a form of open government, based on an enlarged partnership and brotherhood," whose purpose was to protect economic gains and resist outside powers, with every member holding equal rights in governance through election, participation, and dismissal at general meetings (pp. 103–104). He traces the institutional roots of this system to the *huo-chi* partnership structure of the 12th century, through its elaboration during the Ming dynasty among sea-merchants and illegal miners, and into the 18th-century mining *kongsi* of Borneo and the Straits Settlements.

Wang then extends the argument beyond Southeast Asia, contending that the T'ien-Ti Hui and the Taiping Revolution were misread as restorationist or merely peasant movements when they were in fact attempts to institute Brotherhood government as a political alternative to monarchy. He criticises the Eurocentric tendency to equate *kongsi* government with Western republicanism, insisting that while superficial resemblances exist in elected representation, the underlying logic—synthesis of extended partnership and sworn brotherhood—was uniquely Chinese and had no Western parallel (pp. 104–105).

### Key Findings

- The Straits Settlements administration did not formally distinguish *hui-kuan* from *hui* (brotherhoods) until 1892 (S.S.B.B., 1892, 02, 3) (p. 103).
- The *huo-chi* partnership system, which gave rise to a managerial class, had reached maturity by the 12th century and had already spread to Japan and Southeast Asia (p. 105).
- The rebellion of Teng Mao-chi in 1455 is identified as the pioneering event that established a new way of life based on the equality of partnership among illegal miners (p. 105).
- The T'ien-Ti Hui in Penang was a partnership in origin, not a secret society (p. 103).
- Purcell (1952, p. 328) observed that "all Chinese social organisation was necessarily 'secret' whilst it was not recognised or was banned by the Government," a point Wang uses to dismantle the colonial category (p. 103).
- Wang's own definition: *kongsi* was "a form of open government, based on an enlarged partnership and brotherhood" with public criticism, participation, election, and dismissal by general meeting (p. 104).

### Conclusion

Wang's definitive takeaway is that *kongsi* government represents a genuine, indigenous Chinese political tradition—neither a secret society nor a Western-style republic—that was systematically mischaracterised by colonial administrators and subsequently by scholars working within Eurocentric frameworks. The failure of Brotherhood government to take root in China proper, he argues, stemmed not from its inadequacy but from the attempt to fuse it with the old monarchical framework.

## Context

- The article draws on secondary literature (Bezemer, Purcell, Blythe, Ward, Jackson, Somers Heidhues, Chen Yi-shung, Lin Hsiao Sheng) rather than primary archival collections; its contribution is primarily conceptual and historiographical.
- It represents an early 1970s intervention in the revisionist literature on Chinese "secret societies" in Southeast Asia, aligning with Somers Heidhues's (1974) recharacterisation of these groups as vehicles of Chinese autonomy under colonial rule, while pushing further by grounding the *kongsi* in a long-term Chinese political tradition rather than treating it as a colonial-era adaptation.
