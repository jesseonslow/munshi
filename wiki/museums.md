# Museums

Museums in the Malaysian region have served as instruments of scientific collection, national commemoration, and cultural representation since the late nineteenth century, spanning the Malay Peninsula and Borneo. Their functions have ranged from the taxonomic description of newly collected specimens to the construction of official narratives of statehood, and from the preservation of ethnographic artefacts to the curation of archaeological heritage. The records examined here reveal a complex institutional history in which museums operated simultaneously as research centres, colonial administrative appendages, and—after independence—as vehicles for nation-building, each role carrying distinct implications for what was collected, how it was displayed, and whose story was told.

## Colonial-Era Collecting and Scientific Output

The earliest museums documented in the Society's literature functioned primarily as repositories for natural history and ethnographic specimens gathered during colonial expeditions. The Sarawak Museum, under the curatorship of R. H. Shelford in the late 1890s and early 1900s, exemplifies this model. Shelford published short "notes" in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* describing new or previously undescribed specimens from his collections, serving both as formal taxonomic records and as evidence of the museum's research output [[1, p. 69]](#ref-1). His 1901 notes include a detailed morphological and behavioural account of a pit-fall-hunting larva of the fly genus *Vermileo* discovered on Mt. Penrissen, as well as descriptions of butterfly specimens from Mt. Matang that resolved long-standing uncertainties in the literature [[1, pp. 69–71]](#ref-1). The same article records the museum's adoption of a decimal cataloguing system adapted from Melvil Dewey's 1874 library classification, assigning class letters (Mammals A, Birds B, Reptiles C, Amphibia D, Fishes E) followed by successive decimal numbers for family, genus, and species [[1, p. 71]](#ref-1). Shelford noted that Dewey's system had received little attention in "England and her dependencies," revealing the limited penetration of American library-science innovations into colonial administrative practice at the turn of the century [[1, p. 70]](#ref-1).

The Raffles Museum in Singapore occupied a comparable position for the Straits Settlements and the Federated Malay States. In 1923, the German entomologist R. Kleine, based in Stettin, published a taxonomic paper describing eight new taxa of Brentid weevils from the Raffles Museum collection, prompted by Major Moulton, the museum's director, who had sent the material for identification [[2, p. 271]](#ref-2). The paper, translated into English by Dr. Hanitsch from Kleine's original German manuscript, established a new genus, *Holotrachelus*, and provided the first comprehensive faunal checklist of Brentidae known from the southern Malay Peninsula, enumerating approximately 45 species across nine subfamilies for the Penang–Perak–Siam region [[2, pp. 277]](#ref-2). The archival record notes that types were subsequently transferred to the British Museum in London, reflecting the colonial-era practice of European entomologists describing species from collections assembled in British Malaya [[2]](#ref-2).

The Selangor Museum represents another strand of this colonial collecting tradition, with particular emphasis on ornithology. In the early 1900s, its zoological staff regularly collected birds in mountainous parts of the Thai-Malay Peninsula, giving hill stations a prominent role in Malaysian ornithology [[3]](#ref-3). H. C. Robinson published a synopsis of the museum's collections of mountain birds in 1909, drawing connections to the work of other prominent European and American bird collectors [[3]](#ref-3). The museum employed at least two full-time collectors from 1903 onward, referred to in official records only as "the Dyak Collectors" or "the Dyaks attached to the Museum"; Charles Ulok, formerly of the Sarawak Museum, is the single identifiable figure, described by Boden Kloss as "undoubtedly the best [local] collector in the East" [[3, pp. 123–124]](#ref-3). Ulok died in 1920 from dysentery acquired while on duty at Kuala Tahan [[3, p. 136]](#ref-3). In 1914, the Selangor Museum made a large donation of approximately 300 bird specimens to the Liverpool Museums, of which 60 are mountain birds now held in the World Museum, National Museums Liverpool [[3, p. 127]](#ref-3). The 1905 Federated Malay States Museums expedition to Gunung Tahan, the peninsula's highest peak, was partly funded by the British Museum, with specimens divided between that institution and the Selangor Museum [[3, p. 134]](#ref-3). The museum itself was demolished in 1959; its vertebrate collections had been transferred to the Raffles Museum in 1926, with additional holdings at the Natural History Museum, London, and the American Museum of Natural History [[3, pp. 136–137]](#ref-3).

The Perak Museum also featured in the Society's literature as a site of curatorial inquiry. W. Linehan's 1951 note documents a small group of anomalous tin ingots held in the museum that bear no resemblance to the well-known *tampang* (flat, stamped ingots) or "animal" types and lack any local traditional association [[4]](#ref-4). Linehan catalogues these objects—mound-shaped, octagonal, and cylindrical specimens recovered from the Klang River, the Lower Perak railway construction, and various estates in Selangor and Perak—and advances tentative interpretations linking their forms to possible solar cult symbolism, the Hindu *linga*, and the mound-marriage ceremonies of the Malayan aborigines [[4]](#ref-4). The article reads as an internal collection note made accessible to a scholarly audience, with provenance data drawn from museum accession records [[4]](#ref-4).

## Ethnographic Collections and Provenance

Beyond natural history, colonial-era museums accumulated ethnographic collections whose provenance and interpretive frameworks have attracted sustained scholarly attention. The Peabody Museum at Harvard University holds Borneo collections including a *palang* (penis pin) and its associated perforator instrument, traced by G. N. Appell to the 1896–1898 Furness-Hiller Pacific expedition [[5, p. 203]](#ref-5). Appell identifies the objects as likely collected by Furness and Charles Hose during the 1897–1898 exploration of the Baram and Tinjar River headwaters, though the accession records do not specify the collecting group [[5, p. 203]](#ref-5). He reports two further items from the Peabody reference files: an aging photograph of a *palang in situ* that he concludes is probably the earliest known photograph of the object, taken by a member of the expedition in 1897 or 1898, and a handwritten note and drawing attributed to Furness himself [[5, p. 204]](#ref-5). Harrisson W. Smith, an MIT electrical engineering professor who visited Sarawak in 1911–1912, donated 954 photographs to the Peabody, including three of the *palang in situ* [[5, p. 204]](#ref-5).

The most extensive treatment of a Bornean museum collection in the Society's literature is the Earl of Cranbrook's 2013 inventory of the "Everett Collection from Borneo Caves" in the Natural History Museum, London [[6]](#ref-6). The collection was assembled by Alfred Hart Everett during the 1878–9 British Association/Royal Society-sponsored investigation of Borneo caves, an expedition rooted in the post-Darwinian search for the "missing link" [[6, pp. 86–87]](#ref-6). The total expedition fund was £300, of which John Evans personally raised £259 3s 0d between February and November 1878 [[6, p. 87]](#ref-6). Everett visited 32 caves, but the published list records material from only twelve, comprising 109 lots [[6, pp. 88]](#ref-6). A critical problem identified by Cranbrook is that a substantial proportion of the lots were not from controlled excavation but were purchased from gold-washers and Malay intermediaries, often with vague or contradictory provenance statements [[6, pp. 89–91]](#ref-6). Everett's own first quarterly report, his maps and plans, and the human remains examined by George Busk are all lost or unaccounted for [[6, pp. 89–91]](#ref-6). The collection was last systematically checked by Eastham and Simons in 1959, and subsequent unrecorded sorting has further complicated provenance [[6, pp. 104–105]](#ref-6). Cranbrook's most significant empirical contribution is the comparison between Everett's stratigraphic descriptions and the results of the 2008 excavation at Gua Tupak by a Universiti Sains Malaysia team, which confirmed the broad stratigraphic sequence and yielded radiocarbon dates placing the archaeologically rich upper levels in the historic period (AD 1480–1890) [[6, pp. 103–104]](#ref-6).

The Sarawak Museum's ethnographical collection was the subject of R. S. Shelford's 1905 illustrated catalogue, published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* [[7]](#ref-7). This work, spanning 67 pages, represents one of the earliest systematic attempts to document and display the museum's holdings of material culture from the region.

## State Museums and the Construction of National Memory

After independence, Malaysian museums were repurposed as instruments of nation-building, a transformation that has drawn critical scrutiny from scholars working within the Society's journal. Abu Talib Ahmad's 2008 article evaluates how state museums construct and project official narratives of the past, arguing that these institutions function as instruments that selectively foreground Malay achievement while marginalizing other ethnic groups, political movements, and uncomfortable historical episodes [[8, p. 45]](#ref-8). Ahmad's site visits to museums in Kelantan, Terengganu, Kedah, Penang, Selangor, Kuala Lumpur, and Sabah reveal a structural problem: the same group of writers and historians serve both the Ministry of Education's school history texts and the Ministry of Unity, Culture, Arts and Heritage's museum narratives, creating a collusion that reinforces a single official version of the past [[8, p. 46]](#ref-8).

The Kedah State Museum claims Kedah as the oldest sultanate in Malaysia and the first Islamic state in South-East Asia, based on an unbroken line of 28 rulers since 1136, a date drawn from the semi-official *Al-Tarikh Salasilah Negeri Kedah* (first published 1928) [[8, p. 48]](#ref-8). Yet the museum omits the Kulim War of 1888, the role of Chinese secret societies, the chettiar moneylenders, the Sam-sam mixed-ethnic group, and the Kesatuan Melayu Muda's pre-war nationalist activities [[8, pp. 51–52]](#ref-8). The Malayan People's Anti-Japanese Army (MPAJA) is consistently banished from anti-Japanese resistance sections and instead placed in the Emergency narrative as villains, a treatment Ahmad contrasts unfavourably with Singapore's museums, which do not hide the negative aspects of the Occupation [[8, pp. 62–63]](#ref-8). The Kelantan museums, by contrast, project an idealized vision of "Malayness" through folk culture, royal ceremony, and handicrafts, with the silver jubilee exhibition of Sultan Ismail Petra explicitly designed to instil "love and loyalty" toward the royal institution [[8, pp. 56–57]](#ref-8).

The Lembah Bujang Archaeology Museum, established in 1977, emerges as a relative positive example, preserving the Hindu-Buddhist legacy of ancient Kedah and corroborating local chronicles through archaeological evidence [[8, p. 53]](#ref-8). The Bujang Valley covers 400 square kilometres with 41 historical sites identified over 160 years of archaeological research; eight ancient temples have been rebuilt as heritage sites [[8, p. 52]](#ref-8). Ahmad notes that out of seven administrative capitals mentioned in the *Hikayat Merong Mahawangsa* and six in the *Al-Tarikh Salasilah Negeri Kedah*, archaeological evidence has confirmed five, lending historical credibility to indigenous sources [[8, p. 53]](#ref-8). Yet even here, the museum's emphasis on temple reconstruction for tourism over stratigraphic research reflects a heritage-driven rather than research-driven priority [[8, p. 55]](#ref-8).

The Tun Abdul Razak Memorial Museum, opened on 6 May 1982 at the former official residence Sri Taman in Kuala Lumpur, represents the most developed example of state-engineered commemoration [[9, pp. 5–6]](#ref-9). The date was deliberately chosen to coincide with both the formation of the 1956 Education Committee chaired by Tun Abdul Razak and the 1960 launch of the Red Book [[9, pp. 5–6]](#ref-9). The memorial housed 700 exhibits at opening and had grown to 1,650 by 2010 [[9, pp. 5–6]](#ref-9). All three of Malaysia's first prime ministers have dedicated memorial museums in Kuala Lumpur, all under the custody of the Ministry of Information, Communication and Culture with input from the National Archives [[9]](#ref-9). Ahmad identifies a consistent pattern of "exclusions and silences": the memorial omits Tun Abdul Razak's membership in the British Labour Party and Fabian Society, his socialist reading list, the role of leftist intellectuals in formulating the New Economic Policy, the University and University College Act of 1971, and the political fate of many development projects after 1976 [[9]](#ref-9). The memorial's primary audience is schoolchildren on organized tours, for whom the experience largely replicates the school history textbook [[9]](#ref-9).

A separate but related concern is the accuracy of museum displays. Serge Jardin's 2025 article critically assesses the "Melaka Fort Gateway Model" displayed in the lobby of the Galeri Warisan Kota Melaka, a stone-built gate mock-up presented as a representation of the Sultanate-era fortification [[10]](#ref-10). Jardin demonstrates that the model is derived from an anonymous Dutch print published in Leiden in 1706 by Pieter van der Aa as an illustration for a translation of Joao de Barros' *Décadas da Ásia*, not from any contemporary fifteenth- or early sixteenth-century source [[10, pp. 5–6]](#ref-10), [[1]](#ref-1). The print's artists, working two centuries after the events depicted, produced anachronistic elements including Dutch gables, East-Indiamen in place of Portuguese carracks, and European pine trees in place of casuarinas [[10, pp. 13–14]](#ref-10). Jardin systematically reviews the full corpus of contemporary written sources—Chinese, Malay, and European—and finds that none confirms the existence of stone walls or a stone gate during the Sultanate period [[10, pp. 7–10]](#ref-10). The 2006–07 archaeological excavations by Universiti Sains Malaysia and the National Heritage Department along the Portuguese fort wall alignment likewise yielded no evidence of a prior stone-built Malay fortification [[10, p. 10]](#ref-10). The article further dissects thirteen academic articles published between 2014 and 2023 by nineteen authors that have argued for a stone-walled Melaka, identifying fabricated sources, misattributed images, faulty translations, and wholesale replication of errors across successive publications [[10, pp. 12–17]](#ref-10).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature documents a clear evolution in how museums have been studied and represented. In the colonial period, the journal served as a publication venue for museum curators themselves: Shelford's "Notes from the Sarawak Museum" (1901) and his illustrated catalogue of the ethnographical collection (1905) represent the standard practice of colonial-era curators publishing short notes to describe new specimens and document holdings [[7]](#ref-7), [[1]](#ref-1). Kleine's 1923 paper on Brentidae from the Raffles Museum continues this tradition, with the museum director initiating the exchange of material for identification [[2]](#ref-2). These early contributions treat the museum as a research instrument, its collections as raw data for taxonomic and ethnographic description.

From the mid-twentieth century onward, the focus shifts from the museum as producer of knowledge to the museum as object of critical analysis. Linehan's 1951 note on tin ingots in the Perak Museum retains a curatorial voice but introduces interpretive speculation that goes beyond mere description [[4]](#ref-4). Appell's 1968 article on the *palang* at the Peabody Museum marks a transition toward provenance studies and the critical use of visual evidence, raising questions about the discontinuous distribution of the object and its implications for cultural boundary-making [[5]](#ref-5).

The post-independence period sees the emergence of a distinct critical tradition in museum studies. Virginia Matheson Hooker's 2003 work on the National History Museum and Nigel Worden's 1987 study of Melaka established the framework that Abu Talib Ahmad extends in his 2008 comparative survey of state-level institutions [[8]](#ref-8). Ahmad's 2011 article on the Tun Abdul Razak Memorial applies Benedict Anderson's distinction between popular and state-engineered nationalism to a post-independence political memorial, systematically cataloguing the "exclusions and silences" that distinguish the official commemorative narrative from the more complex picture revealed by archival and biographical research [[9]](#ref-9). Jardin's 2025 article on the Melaka Fort Gateway represents the most recent intervention, applying forensic source criticism to a specific museum display and exposing the chain of errors—fabricated sources, misidentified images, anachronistic details—that has accumulated in both popular and academic literature [[10]](#ref-10).

The Earl of Cranbrook's 2013 re-inventory of the Everett Collection stands apart as a work of archival and palaeontological scholarship that treats the museum collection itself as a primary source requiring systematic re-examination [[6]](#ref-6). It is the first comprehensive re-inventory since the 1959 Eastham/Simons check, and it directly links the 1878–9 expedition to the 2008 Universiti Sains Malaysia/Sarawak Museum re-investigation of Gua Tupak, providing the first radiocarbon-dated comparison with Everett's stratigraphic descriptions [[6]](#ref-6). John-James Wilson's 2021 article on colonial birding in the Thai-Malay Peninsula similarly recovers the largely anonymous labour of the Selangor Museum's indigenous collectors and situates the Liverpool collection within the broader network of colonial-era specimen transfers between the Federated Malay States Museums, the British Museum, and other institutions [[3]](#ref-3).

Taken together, these records trace a trajectory from the museum as colonial research outpost, through its post-independence repurposing as a nation-building instrument, to its current position as a site of critical historiographical contestation, where the accuracy of displays, the politics of inclusion and exclusion, and the integrity of provenance documentation are all subjects of active scholarly debate.
