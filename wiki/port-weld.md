# Port Weld

M.E. Wayte's 1959 article "Port Weld" examines the rise and fall of a small Perak coastal port created in 1885 as a tin-export outlet for the Larut mining district, arguing that its trajectory exemplifies how an artificial port, built to serve specific economic demands, inevitably declined when its natural advantages proved insufficient for a developing economy. The study, based on 1955 fieldwork and archival research, traces four distinct phases from heyday to near-obsolescence over roughly seventy years.

## Summary

Port Weld was established in 1885 at the mouth of the Dindings River to provide a sea outlet for tin from Larut, Kamunting, and the surrounding district, which at the time constituted Malaya's leading mining area. Its creation was inseparable from the construction of Malaya's first railway, an eight-and-a-quarter-mile line from Taiping to Sapetang built by Ceylonese engineers and labourers and opened on 1 June 1885 (pp. 157–159). For a brief period the port ranked among Malaya's leading ports, second only to Malacca and Georgetown, with ten trains daily and regular steamship connections to Penang (p. 160).

The port's decline was driven by two converging forces: the geographic shift of tin mining from Larut to the Kinta valley, and the progressive completion of the Malayan trunk railway system that bypassed it. Larut's tin output fell from 135,889 standard pikul in 1884 to 37,373 by 1902, while Kinta's rose from 26,279 pikul in 1889 to 134,166 in 1898 (p. 161). The 1895 completion of the Ipoh–Tapah Road line diverted Kinta's trade to Telok Anson, and the 1902 opening of the Taiping–Prai section of Swettenham's trunk railway allowed Larut's ore to reach Penang directly by rail, eliminating the need for trans-shipment at Port Weld entirely (pp. 163–164).

By the 1950s the port was functionally defunct. The mangrove zone, now seven miles wide and advancing seaward at a furlong every twelve to fourteen years, had reduced the main channel to less than a foot of water at low tide (pp. 154–155). The town's economy had shifted entirely to local extraction: charcoal manufacture (40,000 pikul per month), firewood production (160,000 pikul per month), and small-scale fishing, with three-quarters of the population engaged in these activities (pp. 166–167).

### Key Findings

- Larut tin production collapsed from 135,889 standard pikul (1884) to 37,373 (1902), while Kinta valley output rose from 26,279 (1889) to 134,166 pikul (1898), shifting the centre of gravity of Perak's mining economy away from Port Weld's hinterland (p. 161).
- The 1895 opening of the Ipoh–Tapah Road railway caused an immediate trade shock: Port Weld imports dropped by $876,000 in 1896 while Telok Anson imports rose by $1,120,000 in the same year (p. 163).
- The mangrove forest at Port Weld was seven miles wide in 1957 and advancing seaward at a rate of one furlong per twelve to fourteen years; the main channel (Kuala Sangga Besar) was barely eight feet deep at high water and less than a foot at ebb (pp. 154–155).
- Population grew modestly from 1,572 (1931 census) to 2,351 (1947 census), but this reflected expansion of local charcoal and fishing industries rather than any revival of port trade (p. 165).
- Of 280 vessels registered at Port Weld in 1951, 201 were under five tons burden, reflecting the shallowness of the waterways and the localised nature of remaining maritime activity (p. 166).
- By 1939, rubber exported through Port Weld accounted for only 2.5% of all rail-borne rubber exports of Malaya, compared with 22.2% through Prai and 41.5% through Port Swettenham (p. 164).

### Conclusion

Wayte concludes that Port Weld's decline was structurally determined by the spatial logic of the Malayan railway system: the trunk line's north-south orientation, combined with a deliberate policy of concentrating trade at the major ocean ports of Penang and Singapore, rendered small transverse ports like Port Weld economically redundant. Because the wharf and harbour installations were owned by the Malayan Railways, the port's fate was entirely dependent on railway traffic policy, and with no independent commercial base, it was destined to remain "semi-isolated from the rest of the country and living a life based on its immediate environment" (pp. 165–167).

## Context

- The article draws on F.M.S. Annual Reports (1896–1939), the Perak Government Gazette (1888–96), and the Straits Settlements Government Gazette (1875–85), all held in colonial administrative archives (p. 167).
- Wayte's fieldwork in 1955 and the accompanying maps (drawn by C.A.G.H. in 1957) provide a rare contemporaneous physical description of a declining colonial port in the early post-war period (pp. 156, 162).
