# Sir Lawrence Nunns Guillemard

## Early Career and Appointment to Malaya

Sir Lawrence Nunns Guillemard (1862–1952) was a British civil servant who served as Governor of the Straits Settlements and High Commissioner for the Federated Malay States (FMS) from 1920 to 1927. Educated at Cambridge, he spent nearly thirty-four years in Home Service, principally in the Treasury, holding posts including Private Secretary to the Chancellor of the Exchequer, Deputy Chairman of the Board of Inland Revenue, and Chairman of Customs and Excise before his colonial appointment [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1). His Malayan posting was secured through the intervention of Lord Alfred Milner, then Secretary of State for the Colonies, who regarded him as one of his "most go-ahead Governors" [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1). Guillemard arrived in Malaya in February 1920 as a complete novice in colonial administration, yet he exuded in public a sense of congeniality, humour, and accessibility that contrasted sharply with the hard-driving manner of his principal adversary, Chief Secretary Sir William George Maxwell [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1).

## The Power Struggle with Maxwell, 1921–1925

Guillemard's tenure was dominated by a bitter constitutional dispute with Maxwell over the degree of control the High Commissioner should exercise over the FMS. Guillemard insisted that the Chief Secretary occupied "exactly the same position under me (as High Commissioner) as the Colonial Secretary in the Colony occupies under me (as Governor)" and that both "should work as equals" [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1). Maxwell, who hailed from a family with nearly seventy years of Malayan public service, rejected this equivalence, maintaining that the two posts "are not and cannot be equals" [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1).

The conflict was intensified by the post-war trade recession, which rendered Guillemard's ambitious development budget of over $114 million for 1921 untenable [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). The resulting financial crisis, compounded by the FMS's inability to float its own £10 million loan in London, deepened inter-territorial suspicion and drew criticism from the Unofficial members of the Federal Council, who petitioned for the restoration of the title of Resident-General to affirm the Chief Secretary's independent authority [[1, pp. 4–7]](#ref-1). The Colonial Office rejected the petition in May 1923, with Secretary of State Devonshire affirming that "unity of responsibility and control in the hands of the High Commissioner is of the first importance for the well-being and development of Malaya as a whole" [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1).

Guillemard's strategy to consolidate control included the appointment of A.M. Pountney as joint Financial Adviser, which strengthened his grip over FMS finance [[1, p. 8]](#ref-1). He also cultivated the goodwill of the Malay Rulers, particularly Sultan Iskandar of Perak, bypassing Maxwell and the Residents in direct consultation [[1, pp. 16–17]](#ref-1). By late 1924, with Whitehall's confidence in Maxwell eroded, Guillemard mounted a decisive offensive: he proposed a radical decentralisation programme that would effectively abolish the Chief Secretaryship, and he secured the premature retirement of Maxwell, who was ordered to retire on 17 March 1925, two years before his normal retirement age [[1, pp. 18–22]](#ref-1).

## Legacy and Documentation

The principal scholarly treatment of Guillemard's tenure is Yeo Kim Wah's 1981 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, which reconstructs the power struggle from Colonial Office despatches, the Milner Papers, and Selangor Secretariat Files [[1]](#ref-1). Yeo's analysis foregrounds the personal and political dimensions of the Chief Secretary–High Commissioner relationship, demonstrating how the 1920s financial crisis and the question of decentralisation were inextricable from the interpersonal dynamics at the top of the Malayan administration [[1]](#ref-1). The article fills a gap in the literature on FMS constitutional development by showing that the Guillemard–Maxwell conflict was "more bitter, strident, and wide-ranging than any that befell their predecessors or successors" [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1).
