# Main peteri: synopses of three shamanistic performances

Carol Laderman's 1987 article presents detailed ethnographic synopses of three *Main Peteri* shamanistic seances observed in Trengganu and Kelantan during the 1970s and early 1980s, arguing that the ritual embeds a sophisticated psychotherapeutic theory of personality—centered on "Inner Winds" (*angin*)—that challenges the prevailing scholarly assumption that shamanistic healing operates solely through external projection of illness onto spirits (pp. 55–57).

## Summary

Laderman situates her work within a long historiographical tradition stretching from British colonial observers (W. Maxwell 1881, G. Maxwell 1907) through early folklore scholars (Skeat 1900, Annandale and Robinson 1903–04), Winstedt's search for Hindu and Sufi substrata (1951 [1925]), Cuisinier's landmark study of the ritual's dramatic and symbolic dimensions (1936), and Firth's treatment of *Main Peteri* as social drama (1957). She identifies a persistent flaw in this literature: the tendency to reduce the seance to either entertainment, social commentary, or a form of "remission without insight" that merely displaces a patient's problems onto external entities such as spirits and ghosts (pp. 55–57). Drawing on the work of Kiev, Devereux, and Frank, she notes that scholars have located the essential difference between biomedical and shamanistic psychotherapy in the latter's reliance on external projection rather than internal self-understanding.

Laderman's core argument, developed through three detailed performance synopses, is that this binary is false. Embedded within the *Main Peteri* is a mode of trance-healing in which the shaman helps patients confront their own personality, conceptualized as powerful Inner Winds representing talents, drives, and creative energies. If these Winds blow freely, the individual leads a healthy life; if they are thwarted and shut up within the breast, they produce emotional and physical illness (p. 57). The three synopses illustrate a spectrum: the first ("Breaking Contracts with the Spirit World") is a straightforward exorcism; the second ("Seance for a Sick Shaman") is primarily exorcistic but carries implications of Inner Wind damage; the third ("A Stifled Talent") is fundamentally a case of thwarted Inner Winds compounded by spirit interference (p. 57).

The synopses are grounded in Laderman's participant observation, conducted under the auspices of the Institute for Medical Research in Trengganu, where she was informally adopted by the shaman Pak Long Awang bin Ali and became a regular attendant at his performances (p. 56). The article thus provides rare access to the full arc of a *Main Peteri*—from elaborate preparations and invocations through spirit dialogues, divinations, bargaining, and the final release rituals—captured in the colloquial, often bawdy register in which the spirits actually speak (pp. 55–56).

### Key Findings

- In "Breaking Contracts with the Spirit World," the patient Mat Din had made pacts with sea spirits (Panglima Hitam, Panglima Garang), earth spirits (Jin Tanah, Jembalang Tanah), demigods up to Nenek Betara Guru, and the entire *Wayang Jawa* shadow-play lineage; the seance required physically transporting offerings—fried corn, beans, rice, rice-flour figurines of animals and people, and a letter—into the undergrowth, where men hammered nails into a piece of wood while reciting binding formulas (pp. 57–61).
- In "Seance for a Sick Shaman," popped-rice (*bertih*) divination revealed that Pak Long Awang's migratory pains were caused by earth spirits angered when a patient's family failed to deliver promised offerings after a prior *Main Peteri*; the spirit threatened to free the family's trained coconut-gathering monkey as a first act of retribution (pp. 62–63).
- In "A Stifled Talent," retired *dalang* Toki Latip had been housebound with severe asthma for three years; divination attributed his illness to his unexpressed Inner Wind for the shadow play, the spirits of his stored puppets (Pak Dogol and Wak Long), earth spirits, and his birth-brother (placenta); the *Hantu Raya* promised recovery within seven days, which was fulfilled when Laderman observed him bicycling to the seashore (pp. 63–67).
- The therapeutic mechanism of the *Main Peteri* involves the *minduk*'s Bestirring Song (*Gerak Angin*), which evokes the shaman's hereditary Inner Winds to induce trance, and the subsequent invocation of the patient's own Winds to allow expression of pent-up emotions—for example, Latip performing *silat* in trance and manipulating his puppets with restored grace (pp. 62, 66–67).
- The ceremony concludes with the use of *pelepas* (palm-frond strips tied in double slipknots), which the shaman unties while reciting "One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, you are released," thereby freeing all participants from the spiritual vulnerability inherent in dealings with the spirit world (pp. 61, 71).
- *Main Peteri* is rarely undertaken before patients have exhausted other remedies including dietary adjustments, herbal treatments, incantations, massage, and biomedical consultation; the ritual is expensive when it employs multiple shamans, a full orchestra, and audience refreshments (p. 56).

### Conclusion

Laderman's definitive takeaway is that the *Main Peteri* constitutes a coherent indigenous psychotherapeutic system in which the shaman, using the power of words and the patient's own personality as the operative force, helps individuals express thwarted drives and talents in trance, thereby restoring health. The three synopses demonstrate that exorcism and Inner Wind therapy are not mutually exclusive but exist on a continuum, with most cases involving both, and that the ritual's apparent "irrationality" conceals a rational therapeutic logic that parallels—and in some respects exceeds—the self-insight offered by Western psychotherapy (pp. 56–57, 67).

## Context

- Fieldwork was conducted under the Institute for Medical Research, Malaysian Ministry of Health (1975–77), with subsequent visits (1982–85); Laderman was informally adopted by Pak Long Awang bin Ali, granting her sustained access to performances ranging from private rituals to large public healing ceremonies (p. 56).
- Historiographically, the article challenges the dominant transcultural psychiatry framework (Kiev, Devereux, Frank) that positions shamanistic healing as inferior to Western psychotherapy by demonstrating that the *Main Peteri* contains an indigenous theory of personality operating on the patient's own psyche rather than merely projecting problems onto external entities (pp. 56–57).
