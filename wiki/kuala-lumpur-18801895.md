# Kuala Lumpur, 1880–1895

A.M.S. published this memoir of Captain Francis Light in 1895, on the centenary of Light's death, to recover the personal and administrative record of the man who founded Penang in 1786. The author's overarching thesis is that Light was a singularly capable pioneer whose eight years of solitary governance established a settlement whose commercial and strategic merits were only fully vindicated by the East India Company's own despatch of 1805, a decade after his death.

## Summary

The memoir reconstructs Light's career from his first correspondence with Warren Hastings in 1771 through his death in October 1794, drawing on Light's own diary, private letters, and official despatches preserved in Logan's Journal and the British Museum. The narrative is organised thematically rather than strictly chronologically: the selection of Penang over the earlier Junk Ceylon scheme, the physical establishment of the settlement in the jungle, the governance of a mixed population of Malays, Chinese, Chulias and Europeans without a proper judicial system, the financial difficulties of a colony that the Bengal Government regarded with suspicion, and the political controversy over whether the Raja of Kedah had the competence to cede the island.

A substantial portion of the memoir is devoted to defending Light's judgment in negotiating directly with Kedah rather than seeking Siamese consent. The author marshals Colonel Low's own admissions in Logan's Journal to demonstrate that the "suzerainty" question was a later invention of the 1840s political controversy, and that in 1786 the relevant geopolitical factor was Dutch hostility, not Siamese overlordship. The memoir also corrects the persistent myth (repeated even in Balfour's *Encyclopædia of India*) that Penang was given as a marriage portion, identifying Light's wife as Martina Rozells, a Portuguese Christian from the Roman Catholic mission.

The author uses the 1805 Court of Directors' despatch establishing the Presidency Government as the definitive contemporary verdict on Light's work, and closes with the land-grant controversy of 1794–96 to illustrate how Light's local political courage saved the settlement from a restrictive policy that would have stifled its growth.

### Key Findings

- Light arrived at Penang on 15 July 1786 with 100 native marines, 30 lascars, 15 European artillerymen, and 5 officers; formal possession was taken on 11 August 1786 (pp. 2–4).
- The settlement's population reached approximately 10,000 by the end of 1789 and over 20,000 by 1795, of whom the Chinese numbered not much over 3,000 (p. 4).
- Light's salary as Superintendent was Rs. 1,000 per month; he proposed in 1790 that he be precluded from trading in exchange for an increased salary sufficient to "support the office with decency" (p. 7).
- In 1791, Light's force of 400 men defeated a Kedah force that had swelled to over 8,000 Malays at Kuala Pry; Penang has not been attacked by any enemy since (p. 7).
- The first *ad valorem* customs duty (2 per cent on tin, pepper and betel-nut) was levied in 1801 and produced $13,076 that year; Penang remained a customs port until 1826 (pp. 5, 14).
- The Governor-General's resolution of 1 August 1794 limiting land grants to five-year leases was rescinded on 22 August 1796, after Light's death, when the immediate successor Mannington took the same view that publication would "instantly stop all further advance" (pp. 14–15).

### Conclusion

The author's definitive takeaway is that Light's eight years of governance constitute "a truly successful record of what British courage and perseverance, local experience amounting to adroitness, and a large-minded sense of public duty can achieve, even when almost unsupported" (p. 16). The memoir positions Light as the worthy forerunner of Raffles, whose Singapore was founded on "very similar lines" thirty-five years later, and argues that the settlement's subsequent prosperity is the most convincing vindication of the founder's judgment.

## Context

- The author signs only "A.M.S." and dates the piece "Penang, 21st October, 1894" (the centenary of Light's death), indicating a contemporary Straits resident writing for the JMBRAS rather than a professional historian.
- The memoir draws primarily on Logan's Journal (Vols. III–V), Light's diary, and the 1805 Court of Directors' despatch; its historiographical contribution lies in its early and sustained defence of Light's political judgment against the Colonel Low school of Siamese suzerainty, a debate that would occupy Straits historiography for decades.
