# Kota Tampan, Perak. Geological and archaeological evidence for a late Pleistocene site. Zuraina Majid and H.D. Tjia

Zuraina Majid and H.D. Tjia (1988) present the results of their 1987 excavations at Kota Tampan, Perak, arguing that the site is an undisturbed late Upper Pleistocene stone tool workshop dated to approximately 31,000 BP, rather than the Middle Pleistocene marine terrace site proposed by Walker and Sieveking in 1962. Their work resolves a decade-long controversy over the site's validity and dating, which had threatened to reduce the known time depth of Peninsular Malaysian prehistory to no more than 8,000 years.

## Summary

The article addresses a critical problem in Southeast Asian prehistory: the status of Kota Tampan as the only accepted Palaeolithic site in the Malay Peninsula. Since the 1970s, multiple scholars (Harrison, Verstappen, Shutler, Hutterer, Bellwood) had questioned the original Middle Pleistocene dating and the interpretation of the artifacts as in-situ, which if accepted would have pushed the earliest known human presence in the peninsula back to the Neolithic site of Gua Cha, Kelantan, at roughly 8,000 years ago. Majid and Tjia undertook new fieldwork to test both the archaeological integrity of the site and its geological context.

Their excavation revealed a dense accumulation of quartzite cores, flakes, chips, and small boulders with battered surfaces functioning as anvils. The spatial relationships between artifacts—conjoinable flakes and cores, the proximity of debitage to anvils, and the sharp (untransported) edges of the quartzite—demonstrated that the material was deposited in situ and constituted a lithic production area. A quantitative analysis of flake tool-to-debitage ratios further confirmed a systematic production pattern. The authors argue this evidence decisively refutes the earlier claim that the tools had been river-transported and concentrated by natural processes.

On the geological front, Tjia's geomorphological analysis rejects Walker's eustatic (sea-level) interpretation of the terrace gravel. Instead, the authors propose that the site lay on the shore of a natural palaeo-lake formed by large-scale landslides from Gunung Hong that dammed the Perak River. This palaeo-Cenderoh Lake was over 25 km long, approximately 68 sq km in area, with a general level between 70 and 75 metres above present sea level. The terraces, overfit valleys, and landslide scars in the area are all consistent with this lake model and inconsistent with marine transgression. The dating rests on petrographic identification of volcanic ash in the artifact-bearing and overlying soils, chemically matched to the last eruption of Lake Toba in Sumatra, which has been fission-track dated to 31,000 ± 3,000 BP. The authors suggest the eruption may have caused the sudden abandonment of the workshop, given the high proportion of unfinished tools and blanks.

### Key Findings

- The site is dated to approximately 31,000 ± 3,000 BP based on fission-track dating of Toba volcanic ash identified in the cultural and overlying deposits (Stauffer et al. 1980; p. 128).
- The palaeo-lake that formed the site's setting was over 25 km long, approximately 68 sq km in area, with a general level between 70 and 75 m above present sea level (p. 128).
- The palaeo-lake was formed by large-scale landslides from Gunung Hong (934 m), with three horse-shoe shaped scars on its west flank reaching 720 m in local relief (p. 132).
- The site contained thousands of sharp-edged quartzite flakes (hardness 7), conjoinable cores and flakes, and small boulders with battered anvil surfaces, all in undisturbed spatial association (pp. 125–126).
- The Kodiac caves of Kedah show relative sea-level shorelines of the same age at only 10 m above present, contradicting Walker's claim of a 71.63 m marine transgression (p. 133).
- The presence of a large proportion of unfinished tools and blanks suggests the Toba eruption may have caused sudden site abandonment (p. 128).

### Conclusion

Majid and Tjia conclude that Kota Tampan is a late Upper Pleistocene site with a radiometric date in the vicinity of 31,000 BP, firmly rejecting both Sieveking's Middle Pleistocene date and Walker's eustatic interpretation of the gravel formation. The terraces and gravel accumulations in the Lenggong area are products of palaeo-lake fluctuations, not late Quaternary sea-level changes. This re-dating, while later than the original claim, still establishes a human presence in the peninsula roughly 30,000 years ago—significantly earlier than the Neolithic sites that would otherwise represent the earliest evidence.

## Context

- The study draws on the authors' own 1987 excavation seasons at Kota Tampan, petrographic analysis of soil samples, and geomorphological fieldwork across the Lenggong area, supplemented by earlier radiometric and fission-track data from Stauffer (1973, 1980).
- The article represents a significant historiographical intervention in Southeast Asian Palaeolithic studies, resolving a long-standing dispute and providing the first secure absolute date for a Palaeolithic site in Peninsular Malaysia.
