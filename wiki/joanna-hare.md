# Joanna Hare

The archival record on Joanna Hare is limited to a single Dutch-language letter dated 16 August 1820, preserved in the Malacca Church Records, and the scholarly interpretation of that letter by C.A. Gibson-Hill in 1954. No other primary documents concerning her life, origins, or later years have been identified in the Society's literature.

## Identity and the 1820 Letter

The sole primary source for Joanna Hare is a letter written in Dutch, dated 16 August 1820, found in the Malacca Church Records (Vol. 32, ff. 17 & 18), in which a woman signing herself "Johanna van Hare" petitions the Church Council for confirmation in the faith [[1, p. 186]](#ref-1). In the letter she states that she receives a monthly allowance from "Mr Hare," that she is separated from him, and that she has never lived with him as a concubine in the present [[1, p. 186]](#ref-1). Gibson-Hill, who translated the letter with the assistance of Dr. P. S. de Josselin de Jong, argues persuasively that she was the acknowledged mistress of Alexander Hare during his Malacca residence and the mother of his only recognised son, Alexander Hare junior [[1, p. 186]](#ref-1).

The younger Alexander Hare died at Batavia on 12 January 1834, aged twenty-seven, placing his birth at approximately 1807; the Java Register entry notes that his mother's name was not known [[1, p. 185]](#ref-1). This absence of a maternal name in the official register is consistent with the social position of a woman who, while publicly acknowledged by her partner's name, did not hold the status of a wife. Gibson-Hill's identification of Johanna van Hare as the child's mother rests on the convergence of the letter's date, the child's approximate birth year, and the fact that no other woman is attested in Hare's Malacca household [[1, pp. 185–186]](#ref-1).

## Domestic Context at Malacca

Gibson-Hill situates Johanna van Hare's relationship with Hare within the conventions of early British colonial society at Malacca. Hare, who reached Malacca from Calcutta in or before 1807, was, according to John Clunies Ross, the only English merchant in the settlement during 1807–11 [[1, p. 184]](#ref-1). His domestic arrangements, Gibson-Hill argues, were conventional by the standards of the period: the Resident, William Farquhar, himself had kept a mistress and four recognised children, and nothing in Hare's private life at that stage would have caused his friend Sir Stamford Raffles any social uneasiness [[1, p. 186]](#ref-1).

The letter of 1820, however, marks a rupture. By that date Hare had been expelled from the Netherlands Indies (the Dutch order bearing the date 5 March 1819) and had reached Bencoolen aboard the *Borneo* in early April 1820, sailing for the Cape of Good Hope at the end of that month [3, p. 187, n. 10]. Johanna van Hare's petition for confirmation—seeking formal entry into the Protestant faith while separated from Hare—suggests a woman attempting to secure her own religious and social standing independently of her former partner's fortunes [[1, p. 186]](#ref-1).

## The Broader Hare Narrative

Gibson-Hill's 1954 article is part of an ongoing series on Alexander Hare that began with the 1952 documentary compilation on the Cocos-Keeling Islands settlement [[1, p. 224]](#ref-1). Within that wider narrative, Johanna van Hare's letter serves a specific historiographical purpose: it demonstrates that Hare's conduct at Malacca was unremarkable, thereby sharpening the contrast with his later behaviour at Banjermassin, where he maintained a harem and received presents of girl slaves from neighbouring sultans, and at the Cocos-Keeling Islands (1826–31), where his manner of living was so scandalous that the editor of a Calcutta journal omitted parts of the official report [[1, p. 188]](#ref-1).

The article also notes that Lady Raffles, in compiling the *Memoir*, silently excised the paragraphs of Raffles's 1813 Batavia address that complimented Hare's work in Borneo, and that the only party still publicly seeking Hare's explanation at the time of writing was the East India Company's auditing department, which had been waiting for an account of a deficiency of Java Rupee 556,770.4 in the final Banjermassin settlement accounts [[1, pp. 190–191]](#ref-1). Johanna van Hare's letter, by establishing the ordinary character of Hare's earlier domestic life, implicitly challenges the sanitised account of his career presented in the *Memoir* [[1, p. 191]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The documentation of Joanna Hare is confined to a single primary source—the 1820 Dutch letter in the Malacca Church Records—and its scholarly treatment by Gibson-Hill in 1954. No further biographical details, no records of her death, and no subsequent correspondence have been identified in the Society's literature. The letter's discovery and translation by Dr. P. S. de Josselin de Jong, and its contextualisation by Gibson-Hill, represent the entirety of the published record. Gibson-Hill's access to the Clunies Ross family papers on Cocos in 1941 was limited by a locked room in Oceania House, said to contain early family correspondence, which was sealed after the death of George Clunies-Ross and whose contents were subsequently sent to Colombo, where their whereabouts remain unknown [[2, pp. 7–8]](#ref-2). Whether that sealed collection contains any material bearing on Johanna van Hare is, at present, unknown. The limits of the record are thus absolute: she is known to us only through a single act of self-petition, preserved in a church register, and the interpretive framework that Gibson-Hill constructed around it.
