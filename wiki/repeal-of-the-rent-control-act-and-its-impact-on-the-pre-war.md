# Repeal of the Rent Control Act and its impact on the pre-war shop-houses in Georgetown, Malaysia

Mohammad Abdul Mohit and Mohd Bashir Sulaiman (2006) examine how the 1997 repeal of Malaysia's Control of Rent Act triggered a wave of conservation-unfriendly renovations and demolitions among pre-war shophouses in Georgetown, Penang, arguing that the Act's accidental role as a preservation mechanism was far more significant to the built heritage than its intended economic function.

## Summary

The article explores the paradox that a piece of economic legislation—the Control of Rent Act 1966 (Act 56)—served as the de facto conservation instrument for Malaysia's pre-war built heritage. By fixing rents on premises built before 31 January 1948, the Act removed the financial incentive for landlords to redevelop or demolish their properties, thereby preserving thousands of shophouses by default. When the Malaysian Government repealed the Act on 1 September 1997, the resulting deregulation unleashed powerful market forces—unregulated rentals, attractive investment returns, and rising property values—that rapidly transformed the urban fabric of Georgetown's historic core (pp. 107–109).

The authors focus on Noordin Street, a 1-kilometre stretch in the Seven Streets Precinct of MPPP's Conservation Zone-1, as their case study. Drawing on a field survey of 122 pre-war shophouses conducted in 2003, Rent Tribunal records from the Majlis Perbandaran Pulau Pinang, and interviews with both owner and tenant occupiers, they document the scale and character of post-repeal renovations. The evidence reveals a pattern in which commercial and office premises were renovated far more aggressively than residential units, and in which the most intensive improvements were those that most compromised the original architectural character of the buildings (pp. 117–119).

A critical finding is that the local authority's conservation framework was too weak to counterbalance the market pressures released by deregulation. The MPPP had identified eight conservation incentive measures—including fast-track plan processing, Transfer of Development Rights, and maintenance grants—but these were not properly implemented. The authors conclude that the combination of rent deregulation and institutional weakness created a window in which heritage loss accelerated faster than conservation policy could respond (pp. 117, 120).

### Key Findings

- The Control of Rent Act 1966 covered premises built before 31 January 1948; Pulau Pinang held 5,057 pre-war buildings (24.3% of the national total), of which Georgetown alone accounted for 4,336 units (85.7%) (pp. 112–113).
- From the repeal date to 31 March 2002, approximately 1,537 appeal cases were reported to the Rent Tribunal; only 20% (307 cases) were settled, while 40% (621 cases) were unresolved and 40% (621 cases) resulted in eviction (p. 115).
- In Noordin Street specifically, 17 shophouses were demolished, 9 tenants were evicted, and 5 rental hikes led to eventual eviction, out of 28 total appeal cases recorded by the MPPP Rent Tribunal (p. 116).
- Of the 122 shophouses surveyed, 82 (67.2%) had undergone some form of renovation; residential units saw the lowest renovation rate (37%), while offices, electronics shops, and retail units reached 100% (p. 118).
- Facade improvements were carried out on only 8 premises (6.5%), indicating that most renovations were interior-focused and conservation-unfriendly in character (p. 117).
- Rental increases in the selected case studies ranged from 20% to 40%, with property values recorded between RM125,000 and RM500,000 (p. 119).

### Conclusion

The authors argue that the repeal of the Rent Control Act, combined with the MPPP's failure to implement its existing conservation incentives, created conditions under which heritage loss outpaced policy response. They recommend that the local authority pursue an adaptive conservation strategy—area-based, selective, and directed at enhancing the re-use of shophouses—developed through mutual dialogue with property owners, rather than relying on strict enforcement of planning measures that would take too long to build the necessary administrative capacity (p. 120).

## Context

- Primary data sources: MPPP Rent Tribunal records (1999–2002), a field survey of Noordin Street (2003), and the 1992/93 Inventory of Malaysian Heritage Buildings.
- Historiographical contribution: One of the first systematic empirical studies to quantify the impact of rent deregulation on heritage conservation in Malaysia, bridging the gap between legal-policy analysis and on-the-ground architectural evidence.
