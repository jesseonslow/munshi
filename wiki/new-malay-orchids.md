# New Malay orchids

H. N. Ridley, a leading colonial-era botanist working on the *Flora of the Malay Peninsula*, published this taxonomic paper in 1903 describing forty new orchid species collected primarily from the Malay Peninsula and secondarily from Sumatra. The article extends the orchid inventory of the region beyond the 530 species in 87 genera already catalogued in his earlier *Orchids of the Malay Peninsula* (1889), and it draws heavily on field collections made by assistants and local collectors across Perak, Selangor, Penang, Johor, Kedah, Pahang, and the Lankawi Islands.

## Summary

Ridley's paper is a straightforward species-description monograph covering new taxa across seventeen genera, including *Liparis*, *Dendrobium*, *Bulbophyllum*, *Dendrochilum*, *Eria*, *Ceratostylis*, *Calanthe*, *Coelogyne*, *Saccolabium*, *Ascochilus*, *Sarcochilus*, *Podochilus*, *Zeuxine*, *Goodyera*, *Hetoeria*, *Platyclinis*, and *Ceratostylis*. Each entry provides a concise morphological description of vegetative and floral characters, a locality citation with collector attribution, and a brief comparative note linking the new species to its nearest known relative. The geographic spread is wide: the majority of specimens come from Perak (particularly the Thaiping Hills and Bujong Malacca), but Ridley also records plants from the Lankawi Islands, the Kwala Lumpur Caves in Selangor, Gunong Banang in Johor, Kedah Peak, and the Tahan River valley in Pahang.

The article reveals the state of orchid collecting in the early 1900s, with Ridley relying on a network of collectors—Curtis, Derry, Goldham, Wray, Kelsall, Barnes, Sir Graeme Elphinstone, A. D. Machado, and Aeria—whose fieldwork supplied the type specimens. Several species were identified only after flowering in the Penang or Singapore Botanic Gardens, underscoring the dependence of tropical taxonomy on cultivation for reproductive characters. Ridley explicitly notes that the orchid flora of Sumatra remains "really very little known" despite long-standing export of showy species, and he flags the northern districts and eastern hill ranges of the Peninsula as likely to yield further discoveries.

### Key Findings

- The Malay Peninsula orchid flora stood at 530 species in 87 genera at the time of writing, with Ridley anticipating many more from northern districts and eastern hill ranges (p. 71).
- Forty new species are described across seventeen genera; *Bulbophyllum* (8 species) and *Saccolabium* (6 species) are the most heavily represented (pp. 71–87).
- *Liparis atrosanguinea* was collected at 4,000 feet elevation on the Thaiping Hills, Perak (Curtis and Derry), and is noted as "a really beautiful plant" with deep red-purple flowers (p. 71).
- *Calanthe mutabilis* from Deli, Sumatra, exhibits a striking colour change in the lip from white-yellow to dull dark orange-red over one or two days, and produces branched scapes in two of three cultivated plants (p. 79).
- *Dendrochilum ellipticum* from Singapore is identified as the only low-country species in its genus, the remainder being mountain plants (p. 77).
- *Saccolabium (Cleisostoma) hortense* is recorded from five localities across the Peninsula (Singapore, Johor, Malacca, Perak, Penang) and Borneo, and Ridley remarks he "cannot think how it has escaped being described for so long" (p. 83).

### Conclusion

Ridley's paper is a product of the imperial botanical infrastructure of the Straits Settlements and the Malay states, in which a single taxonomist in Penang could describe forty new species in one paper by drawing on a distributed collecting network. Its lasting value lies in the morphological descriptions and locality data, which remain the primary reference for several of these taxa, and in its implicit argument that the Peninsula's orchid diversity—particularly in the less-explored northern and eastern highlands—was far from exhausted.

## Context

- Ridley was serving in the colonial botanical establishment (Penang Botanic Gardens) and was actively compiling the *Flora of the Malay Peninsula*; the paper is framed as a supplement to his 1889 *Orchids of the Malay Peninsula* in the *Journal of the Linnean Society*.
- The article relies on the Scortechini herbarium drawings (e.g., drawing 176 for *B. brevipes*, drawing 53 for *S. hortense*) and on living material flowered in the Penang and Singapore Botanic Gardens, reflecting the standard colonial-era workflow of field collection followed by cultivation-based description.
