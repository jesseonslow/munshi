# Hamzah Fansuri

Hamzah Fansuri was a Sufi poet and mystic of the sixteenth century whose works constitute a foundational corpus of classical Malay Islamic literature. His identity is inseparable from two places: Barus (Fansur), on the west coast of North Sumatra, which he claims as his ancestral origin, and Shahr Nawi (Ayutthaya, Siam), which philological evidence increasingly points to as his actual birthplace [[1, pp. 42–43]](#ref-1). His poetry, preserved in several Leiden and Jakarta manuscripts, engages with Sufi cosmology, polemics against heterodox ritual, and the intricate literary conventions inherited from Persian and Arabic mystical traditions [[2, pp. 73–98]](#ref-2). The question of his life and work has attracted sustained scholarly attention, with significant debates over his biography, the authenticity of his verses, and the interpretation of his most difficult passages [[1, pp. 42–51]](#ref-1), [[2, pp. 73–98]](#ref-2).

## Place of Birth and Ancestral Origin

The long-accepted assumption that Hamzah was born in Barus rests on the fact that *Fansuri* (of Fansur) forms part of his name, and that Barus was identical with the earlier Arabic name Fansur [[1, p. 42]](#ref-1). Syed Naguib al-Attas challenged this assumption in 1967 by demonstrating that Hamzah consistently refers to himself as being from both Barus and Shahr Nawi, and that the distinction between these two designations carries precise meaning [[1, pp. 42–43]](#ref-1). In the *Sharabu'l-'Ashiqin*, Hamzah calls himself *al-Mudunayn* (of the two cities), a sobriquet that Doorenbos had conjectured to be a mistaken form of *al-Haramayn* (of the two holy cities, Makkah and Madinah) [[1, p. 43]](#ref-1). Al-Attas argued that Doorenbos was incorrect: the form should read *al-Madinatayn* and refers to Barus and Shahr Nawi, since the tradition of linking one's name to a place typically denotes birthplace, and Hamzah had visited Makkah and Madinah without adopting their names [[1, p. 43]](#ref-1).

The critical verse reads: "Hamzah nin asalnya Fansuri, / Mendapat wujud ditanah Shahr Nawi" (Hamzah is originally of Fansur; he acquired his existence in the land of Shahr-i-Naw) [[1, p. 46]](#ref-1). Al-Attas interprets *asalnya* (his origin) as denoting ancestral rather than natal origin, while *mendapat wujud* (acquired existence) refers to his physical birth into the world [[1, pp. 46–47]](#ref-1). A further verse, "Hamzah Shahr Nawi zahirnya Jawi" (Hamzah of Shahr-i-Naw is born a Malay), reinforces the connection between Shahr Nawi and his physical manifestation [[1, p. 47]](#ref-1). Al-Attas also notes that Hamzah frequently styles himself *gharib* (stranger or emigre), a term that in its non-mystical sense conveys the idea of one whose native country is foreign, consistent with a person born abroad who later settled in Barus [[1, p. 45]](#ref-1).

L.F. Brakel, writing in 1979, situated Barus within its long history as a cosmopolitan entrepôt attested from the second century (Ptolemy's "Barusai") through the sixteenth century (Sidi Ali Celebi, 1554), with a Tamil inscription from 1088 discovered at Lobu Tuwa confirming a permanent Tamil colony [[2, pp. 89–92]](#ref-2). Brakel identified Barus as Hamza's likely place of birth, though his treatment of the question is less detailed than al-Attas's [[2, pp. 89–96]](#ref-2).

## Chronology and the Question of Interpolation

Al-Attas established that Hamzah lived and flourished in the period preceding and during the reign of Sultan 'Ala'ud-Din Ri'ayat Shah of Aceh (1588–1604), and that he was most likely dead before 1607 [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1). This dating rests on the poem *Ikat-ikatan 'ilmu'l-nisa'*, in which Hamzah addresses a "saintly king" identified as Shah 'Alam, the title of 'Ala'ud-Din Ri'ayat Shah, who was also known in Acehnese tradition as *Sayyid al-Mukammal* [[1, pp. 48–49]](#ref-1). A complication arises from the same poem's mention of Mahkota 'Alam, the title of Iskandar Muda, who reigned from 1607 to 1636 [[1, p. 49]](#ref-1). Al-Attas argued that this reference is a later interpolation, noting that the stanza in question is incoherent as it stands, but that excising the bracketed phrase "ada ia khabar ditanah Jawa, dari Aceh marhum Mahkota 'Alam dihimpunkan" restores both the form and the spirit of the passage [[1, pp. 49–50]](#ref-1). He cited parallels in other Malay works, including the *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai*, the *Sejarah Melayu*, and Raniri's *Bustanu'l-Salatin*, which interpolates mention of a later sultan [[1, p. 49]](#ref-1).

## Poetic Craft and Mystical Imagery

Brakel's 1979 study addressed several technical aspects of Hamza's poetry. He identified a systematic phonological play on the word *zahir/lahir* in Hamza's rhyme scheme, arguing that the poet exploited the two distinct realizations of the initial phoneme /z/ in Malay to create a deliberate double-reading within single stanzas [[2, pp. 77–79]](#ref-2). The *taxallos*—the poet's self-identification in the closing stanza—functions both as a literary device borrowed from the Persian *gazal* tradition and as a crucial philological tool for authenticating Hamza's genuine verses against those of his epigones [[2, pp. 79–81]](#ref-2). Brakel noted that Hamza's imitators violate a negative structural rule he himself observed: in rhyme-words borrowed from Arabic, the rhyming vowels must retain the same quantity as in the original Arabic, a constraint his followers consistently fail to observe [[2, p. 80]](#ref-2).

On the question of Hamza's polemics against yogic practices, Brakel reinterpreted them not as attacks on Sufi "shamanistic virtuosities" (Kraemer's earlier reading) but as targeted critiques of a *pranayama* ritual still observable among Balinese Brahman priests [[2, pp. 73–77]](#ref-2). The specific elements Hamza denounces—localisation of the Divine in the fontanel (*ubun-ubun*), externalisation as a divine glow (*diya'*/cahaya), and breath-control (*menghela nafas kedalam hutak*)—correspond precisely to the Balinese *pranayama* cycle, implying that Hindu ritual elements survived in sixteenth-century Aceh [[2, pp. 74–76]](#ref-2).

Brakel also identified the source of Hamza's famous water-cycle imagery in the *Kitab Sarab al-'Ashiqin* as an adaptation of a passage in Faxr ad-Din 'Iraqi's *Lama'at* (third *Lama'a*, p. 380 in Nafsi's 1956 edition), while insisting that the poet's "internal landscape" of imagery is unmistakably Indonesian [[2, pp. 92–96]](#ref-2).

A numismatic detail in al-Attas's study provides further evidence for Hamza's connections to Shahr Nawi. In the *Sharabu'l-'Ashiqin*, Hamza employs an analogy involving the Ashrafi dinar—a gold coin first struck in Mamluk Egypt on 28 December 1425 by al-Malik al-Ashraf Barsbay, which became the preferred gold coin in trade throughout the century [[1, pp. 50–51]](#ref-1). After its introduction to Persia, the name Ashrafi became the usual term for the native Persian gold coin [[1, p. 51]](#ref-1). Al-Attas argued that Hamza's familiarity with the Ashrafi could not have been derived from Barus or Aceh, where the Egyptian coin would no longer have been in use by his time, but only from Persians who used Persian Ashrafis in their trade at Shahr Nawi, substantiating the predominance of Persians in the Muslim trading community there [[1, pp. 50–51]](#ref-1).

## The Kitab al-Muntahi and the Question of Kufr

Brakel offered a new reading of a notoriously difficult passage in the *Kitab al-Muntahi* involving the Pasai saying on *kufr* and *kufu'* [[2, pp. 85–89]](#ref-2). He argued against both Voorhoeve and al-Attas that the passage is self-contained when read in full, rather than truncated as ar-Raniri had quoted it [[2, pp. 86–88]](#ref-2). The full passage continues with a seed-and-tree analogy and the *Ana'l-Haqq* declaration, which Brakel contends makes the meaning self-evident: "being hidden" is a *conditio sine qua non* for mystical encounter rather than an obstacle to be removed [[2, pp. 86–88]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The scholarly investigation of Hamzah Fansuri within the Society's journal spans more than a decade, with distinct methodological emphases. Al-Attas's 1967 article represents an early example of his method of combining Sufi metaphysical exegesis with philological textual criticism to resolve biographical questions in classical Malay literature, an approach that would become characteristic of his later work on Hamzah and the Malay Sufi tradition [[1, p. 42]](#ref-1). His 1966 article on Raniri and the *Wujudiyyah* of seventeenth-century Aceh provides the broader polemical context in which Hamzah's works were received and contested [[3, p. 153]](#ref-3). Brakel's 1969 short notice on Hamza's birth [[4, pp. 206–212]](#ref-4) and his substantially expanded 1979 study [[2, pp. 73–98]](#ref-2) mark a shift toward closer attention to poetic craft, textual authentication, and the cross-cultural origins of Hamzah's imagery. Brakel's work draws primarily on the Jakarta manuscript ML 82 (KBG), which he regards as the most reliable witness to Hamza's poetry, collated against Doorenbos's 1933 edition and al-Attas's 1970 monograph [[2, pp. 73–98]](#ref-2). Historiographically, the 1979 article positions itself between Kraemer's 1921 Leiden thesis and al-Attas's monograph, correcting specific interpretive errors in both while laying groundwork for a critical edition [[2, pp. 73–98]](#ref-2). The primary textual evidence across these studies consists of three Leiden manuscripts—Cod. Or. 2016, Cod. Or. 3374, and Cod. Or. 7291 (i and ii)—held in the Bibliotheca Academiae Lugduno-Batavae [[1, pp. 43–50]](#ref-1).
