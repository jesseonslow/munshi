# Report on Malay studies

C. O. Blagden, Reader in Malay at the University of London, delivered this report in 1921 (read at the joint session of Orientalist Societies in Paris, July 1920) surveying the state of British-led Malay scholarship in the early 1920s. The overarching thesis is that British administrative expansion in the Malay Peninsula had catalysed a new, systematic phase of Malay studies, driven by the Committee for Malay Studies in the Federated Malay States and the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, and that this work could only reach its full potential through integration with broader Indianist and Islamic scholarship.

## Summary

Blagden frames the emergence of organised Malay studies as a response to an administrative problem: as British control over the Peninsula expanded, junior Government officers were no longer isolated in districts where they were the sole European, and the old practice of each officer independently acquiring local knowledge had become inadequate to the needs of governance. The solution was institutional — the creation of a Committee for Malay Studies in the Federated Malay States, which would encourage and coordinate the systematic collection and publication of information on Malay life, customs, history, language, and literature. Blagden credits R. J. Wilkinson (then a Malay scholar and later Governor of Sierra Leone) as the principal intellectual force behind the movement, noting that Wilkinson's 1906 pamphlet *Malay Beliefs* on Malay religion and folklore was intended as the first of a comprehensive monograph series on the Malays of the Peninsula but became the last work he personally produced before the Committee took over the programme.

The Committee's output fell into two main streams. The first was a series of monographs styled "Papers on Malay Subjects," covering Malay history, literature, law, life, customs, industries, the aboriginal tribes of the Peninsula, and the individual histories and constitutions of several Malay states; more than half were written by Wilkinson himself, with Dr. R. O. Winstedt as the most distinguished collaborator. The second was the "Malay Literature Series," which from 1906 onwards issued Malay texts in the Roman character — a deliberate policy to overcome the barrier that the Arabic script (hallowed by religious prestige but phonetically ill-suited to Malay) posed to the non-Muslim population. Blagden also records the continued output of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, founded in 1877, whose Journal had by 1921 reached 81 numbers and which had published important texts including the *Seri Rama*, a Malay prose recension of the *Ramayana* printed in 1916 from an early 17th-century manuscript in the Bodleian Library.

Blagden's broader argument is one of interdisciplinary necessity. He insists that Malay studies cannot be properly conducted in isolation from the two great civilisational forces that shaped the Malay world: Indian civilisation, which influenced the Malay race for at least a thousand years up to the close of the 14th century, and Islam, which replaced Indian influence as the dominant ideal. He appeals to Indianists and Islamic scholars at the Paris meeting to recognise Malay studies as a valuable case study of how their own systems were applied, adapted, and blended with local customs on an alien population.

### Key Findings

- The Committee for Malay Studies was established in the Federated Malay States in the mid-1910s (described as "a few years ago" from the 1920 reading), with Wilkinson as its leader and driving force (p. 31).
- Wilkinson's *Malay Beliefs* (1906) was the first monograph in the series and the last he personally authored; the Committee subsequently issued monographs on Malay History, Literature and Law, Malay Life, Customs and Industries, and the Aboriginal Tribes of the Peninsula (p. 31).
- The Malay Literature Series, begun in 1906, had issued 15 texts in the Roman character by 1921, including the classical *Sejarah Melayu*, *Hang Tuah*, writings of Abdullah bin Abdulkadir, and previously unpublished traditional and modern texts (pp. 32–33).
- Winstedt's grammar of Malay was published by the Clarendon Press in 1913; he also produced an English-Malay dictionary based on Wilkinson's Malay-English dictionary and a history of the Malays in Malay (pp. 31–32).
- The *Seri Rama*, a Malay prose recension of the *Ramayana*, was printed in 1916 from an early 17th-century manuscript in the Bodleian Library, Oxford; Blagden notes that no existing Malay manuscript is much older than this one (p. 33).
- The Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, founded in 1877, had issued 81 numbers of its Journal by 1921, of which approximately 25 appeared in the preceding decade (p. 33).

### Conclusion

Blagden's definitive historical takeaway is that British Malay studies had entered a new era of systematic, coordinated scholarship that, while still small in scale compared to Indian or Chinese studies, was producing work of genuine scholarly value. His central prescription is interdisciplinary: Malay studies must be understood in relation to the Indian and Islamic civilisational currents that shaped it, and conversely, scholars of those broader fields should study the Malay case as a distinctive example of cultural transmission and adaptation. He frames this as part of the proper function of Orientalist societies — to "think in continents" and to link local scholarship with the wider field.

## Context

- Blagden held the chair of Reader in Malay at the University of London, and the report is explicitly framed as a survey of "the work done by British scholars," with Dutch and other non-British contributions acknowledged but excluded for reasons of scope (p. 30).
- The entire enterprise is presented in the idiom of colonial administration: the impetus for systematic Malay studies is "the interests of good administration," and the Roman-script policy is justified as removing a barrier to "the exchange of knowledge" within a cosmopolitan population governed by the British (pp. 30–32).
