# On a serow from Annam

G. L. Koster's 2005 article examines how seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Malay historiography portrayed the Dutch VOC as a means of defending the native political and religious order against the challenge posed by European intrusion. Drawing on three key texts—the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* (Johor, c. 1690), the *Syair Perang Mengkasar* (Goa-Tallo', c. 1670), and the *Misa Melayu* (Perak, c. 1760)—Koster argues that Malay court writers practised a form of literary 'damage control', using the strategy of maintaining literary propriety to neutralize the improprieties inherent in Dutch treaty-making and monopoly policies.

## Summary

Koster's central argument is that Malay historiography of this period functioned as a mechanism for preserving the ideological integrity of the *kerajaan* (kingdom) and Islam in the face of a foreign power whose very mode of operation—concluding written treaties that granted monopolies—was perceived as an infringement on royal sovereignty (*daulat*). The Dutch, unlike the Portuguese before them, were defined in the Malay literary imagination by two interlocking attributes: they were bearers of treaties and they were unbelievers (*kafir*). These two notions formed the core of the image that Malay courts constructed of them, and the historiographical texts Koster examines are all engaged in managing the narrative consequences of that image.

The three texts Koster reads comparatively each face a different configuration of the problem. The *Hikayat Hang Tuah* deals with a treaty voluntarily concluded by Johor in 1639, which brought substantial advantages but also required the cession of Malacca to the Dutch. The text handles this by portraying the alliance as a favour done to the impotent Dutch by mighty Johor, and by giving centre stage to the Johorese nobleman Tun Abdul Jamil in the storming of the fort. The *Syair Perang Mengkasar* confronts the far more painful situation of a kingdom defeated by military force and subjected to a treaty imposed by the VOC. Its damage control is more intensive: the Dutch attack is presented as an unprovoked aggression, the Sultan's decision to negotiate is framed as wise stewardship, and the ultimate defeat is attributed to treason within Macassarese ranks. The *Misa Melayu* presents the most ambivalent picture, depicting a kingdom that benefited greatly from its 1746 treaty with the VOC yet repeatedly staged symbolic demonstrations of royal supremacy over the Dutch to reassure its own court and the surrounding kingdoms of the Sultan's unimpaired *daulat*.

Throughout, Koster reads these texts against the intertextual backdrop of VOC archival sources and modern historical reconstructions, showing precisely where and how the Malay narratives diverge from the documented record. The divergences are not errors but deliberate rhetorical choices governed by the conventions of the genres involved—genealogical chronicle, heroic epic, and their hybrid forms—and by the political needs of the royal patrons who commissioned them.

### Key Findings

- The *Hikayat Hang Tuah* describes the 1639 Johor–Dutch treaty as being chiselled into gold foil and kept among the royal regalia, yet omits any mention of the Dutch monopolies and passes that were part of the agreement (pp. 10–11).
- The *Syair Perang Mengkasar* records that the Treaty of Bungaya (1667) required Macassar to pay compensation of fifty catties of gold and cede the fort at Unjung Pandang to the Dutch, who renamed it Fort Rotterdam (p. 21).
- The VOC fleet that attacked Macassar in 1666 comprised approximately 21 ships and 600 Dutch troops, together with Bugis contingents under Arung Palakka and Ambonese auxiliaries, under the command of Admiral Cornelis Janszoon Speelman (p. 12).
- The 1746 Perak–VOC treaty granted the Dutch a monopoly over the tin trade at a price of 30 Dutch reals per *bahara* of tin, with a toll of two reals, and gave them possession of Tanjung Putus where they built a fortified lodge (p. 23).
- The *Misa Melayu* records that during the 1753 crisis, the Dutch Commissioner requested to buy 300 *bahara* of tin but was granted 500, and that the treaty was subsequently reconfirmed by both parties in writing (p. 29).
- The *Syair Perang Mengkasar* explicitly states its propagandistic purpose: it was composed 'so that other kingdoms might hear what has happened' and so that 'all men of understanding will be in no doubt' about the Sultan's *daulat* (p. 17).

### Conclusion

Koster concludes that Malay historiography of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries consistently portrayed the Dutch through the dual lens of 'treaty' and 'unbeliever', and that the literary strategies employed to manage this image—praising one's own rulers, vilifying the enemy, and suppressing or deferring mention of the most painful improprieties—constitute a coherent form of 'damage control' aimed at preserving the ideological foundations of the *kerajaan* and Islam. The degree of intensity in this damage control varied according to the severity of the historical situation: mild in the case of Johor's voluntary treaty, severe in Macassar's case of military defeat, and moderate but persistent in Perak's case of uneasy coexistence.

## Context

- The study draws on the Skinner edition of the *Sja'ir Perang Mengkasar* (1963), the Kassim Ahmad edition of the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* (1994), the Ahmad Fauzi Mohd. Basri edition of the *Misa Melayu* (1992), and the Watson-Andaya edition of the *Bustan as-Salatin* (1992), read against VOC archival sources as synthesized in Andaya, Watson-Andaya, and van Goor.
- The article extends Koster's earlier study of Portuguese images in Malay historiography (2001) and contributes to the historiography of Malay literary culture by demonstrating how genre conventions and political patronage shaped the representation of foreign powers in court-composed texts.
