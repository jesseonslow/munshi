# Swettenham. Schemer and historian

H.S. Barlow, writing in 1996, examines Sir Frank Swettenham's dual role as both a maker of colonial history in the Malay Peninsula and its most persistent self-mythologising historian, arguing that Swettenham's carefully constructed narrative of British intervention in Malaya—sustained through edited diaries, successive publications, and newspaper letters over six decades—constituted a deliberate and remarkably successful act of historical revisionism that dominated the field until the 1960s.

## Summary

Barlow's central argument is that Swettenham was not merely a participant in the events of 1874–1875 but an active and continuous shaper of their historical record. By comparing Swettenham's early diary entries with his later published accounts, Barlow demonstrates a pattern of embellishment, omission, and selective recollection. The most striking example is the transformation of Raja Mahmood from a man with "anything but a prepossessing face" in a 1874 diary entry to a figure of "a fine open face" who was "extraordinarily alive" in the 1906 version—a shift Barlow attributes to Swettenham's later gratitude after Mahmood saved his life during the escape from Birch's murderers (p. 85). Similarly, the dramatic "light mist" that providentially concealed Swettenham's boat during his escape downriver appears in the authorised version but is contradicted by a late-life oral account in which a Malay woman hid him under sacks of copra (p. 85).

The article traces the evolution of what Barlow terms the "Swettenham doctrine" across five major publications, from *About Perak* (1893) through *Footprints in Malaya* (1942), showing how the narrative was refined but never fundamentally altered: British intervention was presented as a benevolent response to a local appeal for assistance, rather than as an imperial initiative driven by commercial and strategic interests. Barlow demonstrates that the Pangkor Engagement was far less consensual than Swettenham's accounts suggest—only two of the competing claimants attended, one was not even known to Singapore officials, and the atmosphere was one of intimidation (p. 87). The article also examines how Swettenham's personal animosities—toward W.E. Maxwell, Sultan Abu Bakar of Johore, and the Siamese—coloured his historical writing, leading to the complete erasure of Maxwell from his published works and to the fomenting of the *Daily Chronicle*'s vicious attack on Abu Bakar in 1895 (pp. 91–93).

Barlow further explores the mechanisms by which Swettenham maintained his monopoly on Malayan colonial history: editing his diaries, waiting for opponents to die before publishing definitive accounts, and exploiting the magazine *British Malaya* as a platform for continuous self-vindication. The article culminates in the irony of Swettenham's final public act—the "proconsuls' letter" of April 1946—where he opposed the Malayan Union to protect the constitutional role of the Sultans, the very role he had spent a career diminishing through the Pangkor Engagement and his own administrative practice (pp. 95–97).

### Key Findings

- Swettenham's 5 December 1874 diary entry describes Raja Mahmood as having "anything but a prepossessing face," while the 1906 published version recasts him as possessing "a fine open face" and being "extraordinarily alive"—a transformation Barlow links to Mahmood's later rescue of Swettenham (p. 85).
- At the Pangkor Engagement, only Sultan Abdullah and the Mantri attended of the competing claimants; Tunku Yusof was not invited because no Singapore official knew of his existence, and Ismail declined to appear, making the assembly "very unrepresentative" (p. 87).
- Swettenham negotiated a 25,000-acre concession of prime rubber land along the Johore railway trace, to the fury of the Colonial Office, apparently in exchange for fending off demands for a British Adviser in Johore (pp. 92–93).
- *British Malaya* (1906) remained the standard non-scholarly work on the Peninsula until the revisionist scholarship of Parkinson and Malaysian historians in the 1960s, being finally reprinted in 1948, two years after Swettenham's death (p. 91).
- The *Daily Chronicle* published an article in June 1895 describing the recently deceased Sultan Abu Bakar as "of plebeian birth" and "belonging to the realm of opera bouffe," with Swettenham's apparent connivance; a further article followed in December 1895 (p. 92).
- Swettenham's "proconsuls' letter" to *The Times* on 12 April 1946, written two months before his death at age 96, objected in the strongest terms to the means by which Sultans' signatures for the Malayan Union had been obtained (p. 95).

### Conclusion

Barlow's definitive takeaway is that Swettenham's historical legacy is an irreducible paradox: a man of genuine administrative achievement whose self-serving narrative successfully obscured the imperial origins of British intervention, erased the contributions of rivals, and diminished the constitutional role of Malay rulers—only for him to spend his final months defending that very role against further British encroachment. The "Swettenham doctrine" endured not because it was true but because it was repeated with such persistence and authority across so many decades that it became the accepted framework against which all subsequent scholarship had to argue.

## Context

- Barlow draws on Swettenham's diaries (published as *Malayan Journals, 1874–1876*), Selangor Secretariat files bearing Swettenham's annotations, Colonial Office correspondence, Raja Mahmood's Jawi account of the Perak War, and a parallel Siamese account of the 1896 meeting with King Chulalongkorn held in the Thai National Archives, Bangkok (p. 94).
- The article contributes to the historiographical debate between the "Swettenham school" and the revisionist tradition initiated by Parkinson (*British Intervention in Malaya, 1866–1877*, 1960) and Cowan (*Nineteenth-Century Malaya*, 1961), positioning Swettenham's own writings as primary evidence of deliberate myth-making rather than innocent retrospective memory.
