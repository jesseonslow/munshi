# Japanese soldiers who joined communist guerillas in Malaya

## Abstract

At the end of the Pacific War, 200–400 Japanese ex-soldiers stationed in Malaya joined the Malayan Communist Party guerrillas out of fear of retribution by victorious Allied soldiers or to fulfil Japan’s promise to liberate Malaya from colonial rule. However, the MCP led by Lai Teck decided not to wage armed struggle after the Japanese surrender, and Lai Tek ordered the execution of a number of Japanese. Those who remained fought for the liberation of Malaya during the Emergency, but by the late 1950s most of this group had either been killed in action, succumbed to disease, or surrendered. When the MCP ceased armed struggle in December 1989, just two Japanese remained with the group. This article uses Japanese and Malayan documents, including those of the Malayan Communist Party, to identify Japanese who joined the guerrillas as a first step toward allowing historians to evaluate their role.

## Summary

This article reconstructs the largely invisible history of Japanese soldiers and civilians who joined the Malayan Communist Party (MCP) guerrillas after Japan's surrender in August 1945. Hara identifies a significant historiographical gap: while the case of Japanese ex-soldiers who remained in Indonesia to fight the Dutch is well documented, the parallel phenomenon in Malaya has been almost entirely unknown in Japan, and only fragmentarily recorded in Malayan sources. The central problem the article addresses is one of identification and scale—determining who these men were, why they joined, what they did, and what ultimately happened to them.

Hara's core argument is that between 200 and 400 Japanese joined the guerrillas, a figure substantially higher than the approximately 100 suggested by Chinese-language newspaper reports alone. He demonstrates that motivations were heterogeneous: some were ideologically committed to fulfilling Japan's broken promise to liberate Malaya from colonial rule, others feared Allied retribution, and a few were simply desperate. The article also foregrounds a major tragedy within the MCP itself—Secretary-General Lai Teck ordered the execution of a significant number of Japanese who had joined the guerrillas, because their presence became a logistical and political burden once the party decided not to wage armed struggle against the British. Most of those who remained died in action or from disease during the Emergency, and by the early 1960s only a handful survived.

The methodology is distinctive in its cross-referencing of four source traditions: official Japanese military desertion records held in Tokyo, Chinese-language leftist newspapers published in Malaya and Singapore during the occupation and early post-war period, MCP party records and memoirs of former guerrilla leaders, and personal testimony from the two Japanese who survived to the end. Hara acknowledges the difficulty of matching names across these traditions, particularly because MCP sources often refer to Japanese by Chinese pseudonyms, and concludes that only a small number of individuals can be definitively identified across multiple source sets.

### Key Findings

- **Total number of Japanese guerrillas**: 200–400, based on converging evidence from Chin Peng's memoir (400), Hashimoto's testimony (over 200), Sasaki Ken'ichi's recollection (300), and MCP records. Chinese newspaper reports alone suggest only about 100. (pp. 68, 74, 83–84)
- **Lai Teck's execution order**: Chin Peng states that 20–30 Japanese around Kuala Kangsar were executed in the final days of 1945 or first days of 1946, and in a separate 1999 account suggests the total killed at Lai Teck's order may have been as many as 60 or 70. (pp. 82–83)
- **Survivors to 1989**: Only two Japanese remained when the MCP signed peace agreements in December 1989—Hashimoto Shigeyuki (1917–97) and Tanaka Kiyoaki (1912–2000), both former employees of Nichinan Seitetsu in Sungai Patani, Kedah. (pp. 68, 72)
- **Japanese military desertion records**: The National Institute for Defense Studies holds records identifying 41 deserters (39 in official documents, 2 in battalion records), of whom 12 are named. Most desertions occurred in September 1945, when military discipline had collapsed. (pp. 74–77)
- **Combat and technical roles**: The 3rd independent squad of the 8th Regiment, established 25 June 1948 at Sentok in northern Kedah, had approximately 40 fighters including about 10 Japanese who manufactured landmines. Tanaka Kiyoaki was commended by the Anti-British Force high command in January 1949 and received the title 'warrior of internationalism' in 1975. (pp. 85, 91)
- **Casualties**: Nagaoka Tatsukichi (nom de guerre Rong Guang) was killed on 18 December 1949 at Rambutan, Perak; Miyatani Isamu (nom de guerre Hong Guang) was killed in an ambush at Sadao, southern Thailand, in late 1959. Sasaki Ken'ichi reported that many Japanese died during the first two and a half years of the Emergency (mid-1948 to end of 1950). (pp. 74, 89, 92)

### Conclusion

Hara concludes that the Japanese who joined the MCP guerrillas were a small but significant minority who, by their choices, maintained a tenuous human connection between Japan and the peoples of Southeast Asia during a period of profound hostility. Their history remains largely unknown because they hid their Japanese names, most died without recognition, and the two survivors returned to obscurity in Japan. Hara argues that their actions—particularly those who fought to the end—deserve historical attention as evidence that some Japanese rejected the militarist state's betrayal of its own Pan-Asian rhetoric and committed themselves to the liberation of the people they had occupied.

## Context

- Primary archival collections consulted: the National Institute for Defense Studies (NIDS) library in Tokyo (official Japanese military corps histories and desertion lists compiled by the Ministry of Health and Welfare in 1961), the National Archive of Thailand (report on 'Gui Long', 26 March 1954), and the two Chinese-language newspapers *Nan Chiau Jit Pao* (Singapore, 1946–1950) and *Min Sheng Pao* (Kuala Lumpur, 1945–1948).
- Historiographical contribution: This is the first systematic attempt to identify individual Japanese guerrillas by cross-referencing Japanese military records, Chinese-language press, and MCP memoirs. It fills a notable gap in both Japanese and Malayan historiography, where the phenomenon has been almost entirely absent from published scholarship.
