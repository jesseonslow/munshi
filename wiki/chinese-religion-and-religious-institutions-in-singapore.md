# Chinese religion and religious institutions in Singapore

Marjorie Topley, writing in 1956, provides a comprehensive ethnographic survey of Chinese religious institutions in Singapore, arguing that the religious landscape there is best understood not through discrete doctrinal systems but as a syncretic, practice-oriented tradition in which institutional forms—temples, monasteries, vegetarian houses, and syncretic sects—serve as the primary vehicles for both worship and social welfare. The article was based on direct fieldwork conducted in Singapore and draws on membership records of the Buddhist Federation as well as extensive interviews with monks, nuns, and lay devotees.

## Summary

Topley's central structural contribution is a typology of Chinese places of worship in Singapore, organised along the axis of residential versus non-residential function. She distinguishes Buddhist monasteries (of two kinds: the *shih fang ts'ung lin*, open to any ordained monk, and the *tzu sun ts'ung lin*, restricted to a single "family" of disciples), nunneries, vegetarian houses (*chai t'ang*), clan and territorial association temples, medium temples, and general public temples. She demonstrates that the English word "temple" obscures fundamental differences in organisation, function, and the kinds of religious specialists who inhabit or control these buildings, and she catalogues the Chinese terms (*ssu*, *an*, *t'ang*, *kung*, *tien*, *miao*, *yuan*) that once carried precise institutional meanings but have become blurred in the Nan Yang context.

The article then traces the historical contributions of Taoism, Buddhism, Confucianism, and pre-Confucian folk religion to the syncretic mass of Chinese popular religion, emphasising that for the ordinary worshipper these sources are indistinguishable. Topley shows how Taoism absorbed the Five Bushel cult of Chang Ling and various magico-religious practices until it became, in Malaya, little more than a residual category for "non-Buddhist" religion; how Buddhism, particularly the Pure Land and Ch'an schools, provided the institutional framework of monasticism and the doctrinal emphasis on salvation through faith; and how Confucianism contributed the moral force of ancestor worship and the state-sanctioned deification of heroes. She documents the ongoing process of deification in Malaya itself, citing the case of Toa Peh Kong and the recent elevation of a Hainanese boy electrocuted near a temple to local deity status.

The final and most detailed section examines the organisational and economic life of these institutions. Topley describes how temples are administered by committees or boards of trustees and let to caretakers or controlling monks on tender; how income is derived from "oil and incense" donations, sale of paper charms and mock money, fortune-telling, divination by numbered spills, and the performance of occasional rites; and how the Hsien Tien Ta Tao sect's vegetarian houses function as both religious communities and social welfare institutions for elderly, unattached women. She documents the "monk family" system, in which lineages of master and disciples control networks of temples and monasteries across Singapore, and the elaborate ritual of nun "graduation" ceremonies that mark a young nun's qualification for professional funeral work.

### Key Findings

- At least 122 residential places of worship (mainly inhabited by women) were identified in Singapore; six main branches of the P'u Tu Men division of Hsien Tien Ta Tao between them controlled some sixty or more vegetarian houses (pp. 93, 105).
- The Fei Hsia Tsing She, the largest single vegetarian house in Singapore, cost approximately $200,000 (Straits) to build and furnish, accommodates 200 persons, and charges a minimum entry fee of $1,200 for lifetime accommodation, food, and funeral services (p. 109).
- Ancestral temple soul tablet fees varied widely: one smaller association charged a flat rate of $20 for worship in perpetuity, while another charged up to $1,000 for a central altar position, $500 for the left side, $400 for the right side, and $200 for the right corner (p. 95).
- Full Buddhist ordination, requiring a journey to Hong Kong, cost $1,500 (Straits) and involved twelve burns on the head; the cheaper "spoken ordination" (*shuo chieh*) performed locally was the norm for most Singapore nuns (pp. 70–71, 114).
- Key founding dates for institutions discussed include: Shun T'ien Kung (attap shed, 1821), T'ien Fu Kung (1841, with a predecessor "joss house" dated 1821–2), the Jade Emperor temple in Havelock Road (1888), the Kuan Yin T'ang in Waterloo Street (1884), and the Tung Shan T'ang in Devonshire Road (1894) (pp. 98–100, 105).
- The opening of the new Fei Hsia Tsing She building attracted over 6,000 guests and served no fewer than 600 meals (each for approximately eight persons) over one day and night, as reported in a leading Singapore Chinese newspaper (p. 109).

### Conclusion

Topley's definitive historical takeaway is that Chinese religion in Singapore is fundamentally a practice-based, syncretic tradition in which institutional forms serve social as well as spiritual functions, and that the vegetarian house—particularly those of the Hsien Tien Ta Tao sect—represents a vital and growing institution providing social welfare for elderly women who can no longer return to a China transformed by revolution and modernisation. She observes that, far from being moribund, these institutions were expanding in the 1950s, with new and elaborate buildings continually being erected, and that the building of temples and residential houses remained an accepted path to both social and spiritual merit.

## Context

- Topley's data derive from direct fieldwork in Singapore (observations of festivals, temple visits, and interviews with monks, nuns, and lay devotees), supplemented by the Buddhist Federation's published list of member institutions and personal lists provided by monks. She also draws on her own earlier study of Chinese women's vegetarian houses (JMBRAS 27(1), 1954).
- Historiographically, the article is one of the first systematic ethnographic surveys of Chinese religious institutions in Singapore, predating the more theoretically framed studies of Chinese religion that would follow in the 1960s and 1970s. It establishes the institutional typology and economic data that later scholars would build upon.
