# Observations upon some coins obtained in Malaya and particularly from Trengganu, Kelantan and southern Siam

## Abstract

Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles (1781–1826) landed on the shores of the island of Singapore in January 1819. With its growing commercial success, he was celebrated by the British colonial authorities and mercantile community as the visionary founder of an excellent entrepot between East and West. Despite their participation in the anti-colonial struggles, Lee Kuan Yew and his People’s Action Party, which led Singapore into self-government in 1959 and independence in 1965, allowed Raffles to remain crowned as the founder of modern Singapore in school history textbooks and the public psyche. However, the recent launching of Bicentennial planning has unleashed an exceptional wave of scholarly and public interest in the tensions and contradictions embedded in such a commemoration. The spirited discussion has included issues on whether such a moment should be commemorated, the island’s state of being in the *longue duree* stretching back for 700 years, and comparisons of Raffles with Lee Kuan Yew. Underlying the politics and dilemmas of the Bicentennial is an on-going tussle about how the history of a young nation state should be scripted.

## Summary

Huang Jianli examines the political and historiographical tensions surrounding Singapore's Bicentennial commemoration of Stamford Raffles' January 1819 landing, a state-driven initiative officially launched by Prime Minister Lee Hsien Loong in his 2018 New Year's Day message and culminating in a special National Day parade in August 2019. The article traces how the commemoration became entangled with multiple unresolved questions: whether Raffles truly "founded" Singapore or merely "landed" on an island with a 700-year history stretching back to Temasek/Singapura; how to reconcile the PAP's post-independence embrace of a "benign" colonial legacy with the anti-colonial struggles of Chinese-educated leftists; and how to manage the growing body of revisionist scholarship that challenges the "Great Man" narrative enshrined in school textbooks and public monuments since the 1887 Golden Jubilee statue (pp. 104–113).

The author argues that the Bicentennial's preparatory phase—marked by an extraordinary degree of institutional caution, the strategic embrace of a *longue duree* 700-year timeline, and public calls for "warts-and-all" history—created a discursive space that contrasted sharply with the government's simultaneous heavy-handed suppression of revisionist voices, most dramatically in the six-hour parliamentary interrogation of historian Thum Ping Tjin by Minister K. Shanmugam on 29 March 2018. This juxtaposition, Huang contends, revealed that the nation was at a crossroads in determining the future direction of its national history, with the Bicentennial inadvertently driving a wedge between the party-state's anti-revisionist posture and the pluralistic, inclusive tone that its own advisors and the press were advocating (pp. 115–119).

Drawing on newspaper editorials, parliamentary transcripts, academic publications, archaeological reports, and informal meetings with Bicentennial administrators, the article situates the commemoration within the broader post-Cold War reckoning with Singapore's security operations against the political left, particularly Operation Coldstore (1963) and Operation Spectrum (1987), and the ongoing struggle for recognition of left-wing anti-colonial history within the official "Singapore Story" meta-narrative (pp. 116–118).

### Key Findings

- The Bicentennial Office was constituted under the Prime Minister's Office with a Ministerial Steering Committee (co-chaired by Ministers Josephine Teo and Desmond Lee), a 16-member Advisory Committee, and an Executive Director (Gene Tan); the budget was informally disclosed as approximately 30% of the SG50 celebration budget (pp. 105–106, n. 6).
- The Bicentennial strategically adopted a 700-year timeline (counting back from the year 2000), effectively conflating SG200 with SG700 and downgrading Raffles' role from "founding" to "landing"; this was facilitated by archaeological evidence from Fort Canning excavations (begun 1984 by John Miksic) demonstrating a thriving settlement circa 1299–1399 (pp. 107–108).
- Raffles spent only 38 days in Singapore between 1819 and his return to Britain in October 1822, a fact highlighted by Tommy Koh to underscore the disproportionate weight given to his role relative to William Farquhar, who "did all the heavy lifting" (p. 113).
- The Select Committee on Deliberate Online Falsehoods, which indirectly referenced the Bicentennial in invoking "the yoke of colonialism and foreign interference," submitted a 176-page report in September 2018 containing 22 recommendations and declared Thum Ping Tjin "not credible" (p. 117).
- 3,800 volunteers were to undergo training for Bicentennial activities, and more than 60 organizations had been contacted by the time of the January 2018 launch (p. 106).
- The "sleepy fishing village" metaphor, first evoked by John Crawfurd and popularized by Mary Turnbull, described Singapore on the eve of 1819 as having perhaps 1,000 inhabitants (500 Orang Kallang, 200 Orang Seletar, 150 Orang Gelam, 20–30 Malays, and a similar number of Chinese) (p. 109).

### Conclusion

Huang Jianli concludes that while the Bicentennial was a state-driven initiative with societal participation, the rolling-out of events and the spirited public discussion that followed exceeded the expectations of many organizers. The commemoration has played a major role in raising historical consciousness, forcing both the one-party dominant state and its increasingly well-educated citizenry to confront foundational issues including the pre-Raffles past, the colonial and anti-colonial legacy, and the changing nature of history as a discipline. In the long run, these tensions will be valuable for forging deeper reflective bonds between the country and its citizens as it enters its next phase under a new generation of political leaders (p. 120).

## Context

- The article draws on a wide range of contemporary sources including *Straits Times* and *Lianhe zaobao* editorials, parliamentary transcripts from the Select Committee hearings, academic workshop proceedings (NUS History Department, ISEAS-Yusof Ishak Institute), and informal meetings with Bicentennial administrators, positioning it as a near-real-time historiographical intervention rather than a retrospective archival study.
- The study contributes to the historiography of Singapore's national history scripting by documenting the internal contradictions within the PAP state's approach to historical commemoration, and by linking the Bicentennial to the broader post-Cold War battle over the place of left-wing anti-colonial history in the official narrative.
