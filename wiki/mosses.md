# Mosses

The archival record on mosses in the Society's literature is limited to a single short notice: H. N. Dixon's 1928 paper "Spolia mentwiensia: Musci" in the *JMBRAS*, which constitutes the sole documented treatment of the subject in the records provided. [[1]](#ref-1)

## Scope and Historical Definition

The only extant record concerning mosses in the Society's published literature is a brief contribution by H. N. Dixon, titled "Spolia mentwiensia: Musci," which appeared in volume 6, number 1 of the *JMBRAS* in 1928, spanning pages 23 to 24. [[1]](#ref-1) The title itself signals the nature of the work: "Spolia mentwiensia" indicates that the material was collected from Mentawei (the Mentawai Islands, off the west coast of Sumatra), while the Latin subtitle "Musci" identifies the taxonomic group under discussion as mosses. [[1]](#ref-1) The paper is therefore a short notice reporting on moss specimens gathered from that island group, consistent with the broader pattern of botanical and zoological spolia reports that characterised the Society's journal in the early twentieth century, in which contributors described collections made in various parts of the Malay Archipelago. [[1]](#ref-1)

No further details regarding the specific taxa identified, the number of specimens, their ecological context, or any commentary on their distribution are available in the records provided. [[1]](#ref-1) The brevity of the piece—two pages in length—suggests it was a preliminary report or a catalogue of specimens rather than a systematic monograph or a study of moss ecology in the region. [[1]](#ref-1)

## Context within the Society's Botanical Literature

The Dixon notice sits within a wider tradition of natural-historical reporting in the Society's journal, though the records provided do not contain other moss-specific contributions. [[1]](#ref-1) The journal's entomological and botanical notices from the same period—such as H. N. Ridley's 1908 description of the crackling moth (*Nyctipho hieroglyphica*) and L. Wray's 1887 report on the pomeloe moth—demonstrate the Society's sustained interest in documenting the fauna and flora of the Straits Settlements, the Malay Peninsula, and the broader archipelago. [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3) These works, however, concern insects and their economic or behavioural significance, and do not bear directly on the study of bryophytes. [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3)

What can be inferred from the title and the journal's conventions is that Dixon's notice was part of a series of "spolia" reports—collections of specimens from a particular locality, described and identified by a specialist. [[1]](#ref-1) The use of the Latin plural "Musci" rather than a more specific taxonomic designation suggests that the paper may have listed several moss species without detailed morphological description, or that the author was reporting on a collection that had not yet been fully determined. [[1]](#ref-1) The Mentawai Islands, being a volcanic archipelago situated between Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula, would have been a source of botanical material of interest to collectors working in the region during the colonial period. [[1]](#ref-1)

## Research and Documentation

The documentation of mosses in the Society's published literature, as attested by the records available, is confined to this single two-page notice by Dixon in 1928. [[1]](#ref-1) No subsequent papers, reviews, or extended studies on bryophytes of the Malay Peninsula or Borneo appear in the archival evidence provided. [[1]](#ref-1) This represents a notable gap in the Society's coverage of plant groups: while entomology, mycology, and higher plant taxonomy received sustained attention from contributors such as Ridley, Wray, and others, the bryophyte flora of the region appears to have attracted minimal scholarly interest within the journal's pages during the period documented here. [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3)

The absence of further moss-related publications may reflect the general difficulty of studying bryophytes in tropical environments, where species identification often requires microscopic examination and where the groups have historically received less attention from colonial-era naturalists than the more conspicuous flowering plants or economically significant insects. [[1]](#ref-1) Alternatively, it may simply indicate that the records provided do not capture the full extent of the Society's bryological literature. [[1]](#ref-1) In either case, Dixon's "Spolia mentwiensia: Musci" remains the sole documented contribution on the subject in the archival evidence available, and its two-page scope limits the extent to which any broader narrative of moss research in the region can be constructed from these sources alone. [[1]](#ref-1)
