# The early adat constitution of Negeri Sembilan (1773–1824

M.B. Hooker's 1971 article examines the constitutional foundations of Negri Sembilan during the reigns of its first three Yamtuan (1773–1824), arguing that the period was one of political confusion in which the adat system was ill-equipped to accommodate a federal ruler, and that the patrilineal-matrilineal complex commonly associated with the state did not begin to crystallize until the dynasty was established under Yamtuan Radin from 1824. Writing in direct response to Maria Labi's 1969 paper in the same journal, Hooker contends that Labi's reliance on secondary historical sources at the expense of the extensive adat textual tradition led her to mischaracterize the early state as a "centralized" polity and to date the patriliny-matriliny tension too early.

## Summary

Hooker's central intervention is terminological and structural. He demonstrates that the term "centralized state" is inapplicable to Negri Sembilan before colonial reorganization, because the adat perpatih was fundamentally a lineage- and land-oriented rule system in which effective power resided in clan heads (lembaga) and district heads (Undang), not in a determinate power centre. This is confirmed by the distinctive character of the only Negri Sembilan legal digest, the Undang Sungei Ujong, which consists almost entirely of detailed family and land rules rather than the territory-wide prescriptions and Islamic sovereignty claims found in the digests of Pahang, Johor, and other states. The absence of land rules from those other digests, Hooker argues, marks a fundamental distinction between "village law" (adat kampong) and the written "Sultan's law" that presupposes a centralized legal order (pp. 106–107).

The article then addresses the political logic of inviting a Minangkabau overlord. Hooker agrees with Gullick and earlier authorities that the invitation was a response to external aggression (Bugis Rewa invasion) and internal rivalry among the four luak chiefs, but he adds that the choice of a Minangkabau was also a logical extension of adat ideology, which conceived of the chief as the apex of a natural-cosmological hierarchy. Crucially, the adat itself provided no ideological barrier to a single leader because it was concerned with matters of law — inheritance, marriage, land — rather than with political organization. The appointment was a political act that fell outside the adat's jurisdiction, and only after the fact did a new adat (a usage) grow up around the office (pp. 107–108, 110–111).

The most sustained argument concerns the patriliny-matriliny question. Hooker shows that during the period 1773–1824, the first three Yamtuan were not members of local lineages, possessed no property amenable to the pesaka-charian distinction, and had no recognized territorial boundary in adat terms. The early marriage alliances between Raja Melewar and members of the Batu Hampar clan (lineage Ayer Kaki) created a focal point for conflict over pesaka property rather than resolving the Yamtuan's constitutional position. It was not until Yamtuan Radin's reign that a fixed principle of patrilineal succession emerged, and the various adat forms that mediated the two descent principles — notably adat Lengkongan, which required land under female ownership to devolve to individual male ownership when one party to a marriage was a member of the ruling dynasty — developed largely under the influence of colonial rationalization in the later nineteenth century (pp. 108–109, 113–114).

### Key Findings

- The four states (Sungei Ujong, Rembau, Johol, Ulu Muar) revolted from Johor in 1770; Jelebu had already received an independent charter from Sultan Mahmud Shah of Johor in 1757 (pp. 104–105).
- The first Yamtuan, Raja Melewar, was installed in 1772/1773 and took up residence at Sri Menanti in Ulu Muar; the first three Yamtuan held office for life only, with a dynasty beginning under Yamtuan Radin (officially 1824–1861, though actual succession occurred in 1834) (pp. 105, 108, fn. 6).
- The formal obeisance ceremony (mengadap) by the four Penghulu of the Tanah Mengandong was instituted only in 1901, largely under British influence, and therefore throws little light on the eighteenth- or early nineteenth-century constitutional position (p. 111).
- The Sri Menanti Agreement of 4 June 1887 was the instrument by which the Yamtuan's position became unassailable through colonial recognition and protection (fn. 13).
- The "Serambi Undang" system pairs each of the four Penghulu (Ulu Muar, Jempol, Terachi, Gunong Pasir) with a specific Undang (Johol, Jelebu, Sungei Ujong, Rembau respectively), and the Penghulu of Ulu Muar holds "immediate custody of the Royal Family" (p. 112).
- A 1967 case involving a lineage of clan Sri Lemak Minangkabau transferring to Sri Lemak Minangkabau (Lengkongan Yamtuan) illustrates that the Lengkongan adat remains operationally relevant and that reverse transfer is not possible (fn. 11).

### Conclusion

Hooker's definitive takeaway is that the history of the patrilineal-matrilineal relationship in Negri Sembilan does not begin until well into the third decade of the nineteenth century, and that the process of formalizing this complex into a coherent system was driven by colonial rationalization rather than by any internal adat logic. The unity of the "Nine States" was an established ideal predating the first Yamtuan, rooted in the common subjection to Johor, but the exact constitutional relationship among the component parts only became a matter of urgency when colonial administration demanded it. Adat, in this reading, functions as a "charter" of legal and constitutional change, recording the solutions adopted at each stage of the state's transformation (p. 115).

## Context

- Hooker's argument is grounded in the adat textual tradition — perbilangan (customary stanzas), the Undang Sungei Ujong digest, and the legal digests of other Malayan states — which he had previously analysed in his 1968 note on Malayan legal digests. This textual basis is the principal methodological distinction from Labi's 1969 paper, which drew exclusively on Gullick, Josselin de Jong, and Swift.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Negri Sembilan by challenging the then-prevailing narrative of a "centralized state" emerging from the first Yamtuan's appointment, and by repositioning the patriliny-matriliny question as a product of dynastic consolidation and colonial intervention rather than of the original adat constitution.
