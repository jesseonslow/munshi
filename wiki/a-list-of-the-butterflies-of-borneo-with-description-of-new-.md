# A list of the butterflies of Borneo with description of new species

R.S. Shelford, Curator of the Sarawak Museum, published this faunistic checklist in 1904 as Part I of what he intended to be a definitive and final enumeration of Borneo's butterflies, covering the subfamilies Danainae through Amathusiinae. Working from the Sarawak Museum collection and in consultation with leading authorities at the British Museum, Shelford sought to supersede three earlier, now-outdated lists and to describe several new species from material gathered across the island.

## Summary

Shelford's central argument is that Borneo, having been "so well explored faunistically," would yield few further new species, and that a comprehensive list compiled at this juncture could lay claim to "completeness and finality" (p. 81). He justifies this ambition by noting that the most recent prior list, Bartlett's 1896 catalogue, recorded only 139 Lycaenidae, whereas the number known by 1904 already exceeded 300—a gap that illustrates both the pace of discovery and the inadequacy of earlier compilations. The work is structured as a taxonomic checklist running through 101 species in this part, with each entry providing the original description, synonymy, subspecific breakdown, and distribution notes, supplemented by bracketed field observations on behaviour, mimicry, and habitat.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to methodological questions of nomenclature. Shelford adopts the trinomial system, treating well-marked geographic variants (topomorphs) of widespread species as subspecies rather than either lumping them into a single species or splitting them into separate ones (p. 82). He acknowledges that different authorities apply this principle inconsistently—Elwes and Edwards recognise no subspecies in their Hesperiidae revision, while Rothschild and Jordan elevate every topomorph to subspecific rank—and sides with the latter as "the more scientific method." He also corrects several errors in prior literature, including Pryer and Cator's *nomina nuda* and misidentifications of species such as *Euploea binotata* (known only from northern India) and *Discophora celinde* (recorded from Borneo without confirmed specimens).

The article documents extensive mimicry complexes, particularly among the Danainae and Elymniinae, listing the specific mimetic species for each model. Shelford also makes the notable observation that Bornean butterflies do not exhibit the seasonal (dry- and wet-season) morphological variation well known from Indian populations; all *Mycalesis* species in his collection correspond to the wet-season phase, and both seasonal forms of *Melanitis ismene* fly simultaneously regardless of the season (pp. 98, 100). He further identifies a distinct "Philippine element" in the North Bornean fauna, citing species such as *Euploea leucostictos kadu*, *Euploea dufresnei tyrianthina*, and *Clerome phaon* as evidence of biogeographic infiltration from the Philippines (pp. 92–93, 109–110).

### Key Findings

- Four new species are described in this part: *Lethe cerama* (p. 96), *Ypthima abnormis* (p. 98), *Erites thetis* (p. 99), and *Elymnias brookei* (p. 102), the latter named in honour of the Rajah of Sarawak.
- The checklist enumerates 101 species across the subfamilies Danainae, Satyrinae, Elymniinae, and Amathusiinae, with subspecific distributions recorded for each.
- Shelford identifies at least six species confined entirely to Borneo, including *Euploea scudderi*, *Euploea zonata*, *Euploea masina*, *Elymnias hecate*, *Elymnias dara*, and *Thaumantis aliris*.
- The *Elymnias penanga* / *E. borneensis* nomenclatural confusion is resolved by proposing the new subspecific name *E. penanga trepsichroides* for Grose-Smith's 1892 species, whose name was preoccupied by Wallace's 1869 Pierine mimic (pp. 103–104).
- Bornean *Mycalesis* and *Lethe* species consistently lack the dry-season morphs found in Indian and Burmese populations, suggesting that the island's more uniform rainfall regime eliminates the selective pressure for seasonal polymorphism (pp. 98, 100).
- The North Bornean fauna contains a measurable Philippine component, documented through shared subspecies of *Euploea*, *Clerome*, and *Discophora* with the Philippine Islands (pp. 92–93, 109–110).

### Conclusion

Shelford's definitive takeaway is that Borneo's butterfly fauna, while still yielding occasional new species, has reached a stage where a carefully compiled list can serve as a stable reference point for future workers. The work's lasting value lies less in any single new species than in its systematic correction of prior errors, its consistent application of the trinomial system, and its detailed documentation of mimicry and biogeographic patterns that remain foundational to the island's lepidopterology.

## Context

- Shelford wrote as Curator of the Sarawak Museum, an institution established under the White Rajahs; the collection he drew upon was built through colonial-era collecting expeditions, and the new species *Elymnias brookei* is explicitly named for the reigning Rajah (p. 102).
- The primary archival source is the Sarawak Museum collection; Shelford also relied on determinations by Dr. A. G. Butler and Mr. F. A. Heron at the British Museum (Natural History), and on correspondence with the late L. de Niceville (p. 81).
