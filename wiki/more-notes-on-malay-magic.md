# More notes on Malay magic

R.O. Winstedt, a British colonial administrator and scholar based in Perak, published "Notes on Malay Magic" in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in December 1925 as a supplementary companion to his monograph *Shaman, Saiva and Sufi*. The article surveys the syncretic layers of Malay magical practice—indigenous Proto-Malay, Sakai, and Negrito beliefs interwoven with Hindu and Islamic elements—arguing that much of what had been attributed to Indian influence was in fact of Semitic or Muslim provenance.

## Summary

Winstedt organizes his material thematically rather than chronologically, moving through topics ranging from ritual nudity and the *bahdi* (a deer's transmissible aura) to rain-making, sacrifice, tree-burial, and black magic. His central analytical move is comparative: he consistently draws parallels between Malay practices and those of Assyrian, Hebrew, Arabic, Dayak, and Siamese traditions to demonstrate that the Malay magical system is not a simple Hindu overlay on a native substrate but a complex palimpsest in which Semitic magic entered through Islam and was often misidentified as Indian. For instance, the use of dough figures as substitutes in healing, the catalogue-style exorcism that names every possible demon, and the philtre of burnt black-cat ashes are all shown to have direct Semitic antecedents rather than Hindu ones.

The article gives particular attention to the aboriginal peoples of the Peninsula—Sakai, Jakun, Besisi, and Negritos—whose practices Winstedt treats as the oldest stratum of Malay magical belief. Tree-burial of magicians, the seven-day soul-haunting of graves, canoe-coffins, and the paddling of the soul to the afterworld are documented with ethnographic detail from field reports and unpublished manuscripts. Winstedt also examines how Islam accommodated rather than displaced these older practices: the Mantra (a heathen Proto-Malay group) cut the rice-soul with the *Basmala*, Muslim Malays sacrificed pigs on the seashore when all other offerings failed, and the communal buffalo-slaughter at rice ceremonies in Negri Sembilan persisted unchallenged by orthodox Islam.

Primary sources include unpublished Perak manuscripts, Winstedt's own field observations, and a wide range of secondary literature from Skeat, Evans, Newbold, Rosalind Moss, and R.C. Thompson's *Semitic Magic*. The article reads as a series of learned notes rather than a sustained argument, but its cumulative effect is to reframe Malay magic as a system in which the "old bottles" of indigenous belief were filled with the "new wine" of Semitic magic, a process that was so seamless as to be nearly invisible to later observers.

### Key Findings

- The Spectre Huntsman, an avatar of Siva, is identified in Pahang with Jin, son of Jan; the cock sacrificed to him after harvest must be yellow, the colour sacred to Siva (p. 8).
- In Negri Sembilan, a buffalo is slaughtered at communal rice ceremonies held every third or fourth year, with a portion of the flesh planted in each farmer's field corner as an offering to earth-spirits (p. 13).
- A Proto-Malay chief's grave in Johore (1879) featured sixteen horizontal beams forming an enclosure containing a coconut shell, torch, axe-handle, cooking-pot, and shoulder-basket for the deceased's use, with a ditch around the grave for the spirit to paddle its canoe (p. 20).
- The last Malay shaman left unburied in Perak was "stuck up" in a *Lagerstroemia floribunda* tree near Raja Kayu between 1870 and 1875; he is now believed to be a tiger with a white patch (p. 18).
- A Kelantan magician's philtre for seeing through a brass dice-box required seven pieces of thread saturated in the blood of a murdered man and a slaughtered buffalo, combined with the eyes of a tiger and a black cat, all burnt to ashes and mixed with oil (pp. 14–15).
- The Jakun soul-ladder at a grave consists of a five-foot post with fourteen notches—seven ascending (representing surviving relatives) and seven descending (for the dead person's soul) (p. 20).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that Malay magic cannot be understood as a Hindu system corrupted by Islam; rather, it is a layered tradition in which Semitic magic entered through the Islamic conversion and was so thoroughly absorbed into indigenous frameworks that its foreign origin became invisible. The "new wine of Semitic magic" fit so naturally into the "old bottles of Malay belief" that early converts were "intoxicated into acceptance of Islam" without perceiving the substitution.

## Context

- Winstedt served as a British colonial officer in Perak and was a founding figure of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society; his field access to unpublished Perak manuscripts and direct contact with local informants gave him a source base unavailable to most European scholars of the period.
- The article draws on unpublished Perak manuscripts and Winstedt's own ethnographic observations, contributing to the historiography of Malay religion by shifting the explanatory framework from Hindu influence to Semitic-Malayan syncretism.
