# More notes on Malay magic

R.O. Winstedt's "More Notes on Malay Magic" (1927) is a compact ethnographic survey of shamanic possession, divination, and the institutional role of magicians in Malay society, drawing on comparative evidence from Siberian shamanism, modern psychiatry, and Perak legal records to argue that Malay magical practice constitutes a coherent system of spirit-mediated healing and statecraft rather than mere superstition.

## Summary

Winstedt opens by situating the Malay shaman's trance within a broader comparative framework, noting the close resemblance between the *latab* of the Malay and Dayak and the arctic hysteria of the Samoyed, both of which produce cataleptic episodes, mimicry, and stripping of clothing that the Peninsular Malay attributes to spirit possession (p. 342). He then marshals evidence from Jung's clinical work on hysterical delirium—visual hallucinations of animals and processions, amnesia lasting days or weeks, somnambulic dialogue in which the patient copies the voices of the dead, and the pseudo-language of glossolalia—to demonstrate that the Malay shaman's symptoms map precisely onto what modern medicine would diagnose as protracted hysterical delirium (pp. 342–343). Even among magicians showing no prior neurological symptoms, Winstedt observes that the novitiate is deliberately designed to induce an abnormal state: the Hindu practises asceticism, while the Malay shaman keeps vigil in forest depths or beside a grave until he acquires his familiar, the were-tiger, paralleling the Siberian shaman's spirit-helper (p. 343).

The article then catalogues the practical apparatus of the seance: incense, the accelerating drum, the grass brush held rigidly before a candle, and the progressive rigidity and swoon as each spirit enters the body (p. 343). The therapeutic objective is to identify the possessing spirit's name and desire so it can be expelled into the shaman's own body or onto a food receptacle. Winstedt extends the discussion to possession of inanimate objects—the State sword of Perak, flower posies, candle flames used as divining instruments, and the rattan divining-rod whose tip gyrates as the invoked spirit "passes in through the fontanel at the top of the head and down the arm into the butt end of the rod" (p. 345). He notes that while Quranic inscriptions appear on the divining-rod, these symbols have no functional bearing on its use, suggesting the practice predates Islam and was later hallowed with Muslim formulae (p. 345).

The final section shifts to institutional history, drawing on the *Ninety Nine Laws of Perak* to show that in the eighteenth century the magician occupied a formally recognised and tax-exempt position in the social hierarchy, with a parish magician owing immediate attendance on the sick and receiving a gold *paha* for caring for a village (p. 346). At the apex stood the Sultan Muda, the State Shaman, whose wife held the title Raja Puan Muda and whose deputy was the Raja Kechil Muda; his duties included keeping the state weapons alive, conducting feasts for the royal drums, and sacrificing to the genies of the state (pp. 346–347).

### Key Findings

- The Malay shaman's possession symptoms (catalepsy, mimicry, glossolalia, amnesia, voice-changing) correspond exactly to the clinical picture of protracted hysterical delirium as described by Jung, suggesting a natural neurological basis for the phenomenon (pp. 342–343).
- The Malay shaman's novitiate—vigil in forest depths or beside a grave—is deliberately structured to induce the automatism required for mediumship, paralleling the Siberian shaman's acquisition of an animal spirit-helper (p. 343).
- In eighteenth-century Perak, the *Ninety Nine Laws* formally exempted magicians from taxation and forced labour, fixed their fees (a gold *paha* for village care; two *derham* per cultivator for quarterly rice-plant vivification), and required them to be "long-headed, suave, industrious and truthful" (p. 346).
- The Sultan Muda (State Shaman) occupied a position above the Raja Muda and Bendahara in the succession hierarchy; he was chosen for bravery, knowledge of magic, and ability to select medicine-men, and received a state allowance from port dues and opium tax (pp. 346–347).
- The rattan divining-rod, inscribed with pentagrams and Quranic phrases, functions independently of those inscriptions, indicating that the practice is pre-Islamic and was subsequently sanctified with Muslim formulae (p. 345).
- Candle-flame divination was used for multiple purposes: determining whether a shaman could cure a patient, selecting building sites, and even settling disputes between rivals by comparing which of two named candles burned longer (p. 344).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that Malay magic was not a marginal or irrational residue but an integrated system of knowledge and social organisation, formally embedded in Perak's legal and political structures. The magician's tax-exempt status, the hereditary titles of the Sultan Muda's household, and the codified fees for spiritual services all demonstrate that magical practice was a recognised and regulated profession in pre-colonial Malay statecraft, operating alongside—and in some respects above—the Islamic religious establishment.

## Context

- The article draws heavily on the *Ninety Nine Laws of Perak*, a legal code compiled by a family of sayids that included Muslim saints, providing rare documentary evidence of the institutional position of magicians in eighteenth-century Malay polity.
- Winstedt's comparative method, linking Malay shamanism to Siberian and Samoyed practices and to Jungian clinical psychology, reflects the early twentieth-century anthropological tendency to seek universal psychological explanations for culturally specific ritual behaviour.
