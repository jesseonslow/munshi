# Historical sketch of Penang in 1794. With an appendix on the failure of Penang as a naval base and shipbuilding centre, M. Stubbs Brown. .S. Bastin

C. W. S. K. composed this 1899 obituary for Sir William E. Maxwell, K.C.M.G., a Straits Settlements legal and administrative careerist whose death in the Gold Coast was felt as a severe loss to the newly founded MBRAS. The piece functions simultaneously as a professional biography and a historiographical assessment of Maxwell's scholarly contributions to Malay studies, arguing that the Society's early viability depended almost entirely on his personal energy and output.

## Summary

The obituary traces Maxwell's career from his 1855 entry into the Penang Supreme Court—where his father, Sir P. Benson Maxwell, was Chief Justice—through his qualification as an advocate, his service in the Perak War of 1875, his appointment as Assistant Resident of Perak, and his subsequent roles as Commissioner of Land Titles, Resident Councillor of Penang, British Resident of Selangor, and Colonial Secretary of the Straits Settlements before his final posting as Governor of the Gold Coast (p. 2). The author emphasises two threads: Maxwell's administrative legacy, particularly the District Administration system he originated in the Colony and which was later replicated in the Federated Malay States, and his legislative work on the Land Question, which the author notes was initially met with "determined opposition" but whose "good effects must now be admitted by his former critics" (p. 2).

The second half of the piece is devoted to Maxwell's scholarly output for the Society's journal and beyond. The author catalogues his contributions—Malay Proverbs, notes on Perak manuscripts, papers on the Sakais and other aboriginal tribes, a 67-page account of a journey on foot to the Patani Frontier in 1876, a history of Perak from native sources, papers on the Dutch in Perak, shamanism, and Malay land tenure, and three edited Malay fairy tales taken down from rhapsodists' lips (pp. 3–4). Maxwell served as the Society's honorary secretary from 1883 to 1887, a period during which, the author states, "the Society owed its existence in a great measure to the energy of its Secretary, as it received but scanty support in the way of contributions from members" (p. 3).

The author closes with a broader reflection on the fragility of scholarly institutions dependent on single individuals, invoking the fate of Logan's *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* as a cautionary parallel. Maxwell's *Manual of the Malay Language* is identified as his work of widest influence, and his bequest of a "fine library of Malay MSS." to the Royal Asiatic Society is noted as a lasting institutional gift (pp. 4–5).

### Key Findings

- Maxwell served as MBRAS honorary secretary from 1883 to 1887, a period the author characterises as one in which the Society "owed its existence in a great measure" to his personal effort due to scant member contributions (p. 3).
- He was created C.M.G. for services connected with the Nisero affair and later K.C.M.G. for distinguished service in the Ashanti Expedition (p. 3).
- His 1876 paper "A journey on foot to the Patani Frontier" ran to 67 pages and is described as "historical and geographical" (p. 3).
- He edited three Malay fairy tales—*Sri Rama*, *Raja Donan*, and *Raja Ambong*—taken down from the lips of Malay rhapsodists and published with translation and notes (p. 4).
- He bequeathed a "fine library of Malay MSS." to the Royal Asiatic Society (p. 4).
- The author asserts that Maxwell's *Manual of the Malay Language* "introduces every learner of Malay to a scholarly appreciation of Malay style and language, when all the learner's surroundings tend to degrade that language in his eyes" (p. 5).

### Conclusion

The author's definitive takeaway is that Maxwell's influence as a scholar was disproportionate to his formal institutional role: he single-handedly sustained the Society through its vulnerable early years, established scholarly standards in Malay studies that Dutch scholars subsequently validated, and left behind a corpus of work—particularly the *Manual* and the bequeathed manuscript collection—that would outlast his premature death. The obituary is ultimately an argument that the Society must now institutionalise the standards Maxwell set individually, lest it suffer the same fate as Logan's abandoned journal.

## Context

- The author writes as a contemporary Straits Settlements official (initials C.W.S.K.) in the immediate aftermath of Maxwell's death, framing the loss in terms of the Society's institutional survival rather than in detached historiographical terms.
- The piece reflects the colonial administrative culture of the 1890s, in which the boundaries between official duty and scholarly contribution were fluid; Maxwell's "facile pen" and "hours which he could snatch from his all-absorbing official duties" (p. 3) are presented as a model of the colonial gentleman-scholar.
