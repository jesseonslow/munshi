# Colonial birding in the Thai-Malay Peninsula: Birds from the Selangor Museum now in World Museum, Liverpool

## Abstract

In the early 1900s the zoological staff of the Selangor Museum regularly collected birds in mountainous parts of the Thai-Malay Peninsula, giving hill stations a prominent role in Malaysian ornithology. H. C. Robinson published a synopsis of the Selangor Museum’s collections of mountain birds in 1909, drawing connections to the work of other prominent European and American bird collectors. The Selangor Museum made a large donation of specimens to the Liverpool Museums in 1914, and several of these mountain birds are now in the World Museum, National Museums Liverpool. An examination of the provenance of these specimens reveals the synergy between the growth of bird collections from the peninsula, the transition to bird ‘watching’, and the development of some of these hill stations into enduringly popular resorts.

## Summary

Wilson traces the provenance of bird specimens donated from the Selangor Museum to the Liverpool Museums in 1914, now held in World Museum, National Museums Liverpool. Using these specimens as a starting point, the article examines how colonial-era museum collecting on the peninsula's mountains laid the groundwork for the hill stations that remain central to Malaysian ornithology. The core argument is one of synergy: the growth of bird collections, the transition from specimen collecting to bird 'watching', and the development of hill stations into popular resorts were mutually reinforcing processes rather than sequential ones.

The primary evidence consists of specimen labels (accession numbers, localities, dates, collector attributions) cross-referenced against Robinson's 1909 synopsis of mountain birds, the *Journal of the Federated Malay States Museums*, and contemporary accounts of hill station development. Wilson follows Robinson's own ordering of the mountain localities, examining which specimens from each are now in Liverpool and what they reveal about the collecting history. A notable historiographical gap is addressed throughout: the museum's full-time collectors, referred to in official records only as 'the Dyak Collectors', are largely anonymous, with Charles Ulok being the single identifiable figure.

### Key Findings

- Approximately 300 birds were donated from the Selangor Museum to the Liverpool Museums in 1914; 60 of these are mountain birds now in World Museum (p. 127).
- The Selangor Museum employed at least two full-time collectors from 1903 onward, referred to in the *Journal* only as 'the Dyak Collectors' or 'the Dyaks attached to the Museum'; Charles Ulok, formerly of the Sarawak Museum, is the one identifiable collector (pp. 123–124).
- The 1905 FMS Museums expedition to Gunung Tahan (the peninsula's highest peak) was partly funded by the British Museum, with specimens divided between that institution and the Selangor Museum (p. 134).
- The Semangko Pass / Fraser's Hill district accounts for fifteen specimens in World Museum, making it the best-represented locality in the collection and the one Robinson described as 'exceptionally well situated for collecting' (pp. 130–131).
- Charles Ulok, described by Boden Kloss as 'undoubtedly the best [local] collector in the East', died in 1920 from dysentery acquired while on duty at Kuala Tahan (p. 136).
- The Selangor Museum was demolished in 1959; its vertebrate collections had been transferred to the Raffles Museum (now Natural History Museum, Singapore) in 1926, with additional holdings at NHMUK and the American Museum of Natural History (pp. 136–137).

### Conclusion

Wilson's definitive historical takeaway is that the colonial museum collecting enterprise and the subsequent rise of amateur birdwatching were concurrent and mutually reinforcing rather than sequential. Robinson's semi-popular field guide *The Birds of the Malay Peninsula* (1928) catalysed a shift from specimen collecting to live observation, while the very localities that had been intensively collected—Fraser's Hill, Cameron Highlands, Genting Highlands—were simultaneously developing into the hill resorts that remain the backbone of Malaysian birdwatching today.

## Context

- Primary archival collection examined: World Museum, National Museums Liverpool (NML-VZ vertebrate zoology collection), with secondary reliance on the *Journal of the Federated Malay States Museums*, Robinson's 1909 synopsis, and FMS government gazette reports.
- Historiographical contribution: The article recovers the largely anonymous labour of the Selangor Museum's indigenous collectors and situates the Liverpool collection within the broader network of colonial-era specimen transfers between the FMS Museums, the British Museum, and other institutions.
