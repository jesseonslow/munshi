# Dutch in Malaya

The Dutch presence in Malaya, conducted primarily through the United East India Company (*Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie*, VOC), spanned from the conquest of Malacca from the Portuguese in 1641 to the final British transfer under the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824, with a brief interregnum of British occupation from 1795 to 1818 [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1). The Dutch established a fortified *comptoir* (trading post) at Malacca that served as the administrative and commercial hub for their operations across the Straits of Malacca, extending to the Perak River, the Dindings (Pulau Pangkor), and Indragiri on the Sumatran coast [[2, pp. 175–180]](#ref-2), [[3, pp. 36–39]](#ref-3). At its peak, the settlement comprised a small European community of burghers, soldiers, and Company servants alongside a larger population of Malay, Chinese, Javanese, Bugis, and Portuguese Christian residents, as well as a significant slave population [[2, p. 180]](#ref-2), [[1, pp. 121–124]](#ref-1). The Dutch economic footprint was defined by the tin trade, textile commerce, and the collection of harbour tolls, though the settlement remained chronically unprofitable for much of its existence [[2, pp. 181–182]](#ref-2).

## Administrative History and Political Relations

The Dutch acquisition of Malacca on 14 January 1641, following a combined Dutch-Johor siege that lasted from August 1640, marked the beginning of a colonial administration that would endure, with interruptions, for nearly two centuries [[4, pp. 229–232]](#ref-4). The first governor, Johan van Twist, arrived on 1 February 1641, and the succession of Dutch governors continued through at least eighteen named officials by 1725 [[4, p. 233]](#ref-4). The administrative structure centred on the *comptoir* at Malacca, which dispatched virtually all its papers to Batavia from 1641 to 1795, forming the core of the surviving documentary record in the VOC archives at The Hague [[5, p. 4]](#ref-5).

The political relationship between the Dutch and the Malay kingdoms was characterised by a complex interplay of treaty-making, military coercion, and mutual accommodation. The VOC sought to impose monopolies over tin and other commodities through treaties with the rulers of Perak, Kedah, and other states, but these arrangements were routinely circumvented by local traders who sold to competing buyers at higher prices [[2, pp. 177–178]](#ref-2), [[5, pp. 8–9]](#ref-5). Fernando argues that the correspondence between Malacca and Malay rulers reveals an uneasy but often amicable relationship in which the rulers successfully protested against and evaded disadvantageous terms, contradicting the conventional narrative of unchallenged Dutch political hegemony [[5, pp. 8–9]](#ref-5). The 1650 Perak contract, which granted the Company half the tin produce at 31 Spanish reals per *bahara* (a unit of approximately 3 pikuls, or 187.5 kilograms), was followed by the murder of Dutch residents in 1651, a blockade, and a renegotiated agreement in 1655 [[2, pp. 176–177]](#ref-2). Similar patterns of treaty, violence, and renegotiation characterised Dutch relations with Kedah, where the 1642 treaty promising half the tin at a low price was never fulfilled, and Dutch personnel were murdered in 1658 [[2, p. 178]](#ref-2).

The Dutch also maintained a presence at Pulau Pangkor (Pulau Dinding) in the Perak Straits, first establishing a wooden stronghold with a garrison of one sergeant, three soldiers, and three sailors in 1670 [[3, p. 36]](#ref-3). Dampier's 1689 visit found a stone fort and approximately 20 to 30 soldiers [[3, p. 37]](#ref-3). The garrison was massacred in 1690 by Malays under Panglima Kulup, after which the Dutch erected a stone pillar bearing the arms of the VOC and the United Provinces as a monument to their claim and withdrawal [[3, p. 39]](#ref-3), [[6, p. 169]](#ref-6). A second garrison of 30 European and 30 native soldiers was established in 1745 under Baron van Imhoff but withdrawn in 1748 on account of the "insalubrity of the place" [[3, p. 39]](#ref-3), [[6, p. 169]](#ref-6).

The British occupation of 1795–1818 interrupted Dutch administration, and the brief Dutch restoration of 1818–1825 was followed by the permanent British transfer under the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1). The circumstances of the 1795 surrender have been the subject of considerable controversy, with Governor Abraham Couperus accused of treason by pro-French members of the Batavia government who sought to ruin him for his association with the Stadholder's faction [[7, pp. 87–88]](#ref-7). The garrison at the time of surrender numbered 269 men against a British force of 2,860, and Couperus had been requesting reinforcements since at least April 1794 [[7, pp. 113–122]](#ref-7).

## Economic Activities and Trade

The Dutch economy at Malacca was structured around three principal revenue sources: harbour tolls, the sale of Company textiles, and the tin trade [[2, pp. 175–176]](#ref-2). The settlement was chronically unprofitable for the first decades of Dutch rule; in 1662, expenditure of 228,806 guilders exceeded receipts of 112,192, and it was not until 1665 that receipts (234,050 guilders) marginally surpassed expenditure (230,423 guilders) [[2, p. 181]](#ref-2). The Heren XVII, the Directors of the VOC, described this favourable result as "completely unexpected" [[2, p. 182]](#ref-2).

The tin trade was the most strategically important commodity. The Company's exclusive agreements with Perak, Selangor, and Rembau fixed prices at 32 to 38 Spanish dollars per *bahara*, but these were routinely circumvented by smugglers who sold to other buyers at higher rates [[8, pp. 57]](#ref-8). In 1663, approximately 738 *bahara* were transported from Perak to Malacca through a system of guard ships that intercepted vessels leaving the Perak River and issued letters of credit for the tin to be sold at Malacca [[2, p. 177]](#ref-2). By 1665, no increase was expected, as two-thirds of Perak's population had disappeared for reasons not specified in the record [[2, p. 177]](#ref-2). The 1746 Perak–VOC treaty, documented in the *Misa Melayu*, granted the Dutch a monopoly over the tin trade at 30 Dutch reals per *bahara* with a toll of 2 reals, and gave them possession of Tanjung Putus where they built a fortified lodge [[9, pp. 23–24]](#ref-9).

The textile trade was another major component. The Company imported textiles from Coromandel and Bengal, destined for both the local market and native buyers from across the Straits, Java, and Borneo [[2, p. 175]](#ref-2). The Company's cloth trade on the Malayan coast was, however, structurally uncompetitive because private English traders had direct access to weaving villages and could supply cheaper, more fashionable goods than the Company's standardised Indian imports [[8, p. 60]](#ref-8). De Bruijn's 1785 memorandum proposed liberalising the cloth trade for Malacca residents, estimating it would generate 300,000 Rix-dollars in imports and 30,000 Rix-dollars in customs revenue alone [[8, p. 60]](#ref-8).

The Japan trade provided a significant seasonal stimulus. In December, Company ships arrived from Japan laden with copper, silver (cast in the form of small vessels called *schuiten*, each worth 15 guilders), lacquer-work, camphor, and porcelain [[2, p. 176]](#ref-2). These ships were loaded with tin and spices for the return voyage to Bengal, Coromandel, Surat, Ceylon, and Persia. During Van Riebeeck's tenure, annual Japan-bound shipments ranged from 4 to 6 ships with cargoes valued between 1,193,654 and 1,430,675 guilders, while Malacca departures ranged from 6 to 10 ships with cargoes of 1,171,967 to 1,738,819 guilders [[2, p. 176]](#ref-2).

The broader commercial context of the 1780s is illuminated by De Bruijn's memorandum, which argues that Malacca must urgently replicate the lucrative trade that had previously centred on Riau before the Bugis could re-establish a rival exchange port. He provides specific price data for the five principal commodities—opium, tin, pepper, piece-goods, and China goods—and projects total annual revenues of at least 300,000 Rix-dollars for Malacca if all proposed measures were adopted [[8, pp. 58–61]](#ref-8).

## Social Composition and Legal Administration

The population of Malacca in 1667 numbered approximately 7,560, comprising Malays and Minangkabaus who occupied themselves with rice-planting and cattle-keeping, most of the townspeople being fishermen or pedlars [[2, p. 175]](#ref-2). The European community was small: at Van Riebeeck's departure in 1665, there were only 21 Dutch burghers, 200 soldiers (40 of whom held a rank higher than private), and 167 other Company servants [[2, p. 180]](#ref-2). The remaining population of approximately 900 men, 745 women, and 908 children included a significant number of Portuguese Christians, most of whom were Roman Catholic, and 436 male and 381 female slaves, the majority of whom belonged to the Portuguese [[2, p. 180]](#ref-2).

The legal and administrative apparatus of the Dutch *comptoir* is documented in the India Office Records collection (series R/9), comprising 98 boxes of Dutch-language documents spanning 1685 to 1835 [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1). The Orphan Chamber (*Weeskamer*) records, comprising 36 boxes, document its three functions: educational (maintenance of orphan children with deposited capital sums), fiduciary (administration of intestate estates and wills), and financial (lending on mortgage security, effectively substituting for banking) [[1, p. 106]](#ref-1). The Court of Justice (*Raad van Justitie*) records, in 54 boxes, cover criminal and civil jurisdiction including full trial transcripts, civil case files, petition books, and notarial protocol books recording conveyancing, slave transactions, emancipations, and wills [[1, p. 106]](#ref-1).

The criminal records reveal a multi-ethnic society in which defendants were drawn from Malay, Chinese, Arab, Bugis, Javanese, and European communities [[1, pp. 121–124]](#ref-1). Fernando's analysis of court records from 1749 to 1825 documents crimes ranging from petty theft to wilful murder across all ethnic groups, revealing a litigious urban society in which inhabitants were thoroughly familiar with Dutch legal procedures [[5, pp. 11–16]](#ref-5). The General Protocol Books, maintained by a succession of notaries including Jacob van Haak, Johannes Hamel, Francois Adam Lefevre, Jan Hendrik Stecher, Anthonij Stecher, and Daniel Koek, span 1786 to 1825 and record conveyancing, slave sales, emancipations, wills, and powers of attorney [[1, pp. 124–126]](#ref-1).

The slave trade was a significant feature of the local economy. Protocol records and wills demonstrate a thriving slave trade drawing on supplies from Minangkabau, and the slavery registers for Riau dated December 1824 and January 1825 list individual slaves by name, age, race, place of origin, and owner, separated into children under 8 and adults over 8 [[1, p. 132]](#ref-1), [[5, pp. 16–19]](#ref-5). Cross-ethnic economic relations were extensive, including Chinese-Malay partnerships and a local shipbuilding industry [[5, pp. 16–19]](#ref-5).

The mid-eighteenth century saw the Dutch garrison confront sustained military pressure from the Malay hinterland. The *Diary of Malacca* for 1756 records three days of military activity in which the garrison confronted Malay forces allied with Raja Said of Selangor, a relation of Daing Kamboja [[10, pp. 261–267]](#ref-10). The enemy's strategy—raiding outposts, building fortified camps (*bentang*), and planning a coordinated riverine assault on the town during the dry monsoon—revealed a campaign of considerable sophistication that the garrison could only repel through repeated sorties into difficult jungle terrain [[10, pp. 264–266]](#ref-10). The Dutch held the town but could not secure the surrounding countryside, a condition that generated constant alarm among the civilian population [[10, p. 261]](#ref-10).

## Research and Documentation

The documentary record of the Dutch in Malaya is extensive and distributed across several major archival collections. The VOC archives at The Hague, in the national archives of the Netherlands, preserve virtually all papers that Malacca dispatched to Batavia from 1641 to 1795, including the governor's letters, the *dagh register* (daily journal), resolution books of the Political Council, instructions to envoys, memoirs of outgoing governors, account books, and the harbour master's shipping list [[5, pp. 4–7]](#ref-5). Fernando identifies the harbour master's shipping list (1682–1792) as the most complete and detailed register of its kind for any port in Asia during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, recording all European and indigenous vessels passing through the Straits and providing the only source on individual traders operating in the western Malay Archipelago [[5, p. 7]](#ref-5).

The local archive of the Malacca *comptoir*, comprising thirty-eight categories of papers covering the period from the 1740s to the mid-1820s, was discovered in the storage of the Malacca courthouse and deposited in the India Office Library in 1927; following the 1982 reorganisation of British libraries, it is now held in the British Library under the shelfmark R/9 [[5, p. 10]](#ref-5), [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1). Baxter's 1984 finding aid provides a comprehensive item-by-item inventory of all 44 sub-series, making the collection accessible to researchers for the first time [[1, pp. 105–134]](#ref-1).

Early published sources include Valentijn's account of Malacca, translated by Hervey in 1890, which covers the conquest and early administration [[4, pp. 225–246]](#ref-4); Bort's report on Malacca of 1678, translated by Bremner with introduction and notes by Blagden and published in 1927 [[11, pp. 1–232]](#ref-11); and Hervey's 1883 translation of the *Diary of Malacca* for 1756 [[10, pp. 261–267]](#ref-10). More recent scholarship includes Coolhaas's 1965 study of Van Riebeeck's tenure [[2, pp. 173–182]](#ref-2), Irwin's 1956 examination of the 1795 surrender [[7, pp. 86–133]](#ref-7), Koster's 2005 analysis of Dutch images in Malay historiography [[9, pp. 59–96]](#ref-9), and Fernando's 2005 survey demonstrating that the archives, long assumed lost, survive in substantial quantity [[5, pp. 1–36]](#ref-5). The 1785 memorandum of Governor De Bruijn, translated by Harrison in 1953, provides a detailed commercial snapshot of the late VOC period [[8, pp. 56–62]](#ref-8).
