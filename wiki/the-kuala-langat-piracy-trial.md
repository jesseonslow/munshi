# The Kuala Langat piracy trial

John Gullick's 1996 article re-examines the 1874 Kuala Langat piracy trial in Selangor, in which seven men were convicted and executed for the murder of a Melaka vessel's crew off Kuala Jugra. Set against the backdrop of British intervention in the Malay states and the fragile authority of Sultan Abdul Samad, the article weighs Sir Frank Swettenham's later assertion that the condemned men were innocent against the trial record and C.N. Parkinson's 1960 critique, ultimately concluding that the truth cannot be definitively established.

## Summary

The article addresses a persistent historical puzzle: whether the seven men executed at Kuala Langat in February 1874 were genuinely guilty of the piracy of a Melaka trading vessel, or whether a miscarriage of justice occurred under the pressure of British naval presence and the political tensions of the Selangor civil war's aftermath. Gullick reconstructs the sequence of events from the testimony of the two principal prosecution witnesses—Mat Syed, the sole survivor of the attacked crew, and Daga, a Sumatran trader who claimed to have witnessed Raja Yakob's orders—then subjects the trial's structural weaknesses to careful scrutiny. The court, presided over by Tunku Kudin with Davidson and McNair as British-appointed commissioners, lacked a defence advocate, relied heavily on Davidson to conduct witness examination, and operated in an atmosphere Gullick describes as prejudicial, with the British naval escort anchored within range of the istana.

Gullick's central analytical move is to set the open-court evidence—Mat Syed's identification, corroborated by Daga and by property recovered from the accused's boats—against Swettenham's private, uncorroborated informant-based narrative of 1875. He notes that the accused, who understood legal procedure well enough to cross-examine witnesses, conspicuously failed to raise an alibi defence despite Swettenham's later claim that they had all been elsewhere. This silence, Gullick argues, is the principal ground for doubting Swettenham's findings. Yet he also acknowledges that Swettenham's patient, confidential enquiries carried a certain credibility absent from the fraught three-day trial, and that the political context—old scores from the civil war, the Sultan's precarious position, and the British desire to make an example—created conditions in which a collective conspiracy of silence among Selangor notables cannot be ruled out.

The article draws on Davidson's verbatim trial notes, Swettenham's unpublished journal, McNair's 1878 book *Perak and the Malays*, and the official dispatches of the Straits Settlements to triangulate the competing accounts. Gullick is careful to note Swettenham's own inconsistency: in 1875 and 1900 he asserted the witnesses committed deliberate perjury, but by 1906 he retreated to the explanation of mistaken identification on a dark night—a shift Gullick finds inexplicable given the unchallenged fact that Mat Syed had seen the accused in daylight both before and after the attack.

### Key Findings

- The piracy occurred on 16 November 1873; the Melaka vessel (a *perahu nadir*) carried a rattan cargo and a cash box variously valued at $600 or $2,000, and was attacked by men from two local boats who came from the stockade at Kuala Jugra (p. 103).
- Eight suspects were arrested in Melaka in December 1873; seven were executed after the trial of 13–15 February 1874, one juvenile being spared (p. 101).
- Davidson's verbatim notes of the trial evidence run to 8,000–9,000 words; he put nine final questions to Daga to test his credibility but did not need to press Mat Syed on any point (pp. 104, 111).
- Swettenham's journal entry of 28 August 1875 records that he was "convinced that not one of the men executed was guilty," identifying seven "real perpetrators" whose whereabouts he listed: four in Langat, one in Klang, one in Selangor, one in Lukut (p. 112).
- The innocent men's alleged whereabouts at the time of the piracy were: two at Lukut, three at a place near Kuala Selangor, and the remainder at Langat but not at the scene (p. 112).
- The court held there was "no evidence" that Raja Yakob had ordered the piracy, yet accepted Daga's presence at Kuala Langat "at about the time" of the attack as sufficient corroboration of Mat Syed (p. 112).

### Conclusion

Gullick sides with Parkinson in the final assessment: "it is hardly possible to establish the truth of this matter so long after the event," and the case must remain an open verdict. The open-court evidence, tested before experienced judges and apparently corroborated, carries a certain weight that Swettenham's private, uncorroborated informant narrative cannot fully displace; yet the political pressures of 1874 and the possibility of a collective cover-up among Selangor notables mean that the trial's verdict cannot be treated as conclusive proof of guilt either.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the Straits Settlements dispatches (C.1111, C.1320), Davidson's verbatim trial notes, Swettenham's *Malayan Journals 1874–1876* (ed. Burns and Cowan), and McNair's *Perak and the Malays* (1878), with the photograph of the trial court courtesy of Arkib Negara (p. 102).
- Historiographically, the piece intervenes in the debate between Parkinson's 1960 structural critique of the trial and Swettenham's posthumous claims, offering a more nuanced reading that takes Swettenham's 1875 journal entry—previously unpublished—as the key primary document and subjects it to the same evidential scrutiny applied to the trial record itself.
