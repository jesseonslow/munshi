# The ancien regime in Trengganu, 1909–1919

J. de V. Allen (1968) reconstructs the political and administrative life of Trengganu between the assumption of British suzerainty in 1909 and the imposition of a full Adviser in 1919, drawing primarily on the monthly diaries of the British Agents preserved in the Colonial Office files. His overarching thesis is that the "untrammeled Malay rule" of the pre-British period was far more stable, sophisticated, and less oppressive than earlier British accounts suggested, and that the state's subsequent decline was driven primarily by internal power struggles disrupted by British intervention rather than by any inherent inadequacy of indigenous governance.

## Summary

The article sets out to correct a persistent historiographical error. From the 1890s onward, writers such as W. A. Graham and Hugh Clifford had depicted independent Trengganu as a lawless backwater of rampant crime and peasant oppression, a characterisation that Emerson and Winstedt were still repeating in the 1930s and 1960s. Allen demonstrates, using the detailed diaries kept by successive British Agents (Conlay, Scott, Dickson, Maxwell, and Humphreys) and forwarded monthly to London, that this picture was "wildly inaccurate" (p. 23). Trengganu in 1909 was a state of approximately 154,000 people with a capital of 25,000 — the largest urban Malay population in the Peninsula — governed by a functioning if minimal legal system, a State Council in near-permanent session, and a network of district chiefs who, for the most part, neither oppressed nor terrorised their subjects (pp. 24–27). The "loose government" described by Conlay was, in essence, a cheap, low-tax, low-intervention system that a large and industrious peasantry did not resent, provided it delivered peace and stability (pp. 24–25).

The article's more original contribution is its analysis of Sultan Zainal Abidin III as a skilled and deliberate politician rather than the weak, pious youth of conventional accounts. Allen argues that the Sultan maintained and even strengthened central authority through a combination of marriage alliances (particularly with the Rhiau-Lingga royal family), the importation of foreign chiefs, the elevation of commoners and minor chiefs into bureaucratic posts, the deliberate non-renewal of traditional chiefly titles, and the cultivation of support among religious leaders (*hajis* and *seyyids*) whose influence was amplified by the earlier abolition of the *penghulu* system (pp. 32–37). This created a "felicitous equilibrium" among traditional chiefs, outsiders, commoners, and the religious establishment, with the Sultan holding the scales between all groups (p. 37).

The decline that followed British intervention is attributed not to the failure of this system but to its disruption. The Sultan was forced to conciliate more parties than before, including those the British wanted removed. The young chiefs' faction split after 1909, the religious party's loyalty became uncertain once Britain went to war with Turkey in 1914, and the Sultan's death in November 1918 created a vacuum his son — described by the Agents in increasingly unflattering terms — was unable to fill (pp. 41–48). The 1919 treaty imposing a British Adviser was thus the culmination of a process in which the British, by their very presence, had destabilised the delicate political machinery their predecessor Sultan had so carefully constructed.

### Key Findings

- State revenues amounted to only $100,000 per annum in 1909, drawn from revenue farms (opium, gambling, spirits, kerosene, export duties), tin export duty, port dues, and pepper plantation profits; there was no land tax, no quit-rent, and no direct taxation (pp. 23–24).
- The entire law-and-order apparatus in 1909 consisted of twelve policemen, two judges (*hakims*), one gaol of approximately fifty capacity staffed by six men, and one school of thirty to forty boys; by 1918 the school had grown to 103 pupils but still had only one teacher and one room (pp. 24–25).
- Only five death sentences were carried out during the twenty-seven years of Sultan Zainal Abidin's reign — three Chinese and two Malays — and the population was described as "extremely law-abiding" (p. 25).
- In 1909 there were only eleven mining concessions in the State; by 1918 the royal family held in concession 600,000 acres, approximately three-quarters of the known mining area (pp. 29–30).
- The Sultan's *haj* to Mecca in 1913–14 cost $70,000; his annual allowance at death was $6,000, while his son's was slashed from $9,500 to $3,500 upon signing the 1919 treaty (pp. 44, 48).
- Debt-slavery, milder than in other states in that the status was not hereditary, was not abolished until 1919, and its abolition "roused considerable opposition among the ruling class" (pp. 27, 49).

### Conclusion

Allen's definitive historical takeaway is that the "untrammeled Malay Rule" of 1909 was "nothing like as bad as the Grahams and Cliffords made out" and was "in many ways better than what was going on in 1918, when Britain had been practising limited interference for nine years" (p. 53). The British were fundamentally opposed to the very pillars upon which Zainal Abidin had built his state — a reformed aristocracy, a more orthodox Islam, and pride in Trengganu's independence — and their intervention, however well-intentioned, was the principal agent of the disorder they then cited as justification for deeper control.

## Context

- The study is based almost entirely on the Colonial Office file series CO 273 (Trengganu), comprising the Agents' monthly diaries, Conlay's comprehensive 1909 Report, and related correspondence, most of which had been inaccessible to scholars until shortly before publication.
- The article represents the first systematic constitutional study of Trengganu using primary administrative sources, filling a gap that Winstedt had noted as unfilled as late as 1961 and directly challenging the dominant narrative inherited from Graham, Clifford, and the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*.
