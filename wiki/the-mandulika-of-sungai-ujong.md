# The Mandulika of Sungai Ujong

J. M. Gullick's 1991 study of William Maxwell (1846–1897) presents a comprehensive portrait of the Straits Settlements administrator who became the pioneer of first-hand, scholarship-informed Malay studies in the late nineteenth century. Drawing on official records, contemporary correspondence, and Maxwell's own published corpus, Gullick argues that Maxwell's unique combination of legal training, linguistic ability, and administrative immersion made him the first figure to bridge the gap between armchair anthropology and field observation in Malayan scholarship.

## Summary

Gullick traces Maxwell's career from his arrival in Malaya at eighteen as clerk to his father, the first Chief Justice of the Straits Settlements, through his successive postings as magistrate, Assistant Resident of Perak under Hugh Low, Commissioner of Lands and Titles, Resident of Selangor, and finally acting Governor of the Straits Settlements before his transfer to West Africa, where he died in 1897 at the age of fifty-one. The narrative is organised not chronologically but thematically, moving from Maxwell's character and formative experiences to his diplomatic mission to Sumatra over the *Nisero* affair, his land reform programme in Selangor, and finally his scholarly output. Gullick emphasises that Maxwell's legal background gave him a distinctive analytical clarity, while his years in the country districts of Province Wellesley and Melaka provided intimate knowledge of Malay village society that no European administrator of his generation had previously documented with such rigour.

The article devotes substantial attention to Maxwell's role as Hon. Secretary and Editor of the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (1883–87), a period when the Society was struggling with declining membership, insufficient premises, and a lack of professional scholars to sustain its publication. Maxwell's editorial innovations — including the *Notes and Queries* supplement, the publication of Malay texts in Jawi, and the translation of French and Dutch papers — kept the Journal alive during a formative period. Gullick also reconstructs the Society's early institutional history, including the production of its maps of the Malay Peninsula, the reprinting of the *Hikayat Abdullah*, Skinner's geography textbook, and the ambitious but ultimately unprofitable project of republishing earlier scholarly papers under the editorship of Dr. Reinhold Rost.

A central thread of the article is the controversy over Maxwell's theory that the Malay Ruler held a customary right to a tithe of agricultural produce, which he advanced to justify periodic revision of quit-rent in his Selangor Land Code. Gullick is candid that this particular proposition "did not stand up to scrutiny of the evidence" and that Maxwell was "confusing a government tax with the Ruler's obligation to collect the tithe due from Muslims as a charitable due — the familiar zakat" (p. 19). Nevertheless, the broader principles of Maxwell's land code — registered title, periodic rent revision, and provisions for customary smallholdings — were preserved in the FMS Land Code of 1896 and ultimately in the National Land Code of 1965.

### Key Findings

- Maxwell's *Manual of the Malay Language* (1882) included an introductory essay on "the Sanskrit element in Malay" that tabulated Malay words across themes (commerce, cosmology, fauna, political office, religion) with proposed Sanskrit equivalents and comparative Indonesian dialect terms; it was still in print in its 1927 edition and was prescribed alongside Favre's dictionary for new civil service recruits (pp. 16–17).
- The Selangor Land Code of 1890, drafted under Maxwell as Resident, established the foundations of the modern National Land Code; its provision for periodic revision of quit-rent (after 7 years, with an obligation to revise within 30 years) was retained in the FMS Land Code of 1896, and its provisions for "customary" smallholdings anticipated the Malay Reservations Enactment of 1913 (pp. 14, 32, 36).
- Maxwell's collection of 105 Malay books and manuscripts, bequeathed to the Royal Asiatic Society in London, included the *Sejarah Melayu*, *Hikayat Abdullah*, *Taj al-Salatin*, *Hikayat Hang Tuah* (two volumes), and numerous *sha'ir* and legal treatises; some items bear Maxwell's signature with notes on the date, place, or circumstances of acquisition (pp. 17, 39–41).
- During the *Nisero* affair (1883–85), Maxwell negotiated the release of approximately 30 shipwrecked crew members held by the Raja of Tenom, a vassal of the Sultan of Aceh; the Dutch agreed to pay $38,000 as compensation for damage, and Maxwell personally remained as a hostage until payment was confirmed; he was awarded a CMG and £500 (pp. 10–13).
- Maxwell's first contribution to the Journal was a lecture covering 301 selected Malay proverbs, later supplemented by a further 272 collected from French and Dutch sources; his elucidation of their meaning constitutes "a major ethnographic study, albeit in disjointed form" (p. 17).
- The Society's membership declined from 153 (1878) to 111 (1882) before recovering to 157 under Maxwell's editorship by 1891; the first edition of the Society's map of the Malay Peninsula was published in 1881, the second in 1887, and the last of the successive editions in 1911 (pp. 21–22).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive assessment is that Maxwell was "the pioneer" of a new breed of Malayan scholar — the first-hand observer who combined close field observation with scholastic knowledge, particularly in linguistics — and that his legacy, encompassing the preservation of manuscripts, the documentation of oral literature, the establishment of a land tenure system still in use, and the institutional survival of the Society's Journal, constitutes an "immense and varied" contribution to which the field owes "a debt of gratitude" (pp. 15–16, 28).

## Context

- The study draws primarily on Maxwell's official reports (Straits Settlements Despatches, Parliamentary Papers), the Society's own annual reports and Journal issues (JSBRAS 1–22), the Maxwell bequest at the Royal Asiatic Society London, SOAS manuscripts, and the RCS/BAM papers at the Royal Commonwealth Society; it also engages with secondary literature by Sadka, Heussler, Barr, Kratoska, and Wong.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its integration of Maxwell's administrative career with his scholarly output, demonstrating that the two were inseparable, and in its reconstruction of the MBRAS's institutional history during its most precarious early decades — a period previously treated only in fragmentary fashion.
