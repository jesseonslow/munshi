# Some ethnological notes

Jim Warren's 1977 article examines the destruction of the British East India Company's trading settlement at Balambangan in February 1775 and argues that this event was a critical turning point in the consolidation of Sulu Sultanate hegemony over the Sulu zone. Set against the backdrop of the Company's desperate search for trade goods to offset its silver drain in the China tea trade, the article reframes the episode from the Taosug perspective, demonstrating how the short-lived settlement (1773–1775) inadvertently empowered the Sulu Sultanate by introducing opium and advanced munitions into the regional exchange network.

## Summary

Warren challenges the conventional historiography that has treated Sulu's relations with the East India Company primarily through the lens of Alexander Dalrymple's career and British imperial policy. Instead, he centres the Taosug experience, arguing that the settlement's commercial activities—particularly the large-scale retail of opium and the supply of gunpowder, cannon, and firearms—transformed the political economy of the Sulu zone. The chief factor John Herbert, who replaced the dismissed Dalrymple, proved both incompetent and self-serving, flooding the Jolo market with opium on extended credit and amassing vast quantities of munitions while embezzling Company funds. These actions created a new dependency among the Taosug aristocracy on imported stimulants and weapons, while simultaneously intensifying Iranun raiding against the Philippine provinces.

The article then traces the complex inter-ethnic rivalry between the Taosug under Sultan Mohammad Israel and the Magindanao Sultanate of Cotabato, both of whom sought to leverage the English presence to their advantage. Israel's diplomatic manoeuvring—simultaneously courting the Spanish at Zamboanga, the Dutch at the Moluccas, and the English at Balambangan—ultimately failed to secure external military support against his rivals. The Spanish expedition under Colonel Cencelli to Jolo in early 1774 collapsed due to the commander's own incompetence and hostility, further isolating the Sultan. When a personal insult to Datu Teteng, a cousin of Israel, provided the pretext, the Taosug destroyed the settlement in a single night, capturing its entire military stores.

Warren's narrative draws heavily on Spanish colonial correspondence from the Archivo de Indias and the Archivo Histórico Nacional, British records from the India Office Library, and Dutch documents from The Hague, weaving these into a coherent account of how the Taosug exploited the complementarity between English commercial interests and their own evolving redistributive role to achieve regional supremacy.

### Key Findings

- The *Phoenix* alone delivered 300 chests of opium to Balambangan in August 1773; Herbert sold opium at 550 dollars per chest on Company account and embezzled 1,570 dollars in cash received on the trade investment (pp. 78–79).
- Fugitive *indio* informants reported the settlement held at least 340 barrels of gunpowder (75 lbs each), more than 1,900 muskets in seventy cases, and a large crate of pistols and sabres (p. 82).
- The Taosug captured 45 cannon (calibres 8, 10, and 12), 200 *quintales* of gunpowder, 22,000 rounds of ammunition, 228 rifles, 35 pistols, and 45 swords during the attack of 26 February 1775 (p. 86).
- Sultan Mohammad Israel imposed a 5 percent *ad valorem* customs duty on all commodities imported and exported by private traders within his dominions to assert his monopoly over foreign commerce (p. 83).
- By 1839, the annual opium trade at Sulu averaged twenty-five to thirty chests, each weighing between 100 and 120 pounds (p. 80).
- The total booty captured at Balambangan, including a brig, smaller vessels, opium, textiles, and specie, was valued at over 900,000 Mexican dollars (p. 86, n. 85).

### Conclusion

Warren concludes that the destruction of Balambangan marked the definitive moment after which the Taosug Sultanate evolved as the established centre for trade in the Sulu zone. The munitions traffic that continued from Bengal despite the attack enabled Taosug leadership to consolidate wealth and political power in their favour by 1800, while the Magindanao, deprived of direct access to European trade goods, were forced to subordinate their regional ambitions to Taosug control.

## Context

- Primary archival collections explored include the India Office Library (London), Archivo de Indias (Seville), Archivo Histórico Nacional (Madrid), Algemeen Rijksarchief (The Hague), and the Public Records Office (London).
- The article's principal historiographical contribution is its shift from the British policy-centric interpretation of the Balambangan episode to a Taosug-centred analysis of how the settlement's commercial activities shaped the political trajectory of the Sulu Sultanate.
