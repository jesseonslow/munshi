# Some ethnological notes

C. Boden Kloss, a Fellow of the Royal Asiatic Institute residing in Johore, published this collection of ethnological observations in 1908, drawing on field enquiries among Proto-Malay and Sea-Jakun communities across the Singapore Strait, Johore, and the Riau Archipelago. The article's overarching concern is the identification, classification, and survival of aboriginal peoples in the region, with particular attention to linguistic titles, folkloric traditions, and the degree to which these groups have retained their pre-Islamic cultural distinctiveness.

## Summary

Kloss opens with a comparative analysis of two folkloric traditions that he interprets as distorted memories of the orangutan. Among the swamp-dwellers south of Gunong Pulai in Johore, the *Orang Mawas* are described as devil-men with backwards-turned feet and sharp parangs growing from their elbows like spurs, who kill and devour humans (p. 73). Among the Jakuns of Endau Sembrong, a similar creature called *tuhu* is a huge red hairy man living in trees who speaks the language of whoever he meets (p. 73). Kloss connects the parang legend to Knocker's earlier account of the Orang Belanas, who reportedly plant a parang in the forearm of children, suggesting a shared cultural substrate.

The bulk of the article then turns to the distribution of head-man titles—*Batin*, *Jinang*, *Jukrah* (or *Dukrah*), and *Penghulu*—among Proto-Malay communities in the Kallang estuary, Pulo Batam, and the broader region (pp. 74–75). Kloss argues that *Batin* is the original and most widespread title among these peoples, extending from the far north of Biliton, while *Jinang* and *Jukrah* are subordinate titles specific to the Peninsula. He explains the anomalous "Table of Precedency" recorded by Skeat and Ridley at Kampong Roko as a product of Malay administrative interference, where a Malay-appointed *Jinang* came to outrank the indigenous *Batin* (p. 74). He also corrects Skeat and Ridley's apparent error in classifying the Belandas as "Sakai," arguing they should be designated Jakun or Proto-Malayan (p. 75).

Kloss then documents the *Tukar Ganti* ritual among the Orang Laut of the Riau Archipelago and Singapore creeks, in which human images, ghost-ships, and sick-houses are constructed to absorb and expel evil spirits during times of illness or prophylactic need (pp. 75–76). He records a specific case in which a village *pawang* collected 40 cents per household to build a large vessel into which gathered *hantus* would be sent to sea, with the pawang's house placed under *pantang* to Europeans on the day of dispatch (pp. 75–76). The article closes with observations on the Orang Senimba, the Biduanda Kallang, and the Orang Muka Kuning of Batam's interior, the last being a small nomadic group of fewer than a hundred who still use the *sumpitan* blowpipe with *ipoh* poison and have maintained their paganism in relative isolation (pp. 76–77).

### Key Findings

- The *Orang Mawas* legend of parang-armed devil-men in Johore swamps and the Jakun *tuhu* legend are both interpreted by Kloss as folkloric distortions of the orangutan, with the parang motif linking to Knocker's account of the Orang Belanas (p. 73).
- The title *Batin* is the original and most geographically widespread head-man designation among Proto-Malay peoples, extending from Biliton to the Peninsula, while *Jinang* and *Jukrah* are subordinate titles confined to the mainland (p. 74).
- The reversed precedence of *Penghulu* over *Jinang* at Kampong Roko is explained as an artefact of Malay administrative imposition on a small village "hedged in by dominant Malays" (p. 74).
- The *Tukar Ganti* ritual objects—human images, ghost-ships, and sick-houses—serve both curative and prophylactic functions and are constructed by all inhabitants of the Riau Archipelago and Singapore creeks during times of sickness (pp. 75–76).
- The Orang Muka Kuning of Batam's interior, numbering fewer than a hundred, still use the *sumpitan* blowpipe with *ipoh* poison (of Borneo pattern, obtained by trade) and remain pagans, making them "probably the only island tribes who have maintained practically uncorrupted their paganism and their isolation" (p. 77).
- The Orang Senimba and Biduanda Kallang have lost all ethnographical individuality through Islamisation and absorption into the dominant Malay population, though the creeks of the Old Straits and Johore estuary remain occupied by people "still primitive in habits and appearance" (p. 76).

### Conclusion

Kloss's definitive historical takeaway is that the Proto-Malay aboriginal populations of the Singapore Strait and southern Johore are in a state of rapid cultural dissolution. The inland divisions have largely disappeared south of the Batu Pahat–Sedili line, the coastal Sea-Jakun have been Islamised and absorbed, and only the most isolated interior groups—such as the Orang Muka Kuning of Batam—retain their pre-Islamic practices. He issues an urgent call for "speedy investigation" of these remaining communities before their ethnographical individuality is lost entirely (p. 77).

## Context

- The article engages directly with the work of W. W. Skeat and H. N. Ridley (JSBRAS No. 33) and W. Logan's *Journal of the Indian Archipelago*, positioning Kloss's field observations as corrections and supplements to these earlier studies.
- Kloss's residence in Johore gave him access to communities in the Kallang estuary, Pulo Batam, and the Johore River system that were not readily available to the Society's earlier investigators, making his notes a valuable record of a transitional period in which aboriginal identity was still legible but rapidly eroding.
