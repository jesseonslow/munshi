# The grave-stone of Sultan Mansur Shah of Malacca (1458–1477 A.D.

Ib Larsen's 2012 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the largely undocumented period of Sarawak's governance between the assassination of its first sultan, Sultan Tengah Manga, in 1641 and the arrival of James Brooke in 1842. The central thesis is that the sultanate of Sambas, to the southwest, exercised considerably stronger influence over Sarawak during this two-century gap than has been generally credited in the official Brunei-centric narrative.

## Summary

The article challenges the conventional account that Sarawak was simply under Brunei's authority after Sultan Tengah Manga's death, with four local *datu* governing semi-autonomously. Larsen draws on Sambas royal records, oral traditions, early European maps, and British colonial correspondence to argue that the descendants of Sultan Tengah Manga—who established the Sambas sultanate through his son Radin Suleiman—maintained significant and enduring ties to Sarawak. The author's fieldwork in Sambas, including interviews with the dowager queen and a fifth-generation descendant of the Sambas royal line, provides access to genealogical tables and rewritten royal chronicles that have been largely invisible to Sarawak historiography.

The evidential base is multi-layered. Cartographic sources, notably Francis Valentyn's map of Borneo (c. 1725), place western Sarawak under the Sambas Kingdom. British naval and diplomatic records reveal that the 1812 bombardment of Sambas was a direct response to an act by a Sarawak pangeran identified as a relative of the Sambas sultan, and that Raffles subsequently sent a warning to the "Rajah of Sarawak" in 1813. The 1836 uprising against the Pengiran Mahkota is reinterpreted as a struggle in which the *datu* sought Sambas protection and arms, and in which the Sambas government actively attempted to secure control of the newly discovered antimony deposits. Larsen also traces the territorial expansion of the Sambas royal family along the Borneo coast, from Pelajau in the east to Tanjung Datu at the northern tip, suggesting a pattern of coastal control and trade taxation that encompassed Sarawak.

### Key Findings

- Sultan Tengah Manga was appointed Sultan of Sarawak in 1599 by his brother Sultan Abdul Jalilul Akbar of Brunei and was assassinated at Batu Buaya, Santubong, in 1641 (p. 5).
- Francis Valentyn's map of Borneo (c. 1725) places the western part of Sarawak under "T Koninkryk van Sambas" (Sambas Kingdom) (p. 3).
- In 1812, the British navy bombarded Sambas in retaliation for the burning of the ship *Commence* by "Pangeran Samewda of Sarawak, a relative of the sultan of Sambas" (p. 6).
- In 1831, the Dutch resident in Sambas proposed that Sambas should acquire Lundu, Sematan, Sarawak, and Sadoud from Brunei to stop rampant salt smuggling (p. 7).
- During the 1836 uprising, the rebel *datu* sent a message to the Dutch Assistant Resident in Sambas in May 1839 stating they wished to come under Dutch (Sambas) rule (p. 8).
- A Dutch report from the 1820s records that two Brunei Pengerans extracted three *koyans* of padi and two *pikuls* of birds' nests per year from local Dayaks in Sarawak (p. 7).

### Conclusion

Larsen concludes that the story of absolute Brunei rule over Sarawak between 1641 and 1842 is too simplistic. Sarawak sat on the periphery of both Brunei's and Sambas's spheres of influence, and control likely varied over time and between different parts of the territory. The discovery of antimony in the early nineteenth century intensified competing claims from both sides until the appointment of James Brooke as White Rajah by the Sultan of Brunei finally settled them.

## Context

- Primary sources include the Sambas royal genealogical table, a rewritten version of a record by Sultan Umar Aqam Uddin I (1708–32), manuscripts in the Indonesian National Archives and Leiden, and oral traditions recorded in 1940 and collected during the author's field visits to Sambas in 2004 and 2010.
- The article contributes to pre-Brooke Sarawak historiography by shifting attention from the Brunei-centric narrative to the Sambas connection, drawing on Indonesian archival materials and royal family interviews that have been largely inaccessible to Sarawak historians.
