# Alabastra borneensia

E.D. Merrill published "Alabastra Borneensia" in 1917 in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 77), presenting a comprehensive taxonomic revision of Bornean plant material held by the Philippine Bureau of Science and the Sarawak Museum. The article describes numerous new species across nine families and records several previously known species as new to Borneo, arguing that the island's flora remained vastly underdocumented despite over sixty years of botanical collection.

## Summary

Merrill's central argument is that Borneo's botanical diversity had been dramatically underestimated by the scientific community. Working from relatively small collections, he identifies at least twenty-five distinct species of *Elaeocarpus* in his material alone, of which nine are described as new to science, while approximately sixty distinct species of *Eugenia* are represented, with a confident expectation that the total Bornean list would eventually exceed one hundred (pp. 191–192, 206). He extends this observation to *Fagraea*, *Timonius*, and *Barringtonia*, where his collections reveal multiple undescribed forms in each genus. The article is structured taxonomically by family, with each new species receiving a full Latin diagnosis, English description, type specimen citation, and a brief discussion of its closest allies.

The most substantial sections treat *Elaeocarpus* and *Eugenia*. For *Elaeocarpus*, Merrill provides a preliminary assessment of the Bornean representatives, noting that many specimens are in poor condition for determination (young buds or fruits only), and declines to prepare a key until further collections become available (p. 191). For *Eugenia*, he engages directly with the long-standing taxonomic controversy over the delimitation of *Eugenia*, *Jambosa*, and *Syzygium*, arguing that the intermediate forms make generic separation untenable and that all should be treated under *Eugenia* in the broad sense, with sectional distinctions based on petal connation (p. 206). He also erects a new genus, *Tetraeugenia*, for a Sarawak species with four stamens and calyptrate petals, positioning it as the *Syzygium*-equivalent of *Aphanomyrtus* (pp. 230–231).

The material examined derives primarily from the 1915 Mount Kinabalu expedition (Mrs. Clemens), earlier Sarawak collections by Hose (1894–1895) and Foxworthy (1908), Villamil's 1916–1917 work at Sandakan, and a single Winkler specimen from South-East Borneo (1908). Native collectors employed by the Sarawak Bureau of Science contributed numerous specimens, often without precise locality data, which Merrill acknowledges as a limitation (pp. 192–193, 200).

### Key Findings

- Nine new species of *Elaeocarpus* are described (*E. chrysophyllus*, *E. cupreus*, *E. elliptifolius*, *E. clementis*, *E. nigropunctatus*, *E. hosei*, *E. dolichobotrys*, *E. castaneus*, *E. winkleri*), with at least twenty-five distinct Bornean species identified in the collections overall (pp. 191–200).
- Approximately twenty new species of *Eugenia* are described, including *E. coralina*, *E. capitata*, *E. kiauensis*, *E. paradoxa*, *E. kuchingensis*, *E. baramensis*, *E. heteroclada*, *E. multibracteolata*, *E. perparvifolia*, *E. moultonii*, and *E. havilandii*, among others (pp. 207–227).
- A new genus, *Tetraeugenia* (Myrtaceae), is erected with a single species, *T. caudata*, from Mount Trekan, Sarawak, distinguished by its four stamens and calyptrate petals (pp. 230–231).
- Five new species of *Fagraea* are described (*F. grandifolia*, *F. acuminatissima*, *F. involucrata*, *F. cymosa*, *F. uniflora*), the last being anomalous with a 6-merous calyx and 8–9 corolla lobes (pp. 231–236).
- Six new species of *Timonius* are described (*T. hosei*, *T. villamilii*, *T. involucratus*, *T. hirsutus*, *T. heterophyllus*, *T. clementis*), the latter two from Mount Kinabalu at altitudes of approximately 2000 m (pp. 239–245).
- Two new high-altitude Compositae are described from Mount Kinabalu: *Lactuca retrorsidens* from Low's Peak (above the summit lakelet) and *Lagenophora gibbsiae* from above Paka Cave at approximately 3300 m (pp. 245–247).

### Conclusion

Merrill's definitive takeaway is that Borneo's flora, particularly its tree flora, remained in a state of profound taxonomic obscurity, with the existing collections—already modest—yielding a high proportion of undescribed species. He frames the work as a preliminary contribution, explicitly anticipating that continued botanical exploration would substantially revise and expand the known species lists for every genus treated. The article functions as both a taxonomic monograph and a call for further fieldwork, grounded in the concrete evidence of how many new species a single season's collections could produce.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: Philippine Bureau of Science herbarium (Manila) and Sarawak Museum (Kuching), with type material from the 1915 Mount Kinabalu expedition (Clemens), Hose's 1894–1895 Sarawak series, Foxworthy's 1908 collections, and Villamil's 1916–1917 Sandakan material.
- Historiographical contribution: The article represents a key moment in the transition from 19th-century descriptive botany to systematic revision of Malesian flora, and Merrill's arguments for a broad *Eugenia* and his erection of *Tetraeugenia* influenced subsequent myrtaceous taxonomy in the region.
