# On the nature of military government: the case of the BMA in Negri Sembilan

J.M. Gullick, drawing on his first-hand experience as a Civil Affairs Officer in the British Military Administration (BMA) of Negri Sembilan, published this article in 2006 in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*. The piece examines the nature, structural limitations, and practical challenges of military government in the immediate post-surrender period of 1945–46, using Negri Sembilan as a case study to illustrate how an occupying army's administrative apparatus differs fundamentally from civil government in both scope and capacity.

## Summary

Gullick opens by establishing the theoretical framework of military government: it is a short-term expedient whose functions are far more limited than those of a civil administration. An occupying army must maintain law and order, secure food supplies, and prevent anarchy that would distract or threaten its operational role, but it does not undertake education, tax collection, or civil justice. The BMA personnel themselves were a "mixed bag" — a short-term assembly of commissioned officers drawn from a wartime British Army, many without prior Malayan experience, lacking any career structure or tradition upon which to build an *esprit de corps*. The article argues that these structural constraints, rather than individual incompetence, explain much of the BMA's perceived failures.

The Negri Sembilan narrative then illustrates these constraints in practice. The most acute problem was security: the MPAJA's presence in the countryside, combined with the police's withdrawal from patrolling after a wave of assassinations, created a vacuum that military patrols could not fill. The rice shortage was equally intractable, rooted in disrupted international supply chains and the collapse of local *padi* production under Japanese enforcement. A recurring theme is the BMA's failure to communicate effectively with the Malay population — a problem of language, trust, and the political sensitivity of the post-war moment, when pre-war assumptions about Malay "loyalty" to Britain had been shattered by the 1942 defeat.

The article also addresses the political dimension of the BMA's brief existence: managing the Malay Rulers under the implied threat of non-recognition, distinguishing genuine collaboration from ordinary survival, and preparing the ground for the MacMichael mission and the Malayan Union. Gullick is careful to note that the BMA's role in these constitutional matters was largely incidental — it provided the administrative cover under which the real political work was done by others.

### Key Findings

- The Negri Sembilan BMA detachment comprised approximately a dozen commissioned officers, including the SCAO (a former Malayan Civil Service officer who had twice held posts in Seremban), four others with pre-war Malayan experience, two RAMC doctors, an accountant, and a botanist who served as food controller (p. 6).
- The Japanese rationing system had become so inflated that the number of ration card-holders in Negri Sembilan exceeded the estimated population; the BMA was unable to replace it because the new application forms and cards printed in the UK never arrived during the BMA period (p. 11).
- In early November 1945, Malay villagers at Padang Lebar (Kuala Pilah district) slaughtered forty-two Chinese, mainly women and children, in an act of communal reprisal; the SCAO's prompt intervention and military patrols prevented further violence, and the attackers gave themselves up without resistance (p. 9).
- Pre-war Negri Sembilan had approximately 30,000 acres under wet *padi* cultivation; Japanese enforcement measures — including a low retention limit and compulsory delivery to collection points — resulted in a one-third decline in the total Malayan *padi* crop by 1945, with large areas left overgrown and irrigation channels unusable (pp. 13–14).
- The BMA period lasted approximately seven months, from the landing at Morib in early September 1945 to the restoration of civil government on 1 April 1946, when the SCAO continued as Resident Commissioner and his deputy as Secretary to the RC (pp. 5–6, 17).
- The Japanese had transported approximately 5,000 Roman Catholics (Eurasians and Chinese) from Singapore to forest land at Bahau in Kuala Pilah district; the settlement became malarial, and during the BMA period the settlers returned en masse to Singapore (p. 13).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that the BMA was a necessary but inherently limited instrument — a "sterile mule" that could maintain minimum order and essential services but could not replicate the full range of civil governance. Its failures in law and order, food supply, and public communication were structural consequences of its nature as a short-term military expedient staffed by a heterogeneous and often under-trained cadre. The restored civil government's tendency to ignore or disapprove of the BMA's existence only compounded the injustice to those who had served under genuinely difficult conditions, and the article serves as a quiet vindication of the BMA's personnel against the "whipping boy" reputation that has persisted in the historical literature.

## Context

- The article is a participant-observer account; its primary evidential basis is the author's own service in the Negri Sembilan detachment, supplemented by interviews with contemporaries (notably Major-General H. R. Hone) and a wide range of published secondary sources including the official histories (Donnison 1956; Hone 1946), Cheah Boon Kheng's *Red Star Over Malaya* (1983), and Kratoska's studies of food and the Occupation.
- Historiographically, the piece fills a gap left by the official accounts, which Gullick notes "have little content pertinent to this paper" (p. 85, n. 1), by providing a ground-level, state-specific narrative that situates the BMA's administrative challenges within the broader political and social context of post-war Malaya.
