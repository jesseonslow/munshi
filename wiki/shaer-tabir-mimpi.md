# Shaer Ta’bir Mimpi

H. Overbeck's 1929 article presents a translation and brief analysis of the *Shaer Ta'bir Mimpi*, a versified Malay dream-book published in Singapore in 1908 (1326 AH), situating it within the broader Islamic mantic tradition and assessing its likely composite origins. Overbeck argues that the text is a medley drawing on Arabian, Malay, and possibly Indian sources, and that dream-books of this type cannot reliably serve as evidence for the psychology of the people among whom they are found.

## Summary

Overbeck's primary purpose is philological and comparative rather than ethnographic. He provides a full translation of the *Shaer Ta'bir Mimpi*—a rhymed Malay text organized into chapters covering dreams of celestial events, animals, houses, and bodily phenomena—and appends translations of passages from the prose *Ta'bir Mimpi* excerpted by J. J. de Hollander in his 1845 Malay reader. The two texts are set side by side to allow comparison of the versified and prose traditions of Malay dream-interpretation.

The article addresses a methodological question raised by an unnamed ethnologist: whether dream-books can yield inferences about the psyche of a people. Overbeck rejects this, arguing that such texts "wander far from their original home" and are often transplanted or adapted across cultural boundaries. He identifies Arabian elements (date-fruit, fig, camel), possible Malay substitutions (the mousedeer, a house with a ladder), and a general Islamic doctrinal framework. The prose *Ta'bir Mimpi* appended to the translation reveals a more elaborate system: dreams are interpreted according to the day of the week on which they occur and the first letter of the thing dreamt of, eclipses are read according to a cycle of eight years (the *Windu*) and twelve months, and involuntary body movements (*gerak*) are assigned specific portents. Overbeck notes in a footnote that similar twitching-lore exists in Indian mantic literature, citing Diels's *Beiträge zur Zuckungsliteratur*.

The text itself is a practical manual: it prescribes remedial actions for evil omens (almsgiving, ceremonial bathing, repentance) and distinguishes between auspicious and inauspicious signs with considerable specificity. The versified form reflects a Malay custom of rendering didactic and occult material in verse for popular consumption.

### Key Findings

- The *Shaer Ta'bir Mimpi* was lithographed in Singapore in 1326 AH (c. 1908 AD); Overbeck's copy is described as "not very clear" with peculiar spelling, especially of Arabic loanwords (p. 338).
- The prose *Ta'bir Mimpi* (de Hollander's reader) organizes dream interpretation by day of the week and the initial letter of the dreamt object, with 28 letters of the Arabic alphabet each assigned a distinct portent (pp. 372–373).
- Eclipse interpretation in the *Ta'bir Mimpi* follows a cycle of eight years (the *Windu*: Alif, Ehe, Jimawal, Jimakhir, Dal, Ba, Wawu, Jimakhir), each with twelve monthly subdivisions; the omen depends on both the year-name and the month (pp. 372–374).
- The appendix of the *Ta'bir Mimpi* includes a chapter on involuntary body movements (*gerak*), assigning specific portents to twitching of individual body parts (e.g., right upper eyelid = wealth; left lower eyelid = illness or a distant journey) (p. 375).
- Overbeck identifies the *Shaer* as a "medley" with Arabian elements (date-fruit, fig, camel) and possible Malay substitutions (mousedeer, house with ladder), concluding it was likely adapted from another dream-book rather than being an indigenous Malay composition (p. 338).
- The text prescribes concrete remedial actions for evil omens: almsgiving (*sedekah*), ceremonial bathing with lime (*mandi berlimau*), and repentance (*bertaubat*) to avert calamity (pp. 351, 362).

### Conclusion

Overbeck's definitive takeaway is that the *Shaer Ta'bir Mimpi* is a derivative, composite text of uncertain provenance that cannot be used as a reliable index of Malay popular psychology. Its value lies in documenting the transmission and adaptation of Islamic mantic literature into Malay versified form, and in providing a comparative basis for studying the relationship between prose and verse traditions of dream-interpretation in the Malay world.

## Context

- The primary source is a lithographed Malay text published in Singapore (1908), with comparative passages drawn from de Hollander's *Handleiding tot de kennis der Maleische taal en letter-kunde* (Breda, 1845).
- The article contributes to the early 20th-century philological tradition of cataloguing and translating Malay literary and occult texts for a European scholarly audience, predating modern anthropological approaches to belief systems.
