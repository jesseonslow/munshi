# Dusuns

The Dusun-speaking peoples of Sabah, Malaysia, constitute a linguistically related but culturally diverse cluster of communities inhabiting the West Coast and Interior Residencies of the state. As of the 1960 census, the demographic category "Dusun" encompassed approximately 138,000 individuals, of whom roughly 95 per cent spoke closely related communilects in geographically contiguous areas [[1, p. 133]](#ref-1). The term itself is a colonial and Malay administrative label that masks profound internal heterogeneity; the peoples concerned recognise their own distinct ethnic identities, and the most analytically productive division is between swidden-based and irrigation-based societies, each of which entails a distinct constellation of sociocultural traits from land tenure to residence patterns [[1, pp. 132–133]](#ref-1). The Dusun-speaking world spans a considerable range of ecological and economic adaptations, from the irrigated rice terraces of the Ranau and Tambunan valleys to the shifting-cultivation forests of the Kudat and Keningau districts, and from the riverine communities of the Segama and Klias to the highland settlements of the Lotud and Tuaran areas [[1, pp. 133]](#ref-1), [[2, pp. 61–62]](#ref-2).

### Scope, Definition, and Linguistic Affiliation

The label "Dusun" originated with Malay and then English voyagers who applied it to all peoples encountered between the coast and the mountainous interior of North Borneo, ignoring the self-conscious ethnic distinctions recognised by the peoples themselves [[1, p. 132]](#ref-1). The British colonial administration perpetuated the term as a demographic census category after assuming administrative control of North Borneo in 1882, and it has since leaked into ethnological literature [[1, pp. 132–133]](#ref-1). Appell and Harrison restrict their own use of the term to linguistic reference only, noting that even the degree of linguistic relatedness among these groups is not fully resolved: the Banggi Islanders, for instance, share less than 28 per cent of cognates in a list of basic vocabulary items with the Rungus, suggesting the Banggi language may be more closely related to Indonesian than to the Dusun languages proper [[1, p. 132]](#ref-1).

The earliest systematic linguistic account of the language was published by A. L. Gossens in 1924, who provided a descriptive grammar and an English-to-Dusun (Kadazan) vocabulary of approximately 4,000 to 5,000 headwords [[3, pp. 87–220]](#ref-3). Gossens, a Dutch national serving in British North Borneo, documented the phonological, nominal, and verbal systems of what he termed "Kadazan" (also called Dusun), noting the absence of a definite or indefinite article, the derivation of nouns from adjectives through the prefix *ka/ko* and suffix *-an*, and a verbal system distinguishing active, passive, and deponent voices, with the passive noted as more frequently used in practice [[3, pp. 88–95]](#ref-3). The lexicon reveals a language in active contact with Malay, Arabic, and English, with numerous loanwords integrated into the vocabulary, particularly in domains of religion, administration, and material culture [[3, pp. 96–220]](#ref-3). Gossens also recorded dialectal variation, noting that hill people pronounce *r* more strongly and insert it where lowland speakers at Papar do not [[3, p. 94]](#ref-3).

The question of ethnic origin has attracted occasional scholarly attention. In 1926, I. H. N. Evans published a critical note challenging Hewett's theory that the Dusuns of Borneo are of Chinese descent. Evans argued that the Dusun name "Nabalu" for Mount Kinabalu undermines the "Kina" (Chinese) etymology; that the dragon-and-jewel legend Hewett cited as evidence is a common Malay fairy tale rather than a Chinese-origin tradition; and that the remark about Chinese paternity claims was clearly sceptical, not supportive [[4, pp. 153–155]](#ref-4). Evans concluded that Hewett's account was "a jumble of 'tradition,' real history, supposition and arguments from very slender premises" [[4, p. 155]](#ref-4). An editorial remark appended to the note affirmed that the Dusuns are Indonesians of Mongoloid stock with affinities to the Chinese but no historical descent from them, and that the Dusuns themselves do not claim Chinese origin [[4, p. 155]](#ref-4).

### Subsistence, Settlement, and Material Culture

The introduction of wet rice agriculture—likely introduced by Chinese or Coastal Muslim traditions in the remote past—constitutes the single most consequential external factor in the cultural history of the Dusun-speaking peoples, because it triggered a cascade of changes: settlement shifted from the long-house to clustered family dwellings, land ownership moved from village-held rights to individual rights, and residence patterns began to shift from uxorilocal toward virilocal or utrolocal arrangements [[1, pp. 133]](#ref-1). Mean size of local communities reflects this division: irrigation-based societies sustain populations of 400 to 1,000 persons, while swidden-based communities average 200 to 399 [[1, p. 139]](#ref-1). Subsistence economy estimates indicate that agriculture accounts for 66 to 75 per cent of subsistence in both types of society; animal husbandry contributes 26 to 35 per cent; gathering accounts for 0 to 5 per cent in irrigation-based and 6 to 15 per cent in swidden-based communities; and fishing contributes 6 to 15 per cent in irrigation-based and 0 to 5 per cent in swidden-based settings [[1, p. 136]](#ref-1).

Material culture documentation extends to specific artefacts and technologies. F. H. Fitch described the *alud*, a Dusun dug-out boat on the Segama River, which features a permanent seat (*konkod*) carved as an integral part of the hull at roughly three-foot intervals, with bamboo slats and split rattan lashed across the carved supports [[5, p. 211]](#ref-5). Before descending the severe upper Segama rapids, a temporary bark superstructure (*tambir* in Dusun, *dinding* in Malay) is lashed to a sapling framework (*gagalang*); the bark is taken from *majau* or *lampong* trees, identified by H. G. Keith as *Shorea leptoclados*, *Pentace* sp., *Pterospermum* spp., or possibly *Dryobalanops* sp. [[5, p. 211]](#ref-5). The practice is widespread on East Coast rivers and Kinabatangan tributaries, with material quality varying from bark to palm leaves, and on the Sembakong River the *tambir* are made of soft wood as a more permanent feature [[5, p. 212]](#ref-5).

In the domain of domestic architecture, I. H. N. Evans documented the "house-horns" found on Dusun gable ends, which in most cases are simply untrimmed upward projections of the rafters [[6, p. 165]](#ref-6). The local term *sinasangau* (from *sangau*, "horn") may derive from *kinasangau*, meaning "there were in past time horns," suggesting an earlier use of actual animal horns in the same position [[6, p. 165]](#ref-6). Wealthier households commission carved wooden pairs, as illustrated by a plate from Mangkahak village where the decorative pattern is called *kinagai-kagai* (from *kagai*, "branch") [[6, p. 165]](#ref-6). Evans extended the comparison across a wide geographic range, identifying the Hova Malagasy term *tandro trano* and the Malay *tandok rumah* as etymologically identical, confirming a shared Malayo-Polynesian lexical root for the feature [[6, p. 166]](#ref-6). He advanced the hypothesis that house-horns operated as talismans against evil spirits, grounding this in Dusun religious practice where the roof-beam (*tiningusan*) is understood as an assembly point for spirits called *Koolungung* [[6, p. 167]](#ref-6).

A further dimension of material culture is the message-stick communication system (*tatanda* or *tatanduk*) used by forest-dwelling Dusun and Murut groups in Sabah. P. A. Burrough, based on fieldwork conducted between 1970 and 1972 across southern and central Sabah, documented a versatile, widely distributed non-literal communication system whose remarkable uniformity across linguistically distinct groups scattered over approximately 10,000 km² of forest suggests a very long history of continuous use [[7, pp. 119–123]](#ref-7). The support stick is a vertical pole one to three metres long, stuck in the ground beside forest tracks, with clefts cut to hold carved twigs, leaves, or bark symbols [[7, p. 120]](#ref-7). Signs convey information about hunting successes, land claims and boundary rights, warnings of traps and disease, wedding announcements with detailed bride-price inventories, and communications to the spirit world at graves and altars [[7, pp. 120–121]](#ref-7). The system's geographic and social reach is sharply stratified: the forest-dwelling Labau Dusun use it widely, while the Ranau, Tambunan, and coastal Kadazan Dusun do not know of it at all [[7, p. 122]](#ref-7).

### Social Organisation, Ritual, and Cosmology

The question of descent groups has been contested. Appell and Harrison reviewed Williams' claims of cognatic descent among the Tambunan Dusun and Glyn-Jones' reference to the *bongkawan*, and concluded that no functioning descent groups exist in the Dusun-speaking societies studied, though descent constructs are used for property rights [[1, pp. 137–139]](#ref-1). A significant shared cultural trait across all Dusun-speaking groups is the value premise that sexual relations are potentially deleterious unless properly entered into through marriage institutions; Appell argues this premise provides the rationale for bride-price, though he acknowledges contradictory evidence from Harrison regarding the Ranau and from Evans regarding the Tempasuk Dusun-speakers [[1, pp. 141–142]](#ref-1). The British outlawed the previously existing incipient, nonhereditary form of slavery after assuming administrative control of North Borneo in 1882 [[1, p. 141]](#ref-1).

Ritual and cosmological life is documented in several records. T. R. Williams published in 1960 a full-cycle translated account of a Tambunan Dusun origin myth, collected at Sensuron between September and December 1959. Williams argued that the myth is not merely cosmological speculation but a functional repository of value statements—ethical commands that structure daily social life and are transmitted to children as guides for personal behaviour [[8, pp. 95–103]](#ref-8). The narrative's final movement maps the dispersal episode onto the group's actual seven-sib social structure: Too/how/un, Tah/gas, Tiba/bah/bar, Bundu, Gun/ah, Pal/lu/puh, and Koh/hub (with Kureo as a later offshoot) [[8, p. 103]](#ref-8). Three of these groups were still identifiable in the Tambunan area prior to 1905, when they engaged in mutual head warfare, and the Bundu group is the most geographically widespread, extending from the Klias River to the Labuk headwaters [9, p. 103, n. 4]. The myth prescribes that before hunting in the jungle, omens must be read and food offerings made to Sar/oh, the devil who governs forest animals and birds and still hunts men with his blow-pipe to avenge the death of his children [[8, p. 103]](#ref-8).

The 2015 Mount Kinabalu earthquake provided a rare opportunity to document Dusunic ritual practice in response to a non-agricultural, non-epidemic calamity. J. J. Baptist and Cai Yunci examined the Monolibabou consultation and Tumabur cleansing rituals conducted by the Lotud people, a Dusunic ethnic group living in Tuaran District, to appease the guardians of Mount Kinabalu [[2, pp. 61–78]](#ref-2). The Lotud cosmological system holds that the physical and spiritual realms exist in parallel and must be kept in balance; immoral or disrespectful behaviour in the physical world renders the spiritual world too "hot" (*alasu*), requiring ritual intervention (*sogit*) to restore coolness and equilibrium [[2, pp. 61–62]](#ref-2). The Monolibabou consultation ritual, conducted on the evening of 13 June 2015 at Kampung Bantayan, established contact with the seven named guardians of Mount Kinabalu—Gunsangod, Singkarad, Rangkai, Liso, Rongko, Tanggung, and their leader Raja Gugur—shortly before midnight [[2, pp. 66–71]](#ref-2). The subsequent Tumabur cleansing ritual, performed on 16 June 2015, lasted five hours with nine *tantagas* (ritual priestesses) and one *libabou* (spirit medium) participating [[2, pp. 71–74]](#ref-2). The authors note that fewer than twenty living *tantagas* and *libabou* remain, and that religious conversion and urbanisation have eroded the social conditions that sustain these practices [[2, p. 76]](#ref-2). Additional cleansing ceremonies were conducted by a Dusunic group in Ranau on 27 June 2015 and by the Kadazandusun Cultural Association on 7 July 2015, followed by a dozen more by other Dusunic groups across Sabah [[2, p. 75]](#ref-2).

A further linguistic curiosity is recorded by Evans, who reported that at Kadamaian, a village near Kota Belud, the ordinary Dusun word for "female" (*tonduk*) is replaced by *kigonob*, meaning "skirted" or "the skirted ones," derived from *gonob* (the dark blue skirt worn by Dusun women) with the prefix *ki* equivalent to Malay *ber* [[9, p. 180]](#ref-9). The word is applied to both women and animals, producing the striking phrase "a skirted dog" [[9, p. 180]](#ref-9). Evans considered two possible explanations—slang or taboo substitution—and supported the latter by citing well-documented Dusun taboo practices surrounding in-laws' names, in which speakers substitute alternative words to avoid forbidden terms [[9, pp. 180–181]](#ref-9). However, enquiries with the Kadamaian headman and an elder who consulted his blind mother, a priestess, yielded no specific account of why the word differs at that village; the answer given was simply that it had always been so [[9, p. 181]](#ref-9).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on the Dusun-speaking peoples spans nearly a century and reflects shifting disciplinary priorities. The earliest contributions are linguistic: Gossens' 1924 grammar and vocabulary [[3]](#ref-3) and the 1897 Kimanis vocabulary [[10]](#ref-10) represent the colonial-era practice of documenting indigenous languages for administrative and missionary purposes. Gossens' work is notable for its attention to productive derivational morphology and for acknowledging internal dialectal variation within Kadazan, making it one of the earliest texts to do so [[3, pp. 87–220]](#ref-3).

The 1920s and 1930s saw the emergence of ethnographic and historical inquiry. Evans' 1926 note on the Dusuns and the Chinese [[4]](#ref-4) represents an early critical engagement with racial-origin theories, while his 1949 article on Dusun customary law [[11]](#ref-11) and his 1951 comparative study of house-horns [[6]](#ref-6) reflect the mid-century interest in material culture and legal anthropology. Evans' 1952 note on the Kadamaian term for "female" [[9]](#ref-9) illustrates the value of brief field observations in revealing the operation of taboo systems.

The 1950s and 1960s marked a transition toward more systematic ethnographic methodology. Williams' 1960 publication of the Tambunan Dusun origin myth [[8]](#ref-8) introduced the full-cycle translation procedure—phonetic transcription, English translation, back-translation, and phonetic matching—as a means of preserving the internal logic of a myth without filtering it through Western categorical assumptions. His work was conducted under a United States National Science Foundation grant and represented an early phase of a larger study of Dusun cultural structuring [[8, p. 95]](#ref-8). Appell and Harrison's 1968 ethnographic profiles [[1]](#ref-1) constituted a direct corrective to Murdock's 1967 *Ethnographic Atlas* and to Williams' identification of cognatic descent groups, arguing instead for the analytical primacy of the swidden/irrigation distinction.

The 1970s and subsequent decades saw the documentation of communication systems and ritual practice. Burrough's 1975 article on message sticks [[7]](#ref-7) corrected the near-total absence of systematic documentation of Sabahan message sticks in the colonial-era ethnographic literature and provided the first comparative account spanning six distinct linguistic groups within a single region. Baptist and Cai Yunci's 2016 article on the Mount Kinabalu earthquake rituals [[2]](#ref-2) represents the most recent contribution, documenting a previously unrecorded application of the Tumabur ritual to a non-agricultural calamity and capturing the inter-ethnic negotiation of ritual responsibility between the Lotud of Tuaran and the Kadazandusun of Ranau. The article also highlights the precarious state of Lotud ritual knowledge, with fewer than twenty living ritual specialists remaining [[2, p. 76]](#ref-2).

Across this body of work, several recurring themes emerge: the problem of the "Dusun" label as a homogenising category; the tension between colonial administrative classification and indigenous self-identification; the significance of wet rice agriculture as a transformative external factor; and the ongoing challenge of documenting ritual and cosmological knowledge in the face of religious conversion and urbanisation.
