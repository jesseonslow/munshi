# Jan Samuel Timmerman-Thijssen

Jan Samuel Timmerman-Thijssen (1782–1823) served as the Dutch Governor of Malacca from July 1818 until his death on 15 January 1823, at the age of forty. His tenure coincided with the Dutch final struggle against British efforts to establish Singapore and control trade through the Malacca Strait, a period in which the once-great entrepôt had fallen into decline following the razing of its fort, A Famosa, during the British interregnum of 1795–1817 [[1, pp. 9–10]](#ref-1). No official inquiry was ever conducted into the circumstances of his death, and the *East India Register and Directory* did not gazette it until 1825 [[1, pp. 10]](#ref-1).

### Early Career and Financial Conduct

Born at Hoorn in the Dutch Republic in October 1782, Timmerman-Thijssen had become a wine merchant in Leiden by the age of twenty [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1). His first marriage in 1802 to Hendrina E. Meijer, daughter of a trader who had made a fortune in Tranquebar, brought him into a family whose wealth was subsequently lost during the wars [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1). After his wife's death in 1806, he married Gesina Couperus in Calcutta in 1809; she was the eldest daughter of Abraham Couperus, the former Governor of Malacca (1788–95) [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1).

During the British occupation of Java (1811–1816), Timmerman-Thijssen was appointed magistrate in Batavia and formed the trading firm Timmerman-Thijssen and Westermann, acting as agents for the Calcutta firm Palmer & Co [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1). He cultivated close relations with Stamford Raffles, the British Lieutenant-Governor of Java, who described him as 'a merchant of considerable respectability, and strongly attached to the British Government' [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1). However, the partnership was declared bankrupt in March 1815 [[1, p. 14]](#ref-1). A pattern of financial impropriety emerged early: in Java he borrowed 5,000 Spanish dollars from the Orphan Fund without paying interest, and in Malacca he borrowed 250,000 guilders from the same fund, by March 1822 having 'confiscated what remained of the fund, leaving only a small residual to meet emergency commitments' [[1, pp. 13]](#ref-1).

### Political Interventions and Enmities

Timmerman-Thijssen's governorship generated significant political friction. In December 1822, he and Adriaan Koek led a Dutch contingent that forcibly seized the Johore royal regalia from Princess Hamidah, enabling the investiture of Abdul Rahman as Sultan and reducing Tunku Hussein to ruler of Singapore alone [[1, p. 17]](#ref-1). The chieftain Arong Bilawa of the Riau Bugis, whose people had clashed repeatedly with Dutch trade controls, fled to Singapore in February 1820 with approximately 500 followers after the British Resident refused to hand him over to the Dutch [[1, p. 19]](#ref-1). The trader Hendrik Kraal, a former Dutch East India Company employee who had become an independently successful merchant, was one of the last persons to see the Governor before his death [[1, p. 20]](#ref-1).

### Circumstances of Death

In his final act of office around 13 January 1823, Timmerman-Thijssen directed the Secretary, Eduard van Angelbeek, to draft a letter requesting that 400 piculs of tin be secretly forwarded to Hendrik Kraal to settle a debt [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1). The request could not be fulfilled because the Malay tin trader had just died, and the Governor died one or two days later [[1, pp. 21–22]](#ref-1). His wife Gesina's death notice in the *Bataviasche Courant* stated he had been 'bedridden for only three days' and requested no correspondence or condolences [[1, p. 22]](#ref-1). Timmerman-Thijssen had suffered recurring ill-health throughout his career, including shingles, chest tightness, stomach ailments, and cholera morbus in 1821 [[1, p. 23]](#ref-1). Between May 1825 and January 1828, six individuals connected to him by debt and marriage died in succession, a pattern Kraal notes raises further questions [[1, pp. 24–25]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

Diane Kraal's 2010 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* constitutes the first extended English-language study of Timmerman-Thijssen's administrative and financial conduct, drawing on Dutch archival material translated for the first time from the Ministerie van Kolonien records at The Hague and the India Office Records at the British Library [[1, pp. 9]](#ref-1). Prior English-language treatments by Brian Harrison and C. Mary Turnbull gave the Governor only minor coverage [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1). Kraal's approach is deliberately balanced, presenting evidence of questionable circumstances alongside documentation of chronic ill-health, and explicitly declining to draw firm conclusions on the cause of death [[1, pp. 10]](#ref-1).
