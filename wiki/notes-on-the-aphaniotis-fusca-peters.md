# Notes on the Aphaniotis fusca (Peters

G. Hope Sworder's 1929 monograph on *Aphaniotis fusca* (Peters) presents a detailed morphological and behavioural account of this small arboreal lizard based on 17 specimens from the Malay Peninsula and the Natuna Islands. The paper corrects a significant error in Boulenger's 1912 generic description by documenting the presence of a gular sac, and it establishes head-scale variation across localities as the most promising character for future subspecific differentiation.

## Summary

Sworder's study addresses two interrelated problems in the taxonomy of *Aphaniotis fusca*: the apparent contradictions between his own observations and all previous descriptions of the species, and the question of whether geographic variation within the range warrants subspecific recognition. He resolves the first by attributing the discrepancies to sex-based differences and to the loss of the gular sac in preserved female specimens after prolonged immersion in spirit. The second problem he frames as an open question, arguing that the scales bordering the rostral and nasal show the most striking variation and are the most likely basis for future subspecific splits, while explicitly inviting other workers with access to additional material to contribute detailed descriptions and drawings.

The paper is grounded in a carefully measured series of 16 fully adult specimens (7 males, 9 females) and one immature female, of which 11 were collected by Sworder himself at Gemas, Negeri Sembilan, in July 1928 and examined both in life and after preservation. The remaining specimens were loaned from the Raffles Museum, Singapore. Beyond morphology, Sworder provides valuable natural-history observations: the species is active and arboreal, often found on tree trunks 6 to 20 feet above the ground, capable of springing four to five feet sideways between trunks in a manner he likens to *Draco*, and readily confused with that genus at a distance. Reproductive data show that nearly all gravid females carried two fully developed oval eggs measuring approximately 18 mm by 7 mm.

### Key Findings

- The presence of a well-developed gular sac in males (and a smaller one in females) necessitates correction of Boulenger's 1912 generic description, which stated "no gular pouch" (p. 328).
- Males have irides of bright blue with a cobalt-blue interior of the mouth; females have gold-brown irides and two dark bands between the eyes, making the sexes distinguishable "infallibly even at some distance" (p. 329).
- Total length ranges from 167 mm (P. Tioman female) to 228 mm (North Natuna male); the adpressed hind limb exceeds the snout by 2.6–12 mm in males and by a smaller margin in females (pp. 328, 332).
- Gravid females (all but one of the Gemas series, plus the P. Tioman specimen) contained two fully developed eggs, oval and approximately 18 mm × 7 mm (p. 329).
- Head-scale variation is most pronounced in the rostral–nasal region: the Gemas series shows a normal arrangement of three scales above the rostral, while the Sedili (Johore) and P. Tioman specimens each have a single large scale, and the Parit (Perak) specimen has a rosette-shaped group of five scales (pp. 330–331).
- The Parit, Perak specimen is distinguished by more strongly keeled scales throughout, a nuchal crest on a slight nuchal fold, and a greenish colour in spirit, setting it apart from all other localities (p. 331).

### Conclusion

Sworder's definitive contribution is the demonstration that *Aphaniotis fusca* possesses a gular sac—a character previously overlooked—and that the species exhibits meaningful geographic variation in head-scale architecture. He deliberately refrains from erecting subspecies, instead providing detailed camera-lucida drawings and measurements as a framework for future workers to build upon, with the expectation that combined results from multiple localities will eventually permit "definite conclusions" (p. 330).

## Context

- The study draws on the Raffles Museum collection, Singapore, and Sworder's own field material from Gemas, Negeri Sembilan (July 1928); the Gemas series was presented to the museum by the author.
- The paper represents an early example of systematic herpetological documentation in the Malay Peninsula, employing camera-lucida illustration at magnified scale to resolve fine-scale morphological variation—a methodological approach that anticipated later subspecific revisions of the genus.
