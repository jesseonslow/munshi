# The Guillemard-Maxwell power struggle, 1921–1925

Yeo Kim Wah's 1981 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the bitter administrative power struggle between Governor-High Commissioner Sir Laurence Guillemard and Chief Secretary of the Federated Malay States Sir William George Maxwell during 1921–1925. Set against the backdrop of the post-war trade recession and the structural tensions between the Crown Colony of the Straits Settlements and the protectorate of the FMS, the article argues that the conflict was fundamentally a dispute over the constitutional role of the Chief Secretary and the degree of direct control the High Commissioner should wield over FMS policy—a struggle more strident and wide-ranging than any between their predecessors or successors.

## Summary

The article traces how two men of contrasting backgrounds and temperaments collided over the governance of the FMS. Guillemard, a career Treasury official with no prior colonial experience, arrived in Malaya in February 1920 determined to treat the Straits Settlements and the FMS as a single administrative unit under his direct control. Maxwell, by contrast, was a third-generation Malayan public servant with nearly thirty years of local experience, who conceived of the Chief Secretary as the real head of a quasi-independent FMS government under the Treaty of Federation. The 1920–21 trade recession, which plunged the FMS into acute financial crisis, sharpened the conflict: Guillemard blamed his predecessors for fiscal mismanagement and used the emergency to consolidate his own authority, while Maxwell, already resentful of Whitehall's decision to equalise the Colonial Secretary's salary with his own and to strip him of the Class Two uniform, interpreted every move from Singapore as an encroachment on his constitutional position (pp. 2–8).

The struggle played out across multiple fronts. The most public was the campaign to restore the title of Resident-General, petitioned by the Unofficials of the Federal Council in September 1921 and endorsed by Maxwell, which Guillemard and the Colonial Office rejected on grounds of unity of control. Behind this lay a series of administrative disputes—the appointment of a joint Financial Adviser, the proposed Malayan Medical Service, the creation of joint departments, the ranking of the Chief Secretary relative to the Malay Rulers and the General Officer Commanding—each of which Maxwell used to exclude the High Commissioner from FMS affairs and each of which Guillemard used to demonstrate the Chief Secretary's insubordination. The article draws extensively on Colonial Office despatches (CO 717, CO 273), personal correspondence between the two men, Federal Council proceedings, and the Milner Papers to reconstruct the shifting alignments of support among the Residents, the Unofficials, the Malay Rulers, and Whitehall officials (pp. 7–22).

The turning point came in August 1924, when Sultan Iskandar of Perak visited the Colonial Office and appealed for radical decentralisation that would effectively abolish the Chief Secretaryship as then constituted. Having already lost Whitehall's confidence through his agitation and insubordination, Maxwell was caught between the Rulers' demand for devolution and Guillemard's counter-move to seize the decentralisation initiative for himself. Within months, Guillemard's radical decentralisation programme was approved by the new Secretary of State L.S. Amery, and Maxwell was ordered to retire prematurely in March 1925 (pp. 17–22).

### Key Findings

- Guillemard formulated an FMS budget exceeding $114 million for 1921 to fund a large development programme in health, public works, irrigation, and civil service salaries, a plan that became untenable when the trade recession deepened into an acute financial crisis (p. 3).
- The FMS, as a protectorate of sovereign Malay states, was constitutionally unable to float a £10 million loan in the London market; the Straits Settlements was compelled to raise it as trustee security, a situation that exacerbated long-standing inter-territorial jealousy and was perceived by FMS Unofficials as derogatory to the Federation's status (p. 4).
- The Colonial Office equalised the Colonial Secretary's monthly salary at $2,200 to match the Chief Secretary's, and ruled that both should wear only the Class Three uniform, a decision that Maxwell deeply resented as a further degradation of his position (p. 8).
- The Colonial Office delivered its final rejection of the Resident-General petition on 17 May 1923, with Secretary of State Devonshire affirming that "unity of responsibility and control in the hands of the High Commissioner is of the first importance for the well-being and development of Malaya as a whole" (p. 10).
- In October 1924, Pountney as chairman of the Federal Council Finance Committee moved to scale the 1925 FMS budget down from $81 million to $73 million; Guillemard overruled Maxwell's objection, and Maxwell was subsequently censured for "frequent carelessness and inaccuracy" in his financial memorandum (pp. 21–22).
- Maxwell was officially ordered to retire prematurely on 17 March 1925, two years before his normal retirement age of fifty-five, because of his opposition to the Guillemard decentralisation policy (p. 22).

### Conclusion

Yeo Kim Wah concludes that the Guillemard-Maxwell struggle was less a clash of policy than a collision of personality and constitutional principle, in which the greenhorn Governor-High Commissioner, backed by Whitehall's growing antipathy toward the Chief Secretary, systematically eroded Maxwell's authority until the latter was forced from office. Maxwell's premature retirement, however, proved not to be his final defeat: he remained in Malaya until June 1926 and contributed significantly to the overall defeat of the Guillemard decentralisation policy, suggesting that the constitutional balance of the FMS ultimately resisted the High Commissioner's vision of unified territorial control.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Colonial Office series CO 717 and CO 273 (The National Archives, Kew), the Milner Papers, the National Library of Singapore Collection, and Selangor Secretariat Files, providing a detailed reconstruction of the despatch correspondence and personal minutes that underpinned the power struggle.
- Historiographically, the article fills a gap in the literature on FMS constitutional development by foregrounding the personal and political dimensions of the Chief Secretary–High Commissioner relationship during a critical transitional period, and by demonstrating how the 1920s financial crisis and the question of decentralisation were inextricable from the interpersonal dynamics at the top of the Malayan administration.
