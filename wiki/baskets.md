# Baskets

Baskets constitute one of the most widespread and functionally diverse categories of woven artefact in the material cultures of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo, serving roles that range from domestic storage and transport to ceremonial display and social signalling. The archival record of the Society's journals documents basketry traditions across a broad geographic span—from the Straits Settlements to the interior highlands of Sarawak and British North Borneo—revealing a craft tradition of considerable technical sophistication and symbolic depth. The evidence indicates that basket-making was not a uniform regional practice but a mosaic of locally distinct techniques, materials, and pattern vocabularies, each embedded in specific social and economic contexts.

## Scope and Technical Definition

The term "basket" in the published record encompasses a range of woven containers distinguished by their construction method, material, and intended use. In the Straits Settlements, R.N. Bland documented the *anyam gila* tradition at Tanjong Kling, Malacca, a technique characterised by a deliberately irregular or "crazy" interlacing pattern that sets it apart from more regular basket-weaving methods in the region [[1, pp. 1–7]](#ref-1). The practice was geographically concentrated at Tanjong Kling, indicating a localised craft tradition rather than a widespread regional one [[1]](#ref-1).

In Sarawak, J.C. Swayne recorded the Rejang basket, known locally as *bakan* or *tepoko*, produced by the Seduan and Kenauit peoples of the Rejang River [[2]](#ref-2). While the Rejang basket served as the standard household form across all Kelamantan tribes, the specific name was reserved for the products of these two groups, whose work had been exported for many years and was well known beyond Sarawak [[2]](#ref-2). The average dimensions recorded were eight inches in diameter at the bottom, twelve inches at the top, and eight inches in height [2, p. 182, n. 5].

In British North Borneo, G.C. Woolley surveyed the basketry of the Murut peoples across the Tenom, Keningau, and Pensiangan districts, identifying it as the most developed of their indigenous arts and crafts [[3]](#ref-3). The principal materials across all three regions were rotan (rattan), bamboo, and *bemban* (a palm fibre), supplemented in some cases by *kajang* [[3]](#ref-3). The dye palette varied: Swayne recorded dragon's blood (*jernang*), obtained from the fruit of a rotan, as the source of red dye, and tar purchased from Chinese traders as the source of black [[2, p. 181]](#ref-2). Woolley noted a broader palette including black, red, yellow, and purple, explaining how the interplay of natural fibre colour and dyed strands produced the visual effects of the patterns, including a damask-like quality in plain white work visible only under oblique light [[3]](#ref-3).

## Production and Craft Technique

The manufacturing process documented by Swayne for Rejang baskets provides the most detailed account of production in the record. Men collected *bemban* (sitah) and various rotans, after which the wefts underwent a multi-stage preparation: soaking, splitting, pith removal in four distinct operations, and dyeing [[2]](#ref-2). The pith removal from *bemban* wefts involved four sequential operations: *asid* (cutting over a knife edge), *tuep* (second scraping with a notched blade), *mamoh* (pulling remaining fibres with the teeth), and *trah* (running a blade at right angles over the weft) [[2, pp. 181–182]](#ref-2). The weaving itself proceeded from the bottom upward on a mould constructed from an old rotan basket reinforced with split bamboo and sago palm spathe [[2]](#ref-2).

A notable technical distinction was recorded between the Seduan and Kenauit weavers: the Seduan picked up the upper weft (*matup*) with the right hand and placed the lower weft (*irah*) with the right, while the Kenauit did the reverse, causing bands of colour to run in opposite directions [[2, p. 182]](#ref-2). This difference in weaving hand produced a visible distinction between the two groups' products.

Woolley documented that right-angled plaiting was the universal rule in Murut basketry, with a maximum strand "jump" of six—no strand crossed more than six opposing strands without an intervening "one under" to secure it in place [[3, p. 290]](#ref-3). Patterns were adapted to fit given spaces by curving lines at edges rather than cutting them abruptly, and multiple patterns were frequently combined on a single article [[3]](#ref-3). Border patterns were catalogued separately, with local names recorded for each variant across the different districts, illustrating both a shared vocabulary and local divergence in nomenclature [[3]](#ref-3).

## Pattern Semantics and Social Meaning

The most significant scholarly contribution in the record concerns the symbolic content of basket patterns. Woolley observed that weaving was a women's occupation among the Murut, that men typically deferred to women for explanations, and that in many cases the original meaning of a design had been lost, surviving only as a named convention handed down with instructions for execution [[3]](#ref-3). Nevertheless, he recorded several etiological narratives attached to specific patterns: *Negimpong* ("Quarrel"), *Namboyunan* ("mad"), *Nagulalan* ("The Raider's"), *Nantuapan* ("The Meeting"), and *Pinangulun* ("man")—which encoded stories of betrothal refusal, madness, head-hunting, and social interaction [[3]](#ref-3). These narratives revealed a symbolic system in which geometric forms represented human figures, landscapes, and social relationships, though Woolley cautioned that variants of the same story circulated in different villages and that some attributions may be unreliable [[3]](#ref-3). Only two patterns in his corpus represented human beings: *Nantuapan* (four figures around a central circle) and *Pinangulun* (a single male figure, which when inverted became a fruit bat) [[3, p. 293]](#ref-3).

Swayne explicitly challenged Dr. Otis Mason's suggestion that time had obliterated all symbolic content in Malayan basketry, pointing to Woolley's earlier work as evidence that meaning persisted [[2]](#ref-2). He provided a complete catalogue of pattern names for one illustrated basket, reading from top to bottom, and listed the principal pattern for each of the remaining examples. The names drew on a wide range of referents—fish, bird ticks, bamboo shoots, rotan fishing hooks, pillar tombs, belt toggles, and even a grass used for tying bundles—suggesting a rich associative vocabulary embedded in the craft [[2, p. 183]](#ref-2). Among the recorded names were *Benaro dua* (twilled twos, named after a tying grass), *Ningir Kli* (feeler of a mud-fish), *Blaut* (a fish or an inferior spirit-world member), *Robong* (bamboo sprout), and *Benaro telau* (twilled threes) [[2, p. 183]](#ref-2).

## Economic and Social Dimensions

The economic dimensions of basketry are documented primarily through the Rejang tradition. Swayne noted that Seduan and Kenauit baskets had been exported for many years and were well known beyond Sarawak [[2]](#ref-2), indicating a commercial dimension to the craft. Most of the illustrated baskets in his article were held in the Cambridge Museum of Ethnology [[2, p. 189]](#ref-2), reflecting the role of colonial museum collecting in the preservation and circulation of these objects.

Among the Murut, Woolley recorded that the *tabir* sitting mat, made by men from all-black rotan with a superimposed white strand, commanded a price four or five times that of a small basket, the premium attributed to the fact that it was a man's work [[3, p. 290]](#ref-3). This price differential highlights the social valuation of gendered labour within the craft. In Pensiangan District, small mats measuring one foot by two feet or less were used as decorative hangings on house posts and walls, typically displayed on the occasion of a feast and left up afterwards [[3, p. 289]](#ref-3), indicating a ceremonial function for certain woven items.

Swayne closed his article with a brief comparative observation, noting the similarity in weave and pattern between Rejang baskets and those made in British Guiana and by the Chetamacki Indians of Louisiana, though he did not develop this point further [[2]](#ref-2). This aside reflects the era's interest in transoceanic cultural parallels.

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on basketry spans approximately three decades, from Bland's 1906 note in the *JSBRAS* to Woolley's 1929 survey in the *JMBRAS*, with Swayne's 1933 article extending the record into the early 1930s. The trajectory of this documentation reveals a clear shift in methodological ambition. Bland's piece is a brief ethnographic note of seven pages, consistent with the "notes" format common in the *JSBRAS* for recording single observations or practices [[1]](#ref-1). It documents a localised craft tradition at Tanjong Kling without attempting broader comparative or analytical frameworks.

Woolley's 1929 article represents a significant escalation in scope and analytical depth. At twenty-five pages, it functions as a full ethnographic survey covering multiple districts, multiple article types (mats, baskets, hats, and miscellaneous items), and a systematic treatment of pattern semantics [[3]](#ref-3). Woolley addressed the methodological difficulty of attributing patterns to a single ethnic group, noting the fluid boundaries between Murut, Dusun, and Dyak traditions and the likelihood of mutual borrowing [[3]](#ref-3). His work drew on direct field observation and oral testimony from informants in the Tenom, Keningau, Pensiangan, and Sipitang districts, supplemented by specimens in the Raffles Museum collection [[3]](#ref-3).

Swayne's 1933 article built directly on Woolley's foundation, explicitly engaging with the question of whether symbolic content in Malayan basketry had been lost to time—a question Woolley had raised but not fully resolved [[2]](#ref-2). Swayne's contribution was to provide a complete pattern catalogue with indigenous names and meanings for Rejang baskets, thereby demonstrating that the symbolic system persisted in a form that could be systematically recorded [[2]](#ref-2). His work also combined technical description of the full production cycle with the pattern catalogue, creating a more integrated account than either predecessor.

The three articles collectively document a craft tradition across the Malay Peninsula and Borneo that was, by the early twentieth century, already subject to the pressures of colonial museum collecting and commercial export. The archival record preserves technical details, pattern vocabularies, and social contexts that have since been lost or transformed, making these early publications of enduring value for the study of Malay and Bornean material culture.
