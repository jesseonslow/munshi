# Karayuki-San of Singapore, 1877–1941

J.F. Warren's 1989 article reconstructs the history of Japanese prostitutes (*karayuki-san*) in colonial Singapore from 1877 to 1941, arguing that their presence was structurally indispensable to the city's development as a "coolie town" and that their experiences must be understood through the intersecting forces of agrarian poverty in Kyushu, patriarchal family systems, and colonial sex regulation. The study positions the *karayuki-san* not as "fallen women" but as migrant labourers whose exploitation generated capital for both Singapore's economic expansion and Japan's modernisation.

## Summary

Warren frames the *karayuki-san* within a macro-social analysis that links three structural conditions: the extreme poverty of fishing and farming communities on Amakusa Island and the Shimabara Peninsula in northern Kyushu; the patriarchal family system that treated female children as negotiable property; and the demographic explosion in Singapore driven by Chinese coolie immigration, which created a severe gender imbalance and an insatiable demand for prostitutes. The article traces how the Meiji government's failure to address rural deprivation—despite the 1896 law restricting women's emigration—meant that famine years (notably 1905 and 1934) produced surges in the traffic, with girls as young as eleven sold by parents to procurers (*zegen*) for as little as 2–3 yen per head. Warren emphasises that the patriarchal ideology of filial piety often compelled the women themselves to honour contractual debts incurred by their families, binding them to prostitution even when they might otherwise have left.

The middle sections of the article map the organised infrastructure of the trade: the *zegen* networks (led by figures such as Muraoka Iheiji, Ginji Shibuya, and Tambaya), the smuggling routes via coal ships from Nagasaki through Hong Kong to Singapore, and the spatial organisation of brothels in the Malay Street (*suteretsu*) and Smith Street areas. Warren draws on Chinese Protectorate returns, Coroners' Inquests, water-meter attachment lists, and the 1877 Contagious Diseases Ordinance committee report to reconstruct the distribution and size of brothels, showing how Japanese houses (averaging 5–7 women) differed structurally from the larger Chinese establishments. He also documents the broader economic ecosystem that grew around the brothels—kimono shops, restaurants, banks, physicians, and shipping firms—arguing that the *karayuki-san* were at the "cutting edge" of Japan's economic expansion in the *nanshin*, with the Nagasaki post office alone handling over 200,000 yen annually in remittances.

The final section traces the abolition of licensed Japanese prostitution in Singapore in 1920, situating it within a prolonged internal Japanese debate between those who viewed the *karayuki-san* as a national economic asset (Fukuzawa Yukichi being the most prominent advocate) and those who regarded them as a national disgrace incompatible with Japan's aspiration to be recognised as a "civilised" power. Warren shows that the 1915 Chinese boycott of Japanese goods temporarily derailed abolition efforts because the government still depended on the foreign currency the women remitted, and that the imperial decree was only implemented once Japan's post-war trade surplus had rendered the *karayuki-san* economically surplus. The aftermath—sly brothels, venereal disease, repatriation without support, and social ostracism in Japan—forms the article's closing narrative.

### Key Findings

- By 1905, the Chinese Protectorate recorded 109 Japanese brothels housing 633 *karayuki-san* in Singapore; a broader 1906 estimate placed the total at 2,086, making Singapore the largest single concentration in the region (compared with Vladivostock at 1,087 and Batavia at 970) (pp. 58–59).
- Muraoka Iheiji, the most prominent *zegen*, claimed to have smuggled over 3,200 women from Japan to Singapore between 1890 and 1894, selling them for between 400 and 2,000 yen each, and earning an estimated $100,000 per year in profit (pp. 55, 58).
- In 1913, Consul Fujii cooperated with British authorities to deport 72 Japanese traffickers and pimps from Singapore; a second wave in 1914 saw four principal *zegen* arrested and expelled (p. 72).
- The 1920 imperial decree ordering the repatriation of all Japanese prostitutes overseas was implemented in Singapore with remarkable speed: more than nine-tenths of the *karayuki-san* were compelled to leave within several years, and by 1921 licensed Japanese prostitution had been formally abolished (pp. 74–75).
- A typical Japanese brothel in the *suteretsu* housed 5–7 women and generated approximately 18,000 yen annually in gross revenue; a single *karayuki-san* could earn up to 420 yen per month before the brothel keeper's 40% cut (pp. 65, 68).
- The 1901 Singapore census recorded only 578 Japanese females, yet the *Fukuoka Nichinichi* estimated the Japanese population at approximately 1,800, more than half of them *karayuki-san*—illustrating the extent of under-registration due to illegal entry (p. 58).

### Conclusion

Warren's definitive historical takeaway is that the *karayuki-san* were neither simply victims nor willing participants but migrant labourers caught between the structural poverty of rural Kyushu and the economic demands of colonial Singapore, whose exploitation simultaneously fuelled the city's growth and Japan's accumulation of foreign capital. Their abolition in 1920 was not driven by moral concern for the women but by Japan's desire to project an image of civilised modernity to the West once its post-war trade surplus had made their remittances economically unnecessary—a calculation that left a generation of women repatriated without support and socially ostracised in their home villages.

## Context

- Primary archival sources include Japanese Ministry of Foreign Affairs consular records (JMFA), Straits Settlements Annual Reports (Chinese Protectorate returns), Colonial Office dispatches (CO 273), the 1877 Contagious Diseases Ordinance committee report, Coroners' Inquests, and water-meter attachment lists from the Municipal Office.
- The article builds substantially on Yamazaki Tomoko's oral-history research (*Sandakan Hachiban Shookan*, 1972) and Mikiso Hane's economic history of rural Japan (*Peasants, Rebels and Outcastes*, 1982), applying a social-history and gender-specific framework to Singapore's colonial past that was then relatively underdeveloped in the historiography.
