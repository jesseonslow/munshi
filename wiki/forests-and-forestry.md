# Forests and forestry

The forests of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo have long constituted a central resource for the economic, cultural, and ecological life of the region, serving simultaneously as a source of timber, resin, fibre, and aromatic products, as a habitat for wildlife of international significance, and as the domain of indigenous and rural communities whose livelihoods depended upon forest access. Colonial forestry in the region evolved from early botanical surveys aimed at identifying commercially exploitable species to the establishment of formal conservation reserves and national parks in the 1920s and 1930s, a transition that generated sustained conflict between conservation authorities and local populations. The Society's literature documents this trajectory across more than a century, from Nathaniel Cantley's systematic enumeration of economic plants in the Straits Settlements in 1886 to M. Guérin's detailed account of the negotiations that shaped the King George V National Park in the late 1930s [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2).

### Economic Botany and the Colonial Forest Department

The earliest substantial treatment of forest resources in the Society's literature is Nathaniel Cantley's "Notes on Economic Plants, Straits Settlements," published in 1886 as an appendix to his Annual Report on the Forest Department [[2]](#ref-2). Cantley, who served as Superintendent of the Botanical Gardens in Singapore and held a senior position within the Forest Department, compiled the notes with a view to the encouragement of minor industries that could diversify the colony's economic base beyond its dominant exports of pepper, gutta-percha, and coffee [[2, p. 295]](#ref-2). The work constitutes a systematic colonial agricultural survey, organised by product category—fibres, oils, fruits, beverages, spices, roots and culinary vegetables, dyes, India-rubber and gums, drugs, and miscellaneous products—and draws on the holdings of the Singapore Experimental Nurseries, supplemented by data from Kew Gardens and the Jamaica sugar research station [[2]](#ref-2).

Among the fibre-producing plants surveyed, Cantley recorded that Mauritius hemp (*Furcraea gigantea*) commanded approximately £28 per ton in London, while Manila hemp (*Musa textilis*) fetched between £30 and £40 per ton, and common plantain or banana fibre was valued at about £15 per ton [[2, pp. 295–296]](#ref-2). He noted that lalang (*Imperata cylindrica*) had been found to produce good papermaking material, and that the quantity of material available for paper-making in the Straits, including bamboos, pineapple leaves, and wood, would seem to warrant the establishment of a permanent paper factory [[2, p. 296]](#ref-2). In the category of oils, Cantley identified *Melaleuca leucodendron* (kayu puteh) as a species of which whole forests existed in Malacca, yet little attention was being given to the manufacture of oil from the plant, despite its potential to yield a good margin of profit [[2, p. 299]](#ref-2). He also proposed that indigenous trees yielding wood oil and gutta-percha be planted as pepper supports, calculating that within approximately seven years the temporary supports could be removed and the trees would become a source of considerable revenue, probably greater than the pepper crop itself [[2, p. 299]](#ref-2).

Cantley's notes on gutta-percha (*Dichopsis gutta*) recorded that a plant in the nursery, planted in 1879, had reached twenty-five feet in height and twelve inches in circumference by 1886, yielding an average annual growth of approximately three and a half feet in height and one and a quarter inches in circumference [[2, p. 307]](#ref-2). He observed that the native creeping gutta varieties, particularly the *Willoughbeia* species, grew with great vigour on cleared land and would form large bushes within twelve months, making them more profitable to plant than the larger trees requiring some fifteen years to produce a first return [[2, p. 307]](#ref-2). White pepper was selling at $41 per picul in 1886, a price Cantley judged sufficient to stimulate large-scale European planting [[2, p. 304]](#ref-2). Sago palms (*Sagus rumphiana* and *S. laevis*) required rich swampy ground and became productive in approximately six years after planting from seed; on non-swampy land the seed seldom matured [[2, p. 309]](#ref-2).

The work also included a comprehensive tabular index of systematic names, local names, and native countries for the plants under cultivation or observation in the Straits Settlements, extending from *Allium cepa* to *Zingiber officinale*, and a separate taxonomic treatment of the palms of the region [[2, pp. 317–334]](#ref-2). The notes were reprinted in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* at the suggestion of W. E. Maxwell, who judged that the material deserved "wider publicity than can be obtained by incorporation in a volume of Proceedings of the Legislative Council" [[2, p. 293]](#ref-2).

### Forest Flora, Tree Dimensions, and Altitudinal Zonation

By the 1920s, the Society's literature had shifted from purely economic enumeration toward more rigorous botanical and ecological documentation. F. W. Foxworthy's short note "The size of trees in the Malay Peninsula," published in 1926, recorded the dimensions of the largest trees known from the Peninsula, covering both height and diameter or girth measurements [[3]](#ref-3). Foxworthy observed that while Peninsula trees did not approach the dimensions of the largest North American or Australian trees, they were comparable to the largest trees of other eastern tropical regions [[3, p. 382]](#ref-3). The tallest recorded tree was a Tualang (*Koompassia excelsa*) at 265 feet, measured by B. F. H. Barnard with a hypsometer, followed by a Kapur (*Dryobalanops aromatica*) at 220 feet; the average forest canopy height was a little over 150 feet [[3, pp. 382–383]](#ref-3). The largest tree by girth was a Chengal (*Balanocarpus heimii*) in the Parit Forest Reserve, Kinta District, Perak, at 40 feet 5 inches in girth (154 inches diameter) and an estimated 125 feet to the first branch [[3, p. 383]](#ref-3). The article concluded with a table of average and maximum diameters for various species, compiled from valuation surveys conducted over the preceding five years across most states of the Peninsula [[3, p. 383]](#ref-3).

C. F. Symington's "The flora of Gunong Tapis in Pahang: with notes on the altitudinal zonation of the forests of the Malay Peninsula," published in 1936, extended this line of inquiry by documenting the botanical composition of a specific mountain forest and its relationship to elevation [[4]](#ref-4). The study contributed to the understanding of how forest communities changed with altitude across the Peninsula, a question of direct relevance to both conservation planning and the management of forest reserves.

R. N. Bland's "Notes on kayu gharu," also published in 1886, documented the ethnobotanical and commercial aspects of agarwood as gathered from Malay and *Pawang* informants in Ulu Muar and Johol [[5]](#ref-5). Bland described the *tabak* tree (source of *gharu*), the process by which the *Pawang* located and harvested the diseased heart-wood, and the associated ritual formulae and taboos [[5, pp. 359–360]](#ref-5). He recorded eight diagnostic features for identifying *gharu*-bearing trees in the standing state, and classified eight varieties of the wood—*chandan*, *tandok*, *menjulong-ulong*, *sikat*, *sikat lampam*, *bulu rusa*, *kemandangan*, and *wangkang*—distinguished by density, colour, texture, and buoyancy in water [[5, p. 361]](#ref-5). The *chandan* and *tandok* varieties were noted as the most valuable, priced between $200 and $50 per *pikul*, and used in incense and aromatic oil production by both Chinese and Malay communities [[5, p. 361]](#ref-5).

### Conservation, Wildlife Reserves, and the Negotiation of Access

The most substantial treatment of forest management in the Society's literature is M. Guérin's "Protecting the Forest and Its Fauna against Local Residents in British Malaya," published in 2022 [[1]](#ref-1). The article examines how the creation of large wildlife reserves and the King George V National Park during the 1920s and 1930s generated sustained conflict with local populations who depended on forest resources for their livelihood, and how prolonged negotiations among the colonial administration, Malay sultans, and inhabitants ultimately shaped the regulations governing access to these protected areas [[1]](#ref-1).

The central figure in this narrative is Theodore Hubback, a former big game hunter turned game warden who designed the Krau and Gunung Tahan reserves and championed the National Park project [[1]](#ref-1). The Krau game reserve comprised 53,000 hectares and was gazetted in 1923; the Gunung Tahan reserve encompassed 143,000 hectares and was established in 1925 [[1, pp. 60–63]](#ref-1). The King George V National Park spanned over 4,300 square kilometres across Pahang, Terengganu, and Kelantan, and was formally constituted in 1938–39 [[1, pp. 60–63]](#ref-1). The 1921 Wild Animals and Birds Protection Enactment for the Federated Malay States had established game departments headed by European game wardens and enabled the creation of reserves and sanctuaries [[1, p. 60]](#ref-1).

Hubback's absolutist conservation stance, insisting on the complete exclusion of humans from protected zones, brought him into direct collision with the communities whose lives were disrupted [[1]](#ref-1). Guérin's core argument is that these protected areas were not simply imposed by colonial power but emerged from a complex process of negotiation in which the fault line ran not between the British and the Malays, but between those who defended local inhabitants' access to the forest and those who positioned themselves first and foremost as protectors of wildlife [[1]](#ref-1). The sultans, particularly Sultan Abu Bakar of Pahang, played a decisive mediating role: they supported the creation of the parks while simultaneously forcing the Game Department to recognise pre-existing rights of access, fishing, and collection [[1]](#ref-1).

Article 16 of the Royal Orders establishing the National Park, added at the insistence of the Sultans of Pahang and Terengganu, stipulated that the Trustees could authorise the continued enjoyment of pre-existing privileges—way, fishing, collecting produce—by communities living on or near the park borders [[1, pp. 19–20]](#ref-1). In 1935, Sultan Abu Bakar granted a *kuasa* (a formal letter of authority) to Batin Sidah permitting the Che Wong of his tribe to enter and leave the Krau Reserve at any point, in exchange for reporting poaching of big game [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1). The colonial government's financial contribution to the National Park was $500 per year; the Sultan of Kelantan ceded 198,300 acres (80,249 hectares) in perpetuity [[1, pp. 63–64]](#ref-1).

Guérin also demonstrated that the colonial administration's racialised categorisation of forest peoples paradoxically facilitated their access to the parks, as they were perceived as part of the natural world being preserved rather than as economic actors whose claims needed to be negotiated [[1]](#ref-1). This distinction in treatment between the Batek and Che Wong (who were allowed to live in the reserves, clear swiddens, and hunt small game) and the Malay villagers (who received more limited, permit-based privileges) was itself a product of the negotiations and was not a fixed given [[1]](#ref-1). Hubback was dismissed in 1936 after publishing open criticism of the administration's lack of support for wildlife conservation; his project was completed by Edward Oswald Shebbeare and High Commissioner Shenton Thomas in 1938–39 [[1, pp. 63–64]](#ref-1).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on forests and forestry reveals a clear evolution in scholarly priorities across more than a century. The earliest contributions, exemplified by Cantley's 1886 notes and Bland's contemporary study of *kayu gharu*, were fundamentally economic and ethnobotanical in orientation, aimed at identifying and promoting commercially exploitable species and documenting indigenous knowledge of forest products [[2]](#ref-2), [[5]](#ref-5). These works were produced by officers of the colonial Forest Department and Botanical Gardens, and their primary audience was the administrative and planting community of the Straits Settlements.

By the 1920s and 1930s, the focus had shifted toward ecological and botanical documentation proper. Foxworthy's measurements of the largest trees and Symington's study of altitudinal zonation on Gunong Tapis represent a more systematic, scientific approach to the forest as a natural system, informed by valuation surveys and field observation [[3]](#ref-3), [[4]](#ref-4). These works laid the groundwork for the conservation policies that would follow.

The most recent and most substantial contribution, Guérin's 2022 article, marks a decisive turn toward the social and political history of forest management [[1]](#ref-1). Drawing on archival correspondence between the sultans and British administrators, minutes of village meetings conducted by the District Officer for Lipis in 1939–40, and petitions from forest-dwelling peoples, the article challenges the persistent narrative of Malay sultans as passive instruments of British colonial rule, demonstrating instead their active role in shaping conservation policy and defending the interests of their subjects [[1]](#ref-1). It also contributes to the environmental history of colonial Malaya by centring the perspectives of forest-dwelling peoples and Malay villagers rather than treating them as passive recipients of colonial policy [[1]](#ref-1). The primary archival collections consulted include dossiers from the colonial archives of Kelantan, Pahang, and the Federated Malay States at the National Archives of Malaysia, and Colonial Office papers (CO 576, CO 717) at The National Archives, United Kingdom [[1]](#ref-1).

F. L. Dunn's 1975 study, "Rainforest Collections and Traders: A Study of Resource Utilization in Modern and Ancient Malaya," represents an intermediate phase in this trajectory, examining the commercial networks through which forest products moved from the interior to market, though the available record for this work is limited to its bibliographic entry [[6]](#ref-6).
