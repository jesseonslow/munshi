# Sir Frank Swettenham’s Perak journals, 1874–1876

C.D. Cowan edited and introduced five day-to-day journals kept by Frank Swettenham during his missions in Perak between April 1874 and February 1876, published in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1951. Set against the backdrop of the Pangkor Engagement and the escalating crisis that culminated in the murder of Resident J.W.W. Birch, the journals offer an unfiltered, ground-level account of British intervention in a Malay state whose political institutions the colonial administrators fundamentally misunderstood. Cowan's overarching thesis is that the journals reveal "the extent to which Birch and Swettenham, and probably their colleagues and superiors in the Civil Service, failed to perceive the true character or strength of the (to them) anachronistic institutions which they attempted to sweep away by Proclamation and 'advice'" (p. 3).

## Summary

The publication comprises a substantial introductory essay by Cowan (pp. 1–32) followed by the full text of five journals and appendices containing letters and documents cited in the text. Cowan's introduction reconstructs the political history of Perak from the mid-nineteenth century through the events of 1874–1876, tracing the succession disputes, the rise of the Mantri of Larut, the Pangkor Engagement of January 1874, and the progressive breakdown of relations between Resident Birch and Sultan 'Abdu'llah that led to the Perak War. He situates the journals within the broader transformation of British policy in the Malay Peninsula, arguing that the period 1874–1876 "marks a turning point in the history of Malaya, for in those years the disintegration of the old Malay States was first halted by British intervention" (p. 5).

The journals themselves alternate between the intimate observations of a young civil servant—his boredom on Pulo Pangkor, his shooting expeditions, his attempts to read the Koran with a local Penghulu—and the serious business of negotiating with recalcitrant Malay chiefs over the surrender of the Perak Regalia, the collection of revenues, and the acceptance of British administrative control. The First Journal (April–May 1874) records Birch and Swettenham's journey up the Perak River and their fruitless attempts to secure the Regalia from Ismail at Blanja. The Second Journal (June 1874) documents Swettenham's solo mission to persuade Raja Yusuf to travel to Penang, a task he accomplished through persistent personal diplomacy. The Third Journal (September–October 1875) follows Governor Jervois's expedition and the critical negotiations in which 'Abdu'llah was pressured into accepting British administration. The Fourth Journal (October–November 1875) ends abruptly at Blanja with the news of Birch's murder at Pasir Salak. The Fifth Journal (January–February 1876) is more fragmentary, recording the mopping-up operations and the tracking of fugitives in the military camps.

Cowan's editorial approach is conservative: he preserves Swettenham's inconsistent spellings of names, inserts minimal punctuation, and marks interpolations in square brackets. He notes that Swettenham's renderings of place-names "change from day to day—*Kinter* for instance becomes *Kintar* and finally *Kinta*" (p. 2), reflecting the early stage of his Malayan knowledge. The journals are thus presented as documents of a man still learning, whose observations, while sometimes naive, capture the texture of daily interaction between British officials and Malay political society in a period of profound upheaval.

### Key Findings

- The journals document the complete failure of the Pangkor Engagement's central mechanism: the "advice" system. By mid-1875, Birch's attempts to impose uniform revenue collection had alienated both 'Abdu'llah and the chiefs, who viewed taxation rights as personal property rather than state functions (pp. 22–23).
- Raja Yusuf, whom Cowan identifies as "the only important raja not implicated" in Birch's murder, was effectively invisible to the Straits Government before Swettenham's encounter with him at Senggang in April 1874; neither C.J. Irving nor Allan Skinner, both of whom had investigated Perak affairs, had mentioned his existence (pp. 53, 57).
- The Mantri of Larut, identified as the principal instigator behind Ismail's resistance, maintained a parallel diplomatic channel with the Maharaja of Johore, who sent Ismail a letter asserting that "Perak was his and could not be taken from him" (p. 123).
- Swettenham's Fourth Journal records that on 4 November 1875, at Blanja, he was informed of Birch's murder and immediately resolved to run the gauntlet of the Maharaja Lela's men downstream, writing "I can't turn back" (p. 122).
- The journals reveal that 'Abdu'llah's acceptance of British control on 29–30 September 1875 was extracted under the explicit threat that Yusuf would be made Sultan in his place; Birch withheld the Governor's conciliatory letter until after 'Abdu'llah had signed, then "congratulated" him on being allowed to retain the title (pp. 25–26).
- Cowan notes that the journals "do not add anything to our limited knowledge of the origins of British policy, and contribute little new that is important for the story of events in Perak in these years," but are "of value for the climate of opinion both among the Malay leaders and the British officials" (p. 3).

### Conclusion

Cowan's definitive historical takeaway is that the journals expose a fundamental misapprehension on the part of the British administrators: they treated the Perak succession and the chiefs' rights to levy taxes as problems of political legitimacy and economic efficiency to be resolved by proclamation, without grasping that these institutions constituted the actual social and economic fabric of Malay society. The "anachronistic institutions" that Birch and Swettenham sought to replace were, in Cowan's assessment, far more resilient and functionally integrated than the colonial officials appreciated, and their destruction by force in 1875–76 did not create the stable, centralized state that the Pangkor Engagement had promised.

## Context

- The journals are held in the Swettenham Collection of the Department of Museums, Federation of Malaya, and were microfilmed with the aid of the University of Malaya for this edition (p. 1).
- A variant text of the First Journal, in a different hand and believed to be a clerk's copy, is the property of the Association of British Malaya (p. 2).
