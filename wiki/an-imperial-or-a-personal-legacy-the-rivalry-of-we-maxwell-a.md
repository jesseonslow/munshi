# An imperial or a personal legacy? The rivalry of W.E. Maxwell and F.A. Swettenham in British Malaya

Mohamed Rashidi Pakri's 2011 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the professional rivalry between two senior colonial officers, W. E. Maxwell and F. A. Swettenham, during the formative decades of British rule in Malaya (1870s–1890s). Pakri argues that their personal antagonism shaped administrative appointments across the Straits Settlements and the Protected Malay States, and that their respective legacies—assessed through Maxwell's will and Swettenham's late-life political interventions—reveal a fundamental tension between selfless imperial service and the pursuit of personal historical memory.

## Summary

Pakri situates the Maxwell–Swettenham rivalry within the broader context of early British colonial consolidation in the Malay Peninsula, from the Pangkor Engagement of 1874 through the establishment of the Federated Malay States in 1895. The two men, both young colonial civil servants who arrived in the Straits Settlements within a few years of each other, found themselves repeatedly assigned to overlapping jurisdictions in Perak and Selangor. Their hostility, crystallized by the disputed Panjang Meru incident during the Perak War of 1875–7, forced the Colonial Office to separate them geographically—Maxwell to the Straits Settlements and Swettenham to the Protected Malay States—for much of the 1880s. The rivalry resurfaced when Maxwell was appointed Resident of Selangor to replace Swettenham, who had moved to Perak, and only ended with Maxwell's transfer to the Gold Coast in 1895.

The article's central analytical move is to juxtapose the two men's final acts against their earlier careers. Pakri draws on Maxwell's last will (dated 22 October 1897, less than two months before his death at sea) to argue that Maxwell was primarily an imperial servant who subordinated personal memory to institutional continuity—bequeathing his Malay manuscripts to the Royal Asiatic Society, passing family mementos to his son to sustain the Maxwell colonial tradition, and instructing the destruction of his private letters and diaries to protect both his reputation and the colonial narrative. Swettenham, by contrast, had no children and no family colonial lineage to perpetuate; his late-life opposition to the Malayan Union scheme and his 1946 "proconsuls" letter reveal a man fighting to preserve the federation he had built and to erase the memory of the coercive methods by which he had obtained the Malay rulers' signatures in 1895.

Pakri's source base is relatively narrow: H. S. Barlow's 1995 biography of Swettenham, Maxwell's will (provided by J. M. Gullick), the memorial inscription in Holy Trinity Cathedral, Accra, and the author's own interviews with Maxwell descendants and Gullick in London (2008). The article is less a revisionist intervention than a character study that uses biographical detail to illuminate the personal dimensions of imperial administration.

### Key Findings

- The Panjang Meru incident (1875–7), in which a fugitive was allegedly hanged without trial under Maxwell's jurisdiction in northern Perak, was the catalyst for the lasting hostility between the two men and directly damaged Brigadier General Ross's military career (p. 36).
- Maxwell's will, dated 22 October 1897, bequeathed his Malay books and manuscripts to the Royal Asiatic Society, Albemarle Street, and directed his niece Mildred Isemonger to burn his private letters and diaries "without examination or perusal" (pp. 39–40).
- Three generations of Maxwells served in Malaya for no less than seventy years: Sir Peter Benson Maxwell (Supreme Court Judge from 1856, Chief Judge until 1871), W. E. Maxwell (arrived 1865, died 1897), and Sir William George Maxwell (Perak Civil Service from 1891, retired 1926) (pp. 34, 40).
- Swettenham's "proconsuls" letter of 12 April 1946, written two months before his death, expressed "profound concern" at the manner in which the nine Malay rulers had been "invited" to sign Malayan Union treaties they "imperfectly understood," drawing a direct parallel to his own 1895 federation negotiations (pp. 41–42).
- Swettenham's Malayan artefact collection was sold under the terms of his will to the Malayan Government and is now held in the National Museum, Kuala Lumpur (p. 43).
- Maxwell Hill in Taiping, Perak, was renamed Bukit Larut, while Port Swettenham in Selangor became Port Klang; however, Port Swettenham on Penang Island retains its name (p. 43).

### Conclusion

Pakri's definitive takeaway is that Maxwell embodied the imperial legacy—selfless, institutionally oriented, and willing to suppress personal records to preserve the colonial narrative—while Swettenham, lacking a family to inherit his name, fought to the end of his long life to secure his personal legacy by defending the federation he had created and disavowing the coercive methods by which it was established.

## Context

- Primary sources: W. E. Maxwell's last will (via J. M. Gullick), the memorial inscription in Holy Trinity Cathedral, Accra, and H. S. Barlow's 1995 biography *Swettenham for Frank Swettenham*.
- Historiographical contribution: The article adds a biographical and psychological dimension to the existing administrative history of early British Malaya, drawing on oral history interviews (2008) with Maxwell descendants and Gullick to recover personal motivations behind well-known institutional events.
