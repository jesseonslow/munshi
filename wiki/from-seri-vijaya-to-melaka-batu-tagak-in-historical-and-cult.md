# From Seri Vijaya to Melaka: Batu Tagak in historical and cultural context

J.C. Moulton, Curator of the Sarawak Museum, published this taxonomic revision of the Bornean Papilionidae in 1914 as Part IV of a multi-part checklist of Bornean butterflies in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*. The paper provides a comprehensive species-by-species account of the Pierinae (whites) and Papilioninae (swallowtails) of Borneo, grounded in the Sarawak Museum collection, and advances a biogeographical argument that Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and Borneo share a closely related fauna distinct from that of Java.

## Summary

Moulton's paper is the fourth instalment of a systematic checklist of Bornean butterflies, following earlier parts on Nymphalidae and Lemoniidae by the late Shelford (1904–1905) and Lycaenidae by Moulton himself (1911). The present part treats the two sub-families of Papilionidae: the Pierinae, of which 35 species (plus 5 subspecies, totalling 40 named forms) are recognized, and the Papilioninae, of which 43 species are listed. The work is essentially a taxonomic revision that consolidates the scattered literature from Vollenhoven (1865) through Fruhstorfer's *Seitz's Macrolepidoptera of the World* (1910–1911), resolving numerous synonymies and reassigning forms previously treated as species to subspecific or varietal rank. Moulton introduces diagnostic keys for the genera and species of both sub-families, a departure from the format of the earlier parts, intended to aid field identification.

A significant portion of the paper is devoted to the problem of subspecific nomenclature in the Indo-Malayan region. Moulton criticizes the tendency of recent authors (particularly Fruhstorfer) to erect subspecies on trivial differences, arguing that this practice obscures rather than illuminates the close biogeographical affinities between the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, and Borneo. He proposes a notational system using lines of increasing length to indicate degrees of divergence from the type form, and repeatedly demonstrates through his Sarawak series that forms separated by Fruhstorfer as distinct subspecies are in fact connected by intermediates. Three new names are proposed: *Appias melania athena* form *ochracea* (a pale female form), *Papilio polytes theseus* form *ignea* (a female with a large yellow cell-spot), and *Papilio antiphates itamputi* ab. *aperta* (a male with divided outer black bands).

The biogeographical discussion draws heavily on Wallace's 1865 observations, which Moulton confirms and refines. Of the 43 Papilionine species in Borneo, only four are endemic; 35 also occur in Sumatra and 33 in the Malay Peninsula, but only 25 in Java. Moulton concludes that Java has been isolated long enough to develop a more generalized Indian fauna alongside its specialized Javan element, whereas the other three landmasses share an essentially specialized Malayan fauna. The paper also touches on mimicry, noting cases of Müllerian mimicry between *Prioneris* and *Delias*, and Batesian mimicry in the *Papilio paradox* and *Papilio macareus* groups, which imitate unpalatable *Euploea* and *Danaine* species respectively.

### Key Findings

- 35 species of Pierinae and 43 species of Papilioninae are recognized from Borneo, representing a reduction from Bartlett's 1896 count of 61 Papilionine species, due to the introduction of subspecific nomenclature and broader views on intraspecific variation (p. 33).
- Only 4 of the 43 Papilionine species are endemic to Borneo; 35 also occur in Sumatra, 33 in the Malay Peninsula, but only 25 in Java, confirming Wallace's 1865 observation of a close Sumatra–Borneo–Peninsular relationship and Java's relative isolation (pp. 33–34).
- *Papilio brookiana* (the Brooke's Birdwing) is recorded from Mt. Kinabalu up to 4,500 ft., Lawas, Limbang, Kuching, and Quop; a curious aberration with grey-fuscous costal margin is noted from Kinabalu (p. 39).
- *Papilio karna carnatus*, locally known as "the Sarawak Beauty," is recorded from Mt. Kinabalu, Limbang, Quop, Bau, Paku, and Kuching, preferring sunny clearings or streams in old jungle (p. 50).
- *Delias glauce* Butler, originally described from "Borneo," is re-assigned to Sumatra on the authority of N.D. Riley of the British Museum, who confirmed the type is registered as from Sumatra; Moulton argues it does not occur in Borneo at all (p. 9).
- *Papilio memnon memnon* (the typical form) is one of the very rare instances where Java and Borneo share the same form while Sumatra has a distinct race (*anceus*); four of the five known female forms in Borneo are not found elsewhere (p. 49).

### Conclusion

Moulton's definitive historical takeaway is that the butterfly fauna of Borneo is most closely allied to that of Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula, forming a coherent Malayan biogeographical unit that is sharply differentiated from Java. He argues that the over-splitting of subspecies by contemporary systematists obscures this fundamental pattern, and that a more conservative approach to nomenclature—one that recognizes continuous variation and intermediate forms—better reflects the actual biogeographical relationships of the region.

## Context

- The paper is based primarily on the Sarawak Museum collection, supplemented by references to the British Museum, the Raffles Museum (Singapore), and the Tring Museum collections.
- Moulton's work represents the culmination of a decades-long effort to establish a reliable checklist of Bornean butterflies, building on the foundational work of Wallace (1867), Druce (1873), Distant (1882–1886), and Fruhstorfer (1910–1911), and it remains a key reference for the pre-war entomological literature of British Borneo.
