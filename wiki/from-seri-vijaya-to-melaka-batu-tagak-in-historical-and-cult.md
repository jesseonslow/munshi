# From Seri Vijaya to Melaka: Batu Tagak in historical and cultural context

John N. Miksic's 1987 Annual Lecture to the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society argues that the upright stone monuments (*batu tagak*) of West Sumatra and peninsular Malaysia are not prehistoric megaliths but products of the classical era (c. AD 1000–1500), erected in the context of the political transformations that followed Srivijaya's decline, Adityawarman's court migration into the Minang highlands, and the eventual founding of Melaka. The article synthesizes fieldwork in the Limapuluh Koto and Tanahdatar regencies with epigraphic and historical evidence to propose that these stones mark a period of social differentiation driven by gold extraction and the spread of Malay political culture from estuarine centres into the interior (pp. 1–2).

## Summary

Miksic opens by situating the problem within the aftermath of the 1973 London Colloquy on Early South East Asia, which dismantled the older framework that classified all Southeast Asian megaliths as prehistoric and culturally uniform. Fifteen years later, no replacement framework had been proposed, and the term "megalith" itself had fallen into disuse. Miksic contends that the stones represent "systems of belief expressed in symbolic form" whose code can be recovered if sufficient sites are documented and their historical context established (pp. 1–2).

The bulk of the article is devoted to describing the West Sumatran sites in detail. In the Limapuluh Koto regency, Yuwono Sudibyo located 69 groups of stone monuments in the Guguk district alone, comprising roughly 1,000 individual monuments. The largest site, Bawah Parit in the Mahat valley, contains approximately 380 monoliths arranged in parallel rows within a rectangular area of 125 by 85 metres, most in the kris-hilt form and oriented toward Gunung Sago. Excavations there uncovered human skeletons interred beneath the stones but no datable artifacts (p. 4). In the Sinamar valley, sites include Balubus, Suliki, and Kubang, where stones are fewer but larger and more elaborately carved. Miksic documents living ceremonial practices associated with these stones, including harvest rituals at a tiny *batu keramat* at Tanjung Haru, boundary-marking oaths at Kubang, and the use of four *batu tagak* in *Penghulu* installations at Kototangah (pp. 3–7).

Miksic then constructs the broader historical narrative. Adityawarman, dispatched from East Java around 1345, broke with Srivijayan precedent by establishing his capital at Bukit Gombak in the Tanahdatar valley rather than in the estuarine zone of the Batanghari. His inscriptions, written in Sanskrit with strong Malay grammatical influence, project a Malay rather than Javanese identity. After his death sometime after 1374, no further inscriptions or statues were erected, and his kingdom did not survive as a political entity (pp. 20–23). The subsequent transfer of Malay paramountcy from Palembang through Singapura to Melaka around 1400, under Parameswara, created the conditions for the embrace of Islam by the Malay ruling class. Miksic argues that the *batu tagak* tradition in both West Sumatra and the Negeri Sembilan–Melaka area began as a by-product of the social complexity introduced by Adityawarman's court and was subsequently reinforced by the economic demands of gold mining, which drew labour and status differentiation into the highland zones bordering the Straits (pp. 23, 39–40).

### Key Findings

- The *batu tagak* of West Sumatra and peninsular Malaysia are most likely dated between AD 1000 and 1500, placing them firmly in the classical rather than prehistoric period; circumstantial evidence from associated inscriptions at Kuburajo and Keramat Sungai Udang points specifically to the late fourteenth to mid-fifteenth century (pp. 2, 18–19).
- The Bawah Parit site in the Mahat valley is the largest and most regular *batu tagak* complex yet documented in Sumatra, with approximately 380 monoliths in a bounded rectangular area, many carved with spiral and geometric patterns, and human skeletons interred beneath them (p. 4).
- Adityawarman's inscriptions at Bukit Gombak, Pariangan, and Kuburajo (c. 1347–1375) are spatially proximate to *batu tagak* sites, supporting an inference of contemporaneity and suggesting that the tradition of erecting such monuments began as a by-product of the social institutions he introduced into the Minang heartland (pp. 18–21).
- An inscribed silver plate from the Bengkulu highlands, identified by the National Research Centre for Archaeology as bearing script similar to Adityawarman's fourteenth-century inscriptions, may provide evidence that he was seeking new gold sources beyond the depleted veins of West Sumatra (p. 40).
- Gold mining in the Barisan Range and peninsular Malaysia was extensive in the pre-European period; European mining engineers at Bengkulu reported "a great deal of work has been done by ancient miners," including shafts, stopes, and drives, and stone mortars and grinding implements were found at the sites (pp. 39–40).
- The kris-hilt form of the *batu tagak* is shared between West Sumatra and the Keramat Sungai Udang site near Pengkalan Kempas, and the same shape is used for tombstones in the Tanahdatar area today, indicating a continuous morphological tradition spanning at least six centuries (pp. 4, 18).

### Conclusion

Miksic's definitive takeaway is that the *batu tagak* are best understood as material expressions of a specific historical process: the penetration of Malay political and economic culture from estuarine trading centres into the highland interior during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, a process driven in part by the exhaustion of gold deposits in West Sumatra and the consequent search for new sources in the peninsula. The stones are neither prehistoric relics nor uniform megalithic monuments but locally varied symbols of status, boundary, and ritual that emerged in response to the social differentiation accompanying the rise and fall of classical Malay kingdoms from Srivijaya to Melaka (pp. 39–41).

## Context

- The article was delivered as the MBRAS Annual Lecture for 1987 and draws on Miksic's ongoing comparative fieldwork in West Sumatra, conducted in collaboration with the Indonesian National Research Centre for Archaeology and the West Sumatra Regional Office of Education and Culture (pp. 1, 3).
- Two *batu tagak* from Balai-balai Batu, Mahat, now held uncatalogued in the Museum Nasional, Jakarta, were probably removed by F.M. Schnitger in the early twentieth century and represent the only known examples of Mahat stone art outside their home area (p. 5).
