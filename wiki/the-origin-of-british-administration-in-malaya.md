# The origin of British administration in Malaya

Khoo Kay Kim's 1966 article challenges the prevailing historiographical consensus—rooted in the work of Cowan and MacIntyre—that Lord Kimberley's decision in 1873 to authorize British intervention in the Malay States was primarily motivated by fear of German encroachment. Drawing extensively on Colonial Office minute books and private correspondence, Khoo argues that the true driving force was the protection and development of British commercial interests in the Straits Settlements and the Peninsula, with the "German threat" serving as a convenient public justification to secure Gladstone's approval.

## Summary

The article systematically dismantles two prevailing assumptions about British policy in the Malayan area during the early 1870s. The first, that Britain's primary concern was the China trade route and that the Sumatra Treaty of 1871 represented a willing concession of Dutch sovereignty to the detriment of Straits commerce, is refuted through Percy Anderson's 1874 Foreign Office memorandum and Colonial Office correspondence showing that the treaty was in fact designed to protect Straits trade from hostile Dutch tariffs. The second assumption, that there was "little if any" British investment in the Malay States before 1896, is contradicted by a detailed catalogue of mining concessions, revenue farms, and commercial partnerships held by British firms in Pahang, Perak, Selangor, Sungei Ujong, and Larut from the early 1860s onward.

The core of Khoo's argument concerns the sequence of events in the Colonial Office between January and September 1873. He demonstrates that Kimberley's January 1873 rejection of Hugessen's suggestion of annexation was specifically a rejection of *territorial extension*, not of intervention per se, and that the real obstacle to a more active policy was the Colonial Office's well-documented distrust of Governor Sir Harry Ord, whose "insubordinate opposition" and "dictatorial" tendencies made him an unreliable instrument of policy. Once Sir Andrew Clarke was appointed in May 1873, the path to intervention was cleared. Khoo shows that Kimberley's minute of 7 July 1873 ("I think we must endeavour to put a stop to these disturbances") already implied intervention; the question was one of degree and method, not whether to act.

The most substantial portion of the article traces the relationship between the Colonial Office and the commercial projects of Seymour Clarke and W. H. Read—specifically the proposed submarine telegraph line from Burma through the Peninsula to Singapore and the Selangor Tin Mining Company. Khoo demonstrates that Herbert and Kimberley were actively sympathetic to these ventures, that the Colonial Office provided implicit assurances of support, and that the intervention policy of 1873 was intimately connected to the need to create stable political conditions under which such commercial enterprises could operate. The "German threat" mentioned in Seymour Clarke's letter of 18 July 1873 was never verified, was conspicuously absent from the final instructions to Clarke (having been deleted from the draft submitted to Gladstone), and was never again referenced by Kimberley.

### Key Findings

- British commercial interests in the Malay States before 1874 included: Paterson, Simons & Co.'s Kuantan mining concession (expenditure of $22,500; compensation claim of $25,000); W. T. Lewis's Krian padi lease in Perak (rent $5,000/year; projected yield $150,000); W. H. Read's Klang revenue farm (Selangor trade valued at $1,000,000 in 1867; tin output ~21,000 piculs); and the Sungei Ujong Tin Mining Co. (Dato' Klana received $30,000 for concessions) (pp. 57–59).
- The Sumatra Treaty of 1871 was negotiated primarily to protect Straits commerce from potential hostile Dutch tariffs; Kimberley's minute of 14 Sept. 1871 stated that "the only reason why we have opposed the extension of Dutch influence over the independent native States has been because Dutch influence means exclusion of British commerce" (pp. 54–55).
- The Colonial Office's distrust of Governor Ord was documented across at least seven separate incidents between 1868 and 1873, including Herbert's marginal comment that "the present Governor cannot be trusted to interfere wisely" (Oct. 1872) and Kimberley's remark that Ord was "not the man to unravel" the Selangor situation (Dec. 1872) (pp. 64–67).
- The German threat was deleted from the draft instructions to Sir Andrew Clarke submitted to Gladstone but retained in weakened form in the final despatch of 20 Sept. 1873; Kimberley never again referenced it in subsequent correspondence, and no Malay ruler ever actually communicated with the British about approaching Germany (pp. 62, 71–72).
- The 1871 War Office Committee on Straits defences concluded that Penang and Malacca were "scarcely liable to external attack" and made no reference whatsoever to the Malay Peninsula, undermining any claim that strategic considerations drove the 1873 policy (p. 73).
- Both Seymour Clarke's telegraph scheme and the Selangor Tin Mining Company failed by the end of 1875, yet the Colonial Office had provided substantial implicit support to both, including a proposed Straits contribution of £10,000 to the telegraph line and the Sultan's ratification of the mining concession "under the advice of the British Govt." (pp. 85–89).

### Conclusion

Khoo concludes that Kimberley's policy of 1873 was fundamentally an economic policy disguised in imperial language. The Colonial Office recognized that the Malay States offered "vast economic possibilities" and that political stability was a prerequisite for investment. The German argument was deployed not because it was genuine but because it was the only justification that could move Gladstone and be publicly defended. The true motive was to create the conditions under which British commercial enterprise—already substantial in the Peninsula—could expand securely, a motive shared by both Kimberley and the Permanent Under-Secretary Robert Herbert.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on Colonial Office records (C.O. 273 series), the Swettenham Papers, and Straits Settlements Records, with extensive use of official minutes by Kimberley, Herbert, Cox, Macdonald, and other junior officials.
- Historiographically, the article represents a significant intervention in the debate between Cowan (who emphasized the strategic/imperial motive) and MacIntyre (who focused on the "volte face" of 1873), positioning economic interest as the primary driver and reinterpreting the role of Seymour Clarke's commercial projects as central rather than peripheral to the policy decision.
