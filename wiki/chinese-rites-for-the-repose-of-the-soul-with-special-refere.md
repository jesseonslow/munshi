# Chinese rites for the repose of the soul; with special reference to Cantonese custom

Marjorie Topley's 1952 ethnographic study documents the Cantonese mortuary rites performed in Singapore, with particular focus on the "Paying of Respects" (*ta chai*) ceremony as carried out in the "Death Houses" of Sago Lane. Drawing on direct observation and participant accounts, Topley presents a detailed account of the six-rite sequence for the dead, the role of the *Nam-ma-lo* (Taoist chanting priests), and the elaborate material culture of paper effigies and ritual objects that underpin Cantonese beliefs about the soul's journey through the underworld.

## Summary

Topley's article addresses the practical and spiritual dimensions of Cantonese funeral practice in mid-twentieth-century Singapore, a context in which post-war inflation had made the full traditional cycle of rites increasingly unaffordable for many families. The central problem she explores is how economic constraint intersects with the social obligation to perform "rescue" rites for the dead: failure to arrange such ceremonies carried a "great loss of face" for families who believed in the traditional hell, yet the cost of a first-quality paper house had risen from approximately $300 to $1,000 since the war (p. 148). Topley shows how families negotiated this tension through benefit societies, selective performance of only one or two of the prescribed "seventh" ceremonies, and the use of institutional venues like the Death Houses that bundled services (mortuary, ritual space, priest employment, funeral bands) into a single arrangement.

The article's core contribution is its detailed reconstruction of a single *ta chai* ceremony, performed by a troupe of five *Nam-ma-lo* in a Sago Lane Death House, with a total cost of approximately $200 for the priest's fee and paper things (p. 153). Topley sequences the six rites—Opening the Ceremony, Breaking Hell, Crossing the Bridge, Universal Helping over of the Wandering Spirits, Distributing Flowers, and the final burning of paper objects—interleaving description of the physical layout of the performance space with the actions of the priests and mourners. The "Breaking Hell" rite, in which the priest shatters nine tiles representing the nine courts of the underworld using a flaming sword and a spit of cold water thrown into hot oil, receives the most sustained attention (pp. 153–155). Topley also records a local variant of the Mu Lien legend in which the protagonist's mother was a *mui-tsai* (semi-slave girl) whose sacrilege in a temple transformed her character, a narrative that served as justification for the *Nam-ma-lo* to insist on the rite's performance (p. 151).

The article situates Cantonese practice within a broader dialect-group comparison, noting that Cantonese in Singapore tended to emphasise elaborate ritual performance over the quantity of paper objects burnt, whereas Hokkien and Teochew communities invested more in the latter (p. 148). Topley also documents the social function of the *fan* (mourner's banner) as a marker of estate claims, the gendered division of labour in mourning (women rolling paper money into ingots, men carrying the *fan*), and the hybrid religious character of the rites, which blend Buddhist narrative frameworks with Taoist ritual aggression (the "defeating spirits sceptre" being the sole outward Taoist marker in an otherwise Buddhist-form ceremony) (p. 155).

### Key Findings

- A first-quality paper house for funeral burning cost approximately $300 before the war and approximately $1,000 in the early 1950s; a typical *ta chai* ceremony (priest's fee plus paper things) cost about $200 (pp. 148, 153).
- The "Breaking Hell" structure comprised nine unfired tiles (four on the ground, four elevated on sheet-metal stands, and one central "gate" tile with an iron-studded door), each painted with a tiger's head, arranged in a circle with a fire-clay bridge and saucers of oil at the corners (p. 153).
- The full Cantonese mortuary sequence comprises six rites: *K'ai t'an* (Opening the Ceremony), *Po ti-yu* (Breaking Hell), *Kwoh hsin ch'iao* (Crossing the Bridge), *Pu t'u ku yuan* (Universal Helping over of the Wandering Spirits), *San hua* (Distributing Flowers), and the burning of paper things (p. 150).
- The *Nam-ma-lo* were lay Taoist priests who did not shave their heads, ate meat, and married; in Singapore and Malaya they served at all mortuary rites, unlike in Kwang-Tung where a separate monastic type performed post-funeral rites (p. 150).
- The "Death Houses" in Sago Lane, Singapore, functioned as combined nursing homes, mortuaries, and ritual venues, housing temporary soul tablets (*Lung p'ai*) for up to one hundred days after death (pp. 149–150).
- A *chu show fan* (Cooking Longevity Rice) ceremony was performed only when the deceased was well advanced in years, and was considered very lucky; it involved cooking rice with Chinese cash on a fire, topped with pink dough cakes and twelve pairs of chopsticks (p. 160).

### Conclusion

Topley's definitive takeaway is that Cantonese mortuary practice in Singapore represented a living, adaptive system in which the elaborate material and performative apparatus of the rites served a dual function: spiritually, it ensured the deceased's successful navigation of the bureaucratic underworld and favourable rebirth; socially, it reinforced kinship obligations, estate claims, and community solidarity through the collective labour and expenditure of the mourning group. The persistence of these rites despite economic pressure and the availability of alternative (Buddhist, monastic) forms testifies to their deep integration into Cantonese social identity in the Straits settlements.

## Context

- The article is based on Topley's direct field observation in Singapore, including permission to photograph rites granted through the mediation of the *Nam-ma-lo* themselves, and on oral accounts from Cantonese informants (including a Strait-born Cantonese who provided the local Mu Lien variant) (pp. 150–151).
- The study contributes to the ethnographic record of Chinese religious practice in Malaya at a moment when the *Nam-ma-lo* tradition was already in decline (Topley notes "the only remaining Taoist priest in Malacca," p. 147), making it a valuable documentary source for a performative tradition that has since largely disappeared.
