# The black and white amahs of Malaya

Ooi Keat Gin's 1992 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the Cantonese domestic servants known as "Black and White Amahs" who emigrated to Penang and Singapore in the 1930s, drawing on oral histories collected in Penang between 1981 and 1991. The study traces their emigration, their role in affluent Chinese households, the social institutions that sustained them, and their distinctive refusal of marriage, ultimately positioning them as "pioneer feminists" whose lives represented a rare form of female autonomy in the Chinese diaspora.

## Summary

The article addresses a largely undocumented group of Chinese women who, between 1933 and 1938, emigrated to Malaya in large numbers and filled the domestic labour vacuum left by the suppression of the illegal traffic in women for prostitution. Ooi reconstructs their social world through interviews with former amahs, their employers, and associated institutions, focusing on Penang as the primary case study. The narrative moves from the push and pull factors of emigration—political instability in China, the Aliens Ordinance of 1933 which created a quota for men but not women, and the economic incentives of ticket brokers—to the amahs' daily lives, their relationships with employers, and the institutions that structured their existence from arrival to retirement.

A central thread of the article is the amahs' remarkable degree of independence and self-organization. They maintained single status, formed cooperative housing arrangements in *kongsi pang* lodging houses, entered *chai t'ang* vegetarian halls upon retirement, and sustained religious devotion to *Kuan-yin* as a unifying bond. Ooi connects their reluctance to marry to the documented tradition of marriage resistance in the rural Canton delta, arguing that the amahs were the Nanyang continuation of a broader pattern of female autonomy. The article also documents the gradual disappearance of the amah system from the 1970s onward, as rising literacy, alternative employment, and the arrival of Southeast Asian and South Asian foreign domestic workers rendered the traditional live-in amah obsolete.

### Key Findings

- Between 1934 and 1938, over 190,000 female Chinese deck passengers arrived in Malaya; in the mid-1930s, Chinese female immigration exceeded male immigration in volume (p. 73).
- The Aliens Ordinance of 1933 established a monthly quota for male immigrants but initially imposed no quota on women, making female passage cheaper and effectively creating an "official green light" for women's emigration (p. 72).
- A typical amah served a single household for ten to fifteen years, though some remained with one family until retirement at approximately sixty-five (p. 74).
- In *kongsi pang* run by a coolie head, as many as ten or more new arrivals could share a single room at any given time, though occupancy was rotational as employed amahs moved into their employers' residences (p. 77).
- Of the dozen former amahs interviewed, all but two originated from villages in the south-west delta area of Guangdong province (p. 83, n. 38).
- The average retirement age was sixty to sixty-five; most amahs who retired in Penang did not return to China, citing the absence of living relatives, lack of funds, and the uncertainties of the Cultural Revolution (p. 84).

### Conclusion

Ooi's definitive takeaway is that the Black and White Amahs constituted a rare and historically significant group of Chinese women whose paid employment, financial independence, maintenance of single status, and formation of self-governing sisterhoods represented a form of feminist assertion that was exceptional within the Chinese diaspora. Their story is one of agency within constraint: they navigated the structures of colonial immigration policy, Chinese patriarchal norms, and the domestic economy to carve out a life of dignity, mutual support, and purpose that outlasted the system that produced them.

## Context

- Primary sources consist of oral histories collected in Penang from February to March 1981 and revised in 1991, supplemented by secondary literature including Topley's work on marriage resistance in rural Kwangtung and Ho It Chong's 1958 study of Cantonese domestic amahs.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Chinese women in the Nanyang by shifting focus from the well-documented male labour migration to the female domestic workforce, and by linking the amahs' behaviour to the documented tradition of marriage resistance in the Canton delta.
