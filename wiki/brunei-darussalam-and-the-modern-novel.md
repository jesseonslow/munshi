# Brunei Darussalam and the modern novel

C. H. Gallop's 2004 article, originally delivered as a talk to the MBRAS Annual General Meeting in June 2003, examines the development of the modern Malay novel in Brunei Darussalam and the ideological forces that govern its production and reception. The overarching thesis is that Brunei's national ideology of Melayu Islam Beraja (MIB) functions as an Althusserian "ideological state apparatus" that conditions both what writers produce and how readers interpret it, generating a paradox in which a novelist of evident cosmopolitan empathy is read by local critics as a narrow nationalist hostile to foreigners.

## Summary

Gallop situates Brunei's literary development within the country's distinctive political trajectory: a long period of British protection, the 1962 rebellion, near-total cultural isolation from Indonesia following the repatriation of Indonesian teachers in 1967, and the post-independence promotion of MIB as a unifying national creed. Against this backdrop, the article traces the remarkably sparse early production of the Bruneian novel — two works in the early 1950s, then a gap of more than a decade — and the subsequent flowering in the 1980s, driven by the restoration of ties with Malaysia, the opening of Universiti Brunei Darussalam, and the recruitment of Malaysian novelists to the Brunei Language and Literature Bureau. The article argues that the novel form was slow to take hold partly because Brunei lacked the prior literate tradition found in Peninsular Malaysia and partly because poetry and short stories more readily found publication space.

The analytical core of the article is a close reading of the work of Muslim Burmat, the most prolific Bruneian novelist, whose six novels (1982–1999) consistently focus on dispossessed and marginalised figures — fishermen, farmers, estate workers — and whose treatment of British characters in *Terbenamnya Matahari* departs sharply from the adversarial stereotypes typical of Bruneian fiction. Gallop proposes that Burmat's Kedayan ethnicity, a non-dominant identity within Brunei's Brunei Malay majority, grants him a "lateral" vantage point that enables both social compassion and a degree of cultural objectivity unavailable to writers embedded in the mainstream. This "unuttered ethnicity" — never named in the text but structurally operative — allows Burmat to write from the margins without transgressing the national homogeneity that MIB demands.

Gallop then deploys Louis Althusser's distinction between repressive and ideological state apparatuses to explain the gap between Burmat's actual writing and the way Bruneian critics receive it. Local commentators such as A. Hashim Hamid and Ampuan Brahim read the deaths of non-Muslim characters in *Puncak Pertama* as evidence of the author's "patriotism" and even his hatred of Westerners, while Gallop himself, a British reader, perceives in the same texts a deeply empathetic and evenly distributed compassion. The article argues that the MIB ideology, operating through consensus rather than coercion, produces a reading climate in which certain interpretations appear natural and freely chosen but are in fact induced by the ideological apparatus.

### Key Findings

- The first Bruneian novel, *Mahkota Yang Berdarah* by Yura Halim (1951), was substantially censored by British Special Branch; the second, *Tunangan Pemimpin Bangsa* by H. M. Salleh (1952), was written in detention and smuggled to Singapore for publication (pp. 44–45).
- By 1980 only three Bruneian novels had been published; by 1991 the number had risen to thirty-six, though some remained unpublished (pp. 45–46).
- Muslim Burmat published six novels between 1982 and 1999, culminating in the 800-page *Urih Pesisir* (1999); he received the SEA Write Award in Bangkok in 1986 (pp. 46–48).
- Brunei's population in 1997 was approximately 341,000 across 5,765 square kilometres; two-thirds of the population is Malay (p. 43).
- The MIB ideology was first publicly articulated in Sultan Hassanal Bolkiah's independence speech in 1984 and has been propagated as a traditional rather than novel concept (p. 44).
- Indonesian teachers were repatriated from Brunei in 1967, and Bruneian students in Malaysia were recalled in 1972, producing a period of near-total cultural isolation from the wider Malay-speaking world (p. 44).

### Conclusion

Gallop's definitive historical takeaway is that Muslim Burmat demonstrates the potential of a national literature to exceed the ideological frame within which it is produced and received. The writer's capacity to satisfy the national criteria preferred by Bruneian authorities while simultaneously producing work of genuine cosmopolitan empathy — including sympathetic portrayals of the British — makes him, in Gallop's judgement, a writer "worthy of consideration and, in particular, of further study." The article implicitly contends that the Bruneian literary field, though shaped by MIB as an ideological state apparatus, is not reducible to that apparatus and can generate voices that complicate and enrich the national narrative.

## Context

- The article was originally a talk to the MBRAS AGM (28 June 2003) and draws primarily on Bruneian literary-critical sources published in *Bahana* and by Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka Brunei, supplemented by the novelist's own published works (p. 43, n. 1).
- Historiographically, the piece is one of the few external scholarly treatments of Bruneian novelistic production and the first to apply Althusser's ISA framework to the reception of Bruneian fiction, positioning the study at the intersection of literary criticism and political ideology.
