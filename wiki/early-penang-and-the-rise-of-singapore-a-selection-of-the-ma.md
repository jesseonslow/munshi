# Early Penang and the rise of Singapore: a selection of the manuscript records of the East India Company over the period 1805–32, chosen & edited, with an introduction by C.D. Cowan

Dr W. Linehan, a senior colonial administrator and scholar, published this brief article in 1950 describing a unique tortoise-shell-shaped tin ingot bearing an undeciphered inscription, found at Kuala Dipang in the Kinta District of Perak. The piece situates this artefact within the broader cultural and ritual context of Malay tin mining, arguing that such ingots served sacred and commemorative functions rather than acting as currency.

## Summary

The article centres on a single artefact in the Perak Museum: a tin ingot cast in the shape of a tortoise shell (*kura-kura*), weighing 1 lb. 14¼ ozs, bearing a short legend in slight relief. Originally labelled as bearing "Siamese" characters, the inscription was examined by the Siamese priest Weera Patana, who identified it as Burmese and a *mantra* (a prayer with magical properties). Dr Coedes, who saw a cast and photograph, concurred that it was likely a *mantra* and suggested the characters might be Burmese or Mon (p. 102). Linehan treats this as the only known inscribed example of its type, and the only one whose inscription is neither Malay nor Chinese (p. 104).

Linehan's broader argument concerns the function of animal-shaped tin ingots in Malay society. He explicitly challenges Temple's view that such ingots were a form of currency, contending instead that they were produced for special ceremonial occasions—such as the presentation of offerings by Pahang chiefs during Muharram, or the erection of the *tiang seri* (pillar of Fortune) in a new house (pp. 102–103). Drawing on testimony from the late G. M. Laidlaw and the Malay trader Haji Mat Arshat, he explains that these ingots were cast in duplicate as the *sulong relau* ("eldest-born of the smelting-furnace") or *sulong kelian* ("eldest-born of the mines"), and were bequeathed as heirlooms with sacred or magical significance conferred by the *mantra* (p. 103). He notes the cross-cultural resonance of the tortoise form: an avatar of Vishnu in Indian tradition and a symbol of longevity among the Chinese (p. 103).

The article also addresses the dating of the specimen. Unless it is a "stray" from the north, Linehan argues it cannot post-date 1826, the year James Low expelled Siamese forces from Kampar, the district in which Kuala Dipang is situated (p. 104). He further corrects Mrs A. Hingston Quiggin's 1949 survey of primitive money, which had erroneously attributed the sacred properties of the tortoise-shell ingot to the larger, boat-shaped (*jongkong*) type due to Laidlaw's misdescription (p. 104).

### Key Findings

- The ingot weighs 1 lb. 14¼ ozs and shows no signs of deterioration, indicating a pure tin content (p. 102).
- It was donated to the Perak Museum by A. Hale, Inspector of Mines, Perak, in 1885 (p. 102).
- The inscription is unique in being neither Malay nor Chinese; it is most likely Burmese or Mon and represents a *mantra* (pp. 102, 104).
- Linehan dates the ingot no later than 1826, based on the expulsion of Siamese from Kampar by James Low (p. 104).
- A. Hale's 1885 account records Malay animistic beliefs that tin ore was alive, could move of its own volition, and could reproduce itself, necessitating propitiation of protective spirits (p. 104).
- Linehan distinguishes the tortoise-shell ingot from the *jongkong* (boat-shaped) ingots, which were larger, heavier, and served as currency; the tortoise type was made for ornament and ritual, not commercial use (pp. 102, 104).

### Conclusion

Linehan's definitive takeaway is that the tortoise-shell tin ingot was a sacred object of commemoration and protection, cast to sanctify the commencement of a new mine or dwelling, and that its magical significance—originally encoded in a *mantra*—persisted in modified form even after the original religious and linguistic context had faded. The specimen from Kuala Dipang stands as the sole known example retaining its inscription, making it a unique witness to the intersection of South and Southeast Asian religious traditions in the tin-mining cultures of the Malay Peninsula.

## Context

- The article draws on the Perak Museum collection, the Pitt Rivers Collection, and Temple's 1914 study of obsolete tin currency in the Federated Malay States.
- Linehan's contribution is primarily artefactual and ethnographic, correcting earlier misidentifications (Laidlaw's shape description, Quiggin's functional attribution) and providing a more nuanced account of the ritual economy surrounding tin production in Perak.
