# Memoir of Captain Francis Light, who founded Penang

A.M. Skinner, a Penang resident, published this memoir in 1895 to mark the centenary of Captain Francis Light's death, reconstructing the founder's life and the establishment of the settlement from official despatches, Light's own diary, and contemporary correspondence. The overarching thesis is that Light was a man of probity, practical judgment, and singular purpose whose pioneer enterprise established a colony of enduring commercial and strategic value, and whose reputation was unfairly compromised by later political controversies over Kedah's sovereignty that imported anachronistic questions into the historical record.

## Summary

Skinner's memoir traces Light's career from his first correspondence with Warren Hastings in 1771—recommending Penang as a "convenient magazine for the Eastern trade"—through his death on 21 October 1794. The narrative is organised around three interlocking problems: the political legitimacy of the cession from Kedah, the administrative vacuum in which Light governed a rapidly growing mixed population without a proper judicial system or adequate financial support from Calcutta, and the land-grant controversy that consumed the final months of his life. Light's own diary and despatches form the evidential backbone, supplemented by the 1805 Court of Directors' despatch establishing the Presidency Government, which Skinner treats as the definitive contemporary verdict on the settlement's success.

The Kedah sovereignty question receives extended treatment. Skinner sides with J.R. Logan against Colonel Low's 1848 chronicle, arguing that Low imported into the historical record a Siamese suzerainty claim that had never been raised in the 1780s. The decisive evidence is Light's despatch of 18 June 1787 to Lord Cornwallis, which shows the negotiations turned entirely on the comparative merits of Penang versus Junk Ceylon (Salang) as a site for a British settlement, with no reference to Siamese authority. Skinner further notes that the Dutch, not Siam, were the dominant external power in the region at the time, and that the Raja of Kedah's "bunga mas" tribute was a token of inferior pretensions offered to multiple powers simultaneously, not evidence of exclusive suzerainty.

The administrative narrative highlights Light's integrity in refusing to exploit his dual position as Superintendent and principal merchant, his repeated but unsuccessful appeals to Calcutta for a proper court of justice, and his political courage in quietly resisting the August 1794 regulation that would have limited European land grants to five-year leases—a measure he judged would have "instantly stop[ped] all further advance" in pepper-planting and settlement.

### Key Findings

- Light arrived off Pulau Tikus on 15 July 1786 with 100 Native Marines, 30 Lascars, 15 European Artillerymen, and 5 Officers; formal possession was taken on 11 August 1786 (pp. 2–4).
- The cession from the Raja of Kedah was for $6,000 per year; the initial Malay population encountered was 52 wood-cutters from near Tanjong Tokong (pp. 2, 4).
- The 1791 Kedah crisis saw a force of over 8,000 Malays and 20 Lanoon boats attack Kuala Pry; Light's 400 men dispersed them after a few hours' fighting (p. 7).
- Population reached approximately 10,000 by the end of 1789 and over 20,000 by 1795, of whom the Chinese numbered not much over 3,000 (p. 4).
- The 2 per cent *ad valorem* duty imposed in 1801 on tin, pepper, and betel-nut produced $13,076 in that year; Penang remained a customs port until 1826 (pp. 5, 14).
- The land-grant regulation of 1 August 1794 (no European grants exceeding 300 orlongs; five-year leases only) was rescinded by the Governor-General in Council on 22 August 1796 (pp. 14–15).

### Conclusion

Skinner's definitive historical takeaway is that Light's eight years of almost unsupported governance constitute "a truly successful record of what British courage and perseverance, local experience amounting to adroitness, and a large-minded sense of public duty can achieve." He positions Light as a worthy forerunner to Stamford Raffles, whose Singapore was founded thirty-five years later on very similar lines, and insists that the later political controversies—particularly Colonel Low's Siamese suzerainty argument—were anachronistic impositions that did no justice to the honest judgment Light exercised in 1786.

## Context

- Written by a Penang resident for the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society as a centenary commemoration; the author's local vantage point and access to the colony's public records (including unpublished despatches in Logan's Journal) give the memoir an insider's authority, though it remains a hagiographic exercise in the tradition of colonial founder-mythology.
- The piece draws primarily on Light's diary (printed in Logan's Journal Vol. III), his despatches to Cornwallis and Macpherson (Logan's Journal Vols. IV–V), the 1805 Court of Directors' despatch, and Colonel Low's 1848 chronicle; its historiographical contribution lies in its early use of the 1787 Cornwallis despatch to settle the Kedah sovereignty question in Light's favour.
