# Notes on the Malayan Dipterocarpaceae, VI

C.F. Symington, a botanist at the Forest Research Institute, Kepong, published this sixth instalment of his series on Malayan dipterocarps in 1941, originally intended for the *Gardens' Bulletin, Straits Settlements* but redirected to the *Journal of the Malayan Branch, Royal Asiatic Society* owing to wartime restrictions. The paper describes seven new species of dipterocarps from the Malay Peninsula and one from Borneo, while simultaneously untangling a web of nomenclatural confusion that had accumulated across the genera *Hopea*, *Vatica*, and *Shorea* since the 1870s.

## Summary

Symington's primary contribution is the formal description of seven new species: six *Hopea* (*H. johorensis*, *H. montana*, *H. glaucescens*, *H. kelantanensis*, *H. polyalthioides*), one *Vatica* (*V. heteroptera*), and one *Shorea* (*S. peltata*), together with the long-overdue description of *Shorea pachyphylla* from Borneo. Each new species is accompanied by a full morphological diagnosis, a discussion of its affinities within the relevant infrageneric group, and a concluding "precis" written for the practical benefit of Malayan forest officers, including proposed vernacular names. The descriptions draw on herbarium material from the Forest Research Institute at Kepong, the Singapore herbarium, and collections from Kew, Dehra Dun, and Buitenzorg, with field notes recording girth, bark characteristics, and local names.

The second major thread of the paper is taxonomic revision. Symington addresses the persistent confusion among six species of *Vatica* that had been misidentified or synonymised incorrectly by King, Brandis, Ridley, van Slooten, and Foxworthy. The most consequential correction is the consolidation of at least eight earlier names—spanning *Hopea*, *Synaptea*, *Anisoptera*, and *Vatica*—into a single species, *Vatica odorata* (Griff.) Sym., comb. nov. He also restricts the circumscriptions of *V. perakensis* and *V. Lowii* King, which had each been inflated by the inclusion of specimens belonging to other species. In *Shorea*, he defines the *Barbata* group as a natural subdivision of the section *Eushorea*, characterised by ovate flower buds, broader petals, and bearded staminal appendages, and argues for the inclusion of the genus *Isoptera* within *Shorea* on the basis of floral anatomy and wood structure.

The paper also extends the known distribution of *Shorea sericeiflora* from Tenasserim, Burma, northward through peninsular Thailand to the Langkawi Islands and north-west Kedah, and tentatively unites *S. Talura* Roxb., *S. floribunda* Wall. ex Kurz, and *S. cochinchinensis* Pierre as a single widespread species.

### Key Findings

- *Hopea montana* was discovered at elevations of 3,000–4,000 ft on Gunong Korbu and Gunong Stong, representing the highest elevation at which any *merawan* had been recorded in the Malay Peninsula (p. 135).
- *Hopea kelantanensis* is identified as a morphological connecting link between the *Euhopea* and *Pierrea* groups of *Hopea*, possessing an embryo and germination pattern (first whorl of three leaves) characteristic of *Pierrea* but general habit suggestive of *Euhopea* (pp. 141–142).
- *Shorea peltata* is the first species in the genus *Shorea* described with peltate mature leaves; Symington notes that at least two undescribed Bornean species in the *Richetia* group also show this tendency (p. 158).
- *Vatica odorata* (Griff.) Sym. consolidates no fewer than eight earlier published names, including *Hopea grandiflora* Wall., *Hopea faginea* Wall., *Synaptea odorata* Griff., *Anisoptera odorata* Kurz, *Vatica grandiflora* Dyer, *Vatica faginea* Dyer, *Vatica astrotricha* Hance, and *Vatica Curtisii* King (pp. 154–155).
- *Shorea pachyphylla* from Brunei is recorded as a common timber tree near the Seriah oilfields, with limited strength tests showing it to be superior in all properties to any heavy red meranti previously tested (p. 164).
- The *Barbata* group within *Shorea* is defined by ovate or globose flower buds, shorter broader petals, more numerous stamens, and bearded staminal appendages; three Peninsula members are listed (*S. glauca*, *S. laevis*, *S. Maxwelliana*) (p. 161).

### Conclusion

Symington's definitive takeaway is that the dipterocarp flora of the Malay Peninsula remains substantially under-described, with numerous species still entangled in synonymies that predate modern herbarium practice. By combining careful morphological comparison of type specimens with field observations and wood-anatomical data, he demonstrates that what had been treated as multiple species in *Vatica* and *Shorea* often represent single polymorphic taxa, while genuinely distinct species—particularly in *Hopea*—have been overlooked or misassigned. The paper serves both as a taxonomic correction and as a foundation for the nomenclature he was adopting in a forthcoming semi-botanical forest work.

## Context

- Primary archival collections examined include the Forest Research Institute herbarium at Kepong, the Singapore herbarium, Kew, Dehra Dun, Buitenzorg, and the Bureau of Science herbarium at Manila; the paper also draws on Dr. Kerr's Siamese collections and Dr. H. E. Desch's wood-anatomy observations.
- The study contributes to the historiography of Malayan botanical taxonomy by documenting how the sequential work of King, Brandis, Ridley, van Slooten, and Foxworthy generated layers of misidentification that Symington systematically resolves through direct examination of type material.
