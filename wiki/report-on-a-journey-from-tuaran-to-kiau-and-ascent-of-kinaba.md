# Report on a journey from Tuaran to Kiau and ascent of Kinabalu Mountain

## Abstract

For well over a century, close personal ties underlay relations between the rulers of Kedah and Siam. In the latter part of the nineteenth century, Kedah emerged as Siam’s most favoured Malay tributary, and the state’s rulers pursued parallel agendas of socio-economic and administrative modernization. That special tie ended abruptly in 1909 when Siam signed a treaty ceding the Kedah to Great Britain, but Tunku Badlishah, a key figure in the Kedah administration, was a naturalized citizen of Siam and had close personal ties with King Vajiravudh. After the latter’s death in 1925, the relationship faded away, but Badlishah’s Thai background continued to influence his career in various ways even after he became Sultan in 1943. His reign coincided with Malaya’s push for independence, and he became a key figure in pressing for a continuing leadership role for Malaya’s sultans and a retention of traditional authority.

## Summary

This article traces the personalized relationship between the Kedah and Siamese ruling houses from the early nineteenth century through the mid-twentieth century, using the life of Tunku Badlishah (Sultan of Kedah, r. 1943–58) as its central narrative thread. The author argues that Kedah's unique tributary status—established when a discontented prince submitted himself to the Chakri court as a supplicant seeking military assistance rather than as a ruler—created a bond of mutual personal interest that distinguished Kedah from Siam's other Malay tributaries. This relationship, cultivated through intermarriage, court visits, and the patronage of the Bunnag family, was abruptly severed by the 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty, yet its personal dimensions persisted through Badlishah's adoption by the future King Vajiravudh and his subsequent career in the Siamese civil service. The article thus frames the Bangkok-Kedah tie as a case study in how personalized intra-regional diplomacy could outlast formal political structures.

The narrative then shifts to the post-war period, where Badlishah's Thai background and his commitment to traditional royal authority positioned him as a key figure in the struggle between the Malay rulers and UMNO over the future of Malayan governance. His confrontation with Dato' Onn Jaafar over the proposed Malay Deputy High Commissioner post, and his resistance to the Malayan Union, are presented as expressions of a ruler defending the socio-political status quo ante of colonialism. The article culminates in the reconciliation between Badlishah and his half-brother Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra after the latter's electoral victory, which resolved the tension between traditional and modern authority and allowed both men to claim credit for Malaya's independence.

Primary sources from the Thai National Archives—particularly correspondence between Sultan Abdul Hamid, King Vajiravudh, and Prince Damrong—form the evidential backbone of the article, supplemented by Colonial Office records and the Rhodes House Library's Political Intelligence Journal. The author draws on both Thai-language and English-language documents to reconstruct the personal and diplomatic dimensions of the relationship.

### Key Findings

- The 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty ceded Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan, and Trengganu to Great Britain in exchange for the cancellation of the 1855 Anglo-Siamese Treaty and favourable loan conditions for a Siamese railroad (p. 123).
- Tunku Badlishah was born 17 March 1894, sent to Bangkok around age 7–8, became an adopted son of Crown Prince Vajiravudh, entered the Siamese civil service in the Privy Purse Department around 1921, and rose to the rank of Phra Si Surintharawiwongsa within three years (pp. 124–125).
- The 1905 Loan Agreement between Kedah and Siam, amounting to $2,600,000, created the State Council and the Office of the Adviser, launching Kedah's administrative modernization (p. 128).
- In February 1946, Sultan Badlishah wrote to the Colonial Office declaring his signature on the Malayan Union had been given under duress, citing the threat that "a successor who would sign it would be appointed" if he refused (p. 130).
- The Conference of Rulers, led by Sultan Badlishah, rejected UMNO's proposal to appoint a Malay Deputy High Commissioner in the Federation of Malaya (1948), a decision that deeply affected Dato' Onn Jaafar's attitude toward royalty (p. 132).
- Sultan Badlishah was the first Malay ruler to attend Oxford University (Wadham College, 1919), the first multilingual ruler (Thai, English, Malay), and the only naturalized Siamese subject to serve as ruler of a Malay state (p. 134).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian's definitive takeaway is that Tunku Badlishah represents the last significant exemplar of the personalized Bangkok-Kedah relationship, and that his extraordinary biographical circumstances—foreign upbringing, adoption by a foreign king, naturalization—shaped his bold and outspoken leadership style, enabling him to navigate the transition from traditional to constitutional rulership and to defend the rulers' collective authority against both colonial and nationalist pressures during Malaya's path to independence.
