# Peranakan street culture in Penang: towards revitalization

Tan Sooi Beng (2009) examines the eclectic street performance traditions of Penang's Peranakan (Baba-Nonya) community from the early twentieth century through their post-war decline, arguing that creative, process-oriented arts projects—exemplified by the Anak-Anak Kota programme—offer a viable path toward revitalizing these traditions for younger generations.

## Summary

The article traces the development of Peranakan performing arts in colonial Penang, drawing heavily on *Straits Echo* newspaper reports to document the flourishing of *bangsawan*, *dondang sayang*, *kroncong*, *ronggeng*, and *wayang peranakan* between the 1910s and 1930s. These forms were characterized by a distinctive eclecticism—fusing Malay, Chinese, European, and Indian musical and theatrical elements—and were performed in multi-ethnic public spaces such as amusement parks, theatre halls, and the streets during Chap Goh Meh celebrations. The author emphasizes that Peranakan music "transcended the boundaries of race, language, religion, and class" and functioned as an important site for inter-ethnic interaction (p. 160).

The decline is attributed to the 1930s economic recession, the Japanese Occupation (1941–1945), and the post-war advent of film, television, and popular music. A further structural blow came with the formation of the Federation of Malaya in 1948, which forced diverse communities to identify with one of three racial categories, diminishing the space for mixed identities. Some Baba families subsequently re-Sinified, sending children to Chinese-medium schools and accelerating the erosion of the eclectic culture (p. 161). The author then shifts to contemporary revitalization, documenting the annual Baba Convention (from 1988) and, in greater detail, the Anak-Anak Kota arts heritage programme, which uses heritage walks, oral history, instrument training, and creative composition to engage young people with Peranakan and broader Penang street traditions.

The core argument is that Peranakan culture cannot be "fossilized" but must evolve through active participation. The younger generation must first understand the tradition before they can adapt it; the eclecticism and adaptability that defined Peranakan culture in its heyday remain its most important survival strategies (pp. 162–165).

### Key Findings

- *Straits Echo* (28 May 1918) records a Baba *bangsawan* performance of *Princess Nilam Chahaya* at the Anglo-Chinese School Union Building for the China Flood Fund, with Malay professionals present praising the all-Chinese cast.
- The *Straits Echo* (23 July 1920) reports that the Sultan of Perak, as invited guest, awarded a cup and gold medals to the chief performers of *Ginufifah* or *Herto Brabant* at the Empire Theatre, Penang Road.
- *Penghiboran Hati* (1924) was a published collection of sixteen *kroncong* and *stambul* pieces with musical notation for violin, mandolin, and guitar, including *Stambol Satoe*—later adopted as Malaysia's national anthem (p. 159).
- The annual Baba Convention, initiated by Datuk Khoo Keat Siew in 1988, brought together Peranakans from Penang, Melaka, and Singapore; a Phuket delegation joined from 2002, and the 16th Convention (5–7 December 2003) in Penang included a Thai Baba meal and *Loi Loi Kratong* dancing (p. 161).
- The *Kisah Pulau Pinang* (2006) musical production by Anak-Anak Kota combined heritage walks, oral interviews with veteran performers, and training in *gamelan*, *boria*, *pantun*, and *dondang sayang* instruments, culminating in a community performance in an open space in Georgetown's inner city (pp. 163–165).
- The 15th Baba Convention in Singapore (2002) produced a consensus that Baba culture could not be preserved as it was in the pre-war days; Baba Lee Liang Hai stressed that "the young Peranakan should know what their culture is so that they can adapt it in their own way in the 21st century" (p. 163).

### Conclusion

Tan's definitive historical takeaway is that Peranakan street culture's survival depends not on museum-style preservation but on active, creative engagement by the younger generation who must first understand the tradition before they can reinvent it. The same eclecticism and adaptability that made Peranakan performances attractive to multi-ethnic audiences in the colonial period remain the community's most potent tools for cultural continuity in the twenty-first century.

## Context

- Primary sources include *Straits Echo* newspaper reports (1918–1933), oral interviews with veteran Baba musicians (notably Mr Poh Chin Hean), and the author's own fieldwork and directorship of Anak-Anak Kota productions (*Kisah Pulau Pinang*, *Ronggeng Merdeka*, *Opera Pasar*).
- The article extends Tan's earlier monograph on *bangsawan* (1993) into the broader Peranakan street culture domain and connects historical performance practice to contemporary heritage revitalization methodology, contributing to the growing literature on Peranakan identity and cultural sustainability.
