# Social change in Rembau

Norhalim Hj. Ibrahim's 1977 article, an abstract of his University of Hull MA thesis (1976), examines how British colonialism and its associated institutions—particularly secular education, centralized administration, and the money economy—have eroded the traditional matrilineal kinship structure (*adat perpatih*) of Rembau, Negeri Sembilan. The central argument is that while economic change (rubber cultivation) and political restructuring were necessary preconditions, it was education that proved the most decisive agent in transforming the society's value system and social organization.

## Summary

Ibrahim situates his study within the existing literature on Negeri Sembilan's matrilineal societies, reviewing the work of de Moubray, Swift, Lewis, and Wahab Alwee. He identifies a gap in the scholarship: most prior studies were either descriptive accounts of the traditional system at its ideal level or static structural-functional analyses that failed to adequately trace the *process* of social change. His own contribution is to relate changes in the kinship system to specific transformations in the political, economic, and educational spheres, arguing that these changes are interdependent rather than reducible to a single causal factor.

The article first reconstructs the traditional kinship structure of Rembau in considerable detail—the hierarchical descent groups (*suku*, *perut*, *ruang*, *rumpun*), the rules of exogamy, the system of arranged marriage with its various forms of irregular marriage, uxorilocal residence, and the elaborate sanction mechanisms enforced by *adat* chiefs. It then traces the historical background of Rembau from Minangkabau migration through the Malacca and Johore periods to British intervention, establishing the political and economic conditions under which change occurred.

The analytical core of the article argues that the British did not attempt to preserve the traditional *adat* system of government within the colonial framework but replaced it with a centralized bureaucratic administration. This political restructuring, combined with the introduction of secular education and the money economy (especially rubber), created conditions in which the traditional descent-based groups lost their practical significance. Ibrahim is careful to note, however, that education is both a cause and a consequence of change—it was made possible by political change and in turn produced the mobility and new values that further undermined the *adat* system.

### Key Findings

- The Customary Land Tenure Enactment (Chapter 215), passed in 1909, transferred land administration from *adat* chiefs (*lembaga*, *undang*) to the District Officer, *Penghulu Mukim*, and *ketua kampung*, effectively removing the descent groups' control over the most important material resource (p. 143).
- The society originally had twelve *suku* (clans) but now comprises twenty-five; most members no longer know to which *suku* they belong, and some are clanless because their mothers were from non-*adat* Malay or other ethnic communities (pp. 140, 145).
- The majority of rubber holdings were less than three acres, and the rubber industry's significant economic impact was only felt in the 1950s with government replanting schemes; most people still depended on remittances from children who had migrated to urban areas (p. 144).
- Post-marital residence has shifted from uxorilocal to neolocal, with young couples moving to urban areas after marriage; the prohibition on marriage with a mother's sister's daughter is giving way, and the Religious Department has taken over all matters of marriage and divorce (pp. 145–146).
- In land inheritance cases, the District Officer's decision is final and the *lembaga* serves merely as a witness; in most recent cases Islamic law has taken precedence over *adat* (pp. 143, 146).
- The traditional *adat* chiefs are being replaced by educated men with urban experience who justify liberal interpretations by the *adat* maxim: "if the majority of the people want it, the *adat* can be replaced" (*alah adat kerana mau-fakat*) (p. 146).

### Conclusion

Ibrahim concludes that the traditional matrilineal structure is ceasing to function as the framework ordering social life in Rembau, with the nuclear family and neolocal residence replacing the extended matrilineal household. He qualifies the primacy of education by noting it is embedded in and conditioned by the broader political transformation of colonialism, and he ends with the observation that the *adat* is now in a state of suspended animation—neither actively preserved nor fully abandoned—while a counter-surge from the educated class for its preservation paradoxically emerges from those whose very education prevents them from recreating traditional society.

## Context

- This article is an abstract of Ibrahim's 1976 MA thesis at the University of Hull, drawing on fieldwork in Rembau district; it contributes to the small body of analytical (as opposed to purely descriptive) studies of Negeri Sembilan's matrilineal societies, building directly on Swift (1965) and Lewis (1962).
- The study's historiographical value lies in its explicit attempt to move beyond the static structural-functional model dominant in the literature and to trace the *process* of change by linking kinship transformation to specific institutional interventions (land legislation, educational policy, administrative restructuring).
