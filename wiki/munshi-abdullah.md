# Munshi Abdullah

### Life and Origins

Abdullah bin Abdul Kadir (1795–1854), universally known as Abdullah "Munshi", was an early nineteenth-century writer, teacher, and interpreter whose works are regarded as foundational to modern Malay prose literature. Born in the Tamil quarter of Malacca, he served as a letter-writer, tutor, and court interpreter across the Straits Settlements, and produced the *Hikayat Abdullah* (1843), the *Kisah Pelayaran Abdullah* (1838), and a number of topical *syair* (ballads) that together constitute one of the earliest sustained bodies of Malay-language autobiographical and journalistic writing. His significance lies not only in the literary achievement of his prose but in the complex position he occupied between the European colonial administration, the Malay aristocratic world, and the *Peranakan Keling* (Arab-Tamil Muslim) community from which he was born.

The question of Abdullah's racial and linguistic identity has been the subject of sustained scholarly debate. H.F. O'B. Traill, in a 1979 article, dismantled the long-standing assumption—traceable to J.T. Thomson's 1874 introduction to his English translation of the *Hikayat*—that Abdullah was of Malay or half-Malay heritage. Traill argued, on the basis of Abdullah's own genealogical account and the burial records at Masjid Keling, that Abdullah was a *Peranakan Keling* whose mother-tongue was Tamil rather than Malay [[1, pp. 67–83]](#ref-1). The 1843 original manuscript of the *Hikayat*, discovered in the Library of Congress by Professor A. Teeuw in 1967, confirms this: after naming four letter-writers of Malacca including his own father, Abdullah explicitly states that "all of these were entirely *Peranakan Keling*", a phrase excised in the 1849 lithographed edition [[2, pp. 129–130]](#ref-2). Traill concludes that this deletion "puts Abdullah's race as a Tamil (*Peranakan*) beyond doubt" [[2, p. 130]](#ref-2).

The genealogical details, as reconstructed by Traill from the *Hikayat* and corroborating sources, are as follows. Abdullah's great-grandfather, Shaik Abdul Kadir, migrated from the Yemen to Nagur in Mysore, where he married a Tamil woman; their four sons dispersed across Southeast Asia, one of whom, Muhammed Ibrahim, settled in Malacca [[1, pp. 70–71]](#ref-1). Abdullah's mother, Salamah (more accurately rendered "Sellamah", a common Tamil female name), was the daughter of Tamil forebears from Kedah who had converted to Islam in Malacca [[1, pp. 71–72]](#ref-1). By blood, Abdullah was approximately seven-eighths Tamil and one-eighth Arab [[1, p. 72]](#ref-1). He was born in Kampong Pali, which he identifies as the Tamil name for Kampong Masjid, a Tamil quarter of Malacca [[1, p. 72]](#ref-1).

Traill's 1981 article resolves the disputed question of Abdullah's birth date by arguing that the opening paragraph of the chapter "Peri Abdullah Di-Peranakkan" in the *Hikayat*—which records the precise date and time of the British capture of Malacca from the Dutch in August 1795—was intended as the temporal marker for his own birth, placing it on the morning of 18 August 1795 [[3, pp. 44–45]](#ref-3). The 1843 manuscript confirms this reading: where the 1849 text ambiguously links his birth to "the moment mentioned above", the 1843 version simply states "I was born at the time mentioned above", leaving only one referent—the English capture of Malacca [[2, pp. 130–131]](#ref-2). This corrects the previously accepted date of 1797, derived from Abdullah's erroneous statement of being forty-six years old in 1843; in the 1849 edition he deleted the reference to his age, suggesting he recognised the figure was incorrect [[3, pp. 43–44]](#ref-3).

### Career and Administrative Postings

Abdullah's career as a letter-writer and interpreter began in Malacca, where he was part of a small community of *Peranakan Keling* scribes who served the European administration. The *Hikayat* records a list of Malacca letter-writers on which Abdullah's father appears, and which Abdullah himself joined [[1, p. 31]](#ref-1). His early education included study under three Arab religious teachers in Malacca, a claim examined critically by A. Talib Yusof in 1990. Yusof demonstrated that the first of these, Habib 'Abdullah al-Haddad, the celebrated Hadrami saint and composer of the *Ratib al-Haddad*, died in Tarim in 1719, some seventy-six years before Abdullah's birth, and is known to have travelled only within the Arabian Peninsula; Talib concludes that Abdullah inserted this venerable name to lend distinction to the circumstances surrounding his own birth [[4, p. 29]](#ref-4). The second, Mu'allim Muhyi'l-Din, a non-Hadrami Yemenite from Aceh who taught Quranic recitation (*tajwid*), leaves no trace in Hadrami chronicles, though he is described as charging five dollars per student per year with forty to fifty students enrolled [[4, pp. 27–29]](#ref-4). The third, Saiyyid Shaikh bin Ahmad Ba Fakih, presents a more complex case: Hadrami sources portray him as a great Sufi leader who settled in Surabaya, but the Dutch orientalist L.W.C. Van den Berg recorded him as a failed merchant imprisoned for debt in both Sumenep and Semarang, while local Muslims venerated him as a saint [[4, pp. 31–32]](#ref-4). Talib notes a chronological impossibility—Abdullah was born before Shaikh's known activities in the archipelago—and suggests Abdullah may have met him during a period of economic hardship following his dismissal from Sumenep, again embellishing his own educational pedigree [[4, p. 32]](#ref-4).

Abdullah's relationship with the British colonial administration was established early. He worked as Malay writer and tutor to Stamford Raffles when the latter was in Malacca in 1810–11, and was on friendly terms with him again when Raffles was in Singapore in 1822–23 [6, p. 193, n. 6]. Raffles, on leaving Malacca for Europe, gave Abdullah a letter stating he would receive "a higher salary than Malay people" in Singapore courts—an implicit acknowledgment that Abdullah was not himself Malay [[1, p. 72]](#ref-1). Traill notes that A.H. Hill's translation unjustifiably inserts the word "other" before "Malays", altering the meaning [[1, p. 72]](#ref-1).

The date of Abdullah's first arrival in Singapore has been the subject of careful source criticism by C.A. Gibson-Hill, who demonstrated in 1955 that Abdullah's own claim of arriving "about four months" after the founding of the settlement is irreconcilable with the documentary evidence [[5, pp. 191–195]](#ref-5). Gibson-Hill marshals a series of contradictions: Captain Flint, whom Abdullah describes as already resident as harbour-master, was in England until the end of 1819 and did not reach Singapore until 24 April 1820; Captain Methven's stone house, which Abdullah mentions as already built, was not ready until September 1820; and the first Chinese junk did not arrive until February 1821 [[5, pp. 194–195]](#ref-5). Johanna Hare's letter confirms that the Rev. C.H. Thomsen, Abdullah's travelling companion, was still in Malacca in August 1820, and the earliest references to Thomsen in Singapore date from May 1822 [[5, pp. 194–195]](#ref-5). Gibson-Hill concludes that Abdullah and Thomsen most likely reached Singapore between the second quarter of 1821 and mid-May 1822, making Abdullah's "four months" claim irreconcilable with the available evidence [[5, p. 195]](#ref-5). The implication is that Abdullah was absent during the entire critical founding period and was therefore "clearly so badly misled" about events he never witnessed [[6, p. 111]](#ref-6).

In Singapore, Abdullah's career encompassed teaching, interpretation, and literary production. His social position was that of an intermediate figure: respected by the European ruling power as a teacher and companion, yet regarded with suspicion and jealousy by his fellow *Peranakan Keling* community in both Malacca and Singapore [[3, p. 45]](#ref-3). Traill draws on the *Hikayat*, the *Pelayaran*, and the memoirs of Abdullah's son Mohd. Ibrahim to present a portrait of a man who was simultaneously pedantic and deeply emotional, ascetic in temperament yet capable of warmth and generosity, and courageous in both physical and moral terms [[3, p. 45]](#ref-3).

### Works

Abdullah's major literary output comprises the *Hikayat Abdullah*, the *Kisah Pelayaran Abdullah*, and a number of shorter *syair* and prose pieces. The *Hikayat Abdullah* was completed in 1843 and lithographed in 1849 [[3, p. 43]](#ref-3). The question of who prompted Abdullah to write it was resolved by Traill in 1981, who demonstrated that a document dated 1843, attached by the missionary Alfred North to a copy of the *Hikayat* sent to the Library of Congress, identifies North—not Keasberry, as previously assumed—as the "friend" whom Abdullah credits with urging him to write his memoirs [[3, pp. 36–37]](#ref-3). North's account reveals that he provided Abdullah with a written list of suggested topics, including the character of his father, his early education, descriptions of eminent men he had taught, and remarks on Malay customs, laws, and superstitions; Traill shows that North's suggestions map almost exactly onto the structure of the finished work [[3, pp. 36–37]](#ref-3). Both of Abdullah's major books were deliberately slanted toward a European readership [[3, p. 37]](#ref-3).

The 1843 manuscript, copied "from the autograph" by a Bugis scribe named Husin and dispatched by Alfred North to the Library in October 1843, reveals significant textual differences from the 1849 lithographed edition [[2, p. 126]](#ref-2). The missionaries' editorial hand is evident in the 1849 revision: references to Roman Catholicism were removed or substituted, the cross on St. Andrew's Church in Singapore was excised, and the old church on the hill at Malacca Fort was changed from "gereja orang Sarani" (the church of the Catholics) to "Portuguese" [[2, p. 132]](#ref-2). Abdullah's scathing characterisation of the missionary Thomsen—describing him as coarse, thick-tongued, and speaking "inwards to himself"—was substantially softened, though the 1849 text retains enough to show Abdullah's deep irritation [[2, p. 132]](#ref-2). Conversely, the 1843 text reveals a less flattering portrait of the much-respected Alfred North, who during Abdullah's severe illness in Singapore replied to his request to return to Malacca with "How can you go back, for I have a great deal of work still waiting to be done" [[2, p. 133]](#ref-2).

Traill's 1979 article addresses the literary-historical implications of Abdullah's racial reclassification. He contends that Abdullah's celebrated status as "Father of Modern Malay Literature" rests on an irony: Abdullah himself explicitly tried to model his writing on the classical Malay tradition, using formulaic openings and archaic connectives in deliberate imitation of the *Sejarah Melayu* and *Hikayat Hang Tuah*. His modernism was therefore fortuitous rather than intended, arising from his factual subject matter, his journalistic naturalness, his outsider's critical perspective on Malay feudalism, and the residual "bazaar Malay" practicality that his classical imitations could never fully suppress [[1, pp. 67–83]](#ref-1). Traill supports this with close textual analysis, citing numerous unidiomatic constructions in the *Hikayat* that betray a non-native speaker's limitations [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1). Remarkably, Wilkinson's *Malay-English Dictionary* (1955) drew no fewer than 15,000 illustrative examples from Abdullah's works, a fact Traill finds notable given his argument that Abdullah's Malay was frequently unidiomatic [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1).

The *Kisah Pelayaran Abdullah*, the account of his 1838 mission to Kelantan to secure the release of detained *sampan pukat* trading vessels, has been subjected to critical scrutiny by C. Skinner, who used the *Singapore Free Press* to cross-reference and contextualise the narrative [[7, pp. 23–33]](#ref-7). Skinner demonstrates that the *Kisah* compresses the actual timeline of the voyage, flatters its author at the expense of his Chinese interpreter Ko An, and omits the broader commercial and political context that the newspaper record preserves [[7, p. 27]](#ref-7). The expedition departed Singapore at midnight on 27 March 1838 and returned on 24 April, but the time actually spent in Kelantan was remarkably brief—roughly from midday on Friday 13 April to midnight on Saturday 14 April, a period of about thirty-six hours during which all the incidents Abdullah recounts as occurring in the Kelantan capital were crammed [[7, p. 27]](#ref-7). The *Kisah* depicts Ko An as a persistent coward who repeatedly tried to turn back, wept, consulted oracles, and threatened to desert; Skinner shows, however, that Ko An's own letter to the newspaper presents a different picture, criticising the mission for neglecting its commercial duties and disputing whether the Bendahara's assurances about payment were genuine [[7, p. 32]](#ref-7). Skinner concludes that the *Kisah* gives "an unduly unfavourable picture of Ko An, and an unduly favourable picture of Abdullah" [[7, p. 32]](#ref-7). Abdullah's agreed fee for the mission was eighty dollars; the financing syndicate initially withheld payment, and he only received it after appealing to the magistrates' clerk, Mr. James Loch [[7, p. 32]](#ref-7).

Among Abdullah's shorter works, the *Shaer Kampong Gelam Terbakar*, a ballad composed in March 1847 describing the devastating fire that swept through Singapore's Kampong Gelam district on 12 February 1847, has been the subject of a critical edition by C. Skinner [[8, pp. 21–56]](#ref-8). The poem is notable for its vivid, first-person account of the chaos, its naming of specific individuals including Governor Butterworth and Superintendent Dunman, and its closing moral reflections on the lack of wells and the absence of coordinated command [[8, p. 26]](#ref-8). The fire destroyed over 250 houses, with losses estimated at nearly $100,000 [[8, pp. 25–26]](#ref-8). Skinner's annotations transform the poem from a literary curiosity into a valuable prosopographical source for the European and Asian communities of 1840s Singapore [[8, p. 26]](#ref-8).

Raimy Ché-Ross's 2008 article re-appraises Abdullah's brief *syair* "Malay Poem on New Year's Day (1848)", published in James Richardson Logan's *Journal of the Indian Archipelago and Eastern Asia* [[9, pp. 49–82]](#ref-9). The *syair* comprises 96 lyric sentences forming 24 four-line stanzas, and is not listed in the volume's table of contents or index [[9, p. 63]](#ref-9). Ché-Ross argues that the poem's critical verses—long overshadowed by its formulaic opening praise of Queen Victoria—reveal a more complex and assertive Abdullah who resented the exploitation of his fellow natives by the colonial elite, thereby challenging the reductive "tali barut" (collaborator) caricature that has dominated modern assessments of the Munshi [[9, pp. 49–82]](#ref-9). The opening stanzas of praise, Ché-Ross contends, should be read not as evidence of sycophancy but as customary pro forma acknowledgments of patronage, comparable to loyal addresses published as late as 1934 by the ultra-nationalist *Warta Malaya* [[9, p. 55]](#ref-9). The more caustic middle and closing stanzas register Abdullah's indignation at the physical degradation endured by participants in the New Year's Day sports and his contempt for the *Orang Putih* who orchestrated their humiliation for amusement [[9, p. 55]](#ref-9).

Abdullah's *Hikayat* also contains an eyewitness description of the Malacca Fort as it stood during its demolition in 1807, when Abdullah was eleven years old. A.H. Hill's 1950 article presents a translation and critical commentary on this passage, cross-referencing it with Governor Bort's 1678 handing-over report and earlier Portuguese sources [[10, pp. 84–99]](#ref-10). Hill demonstrates that the account retains sufficient accuracy to illuminate the fort's plan, bastion layout, and internal structures for a period over which official records are otherwise silent [[10, p. 84]](#ref-10). The description includes the granite blocks, sloping bulwarks, eight bastions, four gates, three drawbridges, the moat, the church on the hill, the Governor's residence, and the prison with its torture instruments [[10, p. 84]](#ref-10).

### Legacy and Documentation

Abdullah's works have remained consistently in print for over 170 years, from the 1835 publication of *Hikayat Kalila Daminah* to the 2004 Kassim Ahmad edition of *Hikayat Abdullah* [[9, p. 60]](#ref-9). His status as a literary figure has, however, been contested along multiple axes. Traill's 1979 and 1981 articles challenge the nationalist framing that had absorbed Abdullah into the Malay literary tradition from the 1950s onward, arguing that his racial identity as a *Peranakan Keling* has been systematically obscured and that his modernism was fortuitous rather than intended [[1, p. 83]](#ref-1), [[3, p. 35]](#ref-3). The "British stooge" thesis, which had gained currency in Malay-language scholarship from the 1960s onward, is addressed by Traill in 1981, who argues it rests on a misreading of Abdullah's genuine but limited worldview [[3, p. 35]](#ref-3). Ché-Ross's 2008 article further complicates this picture by demonstrating that Abdullah's shorter topical *syair* contain a "discourse of dissent" that has been systematically overlooked in favour of debates over his radical autobiography [[9, pp. 74–77]](#ref-9).

The material circumstances of Abdullah's later life are documented in his Last Will and Testament, composed in January 1854 in Singapore shortly before his fatal Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca. Raimy Ché-Ross's 2005 article presents the rediscovered *Surat Wasiat*, a legal document written in Abdullah's own Jawi hand and witnessed by three named individuals [[11, pp. 83–111]](#ref-11). The Will is dated 6 Rabiul Akhir 1275 AH (18 January 1854) and bequeaths specific properties—two houses, two plots of land, and a detailed inventory of household goods and jewellery—to his wife Hamidah and his daughter Selamah, while designating his eldest son Hussein as executor and the scholar Habib Ahmad al-Sagoff as the *washi* (legal overseer) with absolute authority to ensure the Will's terms were carried out in accordance with Islamic law [[11, pp. 100–101]](#ref-11). The attached inventory lists specific material possessions including 80 mangkuk (bowls), 50 piring kuih (cake plates), 14 cermin bunga (decorative mirrors), and gold jewellery including a *pending* (necklace) weighing "tengah tiga bungkal" [[11, p. 102]](#ref-11). Abdullah's wife had died in 1840, prompting him to sell his house in Malacca and move permanently to Singapore with his children [[3, p. 53]](#ref-3). His personal library was lost in a separate fire at Kampong Melaka on 2 September 1849; Governor Butterworth described the loss as "irreparable" [[8, p. 26]](#ref-8).

Abdullah's son, Mohd. Ibrahim bin Abdullah, served as Dato' Dalam of the Johore Court, accompanied Sultan Abu Bakar to Europe in 1878, and died in 1904; his *Kisah Pelayaran* was printed in 1919 [[3, pp. 46–47]](#ref-3). A later land bequest by Dato' Munshi Ibrahim, dated 20 Syawal 1305 AH (30 June 1888), transfers two land grants on Jalan Ayer Molek to his wife Fatimah, and serves as a comparative document illustrating the continuity of Malay legal formulae across generations [[11, pp. 107–108]](#ref-11).

The historiographical record of Abdullah in the Society's journal spans from Gibson-Hill's 1955 source-critical study of his Singapore arrival date [[5]](#ref-5) through Hill's 1950 translation of the Malacca Fort passage [[10]](#ref-10), Skinner's 1966 and 1972 articles on the *Kisah Pelayaran* and the *Shaer Kampong Gelam Terbakar* [[7]](#ref-7), [[8]](#ref-8), Traill's trilogy of 1979, 1981, and 1982 [[1]](#ref-1), [[3]](#ref-3), [[2]](#ref-2), Talib Yusof's 1990 study of his Arab teachers [[4]](#ref-4), and Ché-Ross's 2005 and 2008 articles on his Will and his New Year's Day *syair* [[11]](#ref-11), [[9]](#ref-9). Together these contributions establish a pattern of sustained critical engagement with Abdullah's texts as historical sources, his identity as a social and racial figure, and his position within the colonial literary and administrative landscape of the Straits Settlements.
