# A proposed classification of Malayan polished stone implements

H.D. Noone, Field Ethnographer for the Federated Malay States Museums, published this provisional typological scheme in 1941 to address the bewildering variety of polished stone implements recovered from North Malaya. Writing in the context of early Southeast Asian prehistoric archaeology, Noone argues that the diversity of Malayan forms is best explained by the convergence and interaction of at least three distinct technical traditions within the Peninsula, rather than by a single linear developmental sequence.

## Summary

The article tackles the practical and theoretical problem of classifying a large and heterogeneous assemblage of polished stone tools from North Malaya, where individual variation in form, finish, and material produced pieces that resist simple typological schemes. Noone proposes a classification grounded in two criteria: the symmetry or asymmetry of the longitudinal section and the geometry of the cutting edge as it appears in that section (convexo-convex, plano-convex, plano-bevel, and so on). From these criteria he derives three main divisions—Axe, Adze, and Centred-Edge Adze—which subdivide into six named forms: Axe, Notched Axe, Centred-Edge Adze, Simple Adze, Chisel Adze, and Gouge Adze. He is careful to note that the terms "axe" and "adze" are used for convenience of study in the absence of socket holes or other definitive usage indicators.

The classification is informed by surface finds from shell mounds such as Guak Kepah, by the Raffles Museum (Singapore) collection of Far Eastern Prehistory, and by comparative material from Tonkin, Laos, and other regions. Noone acknowledges that many specimens are surface finds subjected to natural wear, subsequent re-working, and even reuse by later populations as hones, hammers, or pounders, all of which complicate interpretation. He also observes that the pointed-butt axe so common in Southern India is absent from the Malayan assemblage, and that no pieces with a true working edge at each end (as occur in Indo-China) have been found in Northern Malaya.

The article culminates in a developmental hypothesis. Noone proposes that the chisel type (rectangular section, steep bevel) is of local or Northern (Bacson?) origin; that the simple adze (asymmetrical, plano-convex edge) is more characteristic of the South; and that the symmetrical axe form is a world-wide type. He argues that the interaction of these three technical traditions in the Malay Peninsula generated the many intermediate and derived varieties, with the Centred-Edge Adze being a notable example of such cross-pollination. The notched axe is treated as a temporary "Archipelago" introduction that had little influence on the already developing local industry.

### Key Findings

- The chisel adze form is described as "perhaps the commonest form of polished implement in North Malaya" and is identified by Heine-Geldern as "the typical Austro-Asiatic tool" (p. 212).
- The gouge adze form, though less common, is "even more typical of the Malayan Peninsula than the plain chisel adze," its characteristic feature being a salient, arched working edge often produced by hollow grinding (p. 213).
- A "beaked" variety of the gouge adze, side-bevelled on a cant to form a pointed working end, has no recorded finds north of Surat in Thailand (p. 213).
- Large simple adze specimens reach up to eighteen inches in length and are stout enough for use in tilling soil, possibly as plough coulters (p. 212).
- Notched axe specimens from the Guak Kepah shell mounds show extensive fracturing or wearing of one end of the working edge, a pattern suggestive of adze or hoe use whose significance Noone leaves unresolved (p. 210).
- The pointed-butt axe so common in Southern India is explicitly noted as absent from the Malayan assemblage, and no double-edged pieces (as occur in Indo-China) have been found in Northern Malaya (p. 209).

### Conclusion

Noone's definitive historical takeaway is that the remarkable diversity of Malayan polished stone implements is not the product of a single linear tradition but the result of at least three distinct technical lineages—Northern (chisel), Southern (simple adze), and a universal symmetrical axe form—converging and interacting within the Peninsula to generate intermediate and derived varieties. The gouge adze, with its fine craftsmanship, hollow grinding, and choice of hard close-grained stone, represents the apex of this technical development; its makers, Noone concludes, were "among the finest craftsmen of polished implements" (p. 214).

## Context

- Noone served as Field Ethnographer for the Federated Malay States (F.M.S.) Museums, a colonial administrative body; the article reflects the museum-curation mindset of the period, with classification serving the practical need to organise and display collections.
- The study draws on the Raffles Museum (Singapore) Hall of Far Eastern Prehistory and on comparative publications from the École Française d'Extrême-Orient, situating Malayan material within a broader Southeast Asian prehistoric framework.
