# A Revision of the Malayan Species of Ficus: Covellia and Neomorphe

In 1933, E.J.H. Corner published a comprehensive taxonomic revision of the Malayan species belonging to the fig subgenera *Covellia* and *Neomorphe* for the Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society. This work challenges previous classifications by King and Ridley, utilizing detailed morphological analysis of receptacles, flowers, and vegetative structures to clarify species boundaries and evolutionary relationships. Corner establishes new diagnostic criteria for identification and records several species previously unreported in the region.

## Summary

Corner’s revision addresses the taxonomic confusion surrounding fourteen species of *Ficus* in Malaya, examining living plants in the field and herbarium specimens in Singapore to correct empirical errors in earlier literature (p. 2). He critiques King’s 1887 monograph, arguing that fundamental knowledge of the genus requires direct observation of the receptacle’s growth pulses and bract arrangements rather than reliance on dried specimens alone (p. 2). The study details the morphological evolution of the fig-receptacle, tracing a progression from primitive axillary positions in *F. lepicarpa* to specialized cauliflorous and geocarpic habits in species like *F. geocarpa* and *F. fistulosa* (p. 6–7).

The author provides a detailed anatomical breakdown of the flowers, correcting King’s assertion that male flowers possess separate perianth pieces by demonstrating they are gamophyllous with lobes (p. 12). Corner confirms that dioecism is the rule across the subgenera, with the notable exception of *F. glomerata*, which is monoecious and features transitional flowers bridging the gap between gall and female types (p. 14, 44). He further elucidates the geocarpic habit in species such as *F. Beccarii* and *F. chamaecarpa*, describing how fruiting branches function as stolons to propagate the plant underground (p. 7–8).

Significant taxonomic adjustments include the description of *F. viridicarpa* as a new species from Negri Sembilan and the reclassification of *F. pomifera* as allied to *Covellia* (*F. obpyramidata*) rather than typical *Neomorphe* (p. 52, 46). Corner also clarifies the synonymy of *F. polysyce*, determining it to be a composite name covering *F. fistulosa*, *F. Miquelii*, *F. Scortechinii*, and *F. variegata* (p. 42). The work concludes with identification keys based on receptacle and vegetative characters, providing a practical tool for botanists working in the region (p. 62–63).

### Key Findings

*   **New Species Description:** *Ficus viridicarpa* is described as a new species from Negri Sembilan, distinguished by its greyish white bark, lack of prominent buttresses, and green-ripening receptacles (p. 52).
*   **Floral Morphology Correction:** Male flowers possess a gamophyllous perianth with 1–3 lobes and two bracteoles, contradicting King’s earlier description of three separate imbricating pieces (p. 12).
*   **Reproductive Strategy:** While most species are dioecious, *Ficus glomerata* is uniquely monoecious, containing male, gall, and female flowers in the same receptacle with transitional forms (p. 44).
*   **Geocarpic Evolution:** The geocarpic habit in *F. Beccarii*, *F. geocarpa*, and *F. chamaecarpa* involves fruiting branches acting as stolons that burrow into the soil to produce subterranean figs (p. 7).
*   **Synonymy Resolution:** *Ficus polysyce* Ridley is determined to be a synonym *pro parte* of multiple species, specifically *F. fistulosa*, *F. Miquelii*, *F. Scortechinii*, and *F. variegata* (p. 42).
*   **Subgeneric Affinity:** *Ficus pomifera* is shown to be closely allied with *F. obpyramidata* in *Covellia* rather than the other Malayan species of *Neomorphe* (p. 46).

### Conclusion

Corner’s definitive historical takeaway is that the traditional subgenera *Covellia* and *Neomorphe* may not represent natural groups, as evidenced by the affinities of *F. pomifera* and the morphological diversity within the defined sections (p. 16–17). He emphasizes that accurate taxonomy requires field observation of living plants to understand reproductive biology and evolutionary pathways, moving beyond the limitations of herbarium-based descriptions. This revision sets a new standard for Indo-Malayan botanical research by integrating detailed morphological data with ecological habits.

## Context

*   **Colonial Scientific Infrastructure:** The study relies heavily on the Singapore herbarium and field collections (denoted by 'S.' numbers), reflecting the British colonial administration's investment in documenting local flora for economic and scientific purposes (p. 4).
*   **Archival Collections:** Corner explicitly cites collections from key colonial botanists such as Ridley, King, and Wray, utilizing their field notes and specimens to validate his revisions (p. 18–61).
