# The Pak Pandir cycle of tales

Amin Sweeney's 1976 monograph-length article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* presents the first systematic study of the Pak Pandir cycle of comic tales in Malay oral tradition, based on fieldwork conducted across all states of Peninsular Malaysia and Patani between 1971 and 1973. The work directly challenges the long-dominant scholarly reliance on the 1908 published *Cherita Jenaka* and R.O. Winstedt's conclusions drawn from it, demonstrating that the oral tradition is far richer, more varied, and structurally distinct from the literary adaptation.

## Summary

Sweeney's central argument is that decades of Malay literary scholarship have been distorted by treating the printed *Cherita Jenaka* as the authoritative source for understanding comical tales in oral tradition. He shows that Winstedt's classification of five comic characters into three types, and his distinction between "indigenous" and "foreign" characters, collapses when confronted with the actual oral material. A 1972 survey of 680 tales collected by 150 students revealed that Pak Pandir dominates the comic tradition with 100 tales (14.7%), while the other four *Cherita Jenaka* characters (Lebai Malang, Pak Kaduk, Pak Belalang, Si Luncai) each yielded only 5–8 tales, almost all of which were uniform versions closely matching the published text. Pak Pandir, by contrast, is the hub of a cycle of 82 distinct tales, comparable in function to the Sundanese Kabayan cycle, and his character is not fixed: he appears as numskull, trickster, or even as a co-deceiver of ogres depending on the tale.

The methodological core of the article involves classifying the 82 tales according to plot structure rather than character type. Sweeney identifies six major plot types: misinterpretation of an order (the most common framework, involving a sequence of order, separation, misinterpretation, reunion, revelation, and reproach); misinterpretation of a situation; simple misinterpretation without conventional framework; misinterpretation arising from another character's action; deceit-deception (subdivided into deception of a numskull and deception of a normal character); and bizarre reaction or result. He explicitly critiques both the Aarne-Thompson tale-type index and Propp's morphological method as theoretically inadequate for this material, while still employing tale-type numbers where relevant to trace the spread of individual tales.

The article provides extensive comparative evidence linking Pak Pandir tales to parallel cycles in Sundanese (Kabayan), Batak (Ama ni Pandir, Si Tagan Dori), Achinese (Pa' Pande / Si Meusekin), Khoja Nasruddin, and the Panchatantra. Sweeney demonstrates that many tales in the cycle are not translations but independent adaptations of shared motifs, and that the oral versions often preserve older or more original forms than the literary text. He also documents the stylistic features of oral performance—PP's characteristic lisp, set phrases, the absence of professional stylization, and the informal, interruptible nature of amateur storytelling.

### Key Findings

- Of 680 comical tales collected in the 1972 student survey, 100 (14.7%) were Pak Pandir tales, compared to only 7 (Lebai Malang), 8 (Pak Kaduk), 5 (Pak Belalang), and 7 (Si Luncai), establishing PP as the dominant comic figure in Malay oral tradition (p. 13).
- The *Cherita Jenaka* (1908), compiled by Raja Haji Yahya bin Mat Ali of Chenderiang under British administrative auspices, contains only a fraction of the oral Pak Pandir cycle; a majority of the 82 oral tales are absent from the book, and where both forms exist, the oral version is often the earlier and more original (pp. 13–14).
- Winstedt's 1958 claim that Malay folklore contains only three comic types embodied in five characters is refuted: the survey revealed "hundreds" of comic characters and far more than three types, with inconsistent type-casting (e.g., PP as both numskull and trickster) (p. 12).
- Tale 65 (the "rag-in-anus" feast tale) is the most widely spread PP tale, occurring in all areas of the peninsula, and exists in two major geographical variants (A: MA departs; B: PP hides before eating); the literary version omits the central vulgar motif, a sanitization Sweeney attributes to Winstedt's Victorian sensibility rather than to the oral tradition (pp. 78–79).
- Tale 70 (PP mistaken for a visitor from the heavens / kayangan) shows possible derivation from the Panchatantra story "The Weaver who loved a Princess," and exhibits the set lisp formula *'Alau ada Andih, 'oleh 'akar 'isang* as a revelation device across multiple versions (pp. 79–80).
- The "stupid ogre" tales (56–58) demonstrate PP's role-shifting: he is simultaneously deceiver and potential numskull, creating an ambiguity absent from standard misinterpretation tales, with informants themselves divided on whether PP's killing of the ogre's child is bungling or deliberate violence (pp. 73–74).

### Conclusion

Sweeney's definitive takeaway is that the Pak Pandir cycle constitutes a living, heterogeneous oral tradition that cannot be reduced to the literary *Cherita Jenaka* or to Winstedt's tidy typology. Pak Pandir functions not as a fixed character but as a narrative hook—a "hub around which revolves a whole cycle of tales"—capable of absorbing motifs from numskull, trickster, ogre-slaying, and aetiological traditions alike. The oral tradition is more varied, more structurally complex, and more resistant to standardization than the published text suggests, and any adequate study of Malay comic folklore must begin with the teller, not the page.

## Context

- Primary fieldwork was conducted by Sweeney personally and by 150 students across all Peninsular Malaysian states and Patani (1971–1973); 156 informants are documented in the table of sequences at the end of the article, with district, sex, and age group recorded for each (pp. 85–88).
- Historiographically, the article represents a decisive turn away from the Winstedt school's literary-text-based approach toward a fieldwork-driven, plot-structural analysis of Malay oral literature, and it established the methodological template for subsequent studies of Malay comic and numskull tale cycles.
