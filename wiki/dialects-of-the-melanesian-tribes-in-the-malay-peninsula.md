# Dialects of the Melanesian tribes in the Malay Peninsula

N. von Miklucho-Maclay, a Russian naturalist and ethnographer, presented this article to the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society in May 1878, drawing on fieldwork conducted during two journeys into the interior of the Malay Peninsula (1875 and a subsequent expedition). The overarching thesis is that the nomadic interior tribes—known to Malays as Orang Utan and Orang Sakai—constitute a surviving Melanesian (Papuan) stock whose languages, though rapidly eroding under Malay pressure, retain cognates that link them to the original population of the region.

## Summary

The article is structured as extracts from two letters addressed to Otto Bohtlingk of the Imperial Academy of Sciences at St. Petersburg. In the first letter, dated 28 May 1875 from the Istana in Johor, Miklucho-Maclay explains his method: gathering groups of men, listening for words that were not Malay, and verifying their non-Malay status with local speakers. He collected a vocabulary of roughly fifty terms from two dialects spoken by the Orang Utan of Johor, noting that only elderly men retained any knowledge of the old tongue. A particularly significant detail is the superstition known as the "Bhasa Kapor" or "Camphor language," whereby jungle-dwellers believed they must speak the old language to successfully locate camphor trees; this belief, he argues, inadvertently preserved linguistic fragments that would otherwise have been lost.

The second letter reports on a second journey through the mountains of Pahang, Kelantan, and as far north as Singora and Ligor (south of Siam), where Miklucho-Maclay encountered what he considered an unmixed Melanesian population still speaking their own language. The comparative vocabulary he assembles—spanning four dialects of the Orang Sakai and two of the Orang Utan—reveals striking lexical continuity across the entire peninsula, from Johor in the south to Ligor in the north. He identifies three words (*Dak* for "Sea," *Koi* for "Head," *Tal* for "Hut") as cognates with Papuan dialects he had recorded on Mount Halmahera in 1873, while cautioning that no definitive conclusion can be drawn from such a small sample. His broader argument is that the Orang Utan of Johor, despite heavy Malay intermixture, still bear traces of Melanesian blood, and that the linguistic evidence confirms a single original population that was progressively driven into the interior as Malay settlement expanded.

### Key Findings

- Three lexical cognates with Papuan dialects of Mount Halmahera (visited 1873): *Dak* (Sea), *Koi* (Head), *Tal* (Hut) (p. 38).
- The "Bhasa Kapor" (Camphor language) superstition—requiring speakers to use the old tongue when searching for camphor trees—served as an unintentional mechanism of linguistic preservation across parts of Johor (p. 38).
- Among the Orang Rayet of Palong (a tributary of the River Moar), only one elderly man retained knowledge of the native numerals, and only up to four; the younger generation had abandoned the language entirely (p. 38, n. 2).
- The comparative vocabulary demonstrates close linguistic connection between the mixed Orang Utan of Johor and the unmixed Orang Sakai of Ulu Kelantan, Ulu Patanis, Palon (Rumpen), and Ulu Indau, spanning the full length of the peninsula from Johor to Ligor (pp. 42–43).
- Miklucho-Maclay's first fieldwork was conducted in Johor in 1875; the second journey covered Pahang, Kelantan, Singora, and Ligor, where he found a population he judged to belong to the Melanesian stock based on physical "habitus" (pp. 41–42).

### Conclusion

Miklucho-Maclay's definitive takeaway is that the interior tribes of the Malay Peninsula represent a single, originally widespread Melanesian population that was progressively displaced into the mountains and forests by Malay expansion. The linguistic evidence—particularly the close correspondence between the heavily mixed Orang Utan of Johor and the relatively unmixed Orang Sakai of the north—confirms his earlier anthropological hypothesis that these groups share a common Melanesian origin, and that their languages, though fragmentary, constitute a "monument of the original race."

## Context

- Miklucho-Maclay was a Russian naturalist and ethnographer working independently in the Straits Settlements and the Malay states; his letters were addressed to a fellow Russian academician, and the article was translated from German for the Society's journal.
- The study draws on no archival collections but on direct field observation and elicitation from informants; its historiographical contribution lies in being among the earliest systematic attempts to document and compare the languages of the Orang Asli, predating most subsequent linguistic work on the subject by several decades.
