# Spolia mentawiensia. Acridiidae (Orthoptera

R. O. Winstedt published "Kedah Laws" in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1928, presenting a previously unknown Malay-language manuscript of port and state regulations from the Kedah Sultanate that had been misattributed to Kelantan. The article's central argument is that the manuscript—obtained from the Dato' Luar of Kelantan as the "only extant" copy of ancient Kelantan law—is in fact a Kedah legal compendium, a conclusion Winstedt establishes through internal evidence of ruler names and administrative titles.

## Summary

Winstedt's intervention begins with a problem of attribution. The manuscript in question contained four distinct legal codes plus a chapter on the *bunga mas* (the golden-flower tribute paid to Siam), the installation of rulers, and betrothal customs. On its face it appeared to be a Kelantan document, but Winstedt identified the rulers named in the text—Sultan Rijalu'd-din Muhammad Shah at Naga, Sultan Muhiyyu'd-din Mansur, Sultan Jamiwa, and Sultan Ahmad Taju'd-din Halim Shah ibni Sultan Abdullah Mukarram Shah—as Kedah sovereigns, not Kelantan ones (p. 2). This single observation reoriented the entire manuscript's significance, transforming it from a curiosity of Kelantan legal history into a primary source for the administrative and commercial life of the Kedah Sultanate in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

The oldest and most substantial code, dated 1060 A.H. (1650 A.D.), is a detailed port-law that regulates every phase of a foreign vessel's stay in Kedah waters: arrival procedures, customs duties, storage fees, the sale of slaves and elephants, the compulsory purchase of tin at a discount below market rate, and the ceremonial reception of diplomatic letters from as far afield as Gujarat, Kalinga, Patani, Perak, Jambi, and the Dutch at Malacca (pp. 3–8). Winstedt prints this code in full Malay with an English outline, and he situates it within a broader manuscript tradition by noting that a maritime section was copied in 1222 A.H. under the Acehnese Sultan Jamal al-'Alam Badr al-munir (r. 1703–1726), and that a parallel edition copied for Raffles survives in the Batavian Society Library at Leiden (p. 3). The later codes—those of Dato' Sri Paduka Tuan (1667) and the Qanun Law of Dato' Kota Star—shift focus from maritime commerce to rural governance (buffalo fencing, rice-field liability, night curfews, zakat collection) and to the ceremonial hierarchy of the Kedah court, including the precise regalia, seating order, and verbal protocols of the *balai besar* (pp. 9–44).

Winstedt's method is that of the diplomatic editor: he does not interpret or theorise so much as he establishes provenance, dates the layers of the manuscript, identifies the officials who compiled each section, and cross-references the text against known Malacca and maritime codes (van Ronkel's *Undang-Undang Melaka*, de Hollander's *Handleiding*, Dulaurier's *Institutions Maritimes*). The result is a document that serves simultaneously as a legal text, a commercial tariff, and a protocol manual for a vassal state in the Siamese sphere.

### Key Findings

- The oldest code is dated 1060 A.H. (1650 A.D.), the year Zai, Friday the 17th of Jumada'l-akhir, and was enacted under Sultan Rijalu'd-din Muhammad Shah at Naga with Raja Setia Bijaya and Raja Jalil Putra as harbour-master (Shahbandar) (pp. 3, 15).
- Customary tribute from foreign vessels was fixed at 400 *mas* for ships from Kalinga and 600 *mas* for ships from Gujarat, with the amount adjusted according to tonnage and cargo (pp. 4, 24).
- Tin from Perak was subject to a compulsory purchase: of every six *bahara* carried, two were bought by the state at a rate one *kupang* of gold below the market price, the code explicitly noting that tin was "a very important article of merchandise" demanded by Gujarati ships (pp. 5, 21).
- The *bunga mas* (golden-flower tribute to Siam) weighed seven *ringgit* of gold for the flower and spear-heads, and twelve *ringgit* of silver for the silver equivalents; in the reign of Sultan Mohamed Jemiwa an additional ten *mas* were added to its weight, and in 1222 A.H. Sultan Ahmad Taju'd-din Halim Shah raised its height because he personally went to Bangkok to do obeisance (p. 2, footnote 13, p. 38).
- The 1784 A.D. code was compiled by the Dato' Bendahara in consultation with the Paduka Maharaja, Dato' Kuraja Chenaka, Paduka Raja, Sri Raja Putra, and Paduka Sri Maharaja Jalil, and it incorporates Sections 1–23 of the *Undang-Undang Melaka* as edited by van Ronkel (1919) (pp. 2–3).
- A stranger entering the country paid six *kupang* of gold for a permit; the export tax on buffaloes was six *kupang* per head, on elephants one *paha* of gold, and on slaves one *emas* each (pp. 3, 15).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive contribution is the correction of the manuscript's attribution and the demonstration that it constitutes a layered legal archive of the Kedah Sultanate spanning at least from 1650 to 1784. By anchoring each code to a named ruler and a named compiling official, he transforms an anonymous "ancient Kelantan law" into a datable sequence of Kedah state documents that illuminate the intersection of Islamic jurisprudence, Siamese vassalage, and Indian Ocean commercial practice in a peripheral Malay polity.

## Context

- Winstedt (C.M.G., D.Litt., Oxon) was a senior figure in the Malayan Civil Service and a leading colonial-era scholar of Malay history and law; his editorial framing reflects the administrative interest of the Straits Settlements and Federated Malay States in recovering pre-colonial legal precedents that might inform contemporary governance.
- The manuscript was deposited by Winstedt with the School of Oriental Studies, Finsbury Circus, London, where it remains a key source for the legal and diplomatic history of the Kedah Sultanate.
