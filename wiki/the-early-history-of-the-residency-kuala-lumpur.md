# The early history of the Residency Kuala Lumpur

H.S. Barlow, writing in 1992 to coincide with the building's dedication as the Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Memorial, traces the architectural and administrative history of the Selangor Residency in Kuala Lumpur from its origins in 1880 through its use as the official residence of Malaysia's first Prime Minister. The article reconstructs the building's design, construction, and early occupancy through a close reading of colonial secretariat files and Swettenham's own contemporaneous notes.

## Summary

The article addresses a specific historiographical gap: the physical and administrative origins of the building that would become one of Malaysia's most significant national landmarks. Barlow situates the Residency's construction within the broader political contest between Bloomfield Douglas and Frank Swettenham for control of Selangor's administration, showing how the decision to relocate the administrative headquarters from Klang to Kuala Lumpur was driven by Swettenham's assessment of the tin boom and the growing Chinese population under Yap Ah Loy's rule. The choice of site on the west bank of the Klang River was dictated by strategic considerations — a vantage point overlooking the town, proximity to the Damansara Road as a line of retreat, and separation from the Chinese shanty-town by the river itself (pp. 25–26).

The core of the article is a detailed reconstruction of the building's design and construction process, drawing on Swettenham's own unsigned minute of March 1889 addressed to A.C. Norman of the Public Works Department. This document, preserved in the Selangor Secretariat Files, reveals how Swettenham personally sketched the plan at Kuala Kangsar, how the design evolved through multiple iterations involving State Engineers Caulfeild and Bellamy, and how Swettenham made on-site alterations during construction — substituting pillars for a wall in the drawing room, adding a verandah all round, and repositioning bathroom staircases. Barlow uses this primary source to demonstrate that the building was essentially a product of Swettenham's personal aesthetic and administrative vision rather than the work of a professional architect (pp. 28–30).

The article also touches on the political tensions surrounding the building's completion, noting that Swettenham's detailed record of costs and decisions was motivated by his anticipation of the investigation that W.E. Maxwell would launch into his administration upon taking office. The narrative closes with the building's post-colonial career as Tunku Abdul Rahman's official residence and its eventual conversion into a national memorial (pp. 31–32).

### Key Findings

- The total cost of the Residency building was approximately $36,000, divided between the years 1888 and 1889 (p. 30).
- Swettenham estimated that initial repairs and basic furniture for the earlier Resthouse would cost $1,500, with $100 per year for maintenance (p. 28).
- On 6 April 1889, two Chinese convicts were shot when they attempted to escape from the Residency working party; one was shot in the head and apparently did not survive (p. 30).
- Out of a furniture vote of $4,000, $2,391.42 had been spent or committed by the time Maxwell's investigation was underway; of $320 spent in Swettenham's final days, $250 went to 'self acting commodes' and 'patent earth closets' (p. 31).
- Twenty convicts were set to work on the site on 10 October 1888, and by 31 October Swettenham requested fifty convicts guarded by Sikhs (p. 30).
- The cast iron ornamental porch on the drive, which Swettenham specified in his 1889 minute, still stands at the foot of the covered approach steps (pp. 30, 33).

### Conclusion

Barlow's definitive takeaway is that the Residency was fundamentally a Swettenham creation — conceived, designed, and supervised by the man himself rather than by a professional architect — and that his meticulous contemporaneous documentation of its construction was a calculated act of self-preservation against the political investigation he knew Maxwell would mount. The building thus stands as a physical monument not only to colonial administrative ambition but to the personal rivalries that shaped the early British presence in the Malay states.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: Selangor Secretariat Files (SSF), Parliamentary Papers by Command No. 3095 of 1881, and JKR detailed building plans.
- Historiographical contribution: The article provides the first detailed architectural and administrative account of the Residency's construction, drawing on Swettenham's own unsigned minute (SSF KL 870/89) which had not previously been published or analysed in depth.
