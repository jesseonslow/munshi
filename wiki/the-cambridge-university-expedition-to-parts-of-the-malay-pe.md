# The Cambridge University Expedition to parts of the Malay Peninsula, 1899–1900: personal accounts. W.W. Skeat and F.F. Laidlaw. {Introd. C.A. Gibson-Hill

C. A. Gibson-Hill, W. W. Skeat, and F. F. Laidlaw published in 1953 the personal accounts of the Cambridge University Expedition to the North-Eastern Malay States and Upper Perak, conducted in 1899–1900 under Skeat's leadership. The volume presents Skeat's abridged diary (prepared by Sir Richard Winstedt from a manuscript completed shortly before his death) and Laidlaw's narrative of his time with the main party and his ascent of Gunong Inas, together with Gibson-Hill's introductory survey of the expedition's origins, personnel, and scientific legacy. The overarching significance lies in the extraordinary breadth of ethnographic, zoological, and botanical material gathered from a region then under Siamese suzerainty and largely unexplored by Western science.

## Summary

The expedition was conceived by Skeat during his service in the Selangor and Perak Civil Services, where he had developed a deep interest in Malay and aboriginal customs, particularly beliefs in magic and the social life of the Orang Asli. Fearing that contact with outside cultures was rapidly disintegrating these traditions, he proposed a scientific expedition to the less disturbed north-eastern states. The venture was modelled on the successful Haddon Expedition to New Guinea and the Torres Strait, and was funded largely by the Worts Fund of the University of Cambridge. The scientific party comprised Skeat (leader), Nelson Annandale (zoologist), Richard Evans (morphologist), D. T. Gwynne-Vaughan (botanist), F. F. Laidlaw (medical practitioner and naturalist), and R. H. Yapp (botanist), accompanied by nine Malays and a Javanese servant (p. 5–6).

Skeat's account covers the expedition's route from Bangkok through Patani, Jalor, Raman, Jambu, Teluban, Trengganu, and up the Kelantan and Lebir rivers to Kuala Aring, before a failed attempt to summit Gunong Tahan. His observations range across burial customs (Siamese tree-burials, Phram sitting-burials, Malay Muslim rites), wedding and circumcision ceremonies, spirit-halls and trance performances, the manufacture of the bunga mas tribute, salt-pan operations, pottery, rice cultivation, and the folklore of vampires, giants, and cave-princesses. The narrative is dense with linguistic notes on the Samsam (Malayo-Siamese) population, on dialectal variation in Malay, and on the Siamese administrative structures governing the states. Laidlaw's shorter account focuses on the journey up the Kelantan and Lebir rivers to Kuala Aring, the encounter with Negritos at Kampong Buntar, and the subsequent expedition to Gunong Inas in Upper Perak, where he and Yapp collected vertebrate specimens and made several new species descriptions (pp. 148–164).

The primary sources informing the narrative are the expedition members' own diaries, field notes, and measurements, supplemented by interviews with local chiefs, priests, medicine-men, and aboriginal informants. Skeat's account is particularly rich in direct quotation and in the recording of ritual procedures observed in real time, giving the volume the character of a field ethnography rather than a retrospective synthesis.

### Key Findings

- The expedition collected material that yielded over twenty published zoological papers, including new species of mammals (*Rattus inas*, *Rattus ciliatus*), reptiles (*Tropidonotus inas*, *Trimeresurus popeorum*), amphibians (*Rana larutensis*), birds (sixteen species from Gunong Inas alone), and invertebrates, as well as botanical specimens from which new species were later described (pp. 171–173).
- The Sleeping Buddha statue in Gua Gambar (Biserat) measured approximately 97 feet in total length, with a left arm of 42 feet and a mouth 1.5 feet wide; it was carved in solid limestone and flanked by colossal cobra figures, one with seven heads in a single hood (p. 39, 44–45).
- The bunga mas tribute from Patani consisted of a gold tree and a silver tree, each at least six feet tall with stand, requiring two months to complete; the combined value was estimated at over $5,000, with the Jalor contribution alone at $1,500 (p. 77–78).
- Setul's government farm revenue (Pajak Besar) rose from $10,000 to $26,000 in two years following the opening of the port, and import/export duties (Pajak Chukai) increased from approximately $900 to $3,180 over the same period (p. 131).
- The attempt on Gunong Tahan lasted from 11 August to 16 September 1899 (over five weeks against an estimated three); the party reached a peak on the eastern range but was turned back by a precipitous cliff, exhausted rice supplies, and continuous monsoon rain, with no fire possible for several nights due to wet matches and unobtainable dry fuel (pp. 99–110).
- At Kota Bharu, Kelantan, the gaol contained approximately forty prisoners in cells measuring six feet by six feet by twenty-two inches; the Sultan supplied an average ration of six gantang of rice per prisoner per day, and prisoners were let out to work in the fields once a week while still chained (p. 112–113).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's introduction frames the expedition as a landmark in the scientific study of the north-eastern Malay Peninsula, producing the foundational ethnographic work *The Pagan Races of the Malay Peninsula* (Skeat & Bladen, 1906) and a substantial body of zoological and botanical literature. The personal accounts preserve, in vivid detail, the social, religious, and economic life of the Siamese-administered Malay states at the close of the nineteenth century—a world that was already undergoing rapid transformation under colonial modernisation.

## Context

- The primary archival basis consists of Skeat's field diaries and notes (completed as a manuscript in 1952, abridged by Winstedt and revised by Gibson-Hill), Laidlaw's personal narrative, and the scientific publications listed in Appendix 3, drawn largely from the *Proceedings of the Zoological Society of London* and the Cambridge University collections.
- The volume's historiographical contribution lies in providing the only contemporary, multi-disciplinary account of the Patani States, Kelantan, Trengganu, Kedah, and Upper Perak from the field, predating the administrative reorganisation that followed British intervention in the 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty and the subsequent establishment of British advisers in the east coast states.
