# ‘Traitor of all traitors’― secret agent extraordinaire : Lai Teck, secretary-general, Communist Party of Malaya

Nathan Porath's 2011 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* applies René Girard's theory of mimetic desire and Stephen Harrison's concept of "fracturing resemblances" to the *Hikayat Patani*, an 1839 Malay manuscript that serves as the principal source for the early history of the Patani kingdom. Porath argues that the text is not an ethno-nationalist chronicle but a symbolically political-cultural charter that constructs Patani's identity through two contrasting mimetic relationships: an affirmation of similarity with Ayutthaya even as it narrates rebellions against that kingdom, and a systematic fracturing of resemblance with Johor at every narrative juncture.

## Summary

Porath's central intervention is to read the *Hikayat Patani* through the lens of pre-modern Southeast Asian state formation as a system of mimetic power relationships between kings and their city-centres. Drawing on Girard, he frames mimesis as a competitive dynamic in which a lesser royal emulates a superior model, eventually entering into rivalry and violence. The text, composed in the late seventeenth to mid-eighteenth century during Patani's decline under Kelantanese-derived rulers, was intended to supply a charter for the political-social institution of the sultanate — to show what conditions were needed for Patani to remain a viable *kerajaan* (p. 47).

The article's most significant reinterpretation concerns the first rebellion of Sultan Mudhaffar Syah against Ayutthaya (c. 1564). Porath argues this was not a secessionist act but a failed usurpation within the Indic frame of mimetic kingdom-hood, analogous to what Raja Thammaracha of Phitsanulok would later do successfully. A previously overlooked Malay phrase — "dari pada sebuah negeri baiklah dua" (rather than one country, two is better) — is read as an indirect reference to the Ayutthaya-Burmese war context, in which the Burmese king Bayinnaung declared that "Hong-sawadi and Ayutthaya will become closely joined royal allies, one great golden country" (pp. 56–58). The sultan's visit to Ayutthaya was thus an alliance to keep that kingdom independent from Pegu, and his subsequent attack was a betrayal of that alliance, not a declaration of Patani's separate national identity.

By contrast, the text's treatment of Johor is one of deliberate differentiation. Porath identifies the episode of Raja Lela's diplomatic mission to Johor as the text's culminating act of mimetic fracturing: the envoy's anxiety about Malay rules of conduct, his insistence on keeping his kris close, and the Johor servant's taunt — "is Patani really different from Johor? If this is Patani and that Johor, then it is Johor in the Patani way" — serve to deny total cultural similarity between the two kingdoms (p. 54). The two skin disease legends in the text (that of Encik Tani and that of the converting king) are interpreted through Jordaan and Josselin de Jong's framework as metaphors for social contracts between ruler and realm, with the conversion narrative encoding the kingdom's political and social contract with Islam as a condition of its unity (pp. 49–50).

### Key Findings

- The *Hikayat Patani* manuscript dates to 1839 and comprises six books composed between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries; it was first copied and translated by Teeuw and Wyatt (1970) (p. 46).
- Bradley (2009) convincingly revised the date of the Kelantan dynasty's installation at Patani to 1649–1651, not 1688 as previously assumed by Teeuw and Wyatt (p. 52).
- The first rebellion of Sultan Mudhaffar Syah against Ayutthaya is confirmed by the Luang Prasoert chronicle and by van Vliet's account; the sultan is described as having ridden one of King Cakkraphat's white elephants in the palace plaza (p. 56).
- King Prasat Thong mobilized an army of 60,000 men, headed by the Governor of Ligor, to attack Patani in the second rebellion (c. 1632–1636); Dutch armed ships were requested but arrived too late (p. 61).
- Van Vliet's 1644 dispatch to Batavia reported that war was imminent between Patani and Johor and that the Johor raja's mother and relatives in Patani were assumed massacred; a second dispatch in October 1644 reported a Patani envoy had arrived to settle differences (p. 54).
- The old Javanese poem *Desawarnana* (completed 1365) mentions Langkasuka and Sai as separate political entities but does not mention Patani, suggesting the kingdom may not have been founded during that period (p. 51).
- Porath rejects the standard date of 1457 for Patani's conversion to Islam (first proposed by Che Man) as unsupported, noting that all evidence suggests Patani was still a Buddhist kingdom in the thirteenth century and an established sultanate by the early sixteenth century (pp. 48–49).

### Conclusion

Porath's definitive takeaway is that the *Hikayat Patani* reveals Patani's political identity as fundamentally constituted by its mimetic relationship with Ayutthaya — a shared Indic cosmos inherited from the kingdom's Buddhist past that was not shared with Johor and could not be shaken off. The text's failure to moralize against Ayutthaya, even after sixty years of warfare, indicates that the scribes understood Patani's viability as a *kerajaan* to depend on the maintenance of hierarchical order with that northern kingdom. Reading the text through a modern ethnicist lens would fundamentally misread its purpose: the sultans are depicted as heroic precisely because of, not despite, their mimetic entanglement with Ayutthaya.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Teeuw and Wyatt (1970) translation of the *Hikayat Patani*, the Thai *Baker et al.* (2005) edition of van Vliet's *Siam*, the *Luang Prasoert* chronicle (via Cushman 2006), and Bradley's (2009) historical contextualization.
- Historiographically, the article extends Bougas's (1994) framework of Patani as situated between two mandala systems by arguing that Patani never perceived itself as subordinate to Johor, and it challenges the tendency in Patani historiography to read early rebellions through a modern secessionist lens.
