# The post-war decade in Malaya

C.M. Turnbull, a former Malayan Civil Service officer and professional historian, delivered this Annual Lecture to the MBRAS in October 1986, drawing on newly released official archives and the private papers of Malcolm MacDonald to reassess the post-war decade in Malaya. The article argues that the transition from colonial rule to independence was shaped less by grand strategic design than by the personal diplomacy of MacDonald, whose ability to build trust with Malay and Chinese leaders proved decisive in averting the kind of communal violence that had destroyed Palestine.

## Summary

Turnbull structures his argument around three interlocking themes: the origins of Malayan nationalism, the problem of racial co-operation, and the relationship between the Federation of Malaya, Singapore, and the Borneo territories. On nationalism, he traces how the Malayan Union scheme was conceived in the Colonial Office Eastern Department during the war, largely by Edward Gent, with minimal consultation of local stakeholders and in ignorance of wartime conditions. The scheme's rapid introduction after the Japanese surrender, combined with its liberal citizenship provisions, provoked a unified Malay response that the British government had not anticipated. Turnbull emphasises that the Malay protest was reasoned and non-violent, and that it was MacDonald's personal intervention—particularly his informal garden party in Penang that broke the deadlock with Dato Onn—that saved the situation from a constitutional crisis with potentially grave consequences.

On racial co-operation, Turnbull recounts how the negotiations for the Federation of Malaya, conducted exclusively between the Rulers and UMNO, had bruised non-Malay susceptibilities to a degree the authorities did not appreciate. MacDonald's response was to forge a Communities Liaison Committee, an unofficial body of "men of goodwill" from all racial communities, which met in private to work through economic and political grievances. The committee's deliberations, though slow and often clouded by suspicion, produced a framework of mutual accommodation that paved the way for the Alliance government and eventual independence.

The third section examines the long-standing British intention to create a broader Southeast Asian dominion uniting the peninsula, Singapore, and the Borneo territories. Turnbull shows that, contrary to earlier assumptions of official inactivity, there was considerable behind-the-scenes work from 1949 onwards, including a Joint Co-ordination Committee between Singapore and the Federation and informal talks on a Borneo federation. But the political earthquakes of 1955—the radicalisation of Singaporean politics and the Alliance's landslide in the federal elections—rendered MacDonald's patient, personal approach obsolete and transferred initiative to local political leaders.

### Key Findings

- The Malayan Union scheme was the "brain child, almost exclusively" of Edward Gent and senior Eastern Department officials, with successive wartime and post-war cabinets merely rubber-stamping it; the War Cabinet stipulated in May 1944 that proposals should not be made public without its permission (pp. 10–11).
- Chinese immigration into Singapore reached an all-time record of 360,000 in the boom year of 1927, and by the 1931 census non-Malays outnumbered the indigenous population (p. 9).
- MacMichael's mission to secure the Rulers' revocation of their treaties was completed within three months of his arrival in Malaya in October 1945 (p. 12).
- The Communities Liaison Committee comprised six Malays, six Chinese, and one each from the Indian, Ceylonese, Eurasian, and European groups; it was chaired by Dato' E.E.C. Thuraisingham with MacDonald attending as observer (p. 18).
- The 1955 federal elections produced an Alliance victory in every constituency but one, with the Party Negara completely wiped out (p. 22).
- Ralph Hone's memorandum of March 1949 on merging Singapore into the Federation concluded that "the difficulties to be surmounted in achieving it will be considerable"—a classic understatement (p. 19).

### Conclusion

Turnbull's definitive takeaway is that the remarkable achievement of the post-war decade was not the absence of fundamental problems but the fact that Malaya coped so well with them. He attributes this to a long-standing sense of goodwill, visible in the dignified conduct of the Rulers, the reconciliation of Dato Onn and Tan Cheng Lock, and the labours of the Communities Liaison Committee. His closing comparison with Palestine—echoing Clementi's 1945 warning—underscores that it was possible to arouse nationalism and create an independent multi-racial nation without replicating the communal catastrophe that had destroyed the British mandate in the Middle East.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the private papers of Malcolm MacDonald (held in the National Archives of Malaysia and elsewhere) and Colonial Office records at Kew, which at the time of writing were open only to 1953 under the 30-year rule (p. 21).
- Turnbull's unique position as both a contemporary participant in the Malayan Civil Service and a later professional historian gives the piece a distinctive dual perspective, combining personal recollection with archival evidence.
