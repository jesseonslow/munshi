# Malacca in the eighteenth century

D.F.A. Hervey published this extract in 1883, a translation from Dutch archival records held in the Government Offices at Malacca, documenting the military engagements of the Dutch garrison against Malay raiding parties in November 1756. The text presents the colonial administration's own account of its defensive operations in the Malacca hinterland, framed as a straightforward military diary rather than an analytical argument.

## Summary

The article is a verbatim translation of entries from the *Diary of Malacca* for 1756, covering three days of military activity (1, 2, and 27 November) in which the Dutch fortress garrison confronted Malay forces allied with Raja Said of Selangor, a relation of Daing Kamboja. The narrative follows a pattern of enemy incursions into the Malacca hinterland—targeting the Attorney-General's country seat at Gevesteyn and establishing fortified positions (*bentang*) at Feringgi—followed by Dutch counter-attacks that drove the raiders back into the jungle. The diary's tone is that of a garrison log: it records troop dispositions, casualty figures, tactical decisions, and the movements of both sides with the matter-of-fact precision of a military journal.

The underlying historical problem the text illuminates is the vulnerability of the Dutch colonial settlement at Malacca to sustained guerrilla pressure from the Malay hinterland during the mid-eighteenth century. The enemy's strategy—raiding outposts, building fortified camps, and planning a coordinated riverine assault on the town during the dry monsoon—reveals a campaign of considerable sophistication that the garrison could only repel through repeated sorties into difficult jungle terrain. The diary makes clear that the Dutch held the town but could not secure the surrounding countryside, a condition that generated constant alarm among the civilian population.

Hervey's contribution is primarily editorial: he revised the Government translator's rendering and added brief explanatory notes on place-names (e.g., Feringgi, Gevesteyn, Panklarame) and local terminology (*bentang*, *Meangkabau guns*), drawing on the assistance of J.E. Westerhout. The text thus functions as an early example of the archival recovery work that the Royal Asiatic Society's journal facilitated for the Straits Settlements.

### Key Findings

- The enemy force at Gevesteyn numbered over 300 on 2 November; at Feringgi on 27 November it exceeded 500 men (pp. 263, 265).
- The 15 Malay defenders of Gevesteyn surrendered after the attackers set fire to the building; two escaped and brought word to the fortress (p. 262).
- In the 27 November assault on the Feringgi *bentang*, the enemy sustained 40 killed and "certainly more than double that number wounded"; the Dutch lost 6 killed (4 soldiers, 1 volunteer, 1 Chinaman) and 5 wounded, none seriously (p. 267).
- The enemy's stated plan, as reported to the Governor, was to wait for the dry monsoon, then invade Bunga Raya and Banda Ilir while simultaneously attacking the town by river on rafts, with the intention of burning all property and massacring the inhabitants (pp. 265–266).
- The Dutch garrison at Malacca comprised European soldiers, a Bugis contingent of 50, and native and Chinese auxiliaries; the 27 November sortie totalled approximately 300 men under Ensign Kruythoff and Sergeant Meyer (p. 266).
- The enemy employed caltrops to obstruct Dutch approach routes and used long-range *Meangkabau* guns from jungle positions, indicating a degree of tactical adaptation to the terrain (pp. 264, 266).

### Conclusion

Hervey's publication preserves a rare first-hand colonial military account of Malacca's frontier warfare in the 1750s, confirming that the Dutch settlement, though nominally secure within its walls, faced persistent and well-organised Malay resistance that could only be checked through costly and repeated sorties into the jungle. The diary's unflinching record of enemy tactical ingenuity—fortified camps, caltrops, long-range firearms, and planned coordinated assaults—undermines any simplistic narrative of effortless colonial dominance in the region.

## Context

- D.F.A. Hervey held a colonial administrative position in Malacca with direct access to the Dutch-language Government Offices; the translation was produced by the official Government translator, and Hervey's revisions and notes were informed by J.E. Westerhout, indicating an institutional rather than independent scholarly enterprise (p. 261).
- The text is presented entirely from the Dutch garrison's perspective, with the Malay enemy characterised through the lens of a military diary ("heroic valour," "infernal project," "dancing and shouting most horribly"), reflecting the contemporary colonial framing of indigenous resistance as a security problem to be suppressed rather than a political movement to be understood (pp. 261, 264–265).
