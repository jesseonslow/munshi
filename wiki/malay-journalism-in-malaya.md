# Malay journalism in Malaya

Zainal Abidin bin Ahmad's 1941 survey traces the sixty-year development of Malay-language journalism in British Malaya, from the lithographed *Jawi Peranakan* of 1877 to the five daily newspapers operating at the outbreak of the Second World War. His overarching thesis is that Malay journalism has progressed from a fragile pioneering effort into a vital institution for modernising Malay society, expanding the language, and shaping public opinion.

## Summary

The article opens with the author's examination of a surviving copy of *Jawi Peranakan* No. 38 (15 October 1877), which he obtained through an editor friend. This primary source allows him to describe the paper's physical format—eight foolscap pages, lithographed, published weekly—its Arabic-influenced terminology (e.g., *'adad* for "number," *qimat ul-akhbar* for "subscription rates"), and its poetic masthead verses. He then traces the succession of short-lived Malay papers from the 1880s through the 1910s, noting their gradual shift from lithography to print Jawi script and their increasing engagement with foreign news, linguistic debate, and religious controversy, particularly the *riba* (usury) and Ahmadiyyah disputes that dominated correspondence columns during and after the First World War (pp. 244–245).

The middle section surveys the contemporary landscape as of 1941—five dailies, several weeklies, monthlies, and quarterlies—highlighting the economic maturation of the industry through the emergence of paid contributors and accredited correspondents. Ahmad devotes considerable attention to the linguistic challenges facing Malay journalists, particularly the difficulty of rendering English technical and political vocabulary into Jawi script, and contrasts the Malayan situation with the more developed Romanised Malay press of the Netherlands East Indies, whose influence on Malayan style he acknowledges (pp. 246–249).

The article closes with an assessment of Malay journalism's social influence, from urban bookshops to rural *kampong* gatherings where a literate reader would read aloud to a listening crowd. Ahmad argues that the press is simultaneously modernising Malay society and revitalising the Malay language itself, and expresses confident optimism about its future given advances in Malay education (pp. 249–250).

### Key Findings

- The first Malay newspaper in Malaya, *Jawi Peranakan*, was published in Singapore from approximately 1877 and survived for 17 years; it was an eight-page lithographed weekly in foolscap format (p. 244).
- By 1941, five daily Malay newspapers were in circulation: *Warta Malaya* (Singapore, est. c. 1931), *Utusan Melayu* (Singapore, est. c. 1941), *Lembaga* (Johore, est. c. 1936), *Majlis* (Kuala Lumpur, est. c. 1932), and *Warta Kinta* (Ipoh, est. c. 1941) (p. 246).
- The *Saudara* of Penang (September 1928) was the oldest Malay newspaper still in publication at the time of writing; its founder and editor Syed Sheikh al-Hady died in 1934 (pp. 246–247).
- *Warta Malaya* was the chief paper paying contributors and correspondents on a "fairly high scale," marking a decisive shift from the earlier practice of compensating writers only with free copies of each issue (p. 248).
- All Malay newspapers in Malaya were published in Jawi script; attempts to publish in Romanised Malay had "proved abortive," whereas the entire Netherlands East Indies Malay press operated in Romanised spelling (p. 248).
- The *Lembaga Malaya* weekly had been publishing continuous instalments of the *Arabian Nights* translated from Burton's English version since its inception, completing several books of the work by 1941 (p. 247).

### Conclusion

Ahmad's definitive takeaway is that Malay journalism has irrevocably established itself as a force for social modernisation and linguistic renewal among the Malays of Malaya. He dismisses any fear that the Malay language is becoming obsolete, arguing instead that the press is actively expanding its vocabulary and injecting new life into it, and he predicts a "brighter future" for the industry given the steady advance of Malay education throughout the country (p. 250).

## Context

- The article draws on the author's direct possession of a rare surviving copy of *Jawi Peranakan* (No. 38, 15 October 1877) and his personal familiarity with the contemporary press landscape; no formal archival collections are cited.
- Written on the eve of the Japanese occupation, the piece represents a snapshot of Malay intellectual self-assessment at a moment of crisis; its optimistic tone is all the more notable given the wartime context in which it was composed.
