# History of Kelantan, Part I

Anker Rentse, a Dutch colonial administrator serving in the Kelantan advisory office, published this foundational reconstruction of Kelantan's early history in 1934, drawing on Malay folklore, Chinese annals, Ptolemaic geography, old European maps, and royal genealogical manuscripts to argue that Kelantan was an ancient trading state that fragmented under Siamese pressure in the seventeenth century and was reconstituted in the eighteenth century through the Long Yunus dynasty. The article establishes the deep Patani–Kelantan connection as the key to understanding the state's political development and challenges earlier, less reliable accounts by Graham and Newbold.

## Summary

Rentse's central task is to piece together a coherent narrative from a state that, in his words, kept no reliable written records. He begins with geography, establishing Kelantan's relative isolation behind mountain ranges and its orientation toward the China Sea, which made it a node on ancient maritime trade routes linking India, Arabia, and China via the Gulf of Siam. He then surveys the etymological debates around the name "Kelantan" (rejecting Gerini's Sanskrit derivations and the folk etymology of *Kilat-Kilatan* in favour of a Sakai or Malay honorific prefix), before moving through the documented suzerainty claims of Palembang (1225), Majapahit (1365), and Malacca (late fifteenth century). The bulk of the article is devoted to the dynastic succession from the Patani sovereigns through the Jimbar and Kubang Labu families to the Long Yunus line, using the *salsilah* compiled by the Dato' Perdana Mantri and cross-referencing it with Marriott's Trengganu manuscript, Newbold's lists, and the oral tradition of Tengku Khalid bin Tengku Bendahara.

The second half of the article focuses on Sultan Muhammad I's reign (1800–1835), the massacre of Chinese gold miners on the Sokor River by Tengku Senik, and the escalating Siamese interference that culminated in the 1832 deportation of over four thousand Patani captives to Bangkok. Rentse uses the 1826 Anglo-Siamese treaty to frame Kelantan's vulnerability and the British government's nominal interest in protecting East Coast trade. Throughout, he treats folklore not as mere legend but as a repository of historical memory, testing tales against archaeological traces (ancient mining on the Sokor, fortifications at Pasir Mas, the *mas dinar* gold coins) and against the physical landscape.

### Key Findings

- In 1225 A.D. the Malay-Buddhist kingdom of Palembang claimed suzerainty over Kelantan, alongside Kedah, Trengganu, and Pahang, as recorded by the Chinese traveller Chao Ju Kua (p. 45).
- The *Nagarakretagama* (1365 A.D.) records Kelantan as subject to Majapahit; an influence Rentse traces in surviving armlets, dialect features, and the *Panji wayang* tradition (p. 45).
- Sultan Muhammad I's seal, dated 1215 A.H. (1800 A.D.), bears the inscription "Khalilu'llah Raja Kelantan / al-Sultan Yunus al-Marhum," confirming his installation year (p. 56).
- The Siamese Praklang removed upwards of four thousand captives from Patani to Bangkok in September 1832, in direct violation of Article 12 of the Anglo-Siamese treaty of 20 June 1826, which guaranteed free commerce in Kelantan and Trengganu (pp. 58–59).
- A *Gantang* (gong) dated 1222 A.H. (1807 A.D.) was found near the mouth of the Sokor River, contemporaneous with Sultan Muhammad I's reign and the Chinese mining operations there (p. 58).
- Royal cemetery inscriptions at Langgar record the deaths of Sultan Mulut Merah (1304 A.H./1886), Sultan Ahmad (1307/1889), Sultan Muhammad bin Sultan Ahmad (1308/1890), and Sultan Mansur bin Sultan Ahmad (1317/1899) (p. 61).

### Conclusion

Rentse's definitive takeaway is that Kelantan's political identity was forged in the interstices between three powers—Siam (via Patani), Trengganu, and the residual Malaccan-Johore dynastic claims—and that the present royal family's legitimacy rests on the Long Yunus installation of approximately 1775–1793, a compromise arrangement brokered by Sultan Mansur Shah of Trengganu. The state's repeated fragmentation and reconstitution, rather than any single founding moment, is the defining feature of its early history.

## Context

- Rentse was a Dutch national serving in the British advisory administration in Kelantan; his fieldwork (visits to Pasir Mas, the Sokor River, and the royal cemetery at Langgar) was conducted in his official capacity, and the genealogical tree was approved by the Sultan and the *Kerabat Negri* in October 1932 (p. 59).
- The article draws primarily on Newbold's 1839 *Political and Statistical Account*, Marriott's 1916 Trengganu fragment, Winstedt's *Malaya* (1923), Graham's *Kelantan* (1908), and Gerini's *Researches on Ptolemy's Geography* (1909), supplemented by Rentse's own field observations and the oral testimony of Tengku Khalid. Its historiographical contribution lies in synthesising these disparate sources into a single chronological framework and in treating Kelantan folklore as a legitimate historical source.
