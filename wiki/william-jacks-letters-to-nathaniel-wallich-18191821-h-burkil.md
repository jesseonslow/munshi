# William Jack’s letters to Nathaniel Wallich, 1819–1821. .H. Burkill

William Jack's letters to Nathaniel Wallich, 1819–1821, edited by A. T. Gage and I. H. Burkill and published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 73, 1916), present the first complete printed edition of correspondence between the young Scottish botanist and the Superintendent of the Calcutta Botanic Gardens, covering Jack's three years of fieldwork at Penang, Singapore, and Bencoolen under Sir Stamford Raffles. The letters, copied from the Records of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Calcutta under Gage's supervision, reveal the day-to-day scientific and political life of the Raffles expedition and constitute a primary source for the natural history of western Sumatra and the Straits of Malacca in the earliest period of British settlement.

## Summary

The publication opens with a biographical introduction by Burkill tracing Jack's education in Aberdeen, his medical training in London, and his arrival in Calcutta in 1813, where he served as a regimental surgeon during the Nepalese war before contracting the lung trouble that would ultimately kill him. It then situates the correspondence within the political context of Raffles' appointment as Lieutenant-Governor of Bencoolen in 1817, the death of Joseph Arnold, and Raffles' assembly of a naturalist staff at Calcutta that included Jack, the French zoologists Diard and Duvaucel, and a missionary printer. Burkill notes that Jack's letters were written with the frankness of a close friend to Wallich, and that certain passages criticizing Diard and Duvaucel have been omitted from the printed text.

The letters themselves are organised into three geographic series—Penang (January–May 1819), Singapore (June 1819), and Bencoolen (August 1819–October 1821)—and they interweave botanical observation with political commentary. Jack's scientific correspondence is dense with new species descriptions, taxonomic queries, and requests for Roxburgh's manuscript characters, while his political observations provide a contemporary account of the friction between Raffles and the Penang Government under Colonel Bannerman, the founding of Singapore, the Acheen succession dispute, and the eventual cession of Pulau Nias. The letters also document the breakdown of the relationship between Raffles and the French naturalists, whose collections were ultimately seized by a committee after they attempted to remove their papers from Bencoolen. Burkill's closing notes record Jack's death in September 1822 at the age of twenty-seven, and the destruction of Raffles' papers and drawings in the fire of the ship *Fame* in February 1824.

The volume concludes with an extensive list of plants known to have been collected by Jack, arranged by family, with references to the specific letters and to the publications in which Jack's descriptions subsequently appeared. This list, compiled by Burkill, serves as a checklist linking the correspondence to the formal taxonomic literature, including the *Malayan Miscellanies*, the *Transactions of the Linnean Society*, and Carey and Wallich's revision of Roxburgh's *Flora Indica*.

### Key Findings

- Jack arrived at Penang on 31 December 1818 aboard the *Nearchus* and produced 130 species descriptions and approximately 40 drawings during his stay there before departing for Bencoolen in May 1819 (pp. 152, 168).
- The letters document the discovery and description of *Rafflesia Arnoldi* (then called *Rafflesia Titan* by Jack) at Bencoolen, where Jack found it "in numbers from all parts of the country" and determined it to be parasitic on a species of *Cissus* and dioecious (pp. 203–204, 208).
- Jack's proposed new genus *Euthemis* (from *euthemon*, "concinus") was described from two Singapore species—*E. leucocarpa* and *E. minor*—and sent to Wallich with Indian ink drawings for the planned illustrated fascicle (p. 205).
- The political correspondence reveals that the Penang Government under Bannerman refused to supply troops for the Singapore settlement "in defiance of their own written promises, and the orders of the Governor General" (p. 169), and that the Acheen investigation produced "upwards of a thousand pages" of Company records (p. 151).
- Jack's final letter, dated 26 October 1821, was in fact the last he ever wrote; he died on 15 September 1822 at Bencoolen, and Raffles recorded that "a finer head or heart there never was" (pp. 237–238).
- The fire aboard the *Fame* on 4 February 1824 destroyed Raffles' papers, drawings, and "a grand map of Sumatra, on which I had been employed since my arrival here," along with Jack's remaining collections (pp. 238–239).

### Conclusion

Burkill's definitive assessment is that Jack possessed "a really excellent understanding of the natural system of classification, a kind of intuition" that, had he lived longer, would have produced work of the first rank (p. 151). The letters confirm that Jack was not merely a collector but a taxonomist of genuine originality who erected new genera (*Euthemis*, *Loxonia*, *Epithinia*, *Neuropeltis*), proposed a new natural order (Cyrtandraceae), and made significant contributions to the botany of Melastomataceae, Nepenthaceae, and the sago palms. His premature death at twenty-seven, compounded by the loss of his collections in the *Fame* fire, represents one of the most significant losses in the history of Malayan botany.

## Context

- The letters were copied from the Records of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Calcutta, under the superintendence of Major A. T. Gage, at the suggestion of H. N. Ridley, and published at the expense of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society (p. 147).
- The publication's historiographical value lies in providing the only contemporary, day-to-day account of the Raffles expedition's scientific and political activities from the perspective of its principal botanist, supplementing and in places contradicting the later *Memoir of Sir Stamford Raffles* (1830) and Lady Raffles' account.
