# Consul Farren and the Philippines

Nicholas Tarling's 1965 article examines the career of J.W. Farren, Britain's first Consul in the Philippines (appointed 1844), and his role in shaping British diplomatic and commercial policy in the archipelago during a period of intense multi-power competition over the Sulu Sultanate. The article argues that Farren's dispatches, drawn from the Foreign Office archives, reveal both the strategic significance Britain attached to the region as a maritime corridor between Australia and China, and the ultimate failure of British policy to secure a durable foothold in Sulu.

## Summary

The article traces Farren's dual mandate: promoting British trade in Luzon and the Visayas (particularly sugar and rice), and reporting on the complex diplomatic contest over the Sulu Sultanate, where Spain, France, the Netherlands, and Britain all held overlapping claims. Tarling uses Farren's private and official correspondence to illuminate how the consul perceived the strategic geometry of the region—its position as a gateway between the Pacific and China Seas, its vulnerability to foreign encroachment, and the ambiguous legal status of Sulu under the 1837 treaty with Spain. Farren's dispatches reveal a diplomat who grasped the stakes but was increasingly marginalised by the Foreign Office, which preferred to work through Sir James Brooke and the Colonial Office rather than through the Manila consulate.

The central historical problem Tarling explores is the "ambiguous area" of Mindanao, the Sulu archipelago, and north Borneo, where Spain had never effectively established control despite two and a half centuries of nominal sovereignty, and where the loosely structured archipelagic empires of the earlier period collided with nineteenth-century imperialism and evolving notions of sovereignty. The interference of France (the Basilan affair of 1844–45) and the Netherlands (their expedition against Sulu in 1848) is shown to have spurred Spanish military action, culminating in Urbiztondo's 1851 expedition that destroyed Sulu's fortifications. These events created a diplomatic tangle—Brooke's 1849 treaty with Sulu, the Spanish confirmation of sovereignty in 1851, and the Foreign Office's eventual abandonment of the Brooke treaty in 1852—that set the stage for the later Sulu-Sabah boundary dispute.

Tarling also draws attention to Farren's broader strategic reflections on the Philippines, in which the consul warned that Spanish authority rested more on moral prestige than physical power, and that the introduction of a rival European presence could destabilise the entire archipelago. These observations, Tarling suggests, anticipated the arguments that would recur in later decades over the future of Sulu and Sabah.

### Key Findings

- Farren was appointed Consul in 1844 specifically to certify the origin of sugar imports under the new British tariff regime that reduced duties on non-slave-grown sugar (p. 258).
- The French arrangement with the Sultan of Sulu over Basilan in 1845 was structured as a 100-year "governance" lease for 100,000 dollars rather than a full cession, and was ultimately abandoned by Guizot's government to preserve the Spanish marriage alliance (pp. 263–264, 266).
- Up to 1,500 people were carried off annually from the coasts of Luzon and the Visayas by Moro pirates, with Sulu serving as the centre of correspondence and combination for these expeditions (p. 267).
- Urbiztondo's 1851 expedition destroyed the fortifications and town of Sulu but left no garrison; a deputation from the Sultan and Datus subsequently offered submission to Spanish sovereignty in April 1851 (pp. 270–271).
- The Foreign Office dropped its attempt to uphold Brooke's 1849 treaty with Sulu in the summer of 1852 following confidential communications from Madrid that cited France's abandonment of its Basilan challenge as precedent (p. 272).
- Farren died in August 1864 in the suburb of Santa Ana, his papers in confusion and debts unpaid, having been effectively sidelined by the Foreign Office after Aberdeen's departure from power (p. 273).

### Conclusion

Tarling's definitive takeaway is that the inactivity of the British Foreign Office in the 1850s—its failure to uphold the Brooke treaty or to challenge the Spanish confirmation of sovereignty over Sulu—contributed to a de facto division of allegiance that was only formally recognised in the 1885 agreement. The earlier diplomatic proceedings, particularly the Foreign Office's hints about the Dalrymple treaties and its protests against Spanish encroachment on Sabah, helped shape the eventual compromise, even though Britain's negotiating position was weakened by its earlier failures to sustain the 1849 treaty or to prevent the 1851 Spanish confirmation.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: Foreign Office records (F.O.72 series, F.O.71/1, F.O.12/7, F.O.12/10), Colonial Office records (C.O.144/8), and India Office Records (Board's Collections 102773), all at the Public Record Office.
- Historiographical contribution: The article sits within Tarling's broader body of work on piracy and British policy in the Malay world (his 1963 monograph *Piracy and Politics in the Malay World* is cited throughout) and uses Farren's dispatches as a primary source to illuminate the diplomatic contest over Sulu that preceded the later boundary disputes.
