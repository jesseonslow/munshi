# The Perak genies

R.O. Winstedt's 1929 article "The Perak Genies" documents the guardian jinn (*jin kerajaan*) of the Perak sultanate, drawing on oral information provided by Raja Haji Yahya bin Raja Muhammad Ali of Chendriang, a Perak aristocrat descended from a former sultan whose family had included a state *pawang* (Sultan Muda). The piece presents a catalogue of state and common genies, their invocations, and the elaborate ceremony by which these spirits were summoned to feast upon the royal regalia.

## Summary

Winstedt's article is essentially a record of ethnographic and ritual knowledge transmitted by a member of the Perak royal circle. The core of the text is a list of the great jinns invoked by the Sultan Muda and his assistant, the Raja Kechil Muda, each identified by elaborate Malay honorifics that blend Hindu, Islamic, and Persian-romance imagery. Among the state genies are figures recognisable as Brahma and Vishnu, the Prophet Solomon, 'Ali son-in-law of Muhammad, and 'Ali the mighty warrior, alongside names drawn from Malay romance literature and even the Persian *Amir Hamzah* cycle (p. 463). The article also records a separate list of common folk genies—nineteen named plus "199 others"—which include the tiger-spirit *Alak Muda*, where *alak* is identified as the Sakai word for shaman (p. 463).

The second half of the article describes the ceremony of inviting the spirits to the feast given to the regalia, as narrated by Raja Haji. The ritual involves the erection of a nine-storeyed hall (*balai*) by men from Pasir Garam, crowned with an image of the *jentayu* (a mythical bird associated with Vishnu), and the construction of an altar (*kengkolong*) and a receptacle (*sangka*) for offerings to the dead. The Sultan Muda and lesser wizards enter trance states (*menyelap*, *berhantu*) to the sound of drums and invocations, during which the spirits speak through the possessed wizards. The climax involves the two state wizards performing obeisance to the regalia and pouring drink onto the royal drums and trumpets, after which the Sultan is taken to bathe in the hall, thereby bathing the genies of the state (pp. 464–466).

Winstedt situates the office of Sultan Muda historically by noting that the twenty-fifth holder of the title was a grandson of Marhum Kahar, who reigned at the end of the eighteenth century, suggesting the office may date back to the foundation of the Perak dynasty itself (p. 463).

### Key Findings

- The four principal Perak genies, identified by Sir Frank Swettenham as guardians of Sultan and state, are Nos. 6 (*Sri Sultan Dunia Udara*), 5 (*Sultan 'Alam Maya Udara*), 14 (*Mahkota Si Raja Jin*), and 4 (*Sultan 'Ali Degar Perkasa*) from the state list (p. 463).
- The common folk genies number 199 in addition to the 19 named in the article, indicating a far larger corpus of local spirit names (p. 463).
- The ceremonial hall is a nine-storeyed structure erected by men from Pasir Garam, crowned with an image of the *jentayu* bird, with *ketupat*, *lepat*, *dodol*, and *wajik* hung at each storey and a pink buffalo's head on the topmost level (p. 465).
- The Sultan paid $100 for the feasting of the regalia and ceremonial bathing; the Sultan Muda and Raja Kechil Muda each retained $25, with the remainder distributed to lesser wizards (p. 466).
- The tiger-spirit *Alak Muda* is identified through the Sakai word *alak* for shaman, linking the Perak royal ritual to indigenous Sakai shamanic traditions (p. 463).
- The state genie list includes a magic horse from the romance of *Shamsu'l-bahrin* and the figure 'Umar Ummaiya, described as "the Ulysses of the Persian romance of Amir Hamzah," demonstrating the syncretic literary sources of Perak spirit nomenclature (p. 463).

### Conclusion

Winstedt presents the Perak genies as a "strange crew" whose names and attributes reveal a layered syncretism of Hindu, Islamic, Persian-romance, and indigenous Sakai elements, all woven into a state ritual that served to ritually nourish and animate the royal regalia. The article's definitive takeaway is that the Perak guardian-jinn system was not a marginal folk practice but a structured state institution, with a hereditary office (Sultan Muda) potentially dating to the dynasty's foundation, and a codified liturgy of invocation that bound the spiritual authority of the sultanate to its material symbols of power.

## Context

- Winstedt was a British colonial administrator and scholar serving in Perak at the time of writing; the article was composed from oral information supplied by a Perak aristocrat (Raja Haji Yahya) rather than from direct observation of the ceremony.
- The piece draws on earlier published accounts, notably Swettenham's *Malay Sketches* (pp. 135–9) and van Ronkel's *Catalogus*, and cross-references Winstedt's own earlier work *Shaman, Saiva and Sufi* (p. 39), situating it within a small body of early twentieth-century colonial-era documentation of Malay ritual life.
