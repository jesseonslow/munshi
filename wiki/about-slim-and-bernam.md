# About Slim and Bernam

H.W.C. Leech published this account in 1879, reporting on an expedition through the Slim and Bernam river valleys in southern Perak undertaken in the company of a coffee planter, Mr. Smith. The overarching thesis is that this little-known section of the Perak interior possesses exceptional soil, climate, and transport advantages for tropical plantation agriculture, alongside unexploited mineral deposits of tin, coal, and gold that await only "intelligence and capital" to be worked (p. 45).

## Summary

Leech's narrative follows a multi-week overland journey from the Slim river upstream through its tributaries, across the watershed to the Songkei and Bidor valleys, and down to the Bernam. The expedition was motivated by two commercial interests: Mr. Smith's assessment of soil suitability for coffee (he compared it favourably to Ouva in Ceylon, p. 39), and Leech's own survey of mineral potential and transport infrastructure. The account is shaped by recurring logistical difficulties—Sakei porters who refused to cross into unfamiliar valleys, three-day delays waiting for replacement guides, the loss of rice supplies to rain, and the near-total absence of visible water on porous upland terrain (pp. 43–44). These practical obstacles frame the scientific observations and underscore the genuine difficulty of penetrating the peninsula's interior.

The article interweaves ethnographic observation with commercial assessment. Leech records the superior wet-padi cultivation of Mandeling foreign Malays on the Slim (800–1,000 gantangs per orlong without manure, p. 36), the physical characteristics and labour economics of Sakei porters, and the tin-mining history disrupted by the 1874 disturbances following the murder of J.W.W. Birch. The drainage hydrology receives sustained attention: Leech identifies the Kinta, Kampar, Batang Padang, and Songkei as the principal arteries of the Lower Perak drainage system, with the Slim and Bernam belonging to a separate southern system (p. 45). His conclusion is that the region's navigable rivers, mild upland climate, and mineral wealth make it a prime candidate for colonial economic development.

### Key Findings

- The Slim river is navigable for boats over one *koyan* up to Kwala Galetin; the Bernam is tidal up to Chankat Menti, reachable by country boats in three tides, with a further three to four days' poling to Kampong Bernam or Kwala Galetin (p. 37).
- Freight from Kampong Bernam to the sea stood at $1 per *bhara* for tin, which Leech judged "not excessive" (p. 37).
- The Batu Gaja pass, the boundary crossing between the Slim and Pahang, sits at 2,500 feet by aneroid, with the true summit approximately 50 feet higher; flanking hills (Gunong Petri to the north) rise a further 1,000 feet (p. 40).
- The Slim–Songkei watershed was crossed at approximately 4,000 feet; the return party of 32 (including female Sakeis) carried loads at an established rate of 30 cents per 50 catties, paid in kind (cloth, tobacco) rather than coin (pp. 41, 43).
- Kampong Bernam contained approximately 80 families, nearly all foreign Malays who had settled originally as tin traders; tin mining on the Perak side of the Bernam had ceased after Raja Asal led miners to Papan on the Kinta following the 1874 blockade (p. 37).
- On Gunong Sandor, streams ran entirely underground through porous soil, leaving only dry channels and collapse holes six feet in diameter; Sakeis extracted water by driving split bamboo horizontally into the hillside until it reached the water-bearing stratum (p. 44).

### Conclusion

Leech's definitive historical takeaway is that the Slim–Bernam interior represents an underdeveloped economic frontier whose natural endowments—navigable waterways, fertile upland soils, salubrious climate, and mineral deposits—demand only the application of European capital and agricultural expertise to yield returns comparable to established plantation colonies such as Ceylon (p. 45).

## Context

- Leech writes from the perspective of a colonial surveyor or administrator conducting a reconnaissance for commercial development; his framing consistently evaluates indigenous landscapes and peoples in terms of their utility to plantation agriculture and mining capital.
- The article draws on no named archival collections; it is a first-hand field report. Its historiographical value lies in providing a contemporaneous baseline of transport infrastructure, labour relations, and settlement patterns in the Slim–Bernam region at the moment of British consolidation in Perak.
