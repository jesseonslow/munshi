# The origin of the Malay surau

This short note by R. A. Kern argues that the Malay *surau* (village chapel) is a linguistic and architectural continuation of a pre-Islamic Batak pagan temple called *suro* or *parsuroan*.

## Summary

Kern challenges the long-standing assumption, based on Junghuhn's 1847 account, that the Batak had no temples. He marshals evidence from Hagen's 1883 observation of a small elevated spirit-house at Nagasaribu and, more substantially, from H. H. Bartlett's fieldwork in the Pardembanan district, which documented a *pasuroan* — a small structure on very high posts, entered only by the *datu* for offerings to *debata* and *padju-padjuan* (p. 179–180). Kern then traces the word *suro* across the region: in Minangkabau it became *surau* (a small mosque not used for Friday prayers); in Mandailing it names the village mosque; in Palembang the old spirit-house (*rumah dewa*) was replaced by the Muslim *surau*; and in Banten the Old-Sundanese *balaj* (mountain-top ancestor sanctuary) became the modern *bale* (village mosque) (p. 180–181). He extends the etymology to Fijian *soro* and Toraja *suru'*, both relating to supplication and offering, and concludes that the Malayan *surau* likely had a pre-Islamic predecessor connected with ancestor-worship (p. 181).
