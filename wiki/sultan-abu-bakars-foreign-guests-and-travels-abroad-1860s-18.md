# Sultan Abu Bakar’s foreign guests and travels abroad, 1860s-1895: fact and fiction in early Malay historical accounts

Abu Talib Ahmad's 2011 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines how the Tun Abdul Razak Memorial Museum in Kuala Lumpur constructs a curated national memory of Malaysia's second Prime Minister, arguing that its systematic and selective representation of Tun Abdul Razak as "father of development" is functionally indistinguishable from the politicization of museums during the colonial period. Drawing on Benedict Anderson's framework of imagined communities, the author contrasts the memorial's linear narrative with the more complex picture emerging from biographical studies, archival materials, and critical scholarship.

## Summary

The article undertakes a detailed walkthrough of the Tun Abdul Razak Memorial, located at the former Sri Taman official residence on Jalan Perdana, which opened on 6 May 1982. The author documents the memorial's two exhibition halls, its library holdings, and its 1,650 exhibits (up from 700 at opening), noting how the narrative is organized around a single dominant theme: Tun Abdul Razak as the architect of national development. This framing encompasses his educational initiatives (the Razak Report, UKM, ITM), rural development programmes (FELDA, the Red and Green Books, regional development authorities), the New Economic Policy, and his foreign policy achievements including ASEAN and the normalization of relations with China.

The author's central critical intervention is identifying what the memorial systematically excludes or silences. These omissions include the University and University College Act (UUCA) of 1971, which curbed student activism; the role of leftist intellectuals such as James Puthucheary, A. Samad Ismail, and Dr Ungku Umar in formulating the NEP's social engineering concept; the sidelining of Abdul Aziz Ishak, who had staked a competing claim to the "father of development" epithet before his expulsion from UMNO and detention under the ISA in 1965; and the "subsidy mentality" that critics within UMNO argued rendered Malays less economically competitive. The memorial also gives no information on Tun Abdul Razak's membership of the British Labour Party and Fabian Society, his employment as a junior district officer during the Japanese occupation, or the numerous capable advisers (Ghazali Shafie, Taib Andak, Tunku Shahriman, Raja Mohar) who were instrumental in policy formulation.

The author situates this selective representation within a broader pattern of state-controlled memory-making, noting that the memorial's narrative closely follows William Shaw's 1976 biography and that more critical or nuanced accounts (Paridah, Dharmala, Nik Anuar et al.) have had no discernible impact on the exhibit's storyline. The article concludes by comparing the memorial's approach to the *Sejarah Melayu*'s court chroniclers, observing that even the traditional Malay annals subjected their rulers to respectful criticism, whereas the memorial offers no such counterpoint.

### Key Findings

- The memorial was opened on 6 May 1982, a date deliberately chosen to coincide with both the formation of the 1956 Education Committee chaired by Tun Abdul Razak and the 1960 launch of the Red Book (p. 4).
- The memorial houses 1,650 exhibits as of the author's visit, compared to 700 at its 1982 opening; these include archival materials, museum objects, and library holdings, with the library containing possibly the most complete collection of Tun Abdul Razak's speeches from 1961 to 1975 (pp. 3, 5).
- FELDA, Tun Abdul Razak's "favourite project," opened 145,000 acres of virgin jungle for agricultural settlement in its first ten years (from 1956), predominantly for the Malay rural poor (p. 20).
- The NEP aimed to raise the Malay economic stake from an initial 3% to at least 30%; by the 1980s the Malay share had reached 17–18%, though the expansion of non-Malay stakes was proportionally higher (p. 21).
- Abdul Aziz Ishak, who headed the Ministry of Agriculture and Cooperatives from 1955 to 1963 and could stake a competing claim to the "father of development" title, was expelled from UMNO and detained under the ISA in January 1965 until 1966, then under house arrest until 1971 (pp. 16–17).
- The National Operations Room, established in 1961 in the Ministry of National and Rural Development, had branches in 13 states and 100 districts, and was used by Tun Abdul Razak to monitor development projects at the micro level using coloured pins, flags, and tags on maps (p. 19).

### Conclusion

Abu Talib Ahmad's definitive takeaway is that the Tun Abdul Razak Memorial, like its colonial predecessors, functions as an instrument of state-sanctioned national memory rather than a site of open historical inquiry. The memorial's "linear entrapment" of Tun Abdul Razak within a single developmental narrative, reinforced by the official history and the popular image, leaves no room for the contradictions, failures, and collaborative dimensions of his premiership. The author expresses hope that emerging visitor feedback mechanisms at Malaysian museums might eventually prompt more nuanced presentations, but acknowledges that the entrenched control of both the national narrative and the memorial makes such change unlikely in the near term.

## Context

- The author, Abu Talib Ahmad, is a historian at the National University of Malaysia (UKM) whose own monograph *The Malay-Muslims, Islam and the Rising Sun: 1942–45* (MBRAS Monograph 34, 2003) is cited in the article; his position within the Malaysian academic establishment gives him both access to and critical distance from the institutional frameworks he examines.
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in applying museum studies and memory theory (particularly Anderson's *Imagined Communities*) to a Malaysian national memorial, a genre that had previously attracted little scholarly attention compared to World War II monuments.
