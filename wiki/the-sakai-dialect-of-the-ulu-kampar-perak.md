# The Sakai dialect of the Ulu Kampar, Perak

H.L.E. Luering, a German-trained linguist holding a doctorate from Strassburg, published this vocabulary of the Sakai dialect spoken along the upper Kampar River in Perak in 1901. The article's central contribution is a phonetically transcribed word list of approximately two hundred entries, positioned within the emerging recognition—established by Blagden in 1894—that Sakai languages belong to the Mon-Khmer branch of the Austro-Asiatic family rather than to the previously hypothesised Tibeto-Burman or Austronesian connections.

## Summary

Luering's work is explicitly framed as a preliminary contribution to a larger desideratum: a complete vocabulary of the Sakai dialects of the Malay Peninsula. He situates his own effort after Hugh Clifford's 1891 notes and C. Otto Blagden's 1894 demonstration of the close affinity between Sakai, Semang, and other Austro-Asiatic languages of the region. The dialect documented is that of the settlements on either side of the upper Kampar River—Ulu Pulai, Ulu Gopeng, Ulu Kampar, and the hills around Batu Gajah—while Luering notes that the inhabitants of the Ulu Kinta speak a language unintelligible to this group.

The article devotes its opening pages to phonological description before proceeding to the alphabetical word list. Luering identifies two distinctive features: the near-consonantalisation of final diphthongs (ai, ao, oi, ui) into semi-vocalic y or w, a pattern he compares to Tagalog; and the pronunciation of final n as an intermediate sound between n and d, which he renders as n(d). He also clarifies the aspirate values of kh and th, distinguishing them from both Arabic and English realisations. The vocabulary itself is carefully annotated: pure Sakai words are given in phonetic transliteration, while Malay borrowings—sometimes corrupted—are marked with an asterisk, reflecting what Luering observes as the increasing penetration of Malay into everyday Sakai speech, particularly in market towns such as Tanjong Rambutan, Gopeng, Kuala Dipang, Kampar, Tapah, and Bidor.

A notable ethnolinguistic observation concerns self-designation. Luering reports that the people call themselves "Mai Srak" (the people of the country or jungle) in contrast to "Mai Gop" (the strangers, i.e. the Malays), and he identifies the earlier term "Sen-oi" used by Clifford as a corruption of "srg-on," meaning simply "man." He never encountered Clifford's reported expression "Gob Malayu" in native usage.

### Key Findings

- The Sakai self-designation is "Mai Srak" (people of the country/jungle); the earlier label "Sen-oi" is identified as a variant of "srg-on" (man), not a tribal name (p. 92).
- Final diphthongs (ai, ao, oi, ui) are pronounced with the second element as a semi-consonant y or w, paralleling Tagalog; e.g. *jngkao* (chin) is realised as *jngkaw* or *jngkav* (pp. 92–93).
- Final n is pronounced as an intermediate sound between n and d, rendered n(d); the Malay word *piggan* (plate) is heard as *pigad* (p. 93).
- Malay loanwords are marked with an asterisk throughout the vocabulary; Luering notes their increasing prevalence in market towns including Tanjong Rambutan, Gopeng, Kuala Dipang, Kampar, Tapah, and Bidor (p. 92).
- The vocabulary encompasses approximately 200 entries covering kinship terms, body parts, flora and fauna, verbs of motion, and cultural items such as the *grōg* (dart) and its poisoned variant *grōg margchgrä'* (pp. 93–104).
- The Ulu Kinta Sakai speak a language unintelligible to the Ulu Kampar group, indicating significant dialectal divergence within what was then treated as a single "Sakai" category (p. 92).

### Conclusion

Luering presents his vocabulary as a deliberately modest but methodologically careful foundation, explicitly inviting further investigators to expand the record. His definitive historical takeaway is that the Sakai languages, long misclassified, now stand firmly within the Mon-Khmer branch of the Austro-Asiatic family, and that the urgent task for future scholars is the compilation of complete dialect vocabularies sufficient to permit systematic comparative work across the full range of Austro-Asiatic languages of the Malay Peninsula and mainland Southeast Asia.

## Context

- Luering was a German linguist (Ph.D., Strassburg) conducting fieldwork in Perak during the colonial period; his article was published in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, the principal scholarly outlet for colonial-era research in the region.
- The article draws on direct field contact with Sakai speakers in the Ulu Kampar settlements and builds on the archival and comparative work of Clifford (1891) and Blagden (1894), both also published in the same journal.
