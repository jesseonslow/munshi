# The botany of Christmas Island

H.N. Ridley, Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, published this field account in 1906 documenting a September–October 1904 expedition to Christmas Island undertaken with zoologist Dr. Hanitsch and a small team of collectors. The article serves as a comprehensive botanical and zoological survey of the island's ecological zones—from the coral-reef shore terraces to the basalt-capped plateau forest—while simultaneously recording the impact of phosphate mining on the island's vegetation and fauna.

## Summary

Ridley's narrative follows the expedition day by day as the party worked through the island's principal biomes. The shore terraces, dominated by *Pisonia*, *Ochrosia*, *Ficus retusa*, and *Pandanus nativitatis*, contrast sharply with the plateau forest of *Eugenia*, *Barringtonia*, *Cryptocarya*, and *Sideroxylon*, a distinction Ridley emphasises as the party moved between zones. The phosphate quarries on Phosphate Hill were already showing signs of ecological disturbance, with introduced weeds spreading from the settlement and coolies felling trees to clear ground for further excavation. Ridley notes that the plateau's dense canopy, visible from the 600-foot cliffs at Andrews' Lookout, appeared from a distance like "grass and bushes" (p. 141), underscoring the island's remarkable vertical stratification of vegetation.

The most significant botanical discovery was a pale-yellow *Balanophora*, a parasitic plant previously unrecorded from any oceanic island, found on the shore terrace near the Waterfall (p. 146). Equally notable was the discovery of *Spondias* (Hog plum) at Andrews' Lookout, a genus characteristic of Polynesian islands whose presence at Christmas Island raised questions about seed dispersal mechanisms, since no bird on the island appeared capable of transporting the fruit (p. 141). The failed attempt to cross the island via Murray Hill to the west coast—where the party became lost, ran short of water, and had to retreat after approximately 15 miles—demonstrates how little of the island's interior had been traversed by Europeans at that time (pp. 150–153).

Zoologically, the expedition documented the nesting behaviour of boobies on the south coast, the abundance of the large pigeon *Carpophaga* on the plateau, and the presence of a feral cat that had escaped from European ownership and become a threat to the coolies' chickens. Ridley raises the broader conservation concern that introduced predators, particularly arboreal civet cats, would pose a far greater danger to the island's terrestrial birdlife than cats, which cannot reach the seabirds roosting in the upper canopy (p. 150).

### Key Findings

- The expedition (20 September – 2 November 1904) documented a *Balanophora* species on the shore terrace, the first record of this parasitic genus from any oceanic island (p. 146).
- *Spondias* (Hog plum) was found at Andrews' Lookout on the North East Point cliffs, a genus characteristic of Polynesian islands; fallen fruit lay untouched beneath the trees, suggesting no effective seed-disperser existed on the island (p. 141).
- Freshwater crabs and prawns were recorded for the first time from Christmas Island, found in a small pool where a stream emerged from the coral reef at the Waterfall (p. 138).
- The Murray Hill crossing attempt covered approximately 15 miles before the party was forced to retreat due to water shortage; the route had never before been completed by anyone on the island (pp. 150–153).
- *Sesuvium portulacastrum*, *Lepturus filiformis*, *Macroglossa* (hummingbird hawkmoth), *Pieris* sp. (butterfly), and *Polypodium phymatodes* (fern) were all new records for the island's flora and fauna (pp. 145–146, 144, 150, 155).
- The phosphate mining operations had already introduced weeds to Phosphate Hill, carried on the clothes of coolies, and the felling of *Sideroxylon* and *Cryptocarya* for quarry clearance was actively altering the plateau forest (p. 140).

### Conclusion

Ridley's account establishes Christmas Island as a biogeographically distinctive island whose flora reflects both long-distance oceanic dispersal (drift seeds from Java, Polynesian *Spondias*) and the ecological consequences of its geology—coral reef overlying basalt creating distinct microhabitats. The article implicitly argues that the island's isolation has produced a fragile equilibrium now under threat from phosphate extraction, introduced species, and the loss of potential seed-dispersers, making further botanical and zoological documentation urgent.

## Context

- Ridley wrote as Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens; the expedition was conducted under the auspices of the Straits Settlements scientific community, and the island's administration by the Phosphate Company of Christmas Island provided logistical support (Mr. Macpherson supplied housing and coolies).
- The article reflects the colonial-era practice of combining scientific collection with administrative observation: Ridley records the coolie labour force, the phosphate tramway, and the settlement infrastructure as part of the natural history narrative, treating human activity as an ecological variable rather than a separate subject.
