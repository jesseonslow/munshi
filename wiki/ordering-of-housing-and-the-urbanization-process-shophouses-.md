# Ordering of housing and the urbanization process: shophouses in colonial Penang

Mai Lin Tjoa-Bonatz's 1998 article traces the transformation of the Penang shophouse from a Chinese courtyard-house form to a British-influenced terrace house, arguing that this shift was driven not by top-down planning but by the gradual accumulation of fire-prevention and public-health legislation under the Straits Settlements colonial government. The study is set against the rapid urbanisation of Georgetown between the 1780s and the 1930s, a period in which the Chinese-dominated building sector implemented its own construction traditions within an increasingly regulated administrative framework.

## Summary

Tjoa-Bonatz approaches the shophouse as a building type whose origins remain contested, reviewing three competing hypotheses: Chinese migrant tradition, European colonial planning, and indigenous Southeast Asian market-house typologies. Rather than resolving the debate, she adopts Goldblum's concept of the shophouse as a hybrid Sino-colonial form and uses Penang's administrative records to trace how legislative interventions progressively reshaped the building's internal spatial logic. The article demonstrates that the term "shophouse" itself first appears in Penang's official records in 1884, far earlier than the post-war origin commonly assumed, and that the five-foot-way originated in the Penang Assessment Regulation of 1826 rather than in Raffles' 1822 Singapore instructions.

The core argument is that the shophouse's evolution followed two distinct legislative phases. The first, from the 1810s through the 1850s, was fire-prevention oriented: the prohibition of attap roofing in 1817, the standardisation of street widths, and the regulation of facade alignment produced a compact, fire-safe town of two-storey brick-and-tile buildings. The second phase, beginning with the Straits Settlements Ordinance of 1896 and intensifying through the 1916 Penang Bye-Laws, was public-health oriented: minimum open-space requirements, compulsory back lanes, rounded house corners, and limits on built area between courtyards effectively dismantled the courtyard-house system and replaced it with the modern terraced house. The author shows that these changes correlated with broader social transformations—the emergence of smaller nuclear family units, the growth of a real-estate sector, and the standardisation of construction techniques using concrete and pre-cast materials.

Primary sources include Penang municipal building plans submitted for approval under the bye-laws, the 1881 census of the building sector, the F.W. Kelly survey maps of 1891–93, and the administrative minutes of the Penang Council. The article also draws on John Thomson's 1862 photographic record and the HIST mapping project at the Technical University of Darmstadt to reconstruct the socio-spatial segregation of the city centre.

### Key Findings

- The term "shophouse" first appears in Penang's administrative records in 1884, not in the post-war period as commonly assumed (p. 125).
- The five-foot-way originated in the Penang Assessment Regulation VI of 1826, which specified a 5-foot space between house and drain, predating Raffles' 1822 Singapore instructions (p. 126).
- The 1881 census recorded 1,362 carpenters in Penang, of whom 1,276 were Chinese, confirming Chinese dominance in the building sector (p. 129, Table 1).
- Georgetown's population surged by 84.6% between 1891 and 1901, driven primarily by Indian migrant labour, reaching 95,296 by 1901 (p. 132, Table 2).
- The 1916 Penang Bye-Laws mandated a minimum open-space width of 10 × 6 feet, a maximum built area of 40 feet between courtyards, rounded house corners of at least 15 feet radius, and compulsory back lanes, effectively ending the back-to-back courtyard-house system (p. 134).
- In 1881, Chinese residents constituted 74% of the commercial district and up to 90% of the harbour area, while women formed only about one-third of the urban population (p. 132).

### Conclusion

Tjoa-Bonatz concludes that the shophouse's transformation from courtyard house to terrace house was a direct product of the colonial state's progressive legislative intervention—first to create a fire-safe city, then to enforce public-health standards—rather than the result of any single planning act or cultural imposition. The built environment of colonial Penang thus reflects a layered process in which Chinese building traditions provided the material and formal vocabulary, while British administrative regulation determined the spatial parameters within which that vocabulary could operate.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Penang Municipal Planning and Public Works Department archives (approved building plans), the Straits Settlements Records (Series A and K), and the 1881 and 1911 census reports held in the Penang Library and the National Archives of Malaysia.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Southeast Asian urban form by shifting analytical focus from the shophouse as a static typological object to the legislative and economic processes that produced its successive spatial configurations.
