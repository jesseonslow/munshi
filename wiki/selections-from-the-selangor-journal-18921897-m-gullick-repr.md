# Selections from the Selangor Journal (1892–1897). .M. Gullick. Reprint 26. A short history of the Society

Amarjit Kaur's 1995 article traces the origins and development of cocoa cultivation across the Malaysian territories from the 1770s through the 1990s, arguing that the crop's rise from marginal experimental planting to a major export commodity was driven not by market forces alone but by successive state interventions shaped by colonial diversification policy, post-independence Malay advancement ideology, and the New Economic Policy. The study covers all three constituent territories—Peninsular Malaysia, Sabah, and Sarawak—and demonstrates how cocoa's trajectory was inseparable from broader political-economic transformations in each.

## Summary

Kaur organises the narrative into three phases corresponding to shifts in commodity prices, investment climate, and political control. The first phase (1880s–mid 1940s) saw cocoa grown largely as an experimental crop, overshadowed by rubber and oil palm. The Department of Agriculture conducted trial plantings at Serdang, Cheras, Kuala Lipis, and Temerloh using seed from Sri Lanka, Java, and North Borneo, establishing that the peninsula's climatic and soil conditions were suitable. The second phase (1948–early 1960s) was defined by the colonial government's post-war diversification strategy, catalysed by E.E. Cheeseman's 1948 feasibility study and the formation of Malayan Cocoa Limited in 1950—a joint venture between Harrisons and Crosfield, Cadbury Brothers, and the Colonial Development Corporation. This phase also saw the promotion of cocoa as a smallholder crop through RIDA land settlement schemes, particularly on the east coast, though the outbreak of Vascular-Streak Dieback in 1958 and the Emergency severely hampered expansion. The third phase (late 1960s onward) coincided with the New Economic Policy, the formation of MARA, and the availability of disease-tolerant hybrid varieties, which made cocoa-coconut intercropping commercially viable for Malay smallholders.

The article's central argument is that cocoa's development cannot be understood through a simple plantation-versus-smallholder binary or through price signals alone. The state was the decisive actor at every stage: first as colonial administrator pursuing diversification, then as post-independence government using cocoa as an instrument of Malay economic advancement. Kaur draws on government reports (Cheeseman's 1948 study, the 1955 working party report), agricultural statistics, and company records to show how policy decisions—such as the deliberate siting of cocoa on the economically 'backward' east coast, or the promotion of intercropping under the Coconut Replanting and Rehabilitation scheme—shaped the crop's geographic and social distribution. The article also notes that Malaysia's refusal to sign the International Cocoa Agreements reflected a strategic calculation: as an efficient producer accounting for only about 9 per cent of world exports, the government preferred unrestricted expansion over price stabilisation mechanisms.

### Key Findings

- Cocoa was first reported fruiting in Melaka in 1778 in the garden of a Portuguese widow; in British North Borneo, native plantings were recorded on the Segaluid River as early as 1886 (pp. 71, 72).
- Malayan Cocoa Limited, formed in 1950 by Harrisons and Crosfield, Cadbury Brothers, and the Colonial Development Corporation, acquired a 1,200-acre lease in Trengganu and established the first commercial monoculture of Trinitario cocoa at Jerangau Estate (pp. 73–74).
- Vascular-Streak Dieback (caused by *Oncobasidin theobromae*) struck in early 1958, reducing yields to a third of expectations and disproportionately affecting the *Amelonado* variety in Trengganu (p. 75).
- Total cocoa acreage in Malaysia expanded from 577 hectares in 1960 to 33,466 hectares in 1976, with Peninsular Malaysia growing from 577 to 20,796 ha and Sabah from 1,538 to 9,827 ha over the same period (p. 78, Table 1).
- The *Amelonado* variety, imported from Ghana, was distributed in 1950 as follows: 3,649 seedlings to the Federation of Malaya, 538 to Sarawak, 570 to North Borneo, and 199 to Singapore (p. 80, n. 9).
- The Malaysian Cocoa Board was established in 1989 to promote the orderly development of the cocoa industry, functioning analogously to the earlier Rubber Research Institute (p. 79).

### Conclusion

Kaur's definitive takeaway is that the pattern of cocoa cultivation in Malaysia was fundamentally determined by the ideology of the state rather than by individual farmer choice or market necessity. The plantation model—whether operated by corporate interests in the colonial period or by government land settlement authorities after independence—served as the institutional vehicle through which technically advanced agricultural practices were transferred to the rural sector, making cocoa a tool of rural modernisation as much as an export commodity.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on government agricultural statistics, the Cheeseman (1948) feasibility report, DOA records, and secondary sources including Allen and Donnithorne's *Western Enterprise in Malaya and Singapore* and Lim Teck Ghee's *Peasants and their Agricultural Economy in Colonial Malaya*.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the literature on Malaysian agricultural diversification and the political economy of the New Economic Policy, situating cocoa within the broader narrative of state-led development and Malay economic advancement.
