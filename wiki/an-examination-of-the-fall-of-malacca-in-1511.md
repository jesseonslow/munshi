# An examination of the fall of Malacca in 1511

R.W. McRoberts (1984) re-examines the Portuguese conquest of Melaka in 1511, arguing against the dominant historiographical tradition that attributes the fall to the moral failings of Sultan Mahmud or to the inevitability of colonial expansion, and instead reconstructs the event from primary Portuguese and Malay sources to demonstrate that Melaka was a militarily prepared, politically cohesive empire whose capital fell due to a combination of divided strategic priorities, the specific geography of the city, and a critical tactical error by its ruler.

## Summary

The article opens by identifying a persistent flaw in the historiography of 1511: both Malay and Portuguese traditions personify the state in the ruler, so that the fall of Melaka is explained through the character of Mahmud rather than through the mechanics of the siege itself. McRoberts traces this tendency from the Sejarah Melayu's invention of a phantom Sultan Ahmad to exculpate the Melakan dynasty, through Pires and Barros on the Portuguese side, to modern commentators such as Wilkinson, Tate, and Loofs, who variously blame moral laxity, cyclical patterns of land-and-sea power, or the apotheosis of colonialism. He argues that very few scholars have examined the events literally from the sources, and that even the basic dates of d'Albuquerque's attacks remain disputed or unexamined in the literature.

McRoberts then reconstructs the political and military situation in Melaka in 1510–1511 from the Commentarios, Cartas, Suma Oriental, and Sejarah Melayu. His central contention is that Mahmud was not the weak, licentious figure of traditional accounts but a capable ruler who had just consolidated sole authority after the death of his uncle, had summoned the full force of his empire to Melaka, and was preparing a campaign against Aru — the state's longest-standing enemy. The appearance of the Portuguese fleet on 1 July 1511 thus introduced a third priority (alongside the Aru campaign and the ongoing Pasai civil war) into an administration that had already committed its resources to a single strategic aim. This confusion of priorities, combined with d'Albuquerque's deliberate presentation of his expedition as a limited operation for the release of Portuguese prisoners, may have led Mahmud to underestimate the seriousness of the threat.

The article's most original contribution is its tactical analysis of why the bridge over the Melaka River was the only viable point of attack for a force of d'Albuquerque's size. McRoberts systematically eliminates all other options — overland approaches blocked by mangroves and open ground, the precipitous slopes of Bukit Melaka, and the danger of street fighting in Upe — to show that only the bridge offered a cramped position where Melakan numerical superiority in men and ordnance could be neutralised. D'Albuquerque's sole technological advantage, the fire-power of his vessels, was deployed as a fixed artillery platform impervious to counterattack, making the bridge both the tactical necessity and the decisive factor in the outcome.

### Key Findings

- D'Albuquerque's force numbered between 1,000 and 1,600 men in at least 16 vessels, against a Melakan garrison reported at up to 100,000 fighting men, 8,000 pieces of ordnance, and 20 war elephants (p. 26).
- The first Portuguese attack was launched on the feast of Saint James, 25 July 1511; the second, decisive assault followed on a Friday, probably 14 August, two hours before dawn (pp. 37–38).
- The city fell on a Tuesday at spring tide, probably 24 or 25 August 1511 — not the 24 August midday date widely repeated in the literature (p. 38).
- Mahmud had assembled at least 3,000 Turkic and Persian ("Khorason") mercenaries and several thousand Javanese troops, the latter paid three months' wages in advance in early August (p. 32).
- D'Albuquerque's sole technological advantage was the fire-power of his vessels, which he employed as a fixed artillery platform impervious to counterattack by Melakan land forces or the smaller Melakan war prahus (p. 37).
- Mahmud's decision to withdraw his forces upriver to Bertam (Mejcura) on the evening of the eleventh day of the second attack proved a fatal tactical error, leaving the burning city to d'Albuquerque (p. 38).

### Conclusion

McRoberts' definitive historical takeaway is that the fall of Melaka in 1511 was neither the end of the Melakan state nor the inauguration of imperialism in Southeast Asia. The empire remained intact and loyal to Mahmud for the next century, and the region had a long history of imperial powers — Srivijaya, Majapahit, Ayuthiya, China — predating the Portuguese. The event should be understood as a remarkable tactical victory achieved through specific geographic and strategic circumstances, not as a civilizational turning point or a cyclical inevitability.

## Context

- Primary sources consulted include D'Albuquerque's *Commentarios* (Hakluyt translation) and *Cartas* (Academia Real das Ciências edition), Pires' *Suma Oriental*, the *Sejarah Melayu*, and Ma Huan's *Ying-yai sheng-lan*.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its systematic rejection of the moral-character framework inherited from both Malay and Portuguese traditions, and in its correction of the established chronology of the siege based on close reading of d'Albuquerque's own dispatches.
