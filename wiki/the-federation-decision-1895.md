# The Federation decision: 1895

A.C. Milner's 1970 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* re-examines the 1895 decision to federate the Protected Malay States, arguing that the commonly accepted explanation—that federation was simply a response to Pahang's financial crisis—reduces a complex, multi-causal process to a single trigger. Milner contends that the decision emerged from a convergence of administrative, security, economic, and ideological factors operating within a broader imperial framework of territorial consolidation.

## Summary

Milner's central intervention is methodological as much as substantive. He challenges the prevailing "neat explanation" that the Malayan Federation was merely a solution to Pahang's fiscal insolvency, demonstrating that by mid-1893 the Colonial Office had already agreed in principle to inter-state financial assistance, rendering federation no longer strictly necessary to resolve that particular problem. The Pahang crisis, he argues, provided the *occasion* for federation but not its *overwhelming* influence on anything beyond timing. Instead, Milner traces a "drift towards consolidation" built from multiple objective processes: the facilitation of financial aid from wealthier to poorer states, security concerns following the Perak War and the 1891 Pahang rising, the promise of administrative efficiency through a unified civil service, the reduction of duplicated administrative machinery, the relief of an overworked Governor, and the economic advantages of a larger borrowing entity.

The article's second major contribution is its attention to the subjective and ideological dimensions of the decision. Milner situates the Malayan case within a wider imperial pattern of consolidation—South Africa, Canada, Australia, Nigeria, the West Indies—and argues that the fashion for federation was itself a powerful motivator. He explores the "minds" of the officials involved, noting their professional ambitions, their taste for the absolute authority of the "Rajah" model, and the aesthetic satisfaction they derived from building larger territorial units. Crucially, Milner also maps the internal political economy of the Colonial Office during the Liberal years of 1892–1895, showing how the tension between the "rigorous economy" faction (Meade, Fairfield, Harcourt) and the "development" faction (Buxton, Rosebery, Chamberlain) shaped the form federation took. Both camps, he demonstrates, ultimately supported consolidation, though for different reasons and with different anxieties—Fairfield, for instance, opposed the scheme primarily because of his distrust of Swettenham's motives and spending habits, not because he rejected the principle of unification.

Primary sources from the Colonial Office records (CO 273 series), the Swettenham Collection at the National Archives of Malaysia, and contemporary press and parliamentary debates underpin the argument. Milner draws on dispatches from Governors Smith and Mitchell, minutes by Lucas, Fairfield, and Meade, and published writings by Keyser, Norman, and Swettenham himself to reconstruct the decision-making process from both the London and Singapore ends.

### Key Findings

- By mid-1893 the Colonial Office had already accepted the principle of one state assisting another, meaning federation was no longer a necessary condition for solving Pahang's financial problem (p. 105).
- Governor Smith reported in 1893 that he had "literally not had one day, even a Sunday, free from work" in the preceding year, illustrating the administrative burden that consolidation was meant to alleviate (p. 108).
- Governor Mitchell, who had served in Natal and Fiji, explicitly drew on his South African experience of consolidation when formulating his 1895 memorandum to the Secretary of State (p. 110, n. 51).
- Fairfield, the Assistant Under-Secretary, opposed the Swettenham/Lucas scheme not on principle but because he distrusted Swettenham's "tendency to extravagant expenditure"; he was won over only when Mitchell declared the existing system "cumbrous, unsatisfactory, and will very presumably ere long, become unworkable" (p. 113).
- The 1897 F.M.S. Durbar, at which all Protected Malay Rulers were assembled for the first time, marked the ceremonial culmination of the consolidation process and gave Swettenham visible satisfaction (p. 111).
- Harcourt, as Chancellor of the Exchequer, refused any reduction in the Straits Settlements' military contribution to Imperial Defence costs, thereby foreclosing the possibility of London directly subsidising Pahang and pushing the problem toward a structural solution (p. 113).

### Conclusion

Milner's definitive takeaway is that the Federation decision was "much more than" a response to the Pahang problem. It was the product of a general drift toward larger territorial units that was simultaneously a matter of administrative logic, imperial fashion, strategic calculation, and individual ambition. By tracing the multifarious motives involved, Milner demonstrates that the event had "a significance beyond the purely Malayan sphere," embedding it within the broader history of British imperial consolidation at the close of the nineteenth century.

## Context

- Primary archival sources include the Colonial Office records (CO 273 series) held in London and the Swettenham Collection at the National Archives of Malaysia (Arkib Negara Malaysia).
- Historiographically, the article responds to and extends the work of E. Sadka (*The Protected Malay States, 1874–1895*) and E. Chew ("Sir Frank Swettenham and the Federation of the Malay States," *Modern Asian Studies*, 1968), while also engaging with Khoo Kay Kim's earlier "Pahang crisis" interpretation. Milner's contribution lies in his insistence on a multi-causal, imperial-contextualised analysis that incorporates the subjective motivations of individual officials.
