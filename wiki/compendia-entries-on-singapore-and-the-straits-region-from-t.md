# Compendia Entries on Singapore and the Straits Region from the 16th to the Early 19th Century

## Abstract

When the Portuguese and Spaniards first arrived in the South China Sea (ca. early 1500s), the commercial worlds they encountered in centres like Melaka and Manila were closely connected with the politics and economy of China under the Ming dynasty (r. 1368–1644). This article examines how the Malay sultanates conceptualized China in three works of Malay epic literature (Malay: *hikayat*)—the *Hikayat Raja Raja Pasai*, *Hikayat Hang Tuah*, and *Sejarah Melayu*. A comparison of these semi-fictional texts with historical accounts provides evidence that during Europe’s Age of Exploration, when Southeast Asia first became the crossroads of global maritime exchange, the sultanates of Southeast Asia were deeply intertwined with Chinese diplomacy in ways that help explain European colonial-era activities in the South China Sea well into the twentieth century.

## Summary

This article examines how the Malay sultanates conceptualized Ming China through three works of Malay epic literature (*hikayat*): the *Hikayat Raja Raja Pasai*, the *Hikayat Hang Tuah*, and the *Sejarah Melayu*. The central argument is that these semi-fictional texts, when cross-referenced with Chinese historical sources—particularly the travel writings of Zheng He's crewmates Ma Huan and Fei Xin, and the observations of the Timurid diplomat Ghayath al-Din Naqqash at the Ming court—reveal that Southeast Asian sultanates were deeply embedded within Chinese diplomatic and commercial networks on the eve of the European Age of Exploration. The author contends that this pre-existing Malay-Chinese entanglement helps explain why the South China Sea became so central to European colonial interests and why European activities in the region were shaped by Chinese diplomatic frameworks well into the twentieth century.

The article traces a spectrum of Chinese-Malay connections across the three texts, moving from cultural proximity to political kinship. The *Hikayat Raja Raja Pasai* depicts China as simultaneously a foreign political power and a culturally local presence, evidenced by the figure of the *pendikar* of *kampong china* (Chinese village) and the incorporation of Chinese material culture—yellow silk, dragon motifs, and the colour *china kepaluan*—into Malay royal ceremonial. The *Hikayat Hang Tuah* elevates the Sultanate of Melaka to a position of parity with China while depicting the Portuguese as disorganized pirate-like interlopers rather than sophisticated merchants. The *Sejarah Melayu* culminates this trajectory with narratives of Chinese-Malay royal intermarriage, including the famous Hang Li-Po story, which the author interprets through the lens of the *heqin* (peace marriage) policy that had structured Chinese frontier diplomacy since the Han dynasty.

The primary sources informing the narrative include the Malay epics themselves (notably British Library manuscript Or. 14350, which collates the *Hikayat Raja Raja Pasai* with the *Hikayat Raja Handik*), Ma Huan's *Ying-yai Sheng-Lan*, Fei Xin's *Hsing-ch'a-sheng-lan*, and Naqqash's account of the Ming court. The author additionally cross-references these with Portuguese maps of Melaka and the writings of the sixteenth-century Persian traveller Ali Akbar Khitayi, who represented Sumatra as part of China's frontier.

### Key Findings

- Ma Huan's *Ying-yai Sheng-Lan* records Chinese Muslim communities in Java and Sumatra who "fled and now live in this country" during the fall of the Yuan dynasty, providing first-hand evidence for the migration of Yuan-era Muslims to Southeast Asia and their role in the spread of Islam in the region (p. 3).
- The *Hikayat Raja Raja Pasai* describes the *pendikar* Ali of *kampong china* as a martial arts master in the Pasai prince's royal entourage; this is corroborated by Portuguese maps of Melaka that reference multiple Chinese villages (*kampong china*) as distinct settlements (pp. 5–7).
- The Pasai prince's royal headdress is described as "fine yellow silk" with the colour of *china kepaluan*, closely paralleling the Timurid diplomat Naqqash's observations of yellow silk as a central element of Ming courtly ceremonial, including the throne covering, imperial edict cord, and canopy (pp. 7–9).
- The *Hikayat Hang Tuah* depicts Hang Tuah's diplomatic mission to the Ming court where he is restricted from meeting the emperor directly and must use a riddle involving long uncut watercress to glimpse the throne, illustrating the strict protocol governing foreign delegations and the exceptional nature of the emperor's subsequent direct audience (pp. 13–15).
- The *Sejarah Melayu*'s Hang Li-Po narrative describes the Ming emperor sending a princess with an entourage of approximately five hundred courtiers to marry Sultan Mansur Shah of Melaka; the author notes that the Ming period did not historically employ the *heqin* policy, making this an anachronism that nonetheless reflects the real patron-client relationship between Ming China and Melaka (pp. 17–19).
- The *Hikayat Raja Handik*, collated with the *Hikayat Raja Raja Pasai* in the same manuscript, was addressed to a mixed audience including Malays, Makassarese, and Chinese listeners, indicating the cultural proximity between Chinese and Malay-speaking communities in Southeast Asia (p. 3).

### Conclusion

The author's definitive historical takeaway is that the Malay epics, despite their legendary and semi-fictional orientation, provide reliable evidence of the depth of Chinese-Malay political, commercial, and cultural integration during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. This integration—spanning martial arts, material culture, diplomatic protocol, and royal kinship—explains why the South China Sea became the crossroads of global maritime exchange when Europeans arrived, and why European colonial activities in the region were shaped by pre-existing Chinese diplomatic frameworks that persisted well into the twentieth century.

## Context

- The study draws on the British Library manuscript Or. 14350 (*Hikayat Raja Raja Pasai* collated with *Hikayat Raja Handik*), Ma Huan's *Ying-yai Sheng-Lan*, Fei Xin's *Hsing-ch'a-sheng-lan*, and the Timurid diplomat Naqqash's account of the Ming court, alongside Portuguese cartographic sources and the writings of Ali Akbar Khitayi.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the emerging field of Chinese-Muslim migration to Southeast Asia (building on Wain 2017) and to the study of Islamic Chinoiserie in material culture (building on Kadoi 2018), while also engaging critically with the historiography of the *heqin* marriage policy and its applicability to maritime frontier states.
