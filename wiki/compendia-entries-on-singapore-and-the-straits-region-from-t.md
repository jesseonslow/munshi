# Compendia Entries on Singapore and the Straits Region from the 16th to the Early 19th Century

## Abstract

During the early modern period in Europe, educated circles drew on dictionaries, lexicons, encyclopaedias, grammars, geographies, and cosmographies to understand the world in which they lived. These compendia built on information found in earlier publications of the same sort, supplemented by new details provided by traders and travellers with first-hand knowledge of different parts of the world. This article examines knowledge about Singapore and the Straits region circulating in Europe between circa 1511 and 1819, focusing on compendia entries touching on the Straits region, especially Singapore, Melaka, Johor, and Bintan. It traces the evolution of compendium knowledge as it moves through cycles of accumulation, transformation, and de-contextualization.

## Summary

This article traces the evolution of European compendia entries on Singapore, Melaka, Johor, and Bintan from approximately 1511 to 1819, identifying four broad phases of knowledge accumulation and transformation. The authors demonstrate that compendia—dictionaries, lexicons, encyclopaedias, and geographies—served as the primary vehicles through which educated Europeans encountered information about the Straits region, functioning simultaneously as practical references for merchants, pedagogical tools, and conversation aids for the growing middle class (pp. 4–5).

The first phase (1512–1596) reveals that pre-Portuguese knowledge of the region existed through Ptolemaic cartography and classical sources, but was substantially expanded by Iberian accounts and returning mariners. By the 1570s and 1580s, Melaka was recognized as an important trading emporium or Portuguese outpost, while Singapore was described with considerable confusion—as a promontory, a city within Siam, or a large island—and Bintan, though prominent on charts, remained largely absent from textual compendia (p. 9). The second phase (1596–1650) saw growing knowledge of Malay politics and the political prestige of Johor and Aceh, with Heylyn's *Cosmographie* (1652) representing the most substantive synthesis of the period, including one of the earliest mentions of Parameswara's flight to Singapore (p. 14).

The third phase (1650–1750) was marked by the adoption of alphabetical ordering, popularized by Moreri's *Le Grand Dictionnaire Historique* (1674), which set a new benchmark but also introduced fragmentation. Singapore suffered most from this decontextualization: Fondeur (1680) created separate entries for *Sincapura* as a city, a strait, and a promontory, and no publication from this period referred to Singapore as an island (p. 18). The final phase (1750–1819) was the most prolific in volume but also the most problematic, as copying and translation perpetuated anachronisms and, critically, the tone toward the region's inhabitants shifted decisively toward negative stereotyping (pp. 23, 25).

### Key Findings

- Before 1500, European knowledge of the Straits region was limited to Ptolemaic cartographic references; the sixteenth century saw expansion through Iberian accounts, though chronicles and Jesuit letters did not effectively feed into compendia entries (p. 9).
- Heylyn's *Cosmographie* (1652) represents the most substantive pre-1750 synthesis, incorporating Ptolemaic, Varthema, and de Barros sources, and is among the earliest compendia to mention Parameswara's coup at Singapore (p. 14).
- Alphabetical ordering, popularized by Moreri (1674), caused severe decontextualization of Singapore: Zedler's *Universal-Lexicon* (1732–54) contains the most entries—three as a strait, three as a cape, and two as a city—yet no publication from this period identifies Singapore as an island (p. 18).
- Brice's *Grand Gazetteer* (1759–60) marks a noteworthy departure by depicting Singapore as a former major trading centre with its own ruler, drawing on Nieuhof and Hamilton, though it still included the erroneous claim of a diamond-yielding mountain (p. 21).
- By the late eighteenth century, compendia entries described the region's inhabitants using a consistent vocabulary of negative stereotypes—cruel, treacherous, lazy, barbarous—with Berenger's edition of Busching (1781) calling the Malay "the most maleficent animal" and Guthrie (1780) attributing their degeneracy to Dutch tyranny (p. 23).
- Information on Johor remained "sparse, woefully inaccurate, and frequently out of date" into the 1810s, with Tuckey (1815) and Hassel (1817) still placing the capital at Batu Sawar despite its relocation to the Riau islands a century earlier (p. 21).

### Conclusion

The authors argue that these compendia entries matter today because they reveal a distinctive European inventory of knowledge about the Straits region that has been neglected by historians. The compendia demonstrate how knowledge was compiled, processed, and represented, and how the mechanical processes of alphabetization, copying, and translation generated fragmentation, anachronism, and stereotyping. The negative characterizations of the region's peoples, sharpened and perpetuated over the late eighteenth century, played an important part in entrenching colonial rule from the early nineteenth century onward (pp. 23, 25).

## Context

- The article draws on a wide range of early modern European compendia in Latin, French, German, Italian, and English, many of which exist in limited archival holdings and have not been systematically studied for their Southeast Asian content.
- The study contributes to the broader historiography of the "circulation of knowledge" in early modern Europe, situating compendia as nodes in a network that connected Iberian primary sources, cartographic production, and the reading public across the continent.
