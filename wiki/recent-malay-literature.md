# Recent Malay literature

## Abstract

For well over a century, close personal ties underlay relations between the rulers of Kedah and Siam. In the latter part of the nineteenth century, Kedah emerged as Siam’s most favoured Malay tributary, and the state’s rulers pursued parallel agendas of socio-economic and administrative modernization. That special tie ended abruptly in 1909 when Siam signed a treaty ceding the Kedah to Great Britain, but Tunku Badlishah, a key figure in the Kedah administration, was a naturalized citizen of Siam and had close personal ties with King Vajiravudh. After the latter’s death in 1925, the relationship faded away, but Badlishah’s Thai background continued to influence his career in various ways even after he became Sultan in 1943. His reign coincided with Malaya’s push for independence, and he became a key figure in pressing for a continuing leadership role for Malaya’s sultans and a retention of traditional authority.

## Summary

This article traces the extraordinary personal and political trajectory of Tunku Badlishah (r. 1943–58), the twelfth son of Sultan Abdul Hamid Halim Shah of Kedah, whose life embodied and ultimately outlasted the centuries-old personalised relationship between the Kedah and Siamese ruling houses. Suwannathat-Pian situates Badlishah within a broader historical arc: from the 1802 succession crisis that made Kedah a Siamese fief, through the Siamese occupation of 1821–41, to the era of close personal ties cultivated by Sultan Zainal Rashid Muazzam Shah and his son Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin Mukarram Shah, who married a Siamese noblewoman selected by the Bunnag family. The 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty severed official tributary relations, but Badlishah's unique status as an adopted son of Crown Prince Vajiravudh (later King Rama VI) kept a personal channel open until the king's death in 1925.

The article then shifts to Badlishah's role in the post-war political landscape of Malaya, where he emerged as the most outspoken defender of the Malay rulers' traditional authority against both British colonial pressure and the rising political party UMNO. Suwannathat-Pian draws on extensive correspondence in the Thai National Archives—letters between Sultan Abdul Hamid, King Vajiravudh, and Prince Damrong—to reconstruct the private negotiations over Badlishah's education, naturalisation, and eventual return to Kedah. The narrative culminates in the complex rivalry between Badlishah and his half-brother Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra, whose 1955 electoral victory and subsequent premiership effectively resolved the tension between traditional royal authority and modern constitutional power.

### Key Findings

- Kedah's tributary relationship with Siam was fundamentally altered in 1802 when Tunku Pangeran (later Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin Halim Shah) submitted himself to King Rama I as a supplicant rather than a ruler, making Kedah "a gift from the Siamese" and a suzerain's fief (pp. 120–121).
- The 1905 Loan Agreement between Kedah and Siam involved a loan of $2,600,000 and introduced the Office of the Adviser and the State Council, which were empowered to enact laws for administrative reform (p. 128).
- Tunku Badlishah was born on 17 March 1894, sent to Bangkok around age 7–8, educated at King's College (Ratchwithayalai), and entered Wadham College, Oxford in 1919 to study law and history (pp. 124–125).
- King Vajiravudh's letter of 6 August 1925 to Sultan Abdul Hamid explicitly "lent" Badlishah to Kedah while keeping his post open in the Siamese Privy Purse Department, where Badlishah had risen to the rank of Phra Si Surintharawiwongsa within three years of joining (pp. 126–127).
- In February 1946, Sultan Badlishah wrote to the Colonial Office in London asserting that his signature on the Malayan Union agreement had been obtained under duress, with the threat that "a successor who would sign it would be appointed" if he refused (p. 130).
- The Conference of Rulers, led by Sultan Badlishah, rejected UMNO's 1948 proposal to appoint a Malay Deputy High Commissioner at the Federal level, a decision that deeply affected Dato' Onn Jaafar's attitude toward royalty (p. 132).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian's definitive takeaway is that Tunku Badlishah stands as the last and most significant exemplar of the personalised Bangkok-Kedah relationship, a bond that predated the modern era and survived the formal severance of tributary ties in 1909 through the sheer force of personal attachment between two royal households. His extraordinary background—raised in Siam, educated at Oxford, naturalised as a Thai citizen, and adopted by a Thai king—shaped his bold and outspoken leadership style, enabling him to navigate the transition from traditional to constitutional rulership and to press effectively for the retention of traditional authority in independent Malaya.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the Thai National Archives (Documents Reign 6, B.3.1/4 series), the UK National Archives (CO 273, CO 535, CO 717, CO 537), and the Rhodes House Library, Oxford (MSS. Ind. Ocn series), alongside Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra's published memoirs.
- The study contributes to the historiography of intra-regional Malay-Siamese relations by demonstrating how personalised royal ties functioned as a parallel diplomatic channel that outlived formal institutional arrangements, and by foregrounding the agency of a single individual within the broader structural shift from tributary to colonial to independent statehood.
