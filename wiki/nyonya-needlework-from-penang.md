# Nyonya needlework from Penang

Hwei-Fen Cheah (2009) examines the needlework traditions of Peranakan Chinese women in Penang, situating them within the broader network of Baba communities across the Straits Settlements and Sumatra. The article argues that while metallic thread embroidery maintained a shared regional style that reinforced a common Baba identity, silk thread embroidery and beadwork became vehicles for expressing distinct local character, with Penang developing a recognizable stylistic signature by the early twentieth century.

## Summary

Cheah's central problem is one of provenance and stylistic attribution: because material culture migrates with its owners and the collectors' market complicates attribution, it is difficult to determine where a given piece of needlework was originally produced. The article addresses this by comparing specific examples across Penang, Melaka, Singapore, and Sumatran centres (Palembang, Padang), identifying which technical and aesthetic features are shared and which are locally distinctive. The three media—metallic thread embroidery, silk thread embroidery, and beadwork—are treated separately because they followed different trajectories of standardization and local variation.

The core argument is that the media were perceived differently by their makers. Metallic thread embroidery, already well established by the time Penang was founded, became associated with hallowed Baba traditions and maintained a uniform style recognizable across communities, reinforcing shared culture and historical ties to neighbouring Malay and Javanese elite dress. Silk thread embroidery and beadwork, by contrast, were more amenable to fashion and local preference. Beadwork in particular was a novelty tied to the mid-nineteenth-century expansion of European seed glass bead production, and its uptake in Penang was facilitated by the presence of German trading firms. The result was a coherent "Penang style" in knot-stitch embroidery and beadwork by the early 1900s, characterized by light pastel palettes, European-derived floral and figural motifs (roses, birds, swans, parrots), and the distinctive use of counted thread canvas as a base material.

Primary sources inform the narrative through a combination of trade statistics from the Straits Settlements Blue Books, examination of extant objects in private and museum collections, and oral testimony from elderly Nyonyas and collectors. The article is explicitly framed as raising questions rather than providing a comprehensive survey, and it connects the material evidence to the broader historiography of Peranakan identity as documented by other contributors to the same volume.

### Key Findings

- Gold and silver thread imports into Singapore peaked at nearly $125,000 (Straits dollars) in 1880, with the majority sourced from Hong Kong; by 1890 the value had fallen to $23,000, two-thirds of which came from China and Hong Kong (p. 146).
- Neoh Lian Ee, a Penang-born Nyonya literate in Chinese and the Confucian classics, ran an orphanage at Kampar where girls produced beadwork, silk thread, and metallic thread embroideries for her store, Khoon Giap Co. at 33 Malay Street, Penang (p. 148).
- Counted thread canvas ("chap-jipor") as a beadwork base material appears to have taken hold first in Penang, facilitated by the presence of German firms Huttenbach Brothers and Katz Brothers, and was available through department stores such as Robinson & Co. from the 1880s (p. 150).
- Nyonya netted beadwork (as distinct from bead embroidery on fabric) is found mainly in Penang and West Sumatra (Padang), and the scarcity of unfinished or "learner" pieces suggests commercial production, possibly imported from China for the Peranakan market (pp. 151–152).
- Pictorial "pattern" couching in metallic thread embroidery—arguably the most technically demanding form—is confined to small objects such as purses and slipper faces, and extant examples probably date to the first quarter of the twentieth century or earlier (p. 148).
- A distinguishing feature of Nyonya knot stitch across Penang, Melaka, and Singapore is the unusually small size of the knots, likely resulting from the use of slender needles shared with beadwork; this technique is absent from vintage Malay embroideries (p. 150).

### Conclusion

Cheah concludes that Penang Nyonyas exploited the needle and thread to reinforce their regional identity at the same time that they sustained historical and cultural ties across the Peranakan world. The stylistic divergence between the standardized metallic thread tradition and the locally inflected beadwork and silk embroidery mirrors the broader pattern of Penang's more assertively "Chinese" character and its position within a "northern network" linking it to Medan, Rangoon, and Phuket, as distinct from the older, more established urban culture of Melaka.

## Context

- The study draws on the Straits Settlements Blue Books (1869–1899) for trade data, private collections (Grace Saw, Regina Wong, Datin Theresa Goh), and oral testimony, but does not rely on a single institutional archive; its evidential base is the extant object itself.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the material culture approach to Peranakan identity by demonstrating how specific craft media carried different symbolic loads—standardization versus local differentiation—and by proposing a "northern network" framework for understanding Penang's cultural connections beyond the Melaka–Singapore axis.
