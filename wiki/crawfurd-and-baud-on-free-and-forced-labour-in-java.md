# Crawfurd and Baud on free and forced labour in Java

John Bastin published this brief article in 1956, presenting a 1857–58 correspondence between the British colonial administrator John Crawfurd and the Dutch statesman J.C. Baud in which the two debated the merits of free versus compulsory labour in colonial Java. The exchange centres on Baud's defence of the Dutch *cultuursysteem* (culture system) of 1832 against Crawfurd's liberal critique, and it illuminates the ideological fault lines between British and Dutch colonial economic philosophy in the mid-nineteenth century.

## Summary

The article reproduces two letters that form a pointed intellectual exchange. In September 1857, Crawfurd sent Baud a copy of his *Descriptive Dictionary of the Indian Islands* (1856), in which he had condemned the 1832 introduction of compulsory cultivation in Java as a regression to "forced deliveries" and *corvée* labour that turned the Dutch government into "a cultivator, a trader, and necessarily… a monopolist trader" (p. 195). Baud, who had served as Governor-General of the Indies (1833–6) and Minister of the Colonies (1840–8), replied in October 1857 with a detailed justification of the culture system. His argument rested on the premise that "abstract truths are not always fit for unmitigated application" in the "eastern world" (p. 196). He traced the failure of Raffles's 1813 abolition of forced labour, noting that the free coffee system had collapsed within two years, that the village community structure made individual free disposal of produce impractical, and that the colony's revenue did not cover its expenditure, compounded by the debt incurred during the Diponegoro War. Baud presented the 1832 reform as a rational choice between two alternatives: allow the colony's exports to dwindle and debts to remain unpaid, or introduce "well paid compulsory labour" on a large scale (p. 197).

Crawfurd's reply of 9 January 1858 mounted a counter-argument grounded in the principle that "no labour that is forced can be said to be well-paid" (p. 198). He conceded Baud's revenue figures but challenged their moral and economic validity, then offered Ceylon as a comparative case: British planters had begun investing in Ceylonese coffee only in 1836, yet by the mid-1850s the island produced 300,000 cwts. under entirely free labour conditions, with Tamil workers earning 6d. a day and producing coffee of superior quality to Java's (p. 199). Crawfurd further noted that the Straits Settlements — "three paltry and barren islands" — generated exports of approximately £2,500,000, much of it from Singapore alone, without any compulsory labour system (p. 199).

Bastin's editorial contribution is minimal; he identifies the archival provenance of the letters and provides brief footnotes on historical references (the Preanger and Blandong systems, the Indian Mutiny, the etymology of "guano"). The article functions primarily as a source publication rather than an interpretive study.

### Key Findings

- Baud claimed that under the pre-1832 free-labour regime, annual payments to Javanese cultivators would not have exceeded fl. 1,750,000, whereas under the culture system they averaged fl. 22,000,000, of which "hardly 1/10th part would have been performed" without compulsion (p. 197).
- By 1832, the Javanese coffee crop had reached 300,000 *pikul*, nearly one-third of it from the Priangan (Preanger) regencies; sugar and indigo production remained "insignificant" (p. 196).
- The debt from the Diponegoro War required an annual remittance to the Netherlands "nearly equalling the value of the entire coffee-crop" (p. 196).
- Crawfurd's Ceylon comparison: Ceylon produced 300,000 cwts. of coffee (c. 1856) against Java's 300,000 *pecul*, despite having only 22 years of British investment (from 1836), a less fertile soil requiring imported guano, and a labour force (Tamil workers at 6d. per day) that Crawfurd considered inferior to the Javanese (p. 199).
- Baud cited a 300 per cent increase in Java's exports over the 22 years from 1832 to 1854 (p. 199).
- Crawfurd countered that the Straits Settlements exported approximately £2,500,000 (largely from Singapore, founded less than 40 years earlier) under a regime of "total exemption from all imposts" (p. 199).

### Conclusion

Bastin presents the correspondence as a self-contained historical document in which two senior colonial administrators articulate, with unusual directness, the competing justifications for compulsory and free labour in Southeast Asian colonies. The exchange captures the essential tension of mid-Victorian colonial economic thought: Baud's pragmatic, revenue-driven defence of state-directed cultivation versus Crawfurd's principled insistence that compulsion is inherently incompatible with fair remuneration and long-term productivity.

## Context

- The primary source is the Baud Collectie (Geheim), Algemeen Rijksarchief, The Hague, no. 889 (Crawfurd to Baud, 9 September 1857); Baud's reply of 2 October 1857 is cited from the same collection (p. 195, n. 2–3).
- The article's historiographical value lies in publishing a direct, contemporaneous exchange between two key figures in the British and Dutch colonial administrations of Java, offering unmediated access to the ideological arguments that underpinned the *cultuursysteem* debate before it was reframed by later Dutch and Indonesian historiography.
