# Chewong (Siwang) in perspective

Rodney Needham published this theoretical commentary in 1984 as a companion to Signe Howell's monograph *Society and Cosmos: Chewong of Peninsular Malaysia* (Oxford University Press, 1984). Writing from the standpoint of a comparativist with personal field experience among the Chewong dating to 1955, Needham situates Howell's ethnography within a scholarly lineage descending from the *Année Sociologique* tradition and offers both a commendation and a pointed critique of its analytical framework.

## Summary

The article originated as an extended foreword to Howell's monograph that proved too long and too critical for that purpose; Howell selected a shorter, more conventional preface for her book, and the original text was subsequently published here in its unaltered form (p. 105). Needham's central task is to identify the intellectual "pegs" on which Howell's analysis hangs, tracing them back through Kirk Endicott's *An Analysis of Malay Magic* and R.H. Barnes's *Kedang* to the broader comparative tradition of Levy-Bruhl, van Gennep, Durkheim, and Mauss. He argues that Howell's achievement lies not in the substantive features she documents (the Chewong lack the lineages, politics, and rituals prominent in many ethnographies) but in her capacity to identify and comprehend what is actually present, deploying concepts of opposition, inversion, and metamorphosis to render Chewong collective representations intelligible (pp. 106–107).

A substantial portion of the commentary is devoted to the problem of classification. Needham highlights Howell's finding that Chewong thought emphasises juxtaposition over hierarchical subordination, and her proposal of a mode she terms "nomenclature" — the assignment of a common name to class members who share "nothing more in common than their being named as such" (p. 214, cited at p. 109). He connects this to Cassirer's notion of "appellation" and to Levy-Bruhl's observation that mythical thought "does no more than juxtapose, it does not classify" (p. 109). He also raises the cultural-historical problem posed by the heavy Malay impress on Chewong vocabulary — including terms for central concepts such as *bajo* (cloak/masking) and *naga* (mythical snake) — which implies long-sustained contact inconsistent with the Chewong narrative of recent displacement from the lowlands (pp. 109, 111 n. 9).

Needham's most pointed theoretical intervention targets the "principle of separation" that Howell elevates as a structuring concept in Chewong thought. Drawing on his own earlier work on blood sacrifice and the mockery of animals among the Penan, Ngaju, and Semang, he argues that separation is not an explanation of social order but a precondition of it: without conceptual separation there could be no objects among which relational order obtains, making the principle a tautology rather than an analytical tool (pp. 110–111).

### Key Findings

- Ogilvie's three early articles recorded 1,021 Chewong words and phrases in English and mapped six main settlements, forming a factual base that proved "largely correct" (p. 105).
- The Chewong language contains a high proportion of words identical to or directly derived from Malay, including terms for central cosmological concepts such as *bajo* (cloak), *naga* (mythical snake), *pantang* (rules), and *tankal* (spell/talisman), implying sustained contact far predating the British administration (p. 109; p. 111 n. 9).
- The *maneden* spirit is described as having "only one arm and one leg — if a right arm then a left leg and vice versa," a unilateral figure assignable to a worldwide class of such representations (p. 108).
- Incest (*tanko*) is punished by thunderbolts striking "the hip or knee joints" of offenders, while the *yinlugn* (spiritual aspect of a dead person) is characterised by having "all its joints loose" and moving at night — the reverse of the living world (pp. 108, 110).
- At a ritual, the Chewong officiant blows incense smoke in the four cardinal directions and utters the Arabic-Malay formula "Salaam alaikom, alaikom assalaam, minta maaf" (p. 93, cited at p. 109).
- Howell's Chewong classification is non-hierarchical: "the main emphasis is upon juxtaposition rather than the formation of a pyramid" (p. 216, cited at p. 109).

### Conclusion

Needham's definitive takeaway is that Howell's monograph constitutes a "fitting companion and continuation" in the scholarly line stemming from the *Année Sociologique*, and that its value lies in the deployment of abstract comparative concepts — opposition, inversion, metamorphosis, polythetic classification — to a society that lacks the institutional features (lineages, politics, rituals) on which much ethnographic analysis typically depends. He closes by framing the work as "a lasting memorial to a way of life that is under pressure and already quickly changing" (p. 111).

## Context

- The article draws no new primary archival material; its evidential basis is Howell's monograph itself, supplemented by Needham's own 1955 fieldwork with Chewong informants Beng and Patong and his 1956 publication in JMBRAS 29 (pp. 46–69).
- Historiographically, the piece positions Howell's work within a specific Oxford-based comparative tradition (Endicott, Barnes, Needham) and intervenes in the ongoing debate over the analytical utility of the "principle of separation" in the study of Orang Asli and broader Southeast Asian cosmologies.
