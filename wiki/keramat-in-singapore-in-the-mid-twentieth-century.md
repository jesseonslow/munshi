# Keramat in Singapore in the mid-twentieth century

P.J. Rivers, a former Singapore Police Force officer and merchant navy master who conducted fieldwork in the 1950s, published this survey of *keramat* (sacred or miraculous) sites in Singapore in 2003, drawing on half a century of personal observation, police informants, and official directories to document a religious landscape that was already vanishing. The article's central argument is that the majority of Singapore's *keramat* were "manufactured" or pseudo-sacred sites arising from the syncretic interplay of pre-Islamic Orang Laut animism, Sufi mysticism, and Chinese popular religion, and that rapid urban development has destroyed nearly all of them.

## Summary

Rivers begins by establishing the conceptual framework for *keramat*, tracing the term from its Arabic root (*karamat*, "miracle-working") through its Malay usage, which extends beyond graves to living persons, animals, natural features, and even objects such as cannons. He distinguishes between "true" *keramat*—sites that acquire power independently of the person interred, as Endicott argued—and the far more numerous pseudo-*keramat*, which are essentially "no cure, no pay" arrangements where devotees make offerings in exchange for specific results such as lottery numbers or cures. This distinction is crucial to his account, because it explains how Singapore, in less than two centuries of modern settlement, could generate so many saints: the sites were often accidental, the graves convenient, and the names apocryphal.

The article then traces the ethnic and historical layers that produced these sites. Pre-Islamic beliefs of the Orang Laut (Sea Gypsies) attached sacred significance to rocks, headlands, and whirlpools that predated the British settlement; these were subsequently overlaid with Sufi-derived sanctity when Muslim migrants arrived. Chinese popular religion, particularly the cult of *Datuk Kong* (local tutelary spirits), progressively absorbed or paired with Malay *keramat*, a process Cheu Hong Tong termed "sinicization." Indian Muslims, Hindus, and even Sikhs contributed their own sites, and the boundaries between communities were porous: the Nagore Durgha shrine on Telok Ayer Street, for instance, is simultaneously a Hindu temple and a Malay mosque, while the Keramat Habib Noh on Palmer Road draws devotees of all races. Rivers documents specific cases—the Sikh grave at the General Hospital, the "Keramat Hitam" in an Indian graveyard, the Javanese princess shrine on Pulau Ubin—to illustrate how a single site could pass through multiple religious identifications over time.

The narrative is informed by Rivers' own 1950s fieldwork among Malay police officers and seamen, supplemented by the *Singapore Guide and Street Directory* (8th edn., 1966) and its successor (15th edn., 1988), which mapped and annotated *keramat* sites. A postscript, contributed by his informant's grandson Major Jamil Amin in the 2000s, provides a devastating coda: of the sites Rivers documented, only four remained—Habib Noh, Iskandar Shah, Radin Mas, and the Nagore Durgha—while new pseudo-shrines continued to sprout in response to popular demand.

### Key Findings

- The Keramat Iskandar Shah at Fort Canning was restored in 1990 at a cost of nearly S$150,000, incorporating twenty wooden pillars carved by Malaccan craftsmen; a 1984 excavation by John Miksic uncovered pottery fragments dating to the fourteenth century, raising the possibility that the grave belongs to Sri Tri Buana (c.1299–1347), founder of the Singapore dynasty, rather than to Iskandar Shah (d. 1413) (pp. 104–105).
- The mausoleum of Habib Noh (d. July 1866) was erected c.1890 by Syed Mohammed bin Ahmed Al-Sagoff; the 1980s renovation of both the *makam* and the adjacent Masjid Mohamed Salleh cost S$800,000 (p. 107).
- The Batu Berlayar ("Lot's Wife"), a natural rock pillar venerated by the Orang Laut and known to navigators for "many hundreds of years," was blown up in 1848 as an obstruction to shipping (p. 102).
- The engraved sandstone block found at the mouth of the Singapore River—10 feet high, 2–5 feet thick, 9–10 feet long, bearing approximately 50 lines of illegible inscription—was blasted to pieces by 1835 under Superintendent of Public Works George Coleman to make way for a bungalow (pp. 104–105).
- A 1990 Sabah State Fatwah prohibited the construction of structures at graves and the placement of articles "such as flags, mosquito nets, lamps, umbrellas, milk bottles, pillows and portraits," reflecting official Islamic orthodoxy's campaign against *keramat* practices (p. 99).
- By the 2000s postscript, only four *keramat* sites remained in Singapore (Habib Noh, Iskandar Shah, Radin Mas, Nagore Durgha), while the Keramat Habib Syed Ismail at the eighth milestone, the Keramat Panjang in Ringwood Road, and the Lorong 40 long grave had all been destroyed by development (pp. 115–116).

### Conclusion

Rivers' definitive takeaway is that his account is best read as "The Lost Keramat of Singapore": the phenomenon was overwhelmingly a product of syncretic popular religion in which the power resided in the site rather than the interred person, and which has been almost entirely erased by the island's modernisation. What survives is a small residue of sites that have either been officially recognised, absorbed into the Chinese pantheon of local saints, or maintained by persistent popular devotion despite official prohibition.

## Context

- Rivers' primary evidential base is his own 1950s fieldwork among Malay police and seafaring informants (notably Superintendent Mahmood), cross-referenced with the *Singapore Guide and Street Directory* (1966) and *Singapore Street Directory* (1988), supplemented by a 2000s postscript from Major Jamil Amin.
- The article extends R.O.W. Winstedt's 1924 typology of *karamat* and Cheu Hong Tong's 1998 analysis of sinicisation, applying both frameworks specifically to Singapore and documenting the material fate of individual sites across a fifty-year span.
