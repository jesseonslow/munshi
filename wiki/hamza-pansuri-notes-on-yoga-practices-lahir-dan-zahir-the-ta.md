# Hamza Pansuri: notes on yoga practices, lahir dan zahir, the ‘Taxallos’, punning, a difficult passage in the Kitab al-Muntahi, Hamza’s likely place of birth, and Hamza’s imagery: with appendix

L.F. Brakel's 1979 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 52, No. 235) presents a series of philological and interpretive notes on the 16th-century Acehnese mystic Hamza Pansuri, arguing that the poet should be understood simultaneously as an imitator of Persian Sufi tradition and an innovator within Malay literary culture. The piece functions as prolegomena to a planned critical edition, addressing technical questions that Brakel deemed too specialized for a general monograph.

## Summary

Brakel's central argument is that Hamza occupied a mediating position between the broader Muslim mystical tradition and the specifically Indonesian cultural milieu. The article unfolds across several interconnected threads. First, Brakel reinterprets Hamza's well-known polemics against yogic practices—breath-control, concentration on the fontanel, worship of a divine glow—not as attacks on Sufi "shamanistic virtuosities" (Kraemer's earlier reading) but as targeted critiques of a pranayama ritual still observable among Balinese Brahman priests. By drawing on Weck's ethnographic work and Soebadio's and Worsley's publications on Balinese theological texts, Brakel demonstrates that the specific elements Hamza denounces (localisation of the Divine in the fontanel, externalisation as radiance, breath-control) correspond precisely to the Balinese *pranayama* cycle, implying that Hindu ritual elements survived in 16th-century Aceh and that Allah was worshipped in a manner highly reminiscent of the Siva cult (pp. 73–77).

The remaining sections address questions of poetic craft and textual interpretation. Brakel identifies a systematic phonological play on the word *zahir/lahir* in Hamza's rhyme scheme, arguing that the poet exploited the two distinct realizations of the initial phoneme /z/ in Malay to create a deliberate double-reading within single stanzas (pp. 77–79). He then analyses the *taxallos*—the poet's self-identification in the closing stanza—as both a literary device borrowed from Persian *gazal* tradition and a crucial philological tool for authenticating Hamza's genuine verses against those of his epigones (pp. 79–81). A substantial section on punning reveals how Hamza extended a technique inherited from Arabic and Persian sources (the *Mansur/Nasir* wordplay, the *ʿArafat* pun) to purely Malay homonyms such as *laut/Lāhūt* and *Adamu*, thereby innovating within the tradition (pp. 82–85).

Brakel also offers a new reading of a notoriously difficult passage in the *Kitāb al-Muntahī* involving the Pasai saying on *kufr* and *kufuʿ*, arguing against both Voorhoeve and al-Attas that the passage is self-contained when read in full (rather than truncated as ar-Raniri quoted it) and that "being hidden" is a *conditio sine qua non* for mystical encounter rather than an obstacle to be removed (pp. 85–89). Finally, he situates Hamza's likely birthplace, Barus, within its long history as a cosmopolitan entrepôt known to Greek, Indian, Jewish, Chinese, European, and Western Muslim sources, and identifies the source of Hamza's famous water-cycle imagery in Faxr ad-Din ʿIrāqī's *Lamaʿat*, while insisting that the poet's "internal landscape" of imagery is unmistakably Indonesian (pp. 89–96).

### Key Findings

- Hamza's anti-yogic polemics target the Balinese *pranayama* ritual specifically: breath-control (*menghela nafas kedalam hutak*), localisation of the Divine in the fontanel (*ubun-ubun*), and externalisation as a glow (*diyaʿ/cahaya*), all of which correspond to elements documented in Weck's and Soebadio's accounts of Balinese Brahman practice (pp. 74–76).
- The *taxallos* (poet's self-naming in the final stanza) serves as a reliable criterion for authenticating Hamza's genuine poetry: verses containing it can be safely attributed to him, while those bearing the names Hasan Pansuri or ʿAbduʿl-Jamal and absent from MS ML 82 (Jakarta) are highly suspect (pp. 80–81).
- Hamza's epigones violate a negative structural rule he himself observed: in rhyme-words borrowed from Arabic, the rhyming vowels must retain the same quantity as in the original Arabic, a constraint his imitators consistently fail to observe (p. 80).
- The *Kitāb al-Muntahī* passage on *kufr/kufuʿ* has been misread because ar-Raniri's selective quotation truncated it at "kanzān maxfiyān"; the full passage continues with the seed-and-tree analogy and the *Anaʾl-Ḥaqq* declaration, which Brakel argues makes the meaning self-evident (pp. 86–88).
- Barus is attested as a trading centre from the 2nd century (Ptolemy's "Barusai") through the 16th century (Sīdī ʿAlī Čelebī, 1554), with a Tamil inscription from 1088 (reign of Kuloṭṭuṅga I) discovered at Lobu Tuwa confirming a permanent Tamil colony (pp. 89–92).
- The water-cycle simile in the *Kitāb Sarāb al-ʿĀšiqīn* is an adaptation of a passage in Faxr ad-Din ʿIrāqī's *Lamaʿat* (third *Lamaʿa*, p. 380 in Nafsi's 1956 edition), which Brakel reproduces in transliteration and rough translation (pp. 92–94).

### Conclusion

Brakel's definitive takeaway is that Hamza Pansuri was a literary genius who "almost singlehandedly" mediated between the world of Western Islam and Indonesia, fusing the strange and the familiar to integrate the Indonesian archipelago into the general realm of Muslim mystical poetry "without making it lose its 'rasa', its own taste and colour" (p. 96). He was an imitator when viewed from the perspective of the broader Muslim tradition, but an innovative genius within the field of Indonesian literature.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Jakarta manuscript ML 82 (KBG), which Brakel regards as the most reliable witness to Hamza's poetry, collated against Doorenbos's 1933 edition and al-Attas's 1970 monograph.
- Historiographically, the article positions itself between Kraemer's 1921 Leiden thesis and al-Attas's 1970 monograph, correcting specific interpretive errors in both while laying groundwork for the critical edition Brakel announced as forthcoming.
