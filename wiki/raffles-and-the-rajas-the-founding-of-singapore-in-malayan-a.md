# Raffles and the Rajas: the founding of Singapore in Malayan and British colonial history

C.H. Wake's 1975 article in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* re-examines the founding of Singapore (1819–1824) by situating it within the internal succession politics of the Johor sultanate and demonstrating that the early British presence constituted a genuine condominium with Malay authorities rather than a mere legal technicality. Wake argues that the "Raffles legend" has obscured both the motives of the Malay rajas—who acted to resolve longstanding dynastic conflicts—and the substantive character of shared governance during the Settlement's formative years.

## Summary

Wake opens by identifying a historiographical blind spot: the celebratory colonial tradition that cast Raffles as Singapore's sole founding genius has left unexplored the questions of why the Malay rajas sanctioned a British factory in 1819 and ceded full sovereignty in 1824, and what the British presence actually entailed in the intervening period. The colonial historians treated the fact that Singapore was not British territory before 1824 as a mere technicality, implying that full powers of government were exercised from the outset. Wake contends this view "fails to do justice to the reality of the situation" and contributes to a larger picture in which the autonomous life of Asian communities is obscured (p. 46).

The article then reconstructs the Johor succession dispute that provided the political context for Raffles' mission. From 1722, when a Bugis prince took control of Riau, the sultanate's politics were dominated by the struggle between the Bugis Yamtuan Muda lineage and the Malay Temenggong line for possession of the capital and, by extension, the throne. After Sultan Mahmud's death in January 1812, the Yamtuan Muda engineered the proclamation of Tengku Abdul Rahman at Lingga, but the throne remained vacant in law because Tengku Putri Hamidah refused to release the sacred regalia for his installation. Tengku Husain, the elder son with a competing claim, and Temenggong Abdul Rahman were reduced to a dependent position at Riau, where the Yamtuan Muda's dominance was reinforced by the arrival of a Dutch Resident and garrison in November 1818. It was this impasse that made the British offer attractive: with British backing, Husain and the Temenggong might hope to displace the Dutch from Riau or establish a rival entrepot that would improve Husain's chances of eventual installation as Sultan (pp. 47–59).

Wake then demonstrates that the 1819 agreements created a substantive condominium, not a nominal arrangement. The Preliminary Agreement described the British settlement as a *loji* (factory), the classic form of extra-territorial accommodation in a Malay country, and the subsequent "Arrangements" of June 1819 defined a mixed-jurisdiction zone in which the rajas' followers remained under their own authority, justice was administered jointly, and the Temenggong retained control of land disposal. The rajas exercised genuine political power: they collected taxes, maintained monopolies, received tribute from visiting vessels, and shared revenue farm proceeds (pp. 60–63). The erosion of this arrangement was driven by Raffles upon his return in October 1822, who sought to give Singapore a "constitution" serving British private traders and to overturn the political agreements he had himself negotiated. Through a series of moves—the 1823 proclamation against private monopolies, the commutation of the chiefs' revenue farm share, the delimitation of land reserves, the introduction of a magistracy, and the Convention of 7 June 1823—Raffles progressively stripped the rajas of their governmental functions while preserving the legal fiction of Malay sovereignty. The final cession of 2 August 1824, negotiated by Crawfurd under pressure (including the suspension of the chiefs' allowances for three months), completed the transfer (pp. 64–70).

### Key Findings

- No properly installed Sultan of Johor existed between 1812 and 1823; the throne was vacant in law because Tengku Putri Hamidah withheld the regalia from Tengku Abdul Rahman, who ruled *de facto* at Lingga (pp. 50–51).
- The Treaty of 6 February 1819 stipulated annual payments of Spanish $5,000 to Sultan Husain and $3,000 to the Temenggong for as long as any British factory was maintained (p. 58).
- The Convention of 7 June 1823 fixed the chiefs' allowances at Ct$1,500 (Sp$1,275) per month for the Sultan and Ct$800 (Sp$680) per month for the Temenggong, and explicitly placed the whole of Singapore (except their reserves) at the Company's disposal (pp. 66–67).
- Land reserves were settled in March 1823: the Temenggong received 200 acres between Tanjong Pagar and Tanjong Telok Belanga; the Sultan received 50 acres around his palace at Kampong Glam (p. 66).
- The Treaty of Cession of 2 August 1824 transferred Singapore "in full sovereignty and property" to the Company; the chiefs received a lump sum of $20,000 each and their allowances were made terminable at death (pp. 69–70).
- The 1823 judicial reform transferred the bulk of court work to a bench of magistrates with "Native Captains" responsible for community order, reducing the rajas' role to association in serious cases and appeals (p. 67).

### Conclusion

Wake's definitive historical takeaway is that the founding of Singapore was the product of convergent chains of events originating in the separate worlds of the Malays and the Europeans, not the realization of Raffles' singular vision. The Malay rajas entered into relations with the British to resolve conflicts within their own political system, and their actions "flowed from out of the Malay past and contributed to the ongoing course of political life in their own world" (p. 71). The condominium, though transitory, had genuine substance and reflects the reality of the relationship between ruling authorities in these early years. Even after the 1824 cession, the British did not impose a thoroughly "British" administration on the Asian population; the Chinese in particular maintained an *imperium in imperio* whose autonomy was not seriously challenged until the 1870s (pp. 72–73).

## Context

- Wake draws on Malay-language sources (*Tuhfat al-Nafis*, *Hikayat Abdullah*), Dutch archival publications (van der Kemp, Netscher), and British official correspondence (SSR files at the India Office), enabling a comparative reading of the same events from Malay, Dutch, and British perspectives.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in challenging the Raffles-centric narrative inherited from late Victorian colonial historiography and in demonstrating that the early colonial period in Singapore cannot be understood without reference to the internal dynamics of the Johor sultanate and the autonomous political life of the Asian communities.
