# The keris and other Malay weapons

Published in 1956 within the Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, A.H. Hill’s monograph *The Keris and Other Malay Weapons* provides a definitive historical and technical survey of Southeast Asian armaments. Hill argues that the *keris* originated in Java during the Majapahit era as a distinct weapon rather than an adaptation of the spear, evolving into a complex cultural symbol intertwined with mysticism and social hierarchy. The work synthesizes archaeological evidence, folklore, and metallurgical analysis to document the weapon's physical characteristics and its pervasive role in Malay life from the thirteenth century to the colonial period.

## Summary

Hill establishes that the *keris* emerged as a distinct weapon in Java around the thirteenth or fourteenth century, coinciding with the rise of the Majapahit empire (p. 19). He refutes the theory that it evolved from a spearhead, noting that the earliest *keris majapahit* had an iron hilt coaxial with the blade, making it unsuitable for spear use (p. 22). The weapon’s construction involved complex damascening techniques using meteoric iron and steel, where the *pamur* patterns gained talismanic value based on social rank and manufacturing technique (p. 8–9). Physically, the *keris* is designed for thrusting rather than slashing, with a unique pistol-grip handle and a widened base (*ganja*) that protects the hand during close-quarters combat (p. 15–16).

Beyond its physical form, the monograph details the *keris*’s profound integration into Malay society, serving as a symbol of wedlock, a tool for execution, and a component of royal regalia (p. 48–53). Hill documents various superstitions surrounding the weapon, including beliefs in its ability to draw fire, kill by pointing (*tuju*), or fly through the air to defend its owner (p. 43–44). The text also surveys other Malay armaments, distinguishing between thrusting and throwing spears (*tombak* and *lembing*) and various daggers like the *beladau* and *tumbok lada*, which often shared metallurgical techniques with the *keris* (p. 56–67).

### Key Findings

*   The *keris* originated in Java during the Majapahit period (14th century), evidenced by temple sculptures at Sukuh dated A.D. 1364 (p. 17).
*   Metallurgical analysis by Rosenhaim (1900) revealed that damascening (*pamur*) resulted from imperfect welds between layers of wrought iron and steel rather than intentional alloying (p. 9–10).
*   The weapon was primarily a thrusting dagger held with the blade at a right angle to the handle, utilizing the *ganja* guard to protect the knuckles (p. 15).
*   Social status was encoded in the weapon, with specific *pamur* designs reserved for religious figures, princes, and merchants (p. 8).
*   The *keris* functioned as a legal symbol in marriage customs, where a proxy marriage could be conducted by sending the weapon to the bride’s family (p. 51).
*   Execution was traditionally carried out by the *keris* in a bloodless manner, distinct from the sword of state used for beheading (p. 53).

### Conclusion

Hill concludes that the *keris* is a unique cultural symptom of Indonesian society, evolving from a practical weapon into a sacred object that embodies the totality of the community and its mystical beliefs. Its survival and proliferation across the archipelago were driven by the scattering of Javanese smiths (*pande*) following the fall of Majapahit, allowing the weapon to adapt to local traditions while retaining its core identity as a symbol of power and protection (p. 24–25).

## Context

*   The text references significant colonial-era collections, including the Raffles Museum and the Ashmolean Museum at Oxford, which preserve early *keris* specimens (p. 17, 36).
*   Hill notes that British colonial regulations in the 19th century attempted to ban the wearing of *keris* in Singapore due to street violence, though local rulers successfully lobbied to maintain the custom (p. 49).
