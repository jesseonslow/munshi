# Some old private letters from the Cape, Batavia and Malacca, 1778–1788. {Comp} P.C. Hoynck van Papendrecht

## Abstract

The early literature on Malay animism and magic includes a passing reference to a concept called *maya*. This reference is hardly noticeable in the literature, and when Kirk Endicott wrote his *Malay Magic*, he omitted the word altogether. In this article the author uses ethnographic material from the Malayan-speaking Orang Sakai of Riau to examine the concept of *maya* (image) as it relates to a ‘lifeless soul’ inherent in material objects, giving physical objects vitality of form, appearance and use.

## Summary

This article by Nathan Porath addresses a lacuna in the literature on Malayan animism: the concept of *maya* (image), which appeared only once in the early colonial-era accounts of Malay belief and magic before being entirely omitted by later scholars, most notably Kirk Endicott in his *Malay Magic*. Porath argues that *maya* is not a peripheral or redundant term but a concept of central importance to the Malayan pan-soul-substance world-view, and that its disappearance from the literature has obscured a key dimension of indigenous ontological thought.

Drawing on eighteen months of ethnographic fieldwork with the Orang Sakai of mainland Riau (Sumatra), Porath reconstructs the concept from first-person experiential accounts of shamanic healing, dreaming, and magical practice. The Sakai world-view is dualist and pan-soul-substance: a generic non-physical substance called *Semanget* manifests in three aspects—spirit (*'antu*), embodied *semanget* (which gives humans life-full consciousness), and *mayo* (image), which gives inanimate physical objects their form, appearance, and use without conferring life. *Mayo* is thus a refined *Semanget*-substance that is ontologically real but not animate. The concept operates across multiple registers: in dreams (where one sees the *mayo* of objects and people), in shamanic ritual (where palm-leaf model offerings are made for their *mayo* and subsequently handed to spirits in the non-physical dimension), and in magical practice (where *ilmu batin* works through *mayo* to affect embodied *semanget*).

Porath situates this analysis within the historiography of Malayan animism, tracing how Tylor's animism framework shaped colonial-era descriptions, how Winstedt and Kruyt introduced the "soul-stuff" model, and how Endicott's 1970 re-interpretation—while analytically valuable in connecting *Semangat* as universal principle to embodied *semanget*—discarded *maya* altogether. The article demonstrates that the early references to *maya* in Annandale and Robinson's account of Patani Malay fishermen (who believed boats and houses developed a *maya* soul once their parts were assembled) and in Winstedt's passing citation were not trivial but pointed to a coherent conceptual system that Endicott's pan-psychic concentration model, while structurally similar, fails to capture in its full experiential and ritual specificity.

### Key Findings

- The term *maya/mayo* appears only once in the early literature, in Annandale and Robinson's (1903) account of Patani Malays, where it is noted that a boat or house develops a *maya* soul (*mayor prahu*, *semangat rumah*) once all its parts are fitted together (pp. 6–7).
- Endicott (1970) omitted the term *maya* entirely, substituting *semangat* and framing the phenomenon as degrees of concentration of a universal *Semangat* substance (p. 7).
- Among the Orang Sakai, *mayo* is defined as the refined *Semanget*-substance (*bahan 'alus*) that gives inanimate objects their form (*'upo*) and use (*guno*) but not life-full consciousness; physical matter (*bahan kasa'*) without *mayo* withers and disintegrates (*melayu*) (p. 13).
- In shamanic healing rituals (*dikei* and *kelonkap*), palm-leaf model offerings (boats, yachts, cars, palaces) are made for their *mayo*; the model shimmers yellow at the start of the ritual and wilts over three days as the *mayo* departs, after which the shaman enters trance to hand the *mayo* to his spirit-familiar (pp. 17–18).
- The "healing the house" (*bu'ubet 'uma') scenario in *kelonkap* involves the shaman striking the four cross-beams with a staff and miming hammering in *paku bulano* (Dutchman nails), strengthening the boundaries of the house's *mayo* and symbolically welding the community (pp. 18–19).
- Photographs are understood as capturing a splinter of a person's embodied *semanget* materialized as *mayo* on paper; medicine men use photographs of people they have never met to locate and approach that person's *semanget* in dreams (p. 16).

### Conclusion

Porath's definitive takeaway is that *maya* is not a redundant synonym for *semangat* but a distinct and central concept that specifies the mode in which *Semanget*-substance relates to the inanimate physical world: it gives form and appearance without conferring life-full consciousness. Restoring the term to the literature corrects a distortion introduced by Endicott's pan-psychic concentration model and reveals that Malayan animic ontology is better understood as an interactive (relational) pan-psychism grounded in first-person experiential knowledge, rather than a static substance-distribution theory. The concept also illuminates the logic of ritual practices—such as the "sham offerings" Annandale observed—that earlier scholars could describe but not fully explain.

## Context

- Primary ethnographic data derive from 18 months of participant-observation fieldwork with Orang Sakai communities in Riau, Indonesia (1995–1999), including direct participation in shamanic healing sessions and magical rites (p. 10, n. 26).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in recovering a term that was present in the primary colonial-era sources (Annandale and Robinson 1903; Winstedt 1924) but systematically dropped from the interpretive literature by Endicott (1970), thereby refining the pan-soul-substance model of Malayan animism with a concept that distinguishes the ontological status of inanimate objects from that of living beings.
