# Two communist pamphlets from Kedah and Penang, 1949

H.S. Barlow's 1994 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines two cyclostyled communist propaganda pamphlets produced in Kedah and Penang during the first half of 1949, at the height of the Malayan Emergency. Barlow argues that the documents reveal a fundamental strategic miscalculation by the Malayan Communist Party (MCP) in attempting to recruit Malay police and military personnel through messaging that was unmistakably Chinese-inspired and linguistically deficient, a failure compounded by the party's inability to secure a credible Malay leadership figure.

## Summary

The two pamphlets, preserved in the Barlow family collection, were discovered by an estate manager in Kedah or Province Wellesley and passed through the agency house of Barlow & Co. to Sir John Barlow, a Conservative MP and partner of Thomas Barlow and Bro. in London, who forwarded them to the Colonial Office. They were returned to him on 15 August 1949 with a polite note of thanks. Both documents are physically ephemeral—thin, coarse paper, 25.5 × 35 cm, cyclostyled in red ink on both sides—and were evidently not intended to survive. The first, dated 5 February 1949, is addressed to police and army personnel and signed by the "Central Committee – People's Soldiers against the British – Kedah and Penang." The second, dated 1 June 1949, targets shotgun owners and hunters in the forest, and is issued by the "National Peoples Association for the Freedom of Malaya" (Kasatuan Kabangsaan Ra'ayat Merdeka Melaya). The second document also carries an accurate Chinese translation of the Malay text, or possibly the reverse (p. 61).

Barlow's central analytical point is that the poor quality of the Malay—frequent misspellings, minimal punctuation, and awkward phrasing—indicates the documents were copied from originals by persons unfamiliar with the language, almost certainly Chinese speakers. This linguistic evidence, combined with the content, suggests the MCP was making a serious error of judgement in believing that a call so obviously Chinese-inspired would resonate with a Malay audience whose nationalistic awareness had been sharpened by the Malayan Union controversy of 1946. The options proposed to a Malay soldier or policeman amounted to replacing a geographically distant colonial power with a locally based, non-Malay group with political links to Mao's Communist China (pp. 61–62).

A more nuanced observation concerns the MCP's evolving use of nationalist rhetoric. The shift in signature from the explicitly anti-British "People's Soldiers against the British" in February to the more broadly nationalist "National Peoples Association for the Freedom of Malaya" in June indicates the party had come to recognise the power of nationalism as a political force in post-war Malaya. The second document also touches lightly on Islam, closely linked to nationalist sentiment at the time. However, the British were quick to exploit this same terrain, promising independence once the Emergency was resolved, thereby drawing Malay nationalists into opposition against the Communists (pp. 62–63).

### Key Findings

- The first pamphlet (5 February 1949) is signed by the "Central Committee – People's Soldiers against the British – Kedah and Penang" and targets non-commissioned police and army personnel, urging them to lay down arms or at least withhold ammunition from the MCP (p. 63).
- The second pamphlet (1 June 1949) is issued by the "Kasatuan Kabangsaan Ra'ayat Merdeka Melaya" (National Peoples Association for the Freedom of Malaya) and specifically addresses shotgun owners and forest hunters, warning them not to report MCP movements to the British (pp. 65–66).
- Both documents are cyclostyled in red ink on thin, coarse paper measuring 25.5 × 35 cm, with different cursive roman scripts used between the two, indicating separate production runs (p. 61).
- The second document includes an accurate Chinese translation of the Malay text (or vice versa), confirming the bilingual character of the MCP's propaganda operation in the region (p. 61).
- The Malay in both documents contains frequent misspellings and minimal punctuation, leading Barlow to conclude they were copied from originals by persons unfamiliar with Malay, almost certainly Chinese speakers (p. 61).
- The documents were returned to Sir John Barlow by the Colonial Office on 15 August 1949, with a polite note of thanks, after he forwarded them from his position as a partner of Thomas Barlow and Bro. in London (p. 61).

### Conclusion

Barlow's definitive takeaway is that the MCP's failure to recruit a credible and prominent Malay leader to head its movement ultimately ensured the failure of its cause among the Malay population. The pamphlets serve as concrete evidence of a party that recognised the strategic value of nationalism but lacked the organisational depth and linguistic competence to deploy it effectively across racial lines, a gap the British colonial administration was quick to exploit through its promise of post-Emergency independence.

## Context

- The primary sources are two physical pamphlets held in the Barlow family collection, transcribed and translated in the appendices with assistance from Malay and Chinese language specialists (p. 62).
- The article contributes to the historiography of MCP propaganda strategy during the Emergency by providing rare primary evidence of the party's attempts to court Malay military and civilian audiences, and by documenting the linguistic and organisational limitations that undermined those efforts.
