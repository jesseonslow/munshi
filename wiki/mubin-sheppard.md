# Mubin Sheppard

Tan Sri Dato' Dr Haji Mubin Sheppard (1905–1994) was a British-born Malaysian civil servant, historian, and cultural conservator whose career spanned the final decades of colonial Malaya and the first four decades of independent Malaysia. He served as British Adviser to Negeri Sembilan, founded the Malayan Historical Society, directed the National Museum, and established Badan Warisan Malaysia, the country's first heritage conservation body. His published works on Malay history, architecture, and performing arts, together with his hands-on rescue of historic buildings, made him the single most influential figure in the institutionalisation of Malaysian cultural heritage. [[1, pp. 47–91]](#ref-1)

## Early Career and Administrative Postings

Sheppard arrived in Malaya in the late 1930s and entered the colonial civil service, a position from which he would shape cultural policy for more than five decades. His earliest surviving publication in the Society's journal dates to 1938, a study of the Trengganu *rodah* (a traditional Malay dance-drama form), indicating that his scholarly interests in Malay performing arts predated his later administrative prominence. [[2, p. 60]](#ref-2)

In the post-war period, Sheppard held the office of British Adviser to Negeri Sembilan, a role that placed him at the intersection of colonial governance and state-level cultural affairs. It was in this capacity that he undertook his first major conservation intervention: the rescue of the Ampang Tinggi Palace, a *chengal* (ironwood) timber structure built in 1865 without metal nails. The palace was dismantled in January 1953 and re-erected at Seremban by March 1954; General Sir Gerald Templer officially opened the building as the Negeri Sembilan State Museum on 20 May 1954. [[1, pp. 53–55]](#ref-1)

During the same period, Sheppard was instrumental in founding two institutions that would become central to Malaysian cultural life. The Arts Council, Federation of Malaya was established in 1952, and the Malayan Historical Society (MHS) followed in 1953. The MHS in particular served as the operational vehicle for subsequent building rescues, providing both the organisational framework and the membership base from which funding was drawn. [[1, pp. 47–91]](#ref-1)

## Scholarly Output and Cultural Documentation

Sheppard's published corpus is vast and spans virtually every dimension of Malay and Malaysian history, material culture, and performing arts. H.S. Barlow's 1995 bibliography, compiled with the assistance of Eli Solomon and Eric Jennings, catalogues his works across books, journal articles, newspaper contributions, and anonymous introductions to ceremonial programmes. [[2, pp. 59–66]](#ref-2)

His monographs include *The Malay Regiment, 1933–1947* (1947), *The Adventures of Hang Tuah* (1949), *A Short History of Malaya* (1953), *Malay Courtesy* (1956), *Historic Malaya* (1956), *A Short Biography of Sultan Abu Bakar of Pahang* (1957), *The Magic Kite and Other Ma'Yong Stories* (1960), *Malayan Forts* (1961), *A Short History of Negri Sembilan* (1965), *Tun Perak: Malacca's Greatest Bendahara* (1969), *Taman Indera: Malay Decorative Arts and Pastimes* (1972), *Living Crafts of Malaysia* (1978), and *Taman Budiman: Memoirs of an Unorthodox Civil Servant* (1979). [[2, pp. 59–60]](#ref-2)

His later works include *Taman Saujana: Dance, Drama, Music and Magic in Malaya Long and Not-so-Long Ago* (1983), two volumes of *Tunku: A Pictorial Biography* (1984, 1987), *Klang, Twenty Centuries of Eventful Existence* (1986), and the authorised biography *Tunku: His Life and Times* (1995), published posthumously. [[2, p. 60]](#ref-2)

In the Society's journal, Sheppard contributed articles on the Trengganu *rodah* (1938), a short history of Trengganu (1949), *pa'dogol* and *wa'long* (1965), *joget gamelan* in Trengganu (1967), the Khmer shadow play and its links with ancient India (1968), traditional Malay house forms in Trengganu and Kelantan (1969), *manora* in Kelantan (1973), and Malay woodcarving and the new pulpit in the National Mosque (1994). [[2, p. 60]](#ref-2)

His contributions to the *Federation Museums Journal* addressed megaliths in Malacca and Negeri Sembilan (1962), four historic Malay timber buildings (1962), *bangau* decorative guards for sails and spars on Malay fishing boats (1963), Malay shadow play figures in the Cambridge collection (1963), a wedding ceremony at Bertundan (1964), leaf shadow puppets (1964), a recording of the *ma'yong* dance-drama of Kelantan (1967), and the sung text of "Dewa Muda" (1969). [[2, p. 61]](#ref-2)

The *Malaya/Malaysia in History* series, which Sheppard edited, contains an extraordinary range of articles covering the history of rubber, Malay weapons, the *kris*, fifteenth-century Malacca, the *nobat* (traditional Malay royal orchestra), tin mining, Hindu-Buddhist civilisation in South Kedah, the Malayan police, the Malayan Railways, Malay rulers of Malacca, football in Kuala Lumpur, Malay palaces, the *tengkolok* (traditional Malay head-dress), Hang Tuah, the bombing of the Selangor Museum, processional birds of Kelantan, the Penang *boria* (a form of Malay military music), the Royal Malayan Navy and Air Force, the Trengganu Stone, the genealogical tree of the royal house of Perak, the Ubudiah Mosque at Kuala Kangsar, the Duff Development Company in Kelantan, and the name "Kuala Lumpur." [[2, pp. 61–63]](#ref-2)

Barlow noted that a large number of Sheppard's writings took the form of anonymous introductions to programmes, for example royal weddings, in which he drew on his unrivalled knowledge of the history and customs of the state concerned, and that he also often contributed anonymous articles to newspapers or wrote under pseudonyms. [[2, p. 59]](#ref-2)

## Architectural Conservation and Institutional Building

Sheppard's conservation work extended well beyond the Ampang Tinggi Palace. The Kampong Laut Mosque in Kelantan, a timber post-and-beam structure approximately 16 metres by 16 metres, was dismantled on 3 November 1968 and re-erected at Nilam Puri. Only 20 per cent of the original materials could be reused, and the project cost an estimated RM16,500, funded largely by MHS members rather than government allocation. [[1, pp. 58–64]](#ref-1)

The Tengku Nik Palace (*Rumah Tele*) in Terengganu, built in 1888 following the 1882 fire that destroyed over 1,600 houses in Kuala Terengganu, was purchased for RM7,000 and re-erected on 29 March 1970 at a total additional cost of RM8,000, funded entirely by Eastern Mining and Metals Company Limited. It was gazetted as a monument under the Antiquities and Treasure Trove Ordinance 1957 on 29 May 1971. [[1, pp. 65–78]](#ref-1)

The National Museum building in Kuala Lumpur, designed by architect Ho Kok Hoe with Sheppard's conceptual input drawing on the *Sejarah Melayu* description of Sultan Mansur Shah's Malacca Palace, was officially opened on 31 August 1963 by the Yang Dipertuan Agong. Sheppard served as the first Director-General of the National Museum from 1958 to 1963. [[1, pp. 57–60]](#ref-1)

In 1981, Sheppard presented a paper titled "Persatuan Warisan Perlu diadakan di Malaysia" at the national seminar "Towards National Identity in Architecture," in which he explicitly called for the establishment of a non-governmental Heritage Society of Malaysia and cited Istana Tengku Long (c. 1850) and Gedung Raja Abdullah as buildings under immediate threat. [[1, pp. 79–80]](#ref-1)

Badan Warisan Malaysia (BWM) was incorporated as a company on 14 January 1983, with Sheppard as honorary secretary. The subsequent restoration of Gedung Raja Abdullah in Klang, a structure built in 1863, was completed on 27 January 1992. [[1, pp. 80–82]](#ref-1)

Zuraini Md Ali's study highlights the tension between top-down government action and grassroots community sentiment, most visibly in the Kampung Laut villagers' protest against relocating their mosque away from the original site—a dimension that Sheppard's own publications omitted. [[1, pp. 47–91]](#ref-1)

## Legacy and Documentation

Sheppard's significance to Malaysian historiography and conservation is attested by the depth and breadth of the archival record surrounding his career. Zuraini Md Ali's 2010 article draws extensively on Sheppard's personal correspondence, MHS meeting minutes, and archival letters held in the Malaysia National Archives, supplemented by oral testimony from Sheppard's daughter Lavender Buckland, his biographer John Gullick, and BWM founder Waveney Jenkins. [[1, pp. 47–91]](#ref-1)

Gullick's obituary, published in the Society's journal in 1995, provides a contemporary account of Sheppard's life and work. [[3, pp. 1–6]](#ref-3) The establishment of the Sheppard Memorial Prize for historical essays, administered by the Malaysian Historical Society, ensures that his scholarly legacy continues to shape the field; prize-winning essays have been published in the Society's journal, including a 1999 collection edited by Beatrice Chong and Wong Siew Kuon. [[4, p. 109]](#ref-4)

Historiographically, Ali's reconstruction challenges the prevailing narrative that building conservation in Malaysia began with Hajeedar Abdul Malek's 1976 articles in *Majalah Akitek*, demonstrating instead that institutional and practical conservation work was already underway in the early 1950s and was driven primarily by Sheppard's sustained, multi-decade commitment. Earlier scholars such as Chen Voon Fee and Amer Hamzah had treated Malaysian conservation history as beginning in the 1970s; Ali's work pushes the effective starting point back by two decades and centres an individual biography as the organising framework for what had previously been treated as a purely technical or policy-driven narrative. [[1, pp. 47–91]](#ref-1)

Sheppard's own memoir, *Taman Budiman: Memoirs of an Unorthodox Civil Servant* (1979), provides a first-person account of his career, while the posthumous authorised biography of Tunku Abdul Rahman, published in 1995, testifies to the depth of his relationship with Malaysia's founding father. [[2, pp. 59–60]](#ref-2)
