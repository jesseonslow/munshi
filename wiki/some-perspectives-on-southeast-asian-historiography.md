# Some perspectives on Southeast Asian historiography

Nicholas Tarling, a leading British historian of Southeast Asia and imperial history, delivered this reflective essay to a Cambridge seminar on international history in 1995, offering a retrospective on four decades of Southeast Asian historiography and its intersections with imperial and international history. His overarching argument is that the field's central controversies—decolonisation, national historiography, and the tension between global and regional perspectives—remain unresolved and mutually illuminating across all three domains.

## Summary

Tarling structures his reflections around three perspectives: Southeast Asian historiography in its own right, imperial history, and international history. He begins by acknowledging the region's well-known source problems—limited written records, the need to draw on epigraphical, oral, mythical, and comparative materials—and notes how historians from J.C. van Leur to the Andayas have turned these constraints into methodological opportunities. The attitudinal problems he identifies are as significant as the practical ones: what counts as acceptable evidence, how to handle bias in colonial and dynastic sources, and whether history-writing itself needed decolonising in the 1950s and 1960s. He argues that the decolonisation controversy, while productive in inviting openness, has largely been superseded by a newer and more troubling trend: the advocacy by some Filipino and Thai historians of a strictly "national" history, writable only by nationals in the national language, which he sees as a reaction to globalisation that risks damaging both national unity and historiographical rigour (pp. 53–54).

The second perspective traces Tarling's own career from a study of British policy in the Malay peninsula and archipelago (1824–1871) through his work on piracy, the sultanate of Brunei, and postwar Burma, to his engagement with the inter-war and postwar periods. He describes how reading British documents "against the grain" gradually shifted his work toward non-European viewpoints, and how the opening of archives under Wilsonian policy enabled study of the full imperial arc. He emphasises that studying Britain's flexible approaches in Southeast Asia—colonies, protectorates, relations with rival colonial powers, and engagement with Siam—illuminates British policy elsewhere, including the influence of Singapore's fall in 1942 on Margery Perham's thinking about Africa (pp. 54–55).

The third perspective addresses international history directly. Tarling argues that Southeast Asian independence movements cannot be explained solely by internal dynamics: Thai political independence depended on international opportunities, and Indonesian success relied on leaders who were better at mobilising international opinion than their Dutch opponents. He also raises the concept of "intervention" as a practice whose justification shifted dramatically between the imperial and post-imperial worlds, and notes the problematic nature of the very term "Southeast Asia," which entered common usage only with Mountbatten's Allied command in 1943 (pp. 55–56).

### Key Findings

- The term "Southeast Asia" entered common usage only during the Second World War with the establishment of Mountbatten's Allied command in 1943; prior designations included "Further India," "the Indies," "the Nanyang," and "the Nanyo" (p. 56).
- Tarling identifies a new historiographical controversy in which some Filipino and Thai historians advocate that national history can be written only by nationals in the national language, a position he distinguishes from the earlier decolonisation debate of the 1950s–60s (pp. 53–54).
- The fall of Singapore in 1942 influenced Margery Perham's thinking about Africa and the emerging concept of "partnership" in British imperial policy (p. 55).
- European empires generally preferred the threat of force over its use, and relied on naval power and forces drawn from minorities or other parts of the empire rather than from the majority population, which was seen as a source of risk (p. 56).
- Tarling's own trajectory moved from a study of British policy in the Malay peninsula and archipelago (1824–1871) through piracy, Brunei, and postwar Burma to the inter-war and postwar periods, with each phase deepening his engagement with non-European perspectives (pp. 54–55).
- The founding of Southeast Asian historiography in the UK is traced to a lineage of British officials who were also scholars, from Arthur Phayre and William Marsden to J.S. Furnivall, Frank Swettenham, and Richard Winstedt, with the postwar gap filled by D.G.E. Hall, Victor Purcell, and Hugh Tinker (pp. 56–57).

### Conclusion

Tarling's definitive takeaway is that the boundaries between Southeast Asian, imperial, and international historiography are more permeable than institutional categories suggest, and that each field gains explanatory power from the others. The imperial phase in particular offers a comparative lens for understanding both the region's own historical development and the broader evolution of the international system from a world of empires to a world of states.

## Context

- This is a modern scholarly reflection by a senior academic historian, not a colonial-era text. Tarling's own career bridged officialdom and academe (he served in the British colonial administration before becoming a university historian), which he acknowledges as a formative influence on his perspective (pp. 56–57).
- The article draws on no single archival collection but synthesises decades of published scholarship across Southeast Asian, imperial, and international history, positioning itself as a historiographical overview rather than a primary-source study.
