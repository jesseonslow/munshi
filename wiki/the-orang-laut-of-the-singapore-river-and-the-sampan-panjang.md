# The Orang Laut of the Singapore River and the sampan panjang

C. A. Gibson-Hill (1952) examines the Orang Laut community that inhabited the Singapore River from Raffles's landing in 1819 until their dispersal in the early 1840s, and traces the development of the Sampan Panjang—a racing and passenger boat form that emerged from their boat-building traditions and was ultimately rendered obsolete by the transition from sail to steam.

## Summary

The article addresses two interrelated historical problems: the demographic and spatial composition of the original inhabitants of Singapore at the time of British founding, and the technological evolution of a distinctive local boat form that has left no surviving hulls. Gibson-Hill reconciles conflicting early accounts—Wa Hakim's oral testimony, Munshi 'Abdu'llah's written narrative, the Temenggong's own figures, and Findlayson's observations—to reconstruct a plausible picture of the river community's size and social structure. He argues that the Orang Laut were not merely passive inhabitants but active economic agents whose boat-building expertise, stimulated by European demand for aquatic sports and ferry services, produced the Sampan Panjang as a vessel of exceptional performance.

The second half of the article traces the Sampan Panjang's development from a modest improvement on the standard Orang Laut dwelling-boat through its emergence as a racing craft in the 1830s, its refinement in the 1860s–1880s, and its final obsolescence. Gibson-Hill draws on Earl's detailed descriptions, newspaper race reports, Davidson's observations, Berncastle's account, and a first-person narrative from the *Singapore Free Press* (1885) to document the boat's dimensions, rig, crew requirements, and racing performance. He identifies the construction of the Tanjong Pagar wharves and the opening of the Suez Canal as the structural factors that eliminated the boat's commercial function, after which it survived only as a racing craft for wealthy Malay patrons before disappearing entirely by the turn of the century.

### Key Findings

- The Singapore River community numbered approximately 450 persons (including the Batin) at the time of their dispersal to Tanjong Rhu in 1842–43; their descendants remained there until Japanese air attacks in December 1942 drove them out, and they are now settled at Kampong Kuchan, Geylang (pp. 162–163).
- The standard Orang Laut dwelling-boat was 22–25 ft long, 6–7 ft beam, with about 30 inches internal depth, built on a keel-less dug-out base; the Sampan Panjang by the 1830s reached 30 ft length with 4 ft beam and two masts (pp. 164–165).
- By the 1880s, racing Sampan Panjang hulls exceeded 40 ft in length with some stepping three masts; the *Kilat Barat* (1885) measured 47 ft by 6 ft 10 inches beam, drew 2 ft in racing trim, and carried a crew of 23 (pp. 169, 171).
- In the 1885 New Year's Day regatta, only two Sampan Panjang were entered for their class, compared with 37 twenty years earlier; by 1902 only four passenger sampans remained in commission, and none survived into World War I (pp. 169–170).
- The first wharf at Tanjong Pagar opened in 1866 (completed 1867), the New Dock was finished in 1879, and the Suez Canal opened in 1869—these developments collectively eliminated the Sampan Panjang's commercial ferry function (p. 169).
- The Sultan of Lingga's Sampan Panjang, built in Trengganu, defeated the European yacht *Maggie Lauder* in an 1849 race over a 14-mile course, arriving with the yacht still seven miles from the finish (p. 166).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive takeaway is that the Orang Laut of the Singapore River and the Sampan Panjang represent a lost chapter of Singapore's maritime history that deserves recognition. The community was not merely a nuisance to be cleared but a source of genuine nautical innovation, and the Sampan Panjang—whose midship section closely resembled that of modern yachts—was a vessel of remarkable design achieved through rule-of-thumb methods. Both the people and the boat have been effectively erased from the historical record, with no surviving hulls and no adequate public acknowledgement of their brief but significant existence (pp. 170–171).

## Context

- The article draws on a range of primary sources including the 1882 published testimony of Wa Hakim, Earl's *The Eastern Seas* (1837), Davidson's *Travel and Trade in the Far East* (1846), Berncastle's *A Voyage to China* (1850), and the *Singapore Free Press* (1885), supplemented by models in the Raffles Museum collection and the U.S. National Museum watercraft collection (Mitman, 1923).
- Historiographically, the article fills a gap left by Logan's 1847 ethnological surveys, which made no mention of the Singapore River Orang Laut by name, and provides the last substantial published account of the Sampan Panjang before the form's complete disappearance.
