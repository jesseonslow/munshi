# A botanical excursion to Gunong Jerai (Kedah Peak)

H. N. Ridley, then Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, published this account in 1900 of a botanical expedition to Gunong Jerai (Kedah Peak) undertaken in June 1893, providing the first published description of the mountain's ascent and flora. The article functions simultaneously as a practical guide for future ascents and as a contribution to the botanical knowledge of the northern Malay Peninsula, documenting numerous species new to science or new to the region.

## Summary

Ridley's narrative traces the journey from Penang to the village of Yan at the mountain's base, through the lower jungle, and up to the summit, interspersing practical travel advice with detailed botanical observations. The account is notable for its comparative framework: Ridley repeatedly benchmarks the flora of Gunong Jerai against that of Mount Ophir (Kedah's other major peak) and the Lankawi islands visible from the summit, arguing that the mountain's vegetation is "typically Malayan" and bears no trace of the distinct northern flora found on Lankawi, which he notes has a closer relation to Tenasserim (p. 30). This comparative approach serves a broader taxonomic purpose, helping to delineate the biogeographic boundaries of the peninsula's mountain flora.

The article also records the human and economic landscape of the Yan valley, including the village's dependence on durian cultivation, timber cutting, and charcoal production, as well as the Siamese-influenced administrative structures under which the local Penghulu Rajah operated. Ridley notes the use of stocks for punishment, the absence of European medicine, and the prevalence of injuries from falling durian fruit, situating the botanical work within a colonial context of limited medical and administrative infrastructure (p. 26).

The upper montane zone, beginning at approximately 2,000 feet, receives the most sustained botanical attention. Ridley documents a sudden shift in vegetation character—smaller, more slender trees, dense orchid and fern cover, and open grassy patches—and records the presence of conifers (*Dammara orientalis*, *Dacrydium elatum*, *Podocarpus cupressinus*) and a striking white *Rhododendron* of about twelve feet that he considered "well worthy of cultivation" but from which he could obtain neither seeds nor young plants (p. 28).

### Key Findings

- The summit altitude was measured at 3,495 feet by aneroid barometer, slightly below the commonly cited figure of "a little over 4,000 feet" (p. 29).
- Upwards of fifty orchid species were collected, of which eight were peculiar to the range; *Bulbophyllum hispidum* was found only at the very summit (p. 29).
- Three new species were described or named: *Xyris Ridleyi* (from grassy patches at mid-elevation), *Hedychium collinum* (white-flowered, near the summit), and *Utricularia involvens* (a yellow-flowered bladderwort from damp streamside grasses) (pp. 27–29).
- *Aneilema giganteum* was recorded as hitherto unknown from the Malay Peninsula, though of wide distribution across Africa, India, Ceylon, the Malay islands, China, and Australia (p. 28).
- The ascent from Yan takes 3½ to 4 hours on foot; the track becomes very slippery after rain (p. 30).
- The flora of Gunong Jerai most closely resembles that of Mount Ophir and is "very different from that of the Main Perak hills," while the Lankawi islands' flora is distinct from any part of the peninsula (p. 30).

### Conclusion

Ridley's definitive takeaway is biogeographic: Gunong Jerai's montane flora is a variant of the Mount Ophir assemblage rather than a northern or Lankawi-influenced one, confirming that the Lankawi islands represent a distinct biogeographic entity with affinities to Tenasserim rather than to the mainland peninsula. The article thus contributes to the emerging understanding of the Malay Peninsula's floral provinces as discrete units.

## Context

- Ridley held the position of Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, and the expedition was conducted under the auspices of colonial botanical administration; the article's framing as a "useful" guide for future ascents reflects the practical, service-oriented purpose of the Gardens' field work (p. 23).
- The account draws on Ridley's own field observations and collections from June 1893, supplemented by his prior knowledge of Mount Ophir and the Lankawi islands (the latter explored by his colleague Mr. Curtis), making it a synthesis of personal fieldwork and comparative botanical experience rather than a study based on archival or herbarium sources.
