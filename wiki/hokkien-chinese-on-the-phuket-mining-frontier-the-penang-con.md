# Hokkien Chinese on the Phuket mining frontier: the Penang connection and the emergence of the Phuket Baba community

Khoo Salma Nasution's 2009 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* traces the economic, social, and cultural entanglement between Penang and Phuket from the early nineteenth century through the 1930s, arguing that the sustained flow of people, goods, capital, and ritual practice between the two islands was the defining force in the formation of a distinct Hokkien Peranakan (Baba) community in Phuket.

## Summary

The article uses the Tan and Khaw family business networks as its primary analytical lens to examine how Penang functioned as far more than a commercial entrepot for Phuket tin. It was a cultural reference point that supplied provisions, credit, religious intermediaries, educational institutions, architects, and marriage partners, all of which shaped the identity of the Hokkien Chinese in Phuket. The author demonstrates that the "Phuket Baba" community did not emerge from a single origin event but from multi-generational, bidirectional exchanges that distinguished Phuket's Hokkien population from other Chinese communities in Siam.

The narrative addresses the historical problem of how a frontier mining settlement, repeatedly ravaged by war, epidemic, and secret society violence, consolidated into a confident urban society with its own Peranakan culture. The article traces this trajectory from the early Chinese mining frontier under Siamese governors, through the Kian Teik–Ghee Hin wars and the 1879 massacre, to the administrative modernization under Khaw Sim Bee, and finally to the consolidation of a Phuket Baba identity through Chinese-language education, Penang-style marriage customs, and urban development modelled directly on George Town. The author draws on Siamese administrative records, British consular and diplomatic reports, Chinese-language temple inscriptions and couplets, family memoirs, and oral history interviews with descendants of old Phuket families to reconstruct this history from multiple vantage points.

A central argument is that the Penang connection was the structural backbone of Phuket's Hokkien society—its commercial lifeline, its cultural mirror, and its social reference point. The eventual severance of this relationship in the mid-twentieth century, through the loss of Penang's free port status, the establishment of Thailand's state smelting monopoly, and the end of direct migration from China, marked the close of a distinct historical chapter in the region's Peranakan formation.

### Key Findings

- The 1879 massacre at Tongkah killed 417 Ghee Hin members; their memorial shrine, Teong Yong Ssu, was built in Kathu in 1888 (signboard dated 1895) (p. 88).
- Captain Trail's 1885 survey recorded approximately 45,000 Chinese to 1,000 Siamese in Phuket; by the 1897 census the population had dwindled to 26,538 (15,188 Siamese, 11,350 Chinese) following the harsh administration of Phraya Thipkosa (pp. 91–92).
- King Rama V's 1890 visit recorded 318 brick shophouses and 367 wooden shophouses in Tongkah, with 2,767 people living along the principal street (p. 91).
- Tongkah Harbour Tin Dredging Co., floated in 1906 and operational by 1907, was the world's first tin dredge, the first offshore dredge, and the first dredge to operate in Asia (p. 97).
- At its height, the three Chinese schools in Phuket (Hua Boon, Seong Teik, Yok Eng) enrolled a combined 2,000 students; Hua Boon alone enrolled over 1,000. The 1934 rebuilding of Hua Boon cost 50,000 baht (pp. 105–106).
- Tan Mah Siang's Penang-style mansion at 98 Krabi Road, completed in 1907, cost 500,000 baht and incorporated materials imported from the Netherlands, Italy, France, Britain, Germany, and the United States (p. 102).

### Conclusion

Nasution's definitive historical takeaway is that the Phuket Baba community was inseparable from the Penang–Phuket relationship: it was a trans-boundary Peranakan formation whose identity, urban landscape, educational institutions, and social hierarchies were all calibrated against Penang as the standard. The article closes by urging further study of this potentially rich area of trans-boundary history, positioning the Phuket case as a necessary complement to the better-documented Peranakan communities of Penang and Singapore.

## Context

- The study draws on Siamese administrative records, British consular reports (Kynnersley 1901; Smyth 1894/1994), Chinese temple inscriptions, the unpublished memoir of Pracha Tandavanitj (1998), and a series of oral history interviews conducted in Phuket and Penang in 2005 and 2007 (p. 112).
- Historiographically, the article extends the Penang-centric Peranakan literature by incorporating Phuket into the regional Peranakan network, building on the foundational work of Songprasert (1986) on Chinese capital in southern Siam and Cushman (1991) on the Khaw family, while introducing a cultural and community-formation perspective that those earlier economic histories did not fully address.
