# Raja Bot bin Raja Jumaat

Mohamed Amin Hassan's 1967 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* reconstructs the life of Raja Bot bin Raja Jumaat (1847–1916), the ruler of Lukut who lost his territory to British-mediated boundary settlements and subsequently became one of the longest-serving members of the Selangor State Council. Drawing heavily on the Selangor Secretariat Files, State Council Minutes, and colonial correspondence, Hassan argues that Raja Bot's trajectory exemplifies the fate of a pre-Residential System Malay chief who, after being dispossessed of his domain, adapted to the new colonial administrative order but was ultimately undone by financial mismanagement.

## Summary

Hassan begins by resolving a genealogical puzzle surrounding Raja Bot's parentage. A letter published in the *Malay Mail* in 1902 identified Raja Bot's father as Raja Senai, son of Sultan Muhammad of Selangor, but official records consistently gave his name as "bin Raja Jumaat." Hassan demonstrates that Raja Senai was in fact a female — the wife of Raja Jumaat of Lukut — and that the confusion arose from a mistranslation of the Malay phrase "anak kapada" (p. 68). This correction establishes Raja Jumaat, a nephew of the Selangor royal house, as the true father, and sets the stage for understanding the political and economic context of Lukut under his rule.

The article then traces the rise and fall of Lukut as a tin-mining district. Under Raja Jumaat, appointed *wakil* by Sultan Muhammad in 1846, Lukut flourished: it developed a uniformed police force, a fortified hilltop, macadamized roads, and a prosperous Chinese trading community that generated revenues of up to $10,000 per month (pp. 70–72). Raja Jumaat's death in 1864 marked the beginning of decline. Raja Bot, though experienced in his father's business affairs, proved less effective at maintaining the allegiance of his subjects. A series of violent incidents — including a rebellion by Raja Sulaiman of Sungai Raya, a fatal confrontation with Enche' Yahya of Langat, and the burning of the Chinese bazaar by a rival claimant named Mamat — drove the Chinese mining population away and reduced Lukut's revenue to between $200 and $300 per month (pp. 73–75). Hassan attributes the collapse to a combination of exhausted tin deposits, the rapid silting of Sungai Lukut, and Raja Bot's comparatively weak governance.

The central political drama of the article concerns the "Lukut Affair" of 1877–1880. After the introduction of the Residential System, the British sought to settle the long-standing boundary dispute between Selangor and Sungai Ujong (Sungai Linggi). The 15 February 1878 agreement, negotiated without Raja Bot's consent, assigned Lukut and Sungai Raya to Sungai Ujong. Raja Bot contested the transfer on the basis of a *kuasa* (grant) issued by Sultan Muhammad to his father in 1841, which contained the phrase "for ever" and stated that "our Heirs and successors are not to claim it hereafter" (p. 80). The dispute over whether this grant was revocable or hereditary consumed years of correspondence between Raja Bot, the Selangor Resident, the Governor of the Straits Settlements, and the Colonial Office. It was finally resolved by a Convention in Singapore on 31 July 1880, which awarded Raja Bot $14,000 in cash, a coastal land grant of up to 3,000 acres, and $3,000 for his existing premises — a total of $17,000, or $20,000 by some accounts (pp. 81–82).

After losing Lukut, Raja Bot entered Selangor's colonial administration. Appointed Penghulu of Sungai Buloh in 1887 and a member of the State Council in December 1888, he served on the Council for twenty-seven years until his death, attending approximately 82% of its 186 sittings (p. 83). Hassan documents his contributions to legislation on imports and exports, railway regulations, port health, compulsory Malay vernacular school attendance, and Muslim law, as well as his role as a trusted courtier to Sultan Suleiman, for whom he arranged the installation ceremony and the marriage to Tunku Maharum (pp. 83–89). Yet his personal finances deteriorated steadily. Despite the Lukut settlement, he was repeatedly in debt by the late 1880s, mortgaging land to chettiars and seeking government loans for unsuccessful ventures in sago, pepper, mining, and cattle farming. He died in Johore Bahru on 11 April 1916, insolvent, with a $9,000 government loan taken out to settle his debts (pp. 89–93).

### Key Findings

- Lukut's revenue under Raja Jumaat reached approximately $10,000 per month; under Raja Bot it fell to $200–$300 per month, and the Chinese population departed almost entirely (pp. 74–75).
- The 15 February 1878 boundary agreement assigned Lukut and Sungai Raya to Sungai Ujong; Raja Bot was not consulted and contested the transfer through a Singapore lawyer (pp. 79–80).
- The 31 July 1880 Convention awarded Raja Bot $14,000 in cash, a coastal land grant of up to 3,000 acres, and $3,000 for his existing houses and premises, totalling $17,000 (or $20,000 per two other sources) (pp. 81–82).
- Raja Bot served on the Selangor State Council from 18 December 1888 until his death in 1916, attending 141 of 186 meetings (approximately 76%); he was elected Council spokesman in May 1889 (pp. 83–84).
- By 1896 Raja Bot held land in Bukit Rajah Nukim (25 acres, valued at $400), Klang Mukim (3 acres 36 poles, valued at $1,000), and a coffee plantation registered under his wife's name; all but the last were mortgaged (pp. 91–92).
- Upon his death in 1916, a government loan of $9,000 was advanced to his family administrators to settle outstanding debts and discharge chettiar mortgages; this loan was not fully repaid until 31 January 1928 (p. 89).

### Conclusion

Hassan presents Raja Bot as a figure whose life encapsulated the transition from pre-colonial Malay chiefdom to colonial administrative subject. His initial resistance to British interference — embodied in the Lukut Affair — gave way to pragmatic adaptation, as he leveraged his education, experience, and royal lineage to secure a prominent position in Selangor's new governance structures. Yet his inability to manage the substantial financial settlement he received, compounded by a pattern of speculative and ultimately unsuccessful business ventures, left him and his descendants in a state of insolvency that contrasted sharply with the wealth his father had once commanded.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the Selangor Secretariat Files (SSF), Selangor State Council Minutes, Colonial Office records (CO 273 series), and Swettenham's unpublished journal, supplemented by Malay-language sources including Wan Mohd. Amin's *Kenangan Selangor* and Jalleh's unpublished thesis on pre-1874 Selangor disorders.
- The study contributes to the historiography of the Lukut boundary dispute and the early Residential System in Selangor by placing a previously under-examined figure at the centre of both events, and by using State Council Minutes to document the administrative role of Malay chiefs in the post-1874 period.
