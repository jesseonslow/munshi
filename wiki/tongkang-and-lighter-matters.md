# Tongkang and lighter matters

C.A. Gibson-Hill, a leading maritime ethnographer of the Straits Settlements, published this article in 1952 to resolve the taxonomic confusion surrounding the Malay word *tongkang*, which had been applied to at least five distinct types of sailing cargo vessel across Malaya, Burma, and North Borneo. His central thesis is that the term should be restricted to sailing lighters of European hull origin (or boats developed from such stock), and that the apparent diversity of "tongkangs" in Malayan waters represents a single evolutionary lineage radiating from the Tamil-manned lighters first brought to Singapore in the 1820s.

## Summary

The article addresses a nomenclature problem in Malayan maritime ethnography. The word *tongkang* is widely assumed to be Chinese, but Gibson-Hill demonstrates it is Malay in origin, probably derived from *belongkang* (a Sumatran river cargo boat term). He then systematically distinguishes the boats legitimately bearing the name from those that do not, arguing that the term should not be extended to the Twakow (a genuinely Chinese hull developed locally) or the Sekochi (a small double-ended Chinese cargo boat of Malay derivation). The article thus establishes a principled boundary for the term's use.

The core of the study traces the evolutionary lineage of the Singapore and Penang tongkangs back to the Tamil-manned lighters of the early Straits Settlements period. Gibson-Hill reconstructs how these lighters, originally rowed and of southern Indian design, were progressively modified—first gaining sails, then passing into Chinese hands, and eventually diverging into the distinct forms now known as the Singapore Timber Tongkang, the Singapore Trader, and the Penang Sailing Lighter. He identifies the now-obsolete *Tongkang Melayu*, a ketch-rigged Malay sailing lighter of the 1880s–1890s, as the direct ancestor of the Timber Tongkang, and shows how the Singapore Trader in turn developed from the Timber Tongkang by the elimination of the bowsprit and a reversion to Chinese batten lugsails over the period 1880–1920.

The article extends its scope to neighbouring territories, identifying cognate forms in Burma (the *thon-kin* and the Rangoon cargo lighter) and North Borneo (the *Tongkang Melayu* of Sandakan Bay, built by Banjermasin Malays), and discusses the Bangkok lorcha as a related but distinct development. Throughout, Gibson-Hill draws on shipping registers, contemporary illustrations, models in the Skeat collection, and his own field observations to build a coherent picture of how a single European lighter design was adapted, adopted, and transformed by successive communities of boatbuilders over roughly 130 years.

### Key Findings

- The word *tongkang* first appears in romanised form in newspaper accounts of the 1830s; the earliest dictionary to record it is Crawfurd's (1852), which defines it simply as "the name of a large kind of boat" (p. 82).
- The Singapore Timber Tongkang measures approximately 85–95 feet in length, 30–33 feet in maximum beam, with a depth of 12–15 feet amidships and a tonnage of roughly 185–200 gross; the capital value of timber in the hull and spars alone was estimated at $10,000–$12,000 at 1950s prices (pp. 85–86).
- A 70-foot Penang Tongkang took two months to build in 1949 and cost $17,000–$18,000, of which half was the cost of timber (p. 91).
- By the 1860s, Cameron estimated that no fewer than 500 tongkangs were visible at one time in the first reach of the Singapore River, each carrying a crew of at least three men, giving a floating population of at least 1,500 men (p. 99).
- The first recorded sailing race between tongkangs took place on New Year's Day 1834, as reported in the *Singapore Chronicle* of 2 January 1834 (p. 97).
- The old timber rafts described by Cameron (1865) were 500–600 feet long and 60–70 feet broad, carried crews of 20–25 men, and could take months to complete a single voyage (pp. 107–108).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive historical takeaway is that the apparently heterogeneous assortment of craft mustered under the name *tongkang* in Malayan waters is in fact a single evolutionary family, all traceable to the European-style sailing lighters first introduced to the Straits Settlements by Tamil boatmen in the 1820s. The term is Malay in origin, not Chinese, and its legitimate application should be confined to sailing cargo vessels of European hull lineage; the Chinese-built boats that now dominate the scene are later adopters of a design that was originally neither Chinese nor, in its earliest form, even sailed.

## Context

- Primary sources include the shipping registers of Singapore and Penang (extracted by the author at the end of 1949), the Skeat collection of boat models at Cambridge University (examined 1950), and the Raffles Museum Archives in Singapore (photographs and paintings from the 1840s–1890s).
- The article represents a significant contribution to the historiography of Malayan maritime trade by providing the first systematic taxonomic treatment of the tongkang group and by reconstructing the ethnic succession (Tamil → Malay → Chinese) in the lighter trade of the Straits Settlements over a 130-year period.
