# On the Patani

William Cameron's "On the Patani" (1883) is a travel and geological account of his 1881 journey up the Patani River through the Siamese-administered provinces of Raman, Jalor, Nunchit, and Patani in the northern Malay Peninsula. The article combines vivid ethnographic and scenic description with a sustained mineralogical and geological survey, arguing that oceanic action rather than river denudation was the principal agent in shaping the region's topography and distributing its alluvial mineral deposits.

## Summary

Cameron's narrative begins with the geography of the Patani River system, tracing its course from the same mountain ranges that feed the Perak, through its extensive delta, and into the Gulf of Siam. He documents the administrative structure of the Patani provinces under Siamese suzerainty via Singora, describing the town of Patani as a trading centre of three to four thousand souls exporting tin, lead, gutta-percha, salt fish, and earthenware, governed by a Raja and overseen by a powerful Chinese official, the Dato of the mines, Chew Beng (pp. 123–126). The middle portion of the article is a richly detailed travelogue of the river journey itself—the poling prahus, the Siamese Buddhist alms processions, the padi-fields and kampongs of Jalor, and the court of Raja Raman at Kota Bharu, where skilled artisans worked in wood, gold, and wrought rotan (pp. 126–130).

The article's substantive contribution, however, lies in its geological and mineralogical observations. Cameron spent fourteen months in the region and presents a systematic account of the alternating limestone and granite ridges that characterise the interior, the water-wearing marks on limestone cliffs indicating prolonged submergence, and the distribution of galena and tin deposits (pp. 130–134). His central thesis is that a clearly defined mineral belt, approximately two miles wide and striking south by west, contains the productive galena lodes, and that the so-called "pockets" of galena found in surface soil are not natural accumulations but detached boulders broken from in-situ lodes by ancient marine action. He further argues that the coexistence of tin and carbonate of lead on the same hills, and the absence of alluvial galena in river beds, confirm that the ocean—not rivers—was the dominant erosive and depositional force in the region's recent geological history (pp. 131–134).

### Key Findings

- The galena lodes in the crystalline limestone contain silver in proportions ranging from 23 to 66 ounces per ton (p. 131).
- A distinct mineral belt approximately two miles wide, striking south by west, contains the productive galena veins; the largest lode ever discovered in Malaya (No. 1 Mine) lies within this belt (p. 131).
- Galena boulders found in surface "pockets" weighed up to six or seven pikuls each, with single pockets yielding as much as seven or eight hundred pikuls; Cameron demonstrates these are detached fragments from overhanging lodes, not in-situ deposits (p. 132).
- Elephants carried between three and a half and five pikuls per load to the galena mines, compared with a buffalo's capacity of approximately 13½ katis, making elephants the economically superior transport (p. 126).
- The prahus used on the Patani measured approximately sixty feet in length and seven feet in width, and in some instances carried about one hundred pikuls of cargo (pp. 127–128).
- Cameron found no galena veins in the granite ranges themselves, and no alluvial galena in the river beds flowing between granite and limestone, which he takes as evidence against river denudation as the primary shaping force (pp. 131, 133).

### Conclusion

Cameron's definitive historical takeaway is geological rather than political: the mineral wealth of the Patani provinces—particularly the galena-silver lodes and the tin deposits on the granite hills—was distributed by ancient oceanic processes, and the present surface topography reflects a slow emergence from submergence over vast periods of time. His fourteen months of field observation, while not framed as a formal geological thesis, provided the Straits Settlements and the broader colonial scientific community with the first systematic account of the Patani mineral belt and its structural geology.

## Context

- Cameron was a European visitor and amateur geologist operating in Siamese Malaya; his access to the interior provinces and the galena mines was facilitated by the cooperation of local Rajas and the Chinese mining community, and his account reflects the administrative reality of Siamese suzerainty over the Patani provinces via Singora.
- The article draws on Cameron's own field observations over fourteen months (1880–1881) and a rough sketch map of the delta and mine locations; it represents one of the earliest detailed geological surveys of the Patani region published in a Straits-based journal.
