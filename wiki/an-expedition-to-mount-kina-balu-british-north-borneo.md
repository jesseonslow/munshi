# An expedition to Mount Kina Balu, British North Borneo

R. Hanitsch, a naturalist attached to the Raffles Museum in Singapore, published this account in 1900 of his March–April 1899 expedition to Mount Kina Balu (Kinabalu) in British North Borneo, in which he abandoned the summit attempt at 4,200 feet and instead conducted intensive zoological collecting on the lower mountain ranges. The article combines a day-by-day field narrative with a comprehensive scientific report that yielded six new taxa to science, including two new genera.

## Summary

Hanitsch's primary objective was a full ascent of Kinabalu (13,698 feet), but after reaching 4,200 feet on 22 March 1899, he calculated that the remaining climb to the summit and descent would consume the entire remainder of his leave without leaving time for collecting. He therefore pivoted to systematic entomological, herpetological, and malacological work at two camps (approximately 2,100 and 4,200 feet) along the Kadamaian River, the upper course of the Tampassuk. The narrative portion, drawn from his diary, documents the Tuaran route from Panjut on the coast through the Dusun villages of Kappa, Kalawat, Bungol, Koung, and Kiou, and records the considerable logistical difficulties of the journey: unreliable carriers, impassable river fords, a collapsed tent in a thunderstorm, and Hanitsch's own near-fainting episode on the return march (pp. 49–62).

The scientific section is the article's principal contribution. Hanitsch's collections were distributed among specialists—Boulenger for reptiles, amphibians, and fishes; Borradaile for crustaceans; Sharp for insects; de Niceville and Shelford for Lepidoptera; and Edgar Smith for molluscs. The results included a new genus of snake (*Oreocalamus*), a new genus of homalopteroid fish (*Glaniopsis*), and new species of gecko, snake, toad, and freshwater crab, all from elevations between 2,100 and 4,200 feet (pp. 66–85). Hanitsch also provided detailed morphological descriptions of two remarkable insect forms: a leaf-like larva of *Lycides* and a luminous wingless beetle he tentatively identified as an adult female *Lampyrides*, which he kept alive for a week and used as a night-light at camp (pp. 75–78).

Beyond taxonomy, the article offers valuable ethnographic and ecological observations. Hanitsch documented the altitudinal zonation of amphibian species from the coast to 4,200 feet, the function of the ventral suckers of *Rana cavitympanum* tadpoles in fast-flowing streams (correcting Mocquard's earlier parasitism hypothesis), and the economic life of the Dusun communities along the route, including their currency practices, barter goods, and the role of the chief Malagup in managing the coolie party (pp. 63–66).

### Key Findings

- Six new taxa were described from the expedition: *Gecko rhacophorus* (n. sp.), *Stoliczkaia borneensis* (n. sp.), *Oreocalamus hanitschi* (n. gen. et n. sp.), *Leptobrachium baluense* (n. sp.), *Glaniopsis hanitschi* (n. gen. et n. sp.), and *Potamon kadamaianum* (n. sp.) (pp. 67–85).
- The summit of Kina Balu stood at 13,698 feet; Hanitsch's highest camp was at 4,200 feet, where temperatures ranged from 60° to 67° F. during March 1899 (pp. 54–56).
- The Kadamaian River ford on 21 March required men to wade up to their necks through a series of cataracts; on the return march (27 March) the same river was easily passable, demonstrating extreme variability in water levels (pp. 53–54, 57).
- The Dusun chief Malagup demanded a fowl and seven eggs as a sacrifice to the mountain spirits before the guides would proceed; when the summit attempt was abandoned, the guides returned the fowl to Hanitsch, who cooked it (pp. 52–55).
- Copper cents were the principal currency in the interior; small silver coins (5-cent pieces) were accepted with distrust, and even the chief Malagup did not know their value. Kerosene oil, soap, and matches were the most requested trade goods (p. 63).
- The luminous beetle (60 mm, adult female *Lampyrides*) was observed to emit light from a circular organ around the eighth abdominal spiracle, measuring 3.5 × 2 mm, and served as a practical night-light during sleepless nights at the upper camp (pp. 77–78).

### Conclusion

Hanitsch's definitive takeaway is that the lower ranges of Kinabalu, between 2,000 and 4,200 feet, constitute a zone of exceptional herpetological and entomological richness that had been underexplored by previous ascents, which had focused on the summit. His decision to forgo the peak in favour of sustained collecting at mid-elevations proved scientifically productive, yielding a disproportionate share of new species relative to the time spent in the field, and establishing the Kadamaian River valley as a priority area for future biological investigation.

## Context

- Hanitsch was a European naturalist operating under the auspices of the Raffles Museum, Singapore, with credentials furnished by the Straits Settlements Government to the British North Borneo Company administration; his work was explicitly in service of colonial scientific collection (p. 46).
- The article draws on no archival collections per se but is grounded entirely in Hanitsch's own field observations and the taxonomic determinations of European specialists (Boulenger, Borradaile, Sharp, de Niceville, Shelford, Edgar Smith), reflecting the standard colonial-era model of specimen extraction and metropolitan identification.
