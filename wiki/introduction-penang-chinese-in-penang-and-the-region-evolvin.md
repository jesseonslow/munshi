# Introduction: Penang Chinese in Penang and the region: evolving identities and networks

Chandran Jeshurun's 2009 article reconstructs the personal and institutional bonds between Malaysia's first Prime Minister, Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Al-Haj, and two universities — his alma mater, Cambridge, and the newly established University of Malaya at Pantai Valley — during the critical window of 1960–1962. Drawing primarily on the Tunku's private papers preserved in the Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Memorial in Kuala Lumpur, Jeshurun argues that the Tunku's indefatigable personal support was indispensable to the University of Malaya's rise as a leading institution within the Association of Commonwealth Universities during the 1960s and 1970s.

## Summary

The article traces two interlocking threads. The first concerns the University of Cambridge's decision in 1960 to confer an honorary Doctor of Law on the Tunku, a distinction that carried deep personal resonance given his undergraduate history at St Catherine's College, where he had once been "sent down" for lateness and lost a year. The correspondence between the Tunku, Vice Chancellor Herbert Butterfield, and the Master of St Catherine's, Dr E. E. Rich, reveals the warmth and informality of these relationships, including the Tunku's good-humoured insistence on paying his £5 MA fee and his acceptance of an Honorary Fellowship at the College. The second thread follows the Tunku's reluctant but ultimately enthusiastic acceptance of the role of first Chancellor of the University of Malaya at its new Pantai Valley campus in Kuala Lumpur, a position he initially resisted on the grounds that a political leader should not head a university, but which he came to embrace after persuasion by the Malay Rulers and the example of Harold Macmillan as Chancellor of Oxford.

A significant historiographical contribution of the article is its documentation of a previously under-appreciated event: on 10 June 1960, the day after his Cambridge degree ceremony, the Tunku called on Lord Perth at the Colonial Office and indicated Malaya's preparedness to consider the full "Grand Design" for a Federation of Malaysia encompassing Malaya, Singapore, North Borneo, Sarawak, and Brunei. Jeshurun notes that the Tunku himself never alluded to this meeting in his writings, and no other Alliance Cabinet minister referenced it, yet it predates the Tunku's well-known public announcement of 27 May 1961 in Singapore. The article situates this within the broader context of the PAP's victory in Singapore's June 1959 elections, which likely compelled the Alliance leadership to act decisively on the federation question.

The narrative of the University of Malaya's early years at Pantai Valley is enriched by the recollections of pioneering staff, particularly Professor Rayson Huang, the first Professor of Chemistry, and by the correspondence between the Tunku and Malcolm MacDonald, the last British Commissioner-General for South-East Asia and former Chancellor of the old Singapore-based university. MacDonald's 1960 convocation address in Singapore, in which he described himself as "an anachronism, a hang-over, a ghost of yesteryears" and urged graduates to love both their multi-racial nation and all of humanity, is presented as a defining moment of decolonial transition.

### Key Findings

- The University of Cambridge conferred an honorary LLD on the Tunku on 9 June 1960; Vice Chancellor Herbert Butterfield wrote the invitation on 19 February 1960, and the Tunku confirmed acceptance on 1 March 1960 (pp. 1–2).
- The Tunku received an Honorary Fellowship at St Catherine's College, Cambridge, the highest honour the College could offer, as resolved unanimously by the Fellows in April 1960 (p. 3).
- On 10 June 1960, the Tunku told Lord Perth at the Colonial Office that he was "prepared to face the possibility of a Federation of Malaya, Singapore, North Borneo, Sarawak and Brunei — in other words the full Grand Design" (p. 5).
- The Tunku was formally installed as first Chancellor of the University of Malaya at the Stadium Negara on 16 June 1962; the ceremony was attended by representatives of all forty-four member institutions of the Association of Commonwealth Universities plus twenty-one others from the US, Europe, and Asia (p. 11).
- The first batch of students enrolled at the Pantai Valley campus in May 1959; by the 1962 graduation, the ethnic breakdown of BA (Hons.) graduates was 28 Malays, 21 Chinese, 16 Indians, and 1 Eurasian, while BEng graduates were all 17 Chinese (p. 13).
- The new Union House at the Pantai Valley campus, declared open by the Tunku in June 1962, cost approximately $200,000 and was described in the student newspaper *Mahasiswa Negara* as "superior to anything of its type in South-East Asia" (p. 14).

### Conclusion

Jeshurun's definitive takeaway is that the personal relationships and dedicated leadership of figures like the Tunku, Malcolm MacDonald, and Vice Chancellor Sir Alexander Oppenheim were the true foundations upon which the University of Malaya was built into a pre-eminent institution of higher learning in Southeast Asia. He laments that, despite the existence of the Dewan Tunku Canselor as a physical memorial, there is virtually no trace on campus of the pioneering academics and administrators who built the university in its formative years, and he calls for a broader recognition of their contributions by Malaysian society.

## Context

- The article is grounded primarily in the Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Papers (MTARP series) held at the Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra Al-Haj Memorial, Kuala Lumpur, supplemented by British Foreign Office documents (FCO 51/154) and contemporary *Straits Times* reporting.
- Historiographically, the piece contributes to the growing body of work on the formation of Malaysia by documenting the 10 June 1960 Tunku–Perth meeting, which challenges the conventional narrative that places the origin of the federation process at the Tunku's May 1961 Singapore announcement.
