# Introduction: Penang Chinese in Penang and the region: evolving identities and networks

Loh Wei Leng's 2009 introduction to the JMBRAS volume *Peranakan Chinese in Penang and the Region: Evolving Identities and Networks* frames a multi-disciplinary workshop project funded by the Toyota Foundation's Asian Neighbors Network Program. Set against the historical backdrop of Southeast Asia's northern littoral from the early modern period through the colonial era, the piece argues that the dominant concept of a singular "Chinese diasporic identity" is inadequate for describing Peranakan Chinese communities, whose open tradition of cultural adaptation produced hybrid and multiple identities instead.

## Summary

Loh situates the Peranakan Chinese within the broader phenomenon of ethnic trade diasporas, drawing on recent scholarship (McCabe et al. 2005; Pomeranz and Topik 2006) to establish that organised merchant families with shared ethnic origin formed the most efficient mechanism for organising commerce across Afro-Eurasia until the nineteenth century (p. 1). These networks were not merely economic; as Curtin (1984) demonstrated, trade diasporas and cultural exchange were "two sides of the same coin" (p. 5). The volume's subregional focus is the northern littoral of Southeast Asia, with Penang as its focal point, functioning as an entrepôt connecting Myanmar, west-coast Thailand, and north Sumatra (p. 4).

The introduction then turns to the question of identity. Loh contends that a diasporic identity is time-bound, applicable perhaps to early sojourner generations but weakening with longer-term settlement (p. 5). Spatial heterogeneity means that a Sino-Thai identifies primarily as Thai, and a Malaysian of Chinese origin first as Malaysian, with Chinese heritage acknowledged secondarily (p. 5). Tan Chee Beng's concept of "multiple identities" is introduced to capture the simultaneous bounded (national) and unbounded (*Huaren*) dimensions of Chinese-origin individuals in the region (p. 6).

Finally, Loh provides a chronological framework. The "Chinese century" (fifteenth to eighteenth centuries) saw Chinese dominance in East Asian maritime trade, facilitated by Chinese restrictions on foreign merchants entering China (pp. 6–7). Early settlers, unable to bring families due to emigration bans, married local women, producing the Malay-speaking, local-born Baba (p. 7). The colonial era, beginning with Penang in 1786, introduced the critical distinction between Baba (local-born) and *Sinkheh* (new immigrants), with the massive mid-nineteenth-century inflow of Chinese labour for tin mines marking a watershed in scale and character (p. 7).

### Key Findings

- Ethnic trade diasporas were "the most efficient way of organising commerce across much of Afro-Eurasia and the Americas until the nineteenth century," functioning as a practical mechanism where contract enforcement across political boundaries was difficult (p. 1).
- Penang's role as an entrepôt required its merchants to build networks spanning the entire supply chain—from producers to maritime transporters, wholesale and retail entrepreneurs, financiers, and bureaucrats (p. 4).
- The concept of a unified "international Chinese diasporic identity" is critiqued as essentialising ethnicity and failing to account for spatial heterogeneity and location-specific identity formation (p. 5).
- The distinction between Baba (local-born, pre-colonial merchant-class offspring) and *Sinkheh* (new immigrants, particularly labour migrants from the mid-nineteenth century) marks a fundamental shift in the composition and linguistic profile of Chinese communities in the Straits ports (p. 7).
- Chinese maritime dominance from the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries was structurally enabled by Chinese state policies restricting foreign merchants, which compelled Southeast Asian rulers to rely on Chinese sailors and ships within the tributary trade system (p. 7).
- Tan Chee Beng's framework of "multiple identities" captures the dual bounded (national citizenship) and unbounded (*Huaren*) self-identification of Chinese-origin individuals in Southeast Asia (p. 6).

### Conclusion

Loh's definitive historical takeaway is that the Peranakan Chinese of the northern littoral cannot be adequately described through the lens of a transnational Chinese diasporic identity. Their long-term settlement, intermarriage, and cultural adaptation produced hybrid and multiple identities that were fundamentally local in orientation, even as transnational networks of commerce and kinship continued to operate. The volume's case studies collectively demonstrate that identity in this context is neither fixed nor singular but evolves across generations in response to specific political, economic, and social conditions in each destination territory.

## Context

- The volume emerged from a Toyota Foundation Asian Neighbors Network Program workshop, bringing together scholars from different disciplines to examine Peranakan Chinese networks across the northern littoral subregion.
- The JMBRAS (Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society) has long served as a key venue for Southeast Asian historical scholarship, and this volume contributes to its ongoing engagement with Chinese diaspora studies in the Straits Settlements and beyond.
