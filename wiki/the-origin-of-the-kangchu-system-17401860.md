# The origin of the kangchu system, 1740–1860

Carl A. Trocki's 1976 article traces the origins of the *kangchu* system—the Chinese riverine headman institution that governed pepper and gambier cultivation in Johor, Riau, and Singapore—back to approximately 1740, arguing that this system was not a nineteenth-century invention but the product of a long evolution from Bugis-ordered plantations at Riau through a period of Chinese autonomy after the 1784 Dutch destruction of the entrepot, to its formal legal recognition under the Temenggong of Johor in the 1840s. Trocki contends that the kangchu system was the foundational economic and political mechanism by which impoverished Malay aristocrats, stripped of their maritime trade base, constructed new mainland states in alliance with Chinese capital and labor.

## Summary

Trocki's central argument is that the kangchu system emerged in stages across the Straits of Malacca, and that its full significance has been obscured by earlier scholarship which treated it as a Johor-specific phenomenon of the mid-nineteenth century. The article reconstructs a continuous developmental arc from the Bugis Yang Dipertuan Muda Daeng Celak's establishment of gambier plantations worked by Chinese laborers at Riau around 1740, through the catastrophic disruption of 1784 when the Dutch destroyed the Riau entrepot and the Malay and Bugis rulers fled, leaving the Chinese planters to govern themselves for over three decades. By the time Stamford Raffles founded Singapore in 1819, the Chinese settlements at Riau had already achieved a degree of economic and military autonomy that prefigured the kangchu system. The shift of the gambier marketplace to Singapore after 1819, the removal of the British import tariff on gambier in 1835, and the subsequent land and labor crises in Singapore's interior together drove the migration of planters across the strait into Johor, where the Temenggong formally recognized their settlements through *surat sungai* grants beginning in 1844.

The article draws heavily on the *surat sungai* records in the Johor Archives, which Trocki notes had only become available to scholars in the 1960s, and which he was the first to use systematically. These documents, combined with the *Tuhfat al-Nafis*, P.J. Begbie's *The Malayan Peninsula*, Dutch sources cited by Netscher, and contemporary Singapore newspaper reports, allow Trocki to reconstruct the institutional architecture of the system: the hierarchical relationship between the Kapitan China (appointed by the Malay ruler) and the individual kangchu (each holding a *surat sungai* for a specific river watershed), the revenue farm system linking Johor to Singapore's opium and spirits syndicates, and the parallel military hierarchy of the Chinese secret societies, particularly the Ngee Heng (Tan Tae Hoe), whose Teochew membership formed the backbone of the Johor settlements. Trocki emphasizes that the kangchu was simultaneously a tax concessionnaire, a local military chief, a manager of the debtor-creditor relationship between planters and shopkeepers, and the wealthiest man in his district—a convergence of roles that made the system both an economic and a political institution.

A significant portion of the analysis is devoted to the economic pressures that drove the system's expansion: the extreme volatility of gambier prices (which fell from $4–5 per pikul in 1834 to $0.90–1.00 by 1849), the exhaustion of cultivable land in Singapore's interior, the exploitative credit arrangements under which two-thirds of plantations were encumbered by 1839, and the secret society conflicts that erupted when the Singapore government attempted to survey and register plantations in the early 1840s. These pressures, Trocki argues, were the direct catalyst for the Ngee Heng's decision in 1846 to order approximately 4,000 planters to migrate to Johor, an event that established the long-term alliance between the Johor rulers and the Teochew secret society.

### Key Findings

- The earliest *surat sungai* in the Johor Archives is dated 9 October 1844, for Sungai Sekudai; the second, dated 22 October 1844, granted Sungai Tebrau to Kapitan Tan Kee Soon. By June 1845, 62 plantations were operating on four rivers with an estimated population of 500 (pp. 144–145).
- By 1862, approximately 37 *surat sungai* had been issued covering 32 *kangkar* settlements: 18 rivers in the Johor Straits, 19 on the Johor River, and 2 on the west coast. A 1859 estimate placed the Chinese population of Johor at 60,000, though Trocki considers 30,000 more plausible based on average *kangkar* populations of 200–2,000 (pp. 150–151).
- Gambier prices at Singapore fluctuated dramatically: $1.30–1.75 per pikul in January 1831, rising to $4.00–5.00 in January 1834, then falling to $0.90–1.00 by January 1849 before recovering to $2.85 by 1861 (p. 141).
- In 1836, Singapore's gambier trade totaled 39,624 pikuls (22,000 local production plus 19,624 imports, mostly from Riau), with exports distributed to Great Britain (9,921 pikuls), Celebes (9,961), Calcutta (7,096), and other destinations (p. 139).
- The Johor opium farm was held jointly with Singapore's from 1846; by 1847, Cheang Sam Teo paid $300 per month for the combined farms. The arrangement was terminated in 1862 when the British became dissatisfied with the Temenggong's share (pp. 148–149).
- A letter in the *Singapore Free Press* of 26 March 1846, from a secret society headman styled "Keeper of the Quinquangular Seal," announced that the Ngee Heng had commanded approximately 4,000 pepper and gambier planters to leave Singapore for Johor in retaliation against government plantation assessments (pp. 143–144).

### Conclusion

Trocki's definitive takeaway is that the kangchu system was not a peripheral or ephemeral arrangement but the structural foundation upon which the modern Malay states of the peninsula were built. The alliance between impoverished Malay aristocrats and Chinese capital, labor, and administrative capacity—first formalized in Johor in the 1840s—established a feudal-cum-capitalist political economy that, remarkably, remained essentially intact through a century of British colonial rule, the Japanese occupation, and two decades of independence. The system's origins in the Chinese autonomy of post-1784 Riau and its consolidation through the gambier economy of Singapore mean that the political map of southern Malaya was, in Trocki's framing, drawn not by colonial administrators but by the economic necessities of a Chinese agricultural diaspora and the survival strategies of displaced Malay nobility.

## Context

- The primary archival collection underpinning this study is the *Buku Daftar Surat-surat Keterangan Berkebun* (1844–1903) in the Johor Archives, which Trocki identifies as having been available to scholars only since the 1960s and which he was the first to use systematically for this topic (p. 130, n. 6).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in correcting the earlier assumption (following Coope, 1936) that the kangchu system was a Johor-specific institution of limited significance, by demonstrating its deep roots at Riau from the 1740s and its centrality to the Singapore economy throughout the nineteenth century.
