# The Perak Museum: Displaying Malaya in the late nineteenth century

## Abstract

In 1883 the British Resident of Perak, Hugh Low, set up a museum in the state capital, Taiping. The colonial administration in Perak was just nine years old, and apart from tin mining little was known about the economic potential of the state. The museum housed artifacts gathered in the state and carried out scientific experiments as part of an effort to make Malaya self-sustaining and financially viable. Low and the museum’s curator, Leonard Wray Jnr, aspired to create a comprehensive collection that reflected Perak, and more broadly all of Malaya. Guided by the interests of the curator and the Resident, the museum developed an idiosyncratic collection that showcased Perak’s flora and fauna, it’s economic potential, and the local culture. Initially preoccupied with economic exploitation, the museum grew increasingly involved with documenting and preserving the state’s heritage.

## Summary

Semine Long-Callesen examines the founding and development of the Perak Museum in Taiping, established in 1883 by British Resident Hugh Low and curated by Leonard Wray Jnr. The article situates the museum within the broader shift in British colonial strategy from entrepôt trade at coastal posts to territorial administration in the Malay interior, a transition that demanded new sources of revenue to justify the expanded presence to the Colonial Office. The museum was conceived as an instrument of economic exploration—mapping tin deposits, testing plantation crops, and analysing geological specimens—but its collection grew to encompass ethnography, natural history, and cultural preservation in ways that exceeded its original mandate (pp. 74–75).

Long-Callesen draws on the Perak Museum Archive, annual reports, Low's published journals, and contemporary travelogues to trace how the institution's character evolved under the competing pressures of financial austerity and curatorial ambition. The museum's encyclopaedic scope—spanning mineralogy, botany, zoology, and ethnography—was shaped as much by the personal interests of Low and Wray as by any coherent institutional policy, producing what the author calls an "idiosyncratic collection" (pp. 77–78). The article also compares the Perak Museum with the Raffles Museum in Singapore, highlighting how the former benefited from direct political backing by the Resident while the latter struggled for resources and space (pp. 79–80).

A central tension runs through the narrative: the museum was simultaneously a tool of colonial extraction and a vehicle for documenting and preserving the very cultures and ecosystems it helped to transform. Wray's efforts to revive "Malayan Art Industries" and his lament over the displacement of indigenous craft patterns by European prints illustrate how the institution became entangled in questions of cultural conservation even as it served imperial economic interests (pp. 82–83).

### Key Findings

- Tin production in Perak rose from 7,149 tons in 1882 to 10,272 tons in 1884, while the number of mining sites increased from thirty to one hundred and fifty over the same period (p. 76).
- The museum's annual income fell from $232 in 1895 to $155 in 1896, and in 1896 the institution could not afford to replace a retiring taxidermist (p. 80).
- In 1895, Wray acquired display cases costing $640—nearly three times the museum's annual revenue—along with 717 bird stands and a Hammond typewriter (p. 80).
- By 1906 the museum had catalogued 13,000 objects, and visitor numbers rose from an estimated 34,256 in 1897 to 46,685 in 1903 (pp. 83–84).
- Wray's herringbone-patterned latex tapping method, adapted from local practice, became the standard for rubber plantations across the peninsula (p. 79).
- The museum's staff numbered only eight in 1898, including a curator-cum-state geologist, a clerk-cum-caretaker, a taxidermist, an assistant taxidermist, two sweepers, one gardener, and one watchman (p. 80).

### Conclusion

Long-Callesen argues that the Perak Museum exemplifies the contradictory institutional practices upon which colonial power was based. Initially an instrument of economic exploitation designed to prove Perak's financial viability, the museum progressively became a site of cultural and natural heritage preservation, creating an ambiguous legacy in which the same institution that facilitated colonial extraction also worked to document and sustain the traditions it was undermining (p. 84).

## Context

- The primary archival source is the Perak Museum Archive (PMA), particularly the Annual Reports on the Perak Museum (PMAR) spanning the 1880s to 1900s, supplemented by Low's published journals and Swettenham's reports.
- The article contributes to the historiography of colonial natural history museums in Southeast Asia by foregrounding the Perak Museum as a case study of how institutional practice mediated between economic imperatives and cultural preservation in the early Federated Malay States.
