# Surveys and Explorations in the Native States of the Malayan Peninsula, 1875–82. Facsimile reprint. With a note P. Kratoska

D. D. Daly, appointed surveyor for the Native States of the Straits Settlements in 1875, presented this paper to the Royal Geographical Society in London in May 1882, describing his topographical surveys of Selangor, Moar, Pahang, Sungai Ujong, and Perak conducted between 1875 and 1877. The account documents the peninsular interior at the very outset of British administrative intervention following the Pangkor Engagement of 1874, when the region was still largely uncharted and its boundaries vaguely defined. Reprinted here with an editor's note by Paul Kratoska, the paper serves as a primary source for the geographical, geological, and administrative conditions of the Malay Peninsula in the first years of the protectorate system.

## Summary

Daly's paper is a first-hand surveyor's narrative commissioned to fill what he calls "the large blank" on maps of the peninsula. The historical problem it addresses is straightforward: when British intervention began in 1873–74, the interior of the Malay Peninsula was almost entirely unknown to the colonial administration. Sir Andrew Clarke, upon arriving as Governor in 1873, found "absolutely no such information of the least value" in the archives of the Royal Geographical Society or the Straits Settlements office (p. 16). Daly's surveys were therefore a foundational instrument of colonial governance, establishing the positions of rivers, strategic points, forts, and stockades in territories that were simultaneously disturbed by internecine warfare and rich in mineral resources.

The narrative moves through each state in the order surveyed, combining topographical description with geological observation and ethnographic detail. In Selangor, Daly documents the transformation of the jungle track between Klang and Kuala Lumpur into a carriage road under the protectorate, and provides a detailed account of alluvial tin geology at the Ampagnan mine. The Moar river expedition is marked by resistance from the Negeri Sembilan states, whose rulers regarded the survey as a precursor to annexation—the Dato of Moar warned that "if we let the needle in, the thread is sure to follow" (p. 7). The crossing to Pahang via Tassek Bera and the encounter with Orang Sakei communities provide some of the earliest European descriptions of the peninsular interior's aboriginal populations. The Perak and Kedah boundary surveys, conducted with a forty-two-man party and seven elephants, reveal the practical difficulties of fixing frontiers between British-protected states and Siamese tributaries, including the flight of 500 Malay conscripts to British territory to escape Siamese military service (p. 11).

The paper's underlying argument, reinforced by the remarks of Clarke and Adamson in the discussion, is that British intervention was both necessary (to protect trade and British subjects from piracy and internecine war) and beneficial (introducing rule of law, ending debt-slavery, and opening the peninsula to agricultural and mining development). Daly's own observations support this framing: he records the contrast between the arbitrary rule of Malay rajahs and the compensation paid for damaged coconut trees under British administration, and notes that the Malay *rayat* "knows that he is protected by the law" in the protectorate states (p. 13).

### Key Findings

- Daly was appointed surveyor in May 1875 by Governor Sir Andrew Clarke; his surveys covered Selangor, Moar, Pahang, Sungai Ujong, and Perak over approximately two years (pp. 2, 5).
- The Ampagnan tin mine in Selangor employed over 1,000 Chinese; the alluvial deposit varied from 20 to 35 feet in depth, with the ore layer ranging from 4 to 30 inches thick; annual output was calculated at 2½ bharas of smelted tin per man, valued at £35 sterling (p. 3).
- During the Moar river expedition, Daly was surrounded by approximately 30 armed Malays at Gumatie and held prisoner for several hours; his Chinese servants were detained and starved for two days (p. 7).
- In Pahang, Daly recorded 19 villages containing 2,110 houses with an approximate population of 4,330 Malays, and was informed that 6,000 Chinese were working alluvial gold mines in the interior (p. 8).
- The Perak–Kedah boundary survey of 1877 involved a party of 42 men and 7 elephants lent by the Sultan of Kedah; a 4-mile base line was measured at Kwala Kupang to fix the position of Gunung Inas and the Titi Wangsa range (p. 11).
- Sir Andrew Clarke stated in the discussion that the peninsula comprised approximately 33,000 square miles with only one acre in every 300 cultivated, and a density of five to six Malays per square mile, compared with 125 per square mile in the British settlements (p. 17).

### Conclusion

Daly's definitive historical takeaway is that the Malay Peninsula remains, even in 1882, a "closed volume" with more than half its area still unexplored, and that the surveys he conducted represent only the first step in making the region's resources and geography available to the colonial administration. His closing appeal—that "the circles of exploration of other countries are narrowing each year"—frames the work as both a geographical contribution and an implicit argument for continued British engagement with the peninsula's development.

## Context

- Daly was a government surveyor employed by the Straits Settlements administration; the paper was delivered to a London audience and explicitly framed British intervention as a civilising and developmental project, a framing reinforced by the remarks of Clarke and Adamson in the recorded discussion.
- The editor's note situates the paper alongside Jacques de Morgan's 1884 explorations in Perak, establishing a small corpus of near-contemporary European accounts of the peninsular interior at the moment of British administrative transition.
