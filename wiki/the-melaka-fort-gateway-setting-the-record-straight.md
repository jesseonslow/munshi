# The Melaka Fort Gateway: Setting the Record Straight

## Abstract

The Bukit Choras archaeological site in present-day Kedah’s Bujang Valley was first reported in 1849. Earlier archaeological interventions were incomplete, however, because they only focused on a section of a seventh-to-ninth-century CE Buddhist *stupa*. In 2023 and 2024, three further excavations exposed several sides of this *stupa*. Among the new items unearthed were two Sanskrit inscriptions in the Southeast Asian style of Brahmi, engraved on narrow rectangular shale-bars, and three life-sized stucco Buddha images. The statues were set in recesses of the laterite projections that marked the cardinal directions of the *stupa*. Both inscriptions bore three verses of the *Sagaramatipariprccha*, similar to three other Buddhist textual artefacts previously discovered in the Bujang Valley. This article focuses on the archaeological contexts of these new inscriptions and, with reference to the three previously found artefacts, describes their significance to the Buddhist practices of Ancient Kedah during the second half of the first millennium CE. By treating the Kedah epigraphs as a whole, taking into account their local contexts and broader cultural connections, they add to growing evidence that Ancient Kedah was an active participant in contemporaneous exchanges of ideas and practices within the larger framework of Asian Buddhist networks, while retaining its distinctive and autonomous features.

## Summary

This article reports on the 2023–24 excavations at the Bukit Choras stupa site in Kedah's Bujang Valley, which revealed two new Sanskrit inscriptions bearing verses from the *Sāgaramatiparipṛcchā*, a Mahāyāna sūtra whose original Sanskrit text is otherwise lost. These join three previously discovered inscriptions of the same type from the Bujang Valley, bringing the total to five. The authors examine the archaeological contexts of the new finds, their palaeographic features, and their relationship to the broader corpus of Buddhist textual artefacts from Ancient Kedah, including the *ye dharma*, *ajñānac ciyate karma*, and *Bodhigarbhalankaralakṣa-dhāraṇī* inscriptions.

The core argument is that the consistent presentation of these three specific verses on narrow, tapering rectangular shale bars—installed in stupa structures—represents a distinctive ritual practice unique to the Bujang Valley, with no parallel in India or elsewhere in South or Southeast Asia. The authors posit that this points to a mature, indigenous Buddhist monastic community that selected these verses independently and developed its own installation ritual over several centuries. Palaeographic analysis shows the inscriptions were produced by different scribes across a span of roughly three centuries, indicating sustained practice rather than a one-off event. The differential use of sandhi between the earlier and later inscriptions further suggests a degree of Sanskrit textual scholarship maintained locally.

The article situates these findings within the wider network of Buddhist textual circulation across Asia, demonstrating that Ancient Kedah was both an active participant in pan-Asian Buddhist exchanges and a site of distinctive local innovation. The authors argue that the earlier phase of Buddhist presence in the Valley likely catered to foreign traders and travellers at the three economic centres (Sungai Muda, Sungai Bujang, and Sungai Batu), while the Bukit Choras site—located away from these centres—suggests the possible presence of a resident monastic community.

### Key Findings

- Two new *Sāgaramatiparipṛcchā* inscriptions (Bukit Choras II and III) were recovered from the southern and northern recesses of the stupa in September 2023 and May 2024 respectively, both engraved on rectangular shale bars of local Mahang formation, measuring approximately 7.65cm and 7.60cm in length (pp. 13–16).
- The stupa structure has five projections on each side, with life-sized stucco Buddha images in *dhyāna* posture set in the cardinal recesses; the northern statue measured approximately 90.0cm in height and 71.5cm knee-to-knee (pp. 10–11).
- Palaeographic analysis dates the five *Sāgaramatiparipṛcchā* inscriptions from the Bujang Valley across a span from the 6th–7th centuries CE (SB1B) through the 8th–9th centuries CE (Bukit Choras II and III), indicating continuous practice over several centuries (pp. 22–23).
- The three-verse combination from the *Sāgaramatiparipṛcchā* is known to survive only in the Bujang Valley; no parallel exists in South or Southeast Asia, and the practice of inscribing verses on narrow tapering rectangular bars appears unique to this region (pp. 19–20).
- The site was first reported by Lieutenant-Colonel James Low in 1847 and subsequently excavated by H.G. Quaritch Wales circa 1936–37, who recovered the Bukit Choras I (*ye dharma*) inscription and identified the laterite stupa platform measuring 6.86m × 7.16m (pp. 8–9).
- The site was saved from quarrying in the 1980s when Kamaruddin Zakaria's survey prompted the Kedah state government to withdraw permits and place it under the *Akta Benda Purba 1976* (Antiquities Act 1976) (p. 10).

### Conclusion

The authors conclude that the Bukit Choras inscriptions demonstrate that Buddhism in Ancient Kedah was not merely a transient phenomenon catering to foreign traders and travellers, but was sustained by a local monastic community with genuine expertise in Sanskrit textual studies. The unique ritual of installing *Sāgaramatiparipṛcchā* verses on tapering rectangular bars into stupas represents an indigenous creative contribution to Buddhist practice that flourished over several centuries, reflecting both participation in pan-Asian networks and distinctive local autonomy.

## Context

- The study draws on the Centre for Global Archaeological Research (USM) excavation records, the Asian Civilisations Museum collection (for the Site 2 and Bukit Choras I inscriptions), and the National Heritage Department of Malaysia (for SB1B).
- Historiographically, the article extends Peter Skilling's work on Kedah's Buddhist epigraphy and challenges the earlier assumption that hilltop sites in the Bujang Valley were archaeologically unproductive—a prejudice dating from Quaritch Wales's 1940 report that persisted for nearly a century.
