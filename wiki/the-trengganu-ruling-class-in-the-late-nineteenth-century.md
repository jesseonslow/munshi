# The Trengganu ruling class in the late nineteenth century

Shaharil Talib (writing as L.R. Robert) published this article in 1977 in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, mapping the social architecture of the Trengganu ruling class on the eve of formal British political control in the late nineteenth century. The central argument is that the ruling class comprised four distinct but interlocking elements—royalty, aristocracy, religious scholars, and court favourites—whose internal hierarchies, marriage strategies, and institutional roles were sufficiently well-defined to constitute a coherent social stratum that survived the imposition of colonial administration.

## Summary

The article sets out to recover the indigenous social structure of Trengganu from beneath the dominant narrative of British-imposed change. Robert begins from the 1917 Trengganu Constitution, which codified the boundaries between *Kerabat Diraja* (royalty) and *Kerabat Am* (aristocracy) in five categories of kinship to the ruling or former Sultan, and then works backward through British official correspondence, local genealogical records, and the observations of early colonial observers to reconstruct how these categories functioned in practice. The *Kerabat Diraja* was anchored by the Trengganu royal house but was significantly expanded through the absorption of royal lineages from Rhio-Lingga and Singapore, whose members married into the Trengganu court or settled permanently in the state. The *Kerabat Am*, though numerically larger, was kept deliberately separate from the royal family: the Sultan's daughters never married local aristocrats, and the royal house maintained a posture of superior dignity vis-à-vis both the local aristocracy and neighbouring sultanates.

The *Ulama* formed a third, hereditary element whose authority derived from religious scholarship rather than royal blood. Robert identifies three principal lineages—the Pulau Manis, Pulau Duyong, and Paluh families—each of which maintained a religious centre, produced successive *Sheikhul Ulama* and *Muftis*, and forged marriage alliances with the *Kerabat* class. The Sayyids of Paluh, in particular, commanded a reputation for sanctity that extended across the East Coast and gave them a quasi-sacred authority distinct from their formal religious offices. The fourth element, the *Orang Keistimewaan* (court favourites), were common-born officials who rose through personal service to the Sultan. Robert traces the career of Hj. Ngah Muhammad b Yusof in detail as a case study of how a court favourite could accumulate enough influence to displace *Kerabat Diraja* contenders for the highest office, while also noting the more spectacular but rarer example of Abdul Rahman b Isahak, who rose from *Budak Raja* to Datuk Lela after carrying out a politically motivated killing in 1885.

Throughout, the article draws on British official dispatches, the Bucknill Commission Report of 1918, H. Clifford's contemporary accounts, and locally compiled *salasilahs* (genealogical trees) to illuminate the internal dynamics of a class that was small in absolute numbers but exercised disproportionate control over the state's political and religious life.

### Key Findings

- The ruling class numbered approximately 1,000 males in a total population of around 100,000; the 1911 Census recorded only 184 government officials (pp. 23–24).
- The 1917 Constitution, Chapter 21, defined *Kerabat Diraja* membership through five categories of kinship to the ruling or deceased Sultan, establishing that once a person qualified, the status was permanent (pp. 22–23).
- Sultan Zainal Abidin III (r. 1881–1918) maintained three formal marriages and a number of *gundeks* (concubines); British agent J.L. Humphreys reported in 1918 that the Sultan had 37 ex-wives in town (p. 29).
- Approximately 6,000 debt slaves existed in Trengganu in 1917; each *Kerabat* household held an average of 5 to 10 domestic debt slaves, and the ruling class strongly opposed abolition until British pressure forced legislative measures in 1919 (p. 24, n. 14).
- Tukku Sayyid Paluh (Sayyid Abdul Rahman b Sayyid Al-Idrus), the most influential *Ulama* of the late period, died on 6 September 1918 at the age of over 100, leaving more than 100 descendants including great-great-grandchildren (p. 43, n. 84).
- Hj. Ngah Muhammad b Yusof was appointed Naib Mentri Besar in 1917, displacing *Kerabat Diraja* contenders, and held the full office of Mentri Besar until his death on 28 June 1940 (pp. 44–45).

### Conclusion

Robert's definitive takeaway is that the Trengganu ruling class, though a microscopic minority, was internally cohesive in its commitment to perpetuating the Sultanate. The four elements competed for limited offices and privileges but were united in maintaining a system from which each derived its status and power. The Sultanate had faced rebels but never revolutionaries, and the same component groups that constituted the pre-colonial ruling class were found holding high positions in the administrative structure imposed by the British, indicating that colonial rule grafted a new bureaucratic layer onto an existing social skeleton rather than replacing it.

## Context

- Primary archival collections include Colonial Office files (C.O. 273 series), High Commissioner's Office files, the Bucknill Commission Report (1918), the 1911 Census, and locally compiled *salasilahs* provided by Trengganu families and the historian Hj. Muhammad Saleh.
- Historiographically, the article extends J.M. Gullick's model of West Coast Malay ruling classes to the East Coast, adapting his three-part division (royalty, aristocracy, commoners) into a four-part framework that gives independent analytical weight to the *Ulama* and *Orang Keistimewaan* as distinct social categories rather than subsuming them under a generic "commoner" heading.
