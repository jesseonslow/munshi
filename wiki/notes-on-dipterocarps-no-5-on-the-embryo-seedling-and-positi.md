# Notes on Dipterocarps. {No. 5} On the embryo, seedling and position of the flowers in various species

Raimy Ché-Ross's 2008 article re-examines Munshi Abdullah's brief 1848 *syair* describing Singapore's New Year's Day carnival, arguing that the poem reveals a more complex and critical dimension of Abdullah's thought than his conventional reputation as a blind Anglophile suggests.

## Summary

The article provides a modern standard Malay transliteration and English translation of Abdullah's 96-line poem (24 four-line stanzas), originally published in 1848 in Logan's *Journal of the Indian Archipelago and Eastern Asia*. The poem records Abdullah's eyewitness observations of the annual New Year's sports and games held at the Padang in Singapore on 1 January 1848, including horse races, sack races, greasy pole climbing, and coins tossed into mud for scrambling crowds (pp. 49–51).

Ché-Ross situates the poem within the broader colonial practice of staging public spectacles to entertain and pacify native populations, drawing on comparative examples from British Africa and other Malayan civic celebrations. She then challenges the conventional reading of Abdullah as a sycophantic collaborator, arguing that the poem's opening praise of Queen Victoria follows established formulaic conventions of loyal address, while its middle stanzas contain pointed criticism of both the colonial exploiters and the native participants who degraded themselves for pittance (pp. 53–59).

The article also speculates on the poem's publication circumstances, suggesting it may have been inserted to fill a gap in the journal's collation, which would explain its abrupt termination mid-narration and its absence from the volume's table of contents (p. 63).

### Key Findings

- The poem consists of 96 lyric sentences forming 24 four-line stanzas, occupying two pages (pp. 81–82) of Volume II (1848) of Logan's journal, printed in type-set Jawi font (p. 63).
- The annual New Year's carnival at the Padang had been held since at least 1834, organised by Singapore's mercantile community 'to amuse the natives only' (pp. 50, 52).
- The poem is absent from the volume's table of contents and index, strongly suggesting a last-minute insertion to fill a collation gap (p. 63).
- The *Straits Times* excused its lack of coverage of the 1848 celebrations, citing the more pressing obligation of preparing the outward mail (p. 50).
- The poem was published to commemorate the 160th anniversary of its original appearance (p. 49).
- Abdullah's contemporary Sheikh Abdullah bin Muhammad al-Misri employed a similarly Juvenalian tone in his *Hikayat Mareskalek*, praising Daendels while embedding sharp subtextual criticism of the Javanese (pp. 54–55).

### Conclusion

Ché-Ross concludes that Abdullah was neither a blind collaborator nor a proto-nationalist, but a complex figure who could simultaneously praise and critique the colonial order, and that his lesser-known topical *syair* deserve renewed scholarly attention as evidence of a more assertive and challenging literary personality than the binary nationalist/collaborator framing allows.

## Context

- Primary archival sources consulted include the original Jawi text at the National Library of Australia (Canberra) and Cambridge University Library Or. 853 (*Syair Singapura Terbakar*).
- The article contributes to the historiography of transitional Malay literature, applying subaltern-studies methodology (via Guha) to argue for closer attention to Abdullah's shorter topical works rather than his dominant memoirs.
