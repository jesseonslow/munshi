# Province Wellesley

## Province Wellesley

Province Wellesley was a British colonial settlement established in 1800 on the north-western coast of the Malay Peninsula, encompassing the coastal plain and riverine interior of what is now the state of Kedah. Named after Sir Henry Wellesley, brother of the Duke of Wellington, the settlement extended from the Merbok estuary in the south to the Patani frontier in the north, and inland to the foothills of the Perak highlands. It was administered as part of the Straits Settlements under the Lieutenant-Governor of Prince of Wales' Island (Penang), and its population was a composite of Malay cultivators, Chinese miners and traders, Jawi-pakan intermediaries, and Siamese-descended communities in the north-eastern villages [[1, pp. 198–200]](#ref-1). The settlement's economic base rested on rice cultivation in the alluvial plains of the Muda and Krian rivers, tin mining in the interior, and its position as a node in the ancient maritime trade network that connected the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea [[1, pp. 175–178]](#ref-1), [[2, pp. 188–189]](#ref-2).

## Scope and Historical Definition

Province Wellesley was formally separated from the Kedah Sultanate in 1800 following a treaty between the Sultan of Kedah and the British East India Company, which ceded the coastal strip from the Merbok to the Patani border in exchange for a British guarantee of the Sultan's throne [[1, pp. 178]](#ref-1). The settlement was initially administered as a dependency of Penang, with a Resident Councillor and later a Lieutenant-Governor exercising authority over the coastal and riverine districts. The interior, particularly the upper reaches of the Krian and Perak rivers, remained under the nominal suzerainty of the Perak and Patani chiefdoms, creating a complex frontier zone where British jurisdiction was contested and often nominal [[3, pp. 19–21]](#ref-3).

The settlement's population was ethnically and socially stratified. The Malays formed the great mass of the population, particularly in the cultivated plains, where they held small land lots and practised wet-rice cultivation [[1, pp. 199–200]](#ref-1). The Chinese, chiefly Hokkien shopkeepers and hawkers and Kwang-Tung paddy planters and rice dealers, were concentrated in the towns and along the rivers, with little social connection to the Malays but increasingly intermarrying with the needier classes [[1, p. 199]](#ref-1). The Jawi-pakans, descendants of Indian Muslim settlers from Kedah, occupied a distinctive intermediary position: they served as police inspectors, rate collectors, land measurers, shroffs, and Malay writers, and maintained hereditary connections with the Kedah court [[1, p. 198]](#ref-1). The Samsams, descendants of inland Siamese who had converted to Islam, formed the majority of inhabitants in many north-eastern villages where Siamese remained the current language [[1, p. 200]](#ref-1).

The administrative structure was characterised by a tension between the formal British legal framework and the informal power of local intermediaries. The *Kali* (religious judge), a figure drawn from Islamic jurisprudence, exercised compulsory jurisdiction over divorce and maintenance cases with no legal authority under the Settlement's statutes, charging substantial fees and widely accused of corruption [[1, pp. 190–192]](#ref-1). The *Penghulu* (village headman) system, intended to provide local governance, was structurally defective: headmen were appointed by the *Penghulu Besar* (police inspectors) without government authority, many were of bad reputation or unable to write, and they had no systematic arrangements for united action in emergencies [[1, pp. 174–175]](#ref-1).

## Security, Governance, and Social Control

The security challenges of Province Wellesley in the 1860s were acute and multifaceted. The settlement was exposed to disturbances from three principal sources: the quarrels of Chinese secret societies, the rivalries of Malay *jumahas* (religious congregations attached to mosques), and the existence of professional banditti in the adjacent Malay states, particularly Perak, which Logan described as "wretchedly misgoverned by a number of Rajas and district chiefs striving with each other who shall excel in habitual rapacity and occasional rapine" [[1, p. 185]](#ref-1). Gang robbery was endemic in the scattered rural population, where a gang of ten or twenty armed robbers could march through the most populous villages, plunder houses, and retire with complete impunity [[1, p. 174]](#ref-1).

In August 1867, J.R. Logan, a long-resident lawyer and ethnographer who had lived in the Muda districts for five years, submitted a memorandum to Lieutenant-Governor A.E.H. Anson proposing a village-based volunteer police system for the Muda and Kreh districts [[1, pp. 173–178]](#ref-1). The plan divided larger villages into *kampongs* (villages) of 20 to 30 houses, each with a *Katua Besar* (senior elder), *Katua Kechil* (junior elder), and *Kweang* (messenger), grouped into *Dairahs* (districts) under a *Penghulu Besar* and *Penghulu Muda* [[1, pp. 176–177]](#ref-1). The enrolled villagers were to be licensed to keep arms, and a system of alarm signals using gongs and wooden *tong-tong* (drums) was prescribed for rapid communication [[1, p. 177]](#ref-1). The plan explicitly excluded the *jumahas* and *mukims* (parish territories attached to mosques) from the organisational structure, to prevent religious leaders from building a "too powerful an organization" that would exert social pressure in the interest of "a stricter and more fanatical observance of Mahomedanism" [[1, p. 176]](#ref-1).

The plan was received with great favour by the villagers, who enrolled 3,663 male adults representing an estimated total population of 20,000 [[1, pp. 196–197]](#ref-1). Logan noted that both married and unmarried women held lands and other property, marriage not affecting the wife's right to her estate, so the influence of the *Katuas* and *Penghulus* embraced a much larger number of persons than appeared from the rolls [[1, p. 197]](#ref-1). The Lieutenant-Governor approved the experiment in part, and the *Penghulus* were appointed as additional constables under Section 21 of the Police Act of 1856 [[1, pp. 178–180]](#ref-1). However, the plan was complicated by the transition of the Straits Settlements from Indian to Colonial Office administration in 1867, a moment of institutional uncertainty that affected the continuity of reform efforts [[1, pp. 184]](#ref-1).

Logan's memorandum also proposed a standing international commission with the Kedah-side *Penghulu Besar* at Kota to regulate ferries, prevent the passage of criminals and stolen property, arrest fugitives, and prevent smuggling and cattle trespass along the shared river boundary [[1, p. 178]](#ref-1). He further argued that the system would subserve the broader objective of gradually educating the Malay population, supplying them with practical legal and administrative knowledge to protect them from the exactions of unscrupulous intermediaries [[1, pp. 193–195]](#ref-1).

## The Interior: Geography, Mining, and the Perak Frontier

The interior of Province Wellesley, beyond the cultivated plains, was a region of dense forest, riverine valleys, and tin-bearing hills that remained largely unexplored by Europeans until the 1870s. The Krian river valley, in particular, was a zone of complex political and economic activity where Chinese mining communities operated under the truck system, bound to Malay landlords by debt and obligation [[3, pp. 8–11]](#ref-3). The tin-smelting technology at Salama and Intan was primitive, and the economics of mining were exploitative: at the Intan mines, Chinese miners paid $24 per *bhara* (a unit of weight) to the Patani chief, received opium at the same price, and sold their tin at Baling for $40 per *bhara* against a Penang price of $62, yielding the Patani government a profit of $16 per *bhara* [[3, pp. 58–59]](#ref-3).

The most significant event to bring the interior into the colonial record was the murder of British Resident J.W. Birch in 1874 and the subsequent campaign to capture Datoh Maharaja Lela, the chief suspect. In March–April 1876, W.E. Maxwell, a political officer and former Stipendiary Magistrate of Province Wellesley, led an expedition of forty Malay followers from Butterworth up the Krian river to Salama, crossing the "thirty-three hills" pass (*Bukit Tiga Puloh Tiga*) on foot to reach the Perak river at Kota Tampan, a strategic hilltop position never before visited by a European [[3, pp. 4–19]](#ref-3). The expedition covered approximately 200 miles of previously unexplored terrain and produced the first European geographical account of the route from the Krian river to the Patani border [[3, pp. 66–67]](#ref-3).

Maxwell's journal is as much an ethnographic and geographical document as a military narrative. He recorded the customs and physical characteristics of the Sakai aborigines, the legendary traditions of Jambai including a solar myth of Che Puteh Jambai, and the complex boundary history between Perak and Patani centred on the Intan and Endah mines [[3, pp. 23–26]](#ref-3). The Patani Malays settled in Ulu Perak at Tampan, Lunggong, and surrounding villages were a distinct community—honest, quiet, and industrious by Maxwell's account—who had been systematically oppressed by Perak chiefs and actively welcomed British protection [[3, pp. 19–20]](#ref-3). The expedition failed in its primary objective: Lela's escape was caused by the accidental coincidence of Maxwell's arrival at Kampong Padang with a village mourning feast, a guest carrying the warning to Lela, who waded the Perak river and abandoned most of his property [[3, pp. 42–43]](#ref-3). Nevertheless, the identification of Si Tuah, one of the five proscribed co-conspirators, as a captive slave at Salama, confirmed upon Maxwell's return to Province Wellesley, led to his trial and condemnation to death [[3, pp. 31]](#ref-3).

The expedition's geographical findings directly fed into the 1877 government survey of Ulu Perak and the subsequent Stanford & Co. map of the Peninsula, making Maxwell's journal a foundational source for the cartography of northern Perak's interior [[3, p. 67]](#ref-3).

## Ancient Settlements and the Archaeological Record

Province Wellesley and the adjacent Kedah coast formed the core of the ancient Indianised city-state identified in Chinese and Tamil sources as Kataha or Langkasuka, a major entrepôt in the maritime trade network connecting the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea from at least the seventh century [[2, pp. 188–189]](#ref-2). The settlement's footprint extended along the Merbok and Muda river systems, and in early historical times the coastal plain would have consisted of islands, mangrove swamps, and sandy *permatangs* (coastal ridges) rather than the continuous plain visible today [[2, p. 189]](#ref-2).

The archaeological investigation of this region began with the survey of Quaritch Wales in 1936–37, which examined twenty-nine sites and established a tentative brick-size chronology [[2, p. 189]](#ref-2). The 1957 excavation campaign by the University of Malaya Archaeological Society, reported by Michael Sullivan, extended the known spatial and chronological range of the settlement and revealed a religious landscape more complex than previously recognised [[2, pp. 188–219]](#ref-2). At Matang Pasir, Sullivan demonstrated that a rectangular shrine with a west-facing orientation, surrounding processional path, and stupa-core block was Buddhist rather than Hindu—a significant correction, since all previously excavated shrines in the Kedah complex had been identified as Hindu [[2, pp. 189–192]](#ref-2). The associated bronze finds, including a Buddha head 11⅛ inches high and a lotus flower, pointed to a Mahayana Buddhist revival in the ninth or tenth century under Sailendra influence [[2, pp. 190–192]](#ref-2).

At Tikam Batu, a large carved sandstone pedestal was reclassified from Wales's suggestion of a fire altar to the *vedika* (enclosure) of a lingam, on the basis of its close formal resemblance to Cham pedestals of the seventh to ninth centuries [[2, pp. 199–200]](#ref-2). The site was dated between the tenth and fourteenth centuries on the basis of Sung–Yuan celadon and stoneware found at the wall base [[2, pp. 198–202]](#ref-2). The most speculative finding came from Kota Aur, where two small rectangular brick structures of a type unknown in Malayan or Indian temple architecture were excavated; the discovery of a crushed iron vessel containing perished organic material as a foundation deposit led Sullivan to tentatively identify them as tombs, possibly of Jain patriarchs, drawing an analogy with the tombs at Moodbidri in South India [[2, pp. 213–215]](#ref-2). If confirmed, this would introduce a new religious dimension to the Kedah complex and would be the first such find in the region [[2, p. 215]](#ref-2).

It is worth noting that the Tikam Batu hill had been systematically dynamited by the Public Works Department to provide stone for the approaches to the new Muda River bridge, destroying the majority of the site before the 1957 excavation could begin [[2, p. 197]](#ref-2). This episode illustrates the tension between colonial infrastructure development and the preservation of archaeological heritage that characterised the region throughout the twentieth century.

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Province Wellesley spans nearly a century and reflects shifting priorities from administrative and ethnographic concern to archaeological investigation. The earliest substantial contribution is Logan's 1867 memorandum, published posthumously in the *JSBRAS* in 1885, two years after his death [[1, p. 202]](#ref-1). The document is a primary source of exceptional value: it provides a detailed account of the social, economic, and political conditions of the Muda districts in the 1860s, the structure of Malay village life, the operations of the *Kali* and *jumahas* systems, and the dynamics of Chinese secret society violence. Colonel Yule's tribute, appended to the publication, describes Logan as having carried "to his too early tomb a vaster knowledge of the races and regions of the Indian Archipelago than any one else is likely to accumulate in our day" [[1, p. 202]](#ref-1). The document was printed but not formally published in Penang in 1863, and its appearance in the journal in 1885 ensured its wider circulation [[1, p. 202]](#ref-1).

Maxwell's 1882 journal of the 1876 expedition represents a different mode of engagement with the region: a military-geographical account that nonetheless produced foundational ethnographic and cartographic material [[3, pp. 1–67]](#ref-3). The article on the "Antiquities of Province Wellesley" by W.E. Maxwell, published in the first volume of the *JSBRAS* in 1878, is known only by its title page in the archival record, and its full text is not available [[4, p. 114]](#ref-4). Its existence, however, indicates that archaeological interest in the region was active as early as the 1870s, predating the systematic surveys of the twentieth century.

The archaeological turn is marked by Sullivan's 1958 report, which represents a methodological shift from the descriptive and administrative concerns of the nineteenth-century literature to a rigorous, typologically grounded investigation of the ancient settlement [[2, pp. 188–219]](#ref-2). Sullivan's work corrects earlier identifications, reasserts Kedah as Kataha following Braddell and Nilakanta Sastri, and introduces the first architectural evidence for Buddhism and the first tentative case for Jainism in the Kedah complex [[2, p. 219]](#ref-2). The article draws on the excavation records of Quaritch Wales and I.H.N. Evans as its primary comparative corpus, and on the 1955–56 aerial and ground surveys as its immediate precursor [[2, p. 219]](#ref-2).

The gap between the 1880s and the 1950s in the Society's published literature on Province Wellesley reflects the broader shift in colonial and post-colonial research priorities: from the administrative and ethnographic concerns of the Straits Settlements period to the archaeological and historical investigations of the independent Malayan states. The region's significance as a site of early Indianised urbanism, its complex multi-ethnic social structure, and its contested frontier geography continue to make it a subject of sustained scholarly interest.
