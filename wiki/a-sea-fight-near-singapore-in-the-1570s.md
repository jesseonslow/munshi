# A sea fight near Singapore in the 1570’s

I.A. MacGregor (1956) reconstructed the first of three naval engagements between the Portuguese and the Achinese off Singapore, fought on 1 January 1577 during the captaincy of Mathias de Albuquerque, and demonstrated that this battle — and its two successors — had been entirely absent from Malayan historiography since 1800 because they do not appear in Diogo do Couto's *Decades*. Drawing on three sixteenth- and seventeenth-century sources, two of them manuscript, MacGregor established the sequence of events, resolved contradictions between the witnesses, and placed the fight within the broader context of Achinese naval dominance in the Singapore and Johore waters of the 1570s.

## Summary

The article addresses a conspicuous gap in the historiography of Portuguese Malacca. Between 1564 and 1585, Achinese war fleets operated with increasing freedom in the straits and rivers of the Malay Peninsula, and by the mid-1570s they could cruise the approaches to Singapore without opposition from Johore, which had itself turned against the Portuguese. When reports of the 1573 Achinese siege of Malacca reached Lisbon, the Portuguese government dispatched Mathias de Albuquerque as Captain-Major of the Seas of Malacca. He arrived in December 1576 with a small fleet and immediately sailed for the Singapore Straits to intercept the annual China trading vessel, where he encountered the Achinese armada.

MacGregor's central methodological task is to reconcile three sources that disagree on critical details: the Jesuit Amador da Costa's narrative (printed in Francisco de Sousa's 1710 *O Oriente conquistado*), an anonymous *Vida de Mathias de Albuquerque* preserved at Évora, and Manuel Godinho de Eredia's 1615 *Historia dos serviços com martírio de Luis Monteiro Coutinho* in the National Library at Lisbon. The biographer and da Costa agree on the date (1 January 1577) and on the broad shape of the engagement — a prolonged bombardment, a sudden shift in wind, a Portuguese charge, and a premature lull that allowed the Achinese to escape. Eredia, by contrast, dates the battle to 1 January 1578 and introduces inconsistencies that MacGregor attributes to confusion with a later combat off Changi Point. The certificate of Antonio Moniz Barreto, reproduced by Eredia, provides an independent official record that MacGregor uses to adjudicate between the witnesses on matters of fleet composition and casualties.

Beyond the battle itself, the article situates the engagement within a sequence of Portuguese operations in the Singapore and Johore area. After the initial victory, Albuquerque attempted to intercept the retreating Achinese fleet among the islands but failed; a subsequent expedition by dom Henrique Bendarra into the Kedah river yielded further prizes. MacGregor notes that the biographer's claim that the Achinese were kept from the seas for years is overstated, since the accession of a new ruler in Aceh in 1579 and the breakdown of the Aceh–Johore alliance created new conditions that Albuquerque sought to exploit with a planned (but unrealised) conquest of Aceh in 1580.

### Key Findings

- The Portuguese fleet comprised 12 vessels (one galleon, two galleasses, three galleys, six foists) per Moniz Barreto's certificate; da Costa gives 13 (three high-sided vessels, three galleys, seven foists); the *Vida* lists 12 with named captains (p. 11, n. 33).
- The Achinese fleet numbered 150 sail, of which 40 were royal galleys with two rows of oars; the *Vida* gives a total of 115 vessels broken down as 36 royal galleys, 50 galliots/foists/launches, and 29 light craft (pp. 12–13, n. 38, n. 50).
- The Achinese were armed with spheres and basilisks firing projectiles "as large as a man's head"; the Portuguese possessed only two spheres (p. 13).
- Portuguese casualties: 13 killed according to da Costa; 10–12 killed and many wounded according to Moniz Barreto's certificate (pp. 15, 17).
- Achinese losses: 1,600 killed and captured per da Costa; 300 Moslems dying per Moniz Barreto's certificate (pp. 15, 17).
- In a follow-up operation, dom Henrique Bendarra burned one *lanchara* and one galley in the Kedah river and captured three galleys carrying pepper and 23 pieces of artillery, three of which bore the Portuguese arms (p. 18).

### Conclusion

MacGregor's definitive takeaway is that the battle of 1 January 1577, while tactically successful, did not break Achinese naval power in the region. The biographer's claim of a prolonged lull in Achinese activity is contradicted by the political changes at Aceh from 1579 onwards, and Albuquerque's own subsequent operations — including the unrealised plan to conquer Aceh in 1580 — show that the Portuguese remained on the defensive. The article's lasting contribution is the recovery of a narrative that the standard Portuguese historiographical tradition (Couto, Faria e Sousa) simply omitted, and the demonstration that the *Vida* and da Costa's account, read critically against Eredia's unreliable text, yield a coherent and credible picture of the engagement.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: Évora Public Library and District Archives (Codex CXV/1–13, *Vida de Mathias de Albuquerque*; Codex CXVI/1–26, *Descripcam dos cercos de Malacca*); National Library, Lisbon, Fundo Geral no. 414 (Eredia manuscript); Arquivo Nacional da Torre do Tombo, S. Lourenco 4 and Livraria no. 320.
- Historiographical contribution: The article fills a gap identified by Tiele (1881) — the absence of events at Malacca between 1575 and 1580 in Couto's *Decades* — and provides the first modern critical edition of the battle narrative, collating three independent witnesses and resolving their discrepancies through internal consistency and meteorological reasoning.
