# Divination

## Divination

Divination, the practice of seeking knowledge of the future or of hidden matters through supernatural or ritual means, has been documented across the Malay Peninsula and among Chinese communities in the Straits Settlements and Malaya. The ethnographic record preserved in the Society's journal reveals a diverse landscape of divinatory techniques, ranging from the simple observation of natural phenomena to complex symbolic systems rooted in cosmological philosophy. These practices served functions that extended well beyond mere fortune-telling, encompassing the resolution of disputes, the validation of social bonds such as marriage, and the protection of domestic and commercial spaces. The evidence, though drawn from relatively brief field observations and descriptive notes, illuminates the coexistence of indigenous Malay, Chinese, and Islamic elements within a single cultural milieu, and raises questions about the boundaries between religious observance, popular superstition, and practical social mechanism.

### Malay Divinatory Practices

The earliest systematic documentation of Malay divination in the Society's literature appears in a note by I. H. N. Evans, who recorded two distinct methods during fieldwork in the early 1910s [[1]](#ref-1). The first, observed at Lenggong in Upper Perak in 1913, involved the floating of needles on water. The procedure required that needles be dried and then placed upon the surface of water in a bowl without breaking the surface film. The interpretation hinged on a binary outcome: if the needles came together, the divination foretold that a betrothed woman's marriage would endure; if they remained apart, it predicted an eventual divorce [[1, p. 247]](#ref-1). Evans noted that a similar practice existed in India, suggesting either a shared South Asian substrate or independent convergence of the technique [[1, p. 247]](#ref-1). The method is notable for its reliance on a physical phenomenon—the behaviour of light objects on a liquid surface—framed within a ritual context that赋予 it prophetic significance. Its application to the specific social question of marital durability indicates that divination in this context functioned not as a general oracle but as a targeted instrument for resolving uncertainty in a matter of considerable personal and social consequence.

The second method, observed in Pahang, was a form of pendulum divination employed to identify a thief. A gold ring was tied to a long hair taken from a woman's head and suspended over a basin divided into eight compartments, each bearing the name of a suspected thief. A blind man held the free end of the hair and recited a Muslim prayer; the ring was then expected to swing and touch the compartment of the guilty party [[1, p. 247]](#ref-1). On the occasion Evans witnessed, the ring swung but did not strike any compartment, leading to the conclusion that the thief was not among those named [[1, p. 247]](#ref-1). Several features of this practice merit attention. The use of a blind man as the operator removed the possibility of conscious manipulation, lending the procedure an appearance of objectivity that would have been socially important in a context where accusations of theft carried serious consequences. The incorporation of a Muslim prayer into the ritual indicates the integration of Islamic devotional practice into a divinatory framework that may otherwise have predated Islam in the region. The eight-compartment structure of the basin, while not explicitly linked by Evans to any cosmological system, echoes the numerical and spatial logic found in other divinatory traditions in the region.

### Chinese Divinatory Systems

The Chinese contribution to the divinatory landscape of Malaya is documented in a note by W. G. Stirling, who described both the physical instruments and the symbolic architecture of two related practices [[2]](#ref-2). The first, the divining blocks, consisted of pairs of kidney-shaped pieces of bamboo root used in temple worship. The ritual procedure was straightforward: the worshipper passed the blocks through incense smoke, posed a yes-or-no question, and threw them to read the answer from their landing position. Both blocks landing convex (designated "Yin") or both landing flat (designated "Yang") indicated a negative response, while one of each orientation indicated an affirmative [[2, p. 72]](#ref-2). The system thus reduced all questions to a binary outcome, a feature that Stirling implicitly contrasts with the more elaborate interpretive frameworks available in other Chinese divinatory traditions. The use of bamboo root as the material for the blocks, and their kidney shape, are details that Stirling records without further elaboration, leaving open questions about the symbolic significance of the form and the choice of material.

Stirling's more substantial contribution is his description of the *Pat Kwa*, or eight-sided diagram, a symbolic structure divided into eight sections of three lines each, based on the Yin and Yang principle [[2, p. 72]](#ref-2). He records its widespread use as a protective charm over doors, on geomancers' compasses, and in priestly robes, indicating that its function extended far beyond divination proper into the realms of apotropaic magic and spatial orientation [[2, p. 72]](#ref-2). Stirling lists the eight trigrams with their compass directions and symbolic meanings, and records the legend that the diagram was revealed to Fuh Hi in 2850 B.C. on the back of a tortoise [[2, p. 73]](#ref-2). This legendary attribution, while not a historical claim, reflects the traditional Chinese understanding of the diagram's origin as a cosmological revelation rather than a human invention. The *Pat Kwa* thus represents a system of considerable complexity, one that integrates cosmology, geomancy, and protective ritual into a single symbolic framework, and whose presence in Malaya testifies to the depth of Chinese cultural transplantation to the region.

### Comparative Observations

The two bodies of evidence, though drawn from different ethnic communities and different decades of fieldwork, permit several comparative observations. Both the Malay and Chinese traditions employ binary or near-binary interpretive structures: the Malay needle divination distinguishes between two outcomes (together or apart), and the Chinese divining blocks reduce all questions to a yes-or-no answer [[1, p. 247]](#ref-1), [[2, p. 72]](#ref-2). This convergence may reflect a common cognitive preference for reducing uncertainty to a manageable number of outcomes, or it may be coincidental. The Malay pendulum method, with its eight compartments, and the Chinese *Pat Kwa*, with its eight trigrams, both employ the number eight in a structurally significant way, though the two systems appear to be independent of one another [[1, p. 247]](#ref-1), [[2, p. 72]](#ref-2). The integration of Islamic prayer into the Malay pendulum ritual and the use of incense smoke in the Chinese block-throwing ritual both indicate that divinatory practice was embedded within, and reinforced by, broader religious observance [[1, p. 247]](#ref-1), [[2, p. 72]](#ref-2).

It is worth noting that neither Evans nor Stirling offers any commentary on the social status of the practitioners or the degree to which these practices were regarded as legitimate or superstitious by the communities in which they were observed. Both authors adopt a descriptive rather than evaluative stance, recording what they witnessed without passing judgement on the efficacy or rationality of the techniques. This restraint is characteristic of the ethnographic approach of the period, in which the observer's role was to document rather than to interpret.

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on divination is, by the standards of the broader ethnographic record, modest in volume but significant in its coverage of distinct cultural traditions. Evans's 1923 note and Stirling's 1924 note, published in successive volumes of the *JMBRAS*, together provide the only systematic documentation of divinatory practice in the journal's early years [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2). Both are brief—Evans's note occupies a single page, and Stirling's spans two—and both are descriptive rather than analytical, reflecting the conventions of the period in which short field notes were a standard form of contribution [[1, p. 247]](#ref-1), [[2, pp. 72–73]](#ref-2).

The chronological proximity of the two publications suggests that divination was a topic of active interest within the Society during the early 1920s, though no sustained programme of investigation is evident. The absence of subsequent articles on the subject in the available record may indicate either a decline of interest or a shift in the Society's priorities toward other areas of Malayan studies. The two notes, taken together, establish a baseline of ethnographic knowledge that later researchers could build upon, but they also reveal the limitations of the early approach: the reliance on single observations, the lack of comparative context, and the absence of any discussion of the social, economic, or political dimensions of divinatory practice. The records, in short, document what was done and how it was done, but they do not yet address why it was done, by whom, and with what social consequences.
