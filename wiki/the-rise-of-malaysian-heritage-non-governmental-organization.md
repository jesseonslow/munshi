# The rise of Malaysian heritage non-governmental organizations (1969‒2005

## Abstract

Two key Malaysian heritage NGOs were founded in the mid-1980s, Badan Warisan Malaysia (BWM) in Kuala Lumpur in 1983 and the Penang Heritage Trust (PHT) in 1985. Other NGOs, such as the Consumers’ Association of Penang (CAP, formed in 1969), Institut Masyarakat (Community Institute, mid-1970s) and Aliran (1977) had previously pursued heritage conservation as part of a broader agenda they termed ‘sustainable development’, opposing a development model used by town councils and state governments, as well as property developers, which replaced heritage buildings with shopping complexes, office towers, and tourist theme parks. This article traces the history of Malaysia’s heritage NGOs and their involvement in decades of ‘heritage battles’ prior to the passing of Malaysia’s National Heritage Act in 2005, reviewing the impact of their ideas of ‘sustainable development’ across several decades.

## Summary

This article traces the origins and development of Malaysia's heritage non-governmental organizations from the late 1960s through 2005, filling a significant gap in the literature where, as Blackburn notes, no comprehensive cross-national study of these organizations existed and no scholarly work had been devoted to the Kuala Lumpur-based Badan Warisan Malaysia (BWM) despite its activity since 1983. The narrative begins with the Penang-based social justice NGOs of the 1970s—Consumers' Association of Penang (CAP), Institut Masyarakat, and Aliran—which treated heritage conservation as one component of a broader critique of the state's development model. From these, specialized heritage bodies emerged in the 1980s: BWM in Kuala Lumpur (1983) and the Penang Heritage Trust (PHT) in 1985, each adopting a distinct strategic posture.

The core argument is that these NGOs developed and deployed a concept of "sustainable development" as an ideological framework that allowed them to challenge the state's growth-oriented urban renewal without being dismissed as anti-development. Through a series of "heritage battles"—the Komtar demolition, the Bukit China dispute, the Penang Hill development threat, the Hotel Metropole demolition—civil society progressively shifted the balance of power, moving from external opposition to active engagement with government planners. Blackburn distinguishes between the activist, community-mobilizing model dominant in Penang and the conservative, resource-rich National Trust model adopted by BWM in Kuala Lumpur, arguing that both approaches were necessary: the former generated public pressure and political will, while the latter supplied the technical expertise and elite access that made large-scale conservation and the 2008 World Heritage listing possible.

The article draws heavily on primary organizational documents—BWM annual reports, PHT newsletters, Aliran Monthly, and CAP's Utusan Konsumer—alongside newspaper coverage and interviews with key figures including Lim Teck Ghee, Khoo Salma Nasution, Ahmad Sarji, and Siti Zuraina Abdul Majid. These sources allow Blackburn to reconstruct not only the public-facing campaigns but also the internal strategic debates within the NGOs, such as the PHT's 1986 forum on whether to be "apolitical" or a "pressure group," and the BWM's deliberate cultivation of elite connections under Ahmad Sarji's presidency from 1995.

### Key Findings

- The Bukit China heritage battle (1983–85) involved a 42-hectare cemetery containing 12,500 graves; the Melaka state government issued a RM2-million quit rent bill in June 1984, and the DAP submitted a petition signed by 300,000 people on 3 November 1984 before Prime Minister Mahathir intervened to defuse the communal tensions (pp. 56–58).
- The Komtar urban renewal project in George Town entailed the demolition of 22 acres comprising 304 pre-war shophouses to make way for a 65-storey tower; CAP's criticism of the project as an architectural "monstrosity" and "white elephant" established the template for subsequent heritage battles (pp. 53–54).
- BWM received RM238,760 in donations in 1986, spent RM150,000 acquiring and restoring Istana Tengku Long in Terengganu, and spent RM195,149 on restoration of historic buildings it did not own in the same year (p. 60).
- The Penang Hill development threat (1990–92) involved 900 acres proposed for luxury hotels, condominiums, and theme parks; the "Friends of Penang Hill" coalition mobilized 40,400 petition signatures, and the project was ultimately abandoned after the Department of the Environment rejected the developer's environmental impact assessment (pp. 64–65).
- The demolition of the Hotel Metropole on Christmas Day 1993, in defiance of a municipal protection order, carried a maximum fine of only RM48,000; this incident directly catalyzed the formation of a heritage advisory committee with the Penang Municipal Council and intensified pressure for national legislation (pp. 65–66).
- The 2005 National Heritage Act rejected BWM's key recommendation for a powerful National Heritage Conservation Corporation with powers comparable to the National Trust of Britain, instead vesting most authority in a single civil servant (the Heritage Commissioner) whose powers were further constrained by the requirement for state-level agreement before gazetting any site (pp. 72–73).

### Conclusion

Blackburn's definitive historical takeaway is that the period from the 1970s to 2005 marked the rise of Malaysian heritage NGOs from positions well outside government decision-making to positions of genuine influence on state action, with the 2005 National Heritage Act and the 2008 joint World Heritage inscription of George Town and Melaka as the culminating achievements. The NGOs' "sustainable development" framework served as the essential ideological bridge that enabled this engagement, allowing them to deflect the charge of being anti-development while progressively extracting legislative, financial, and institutional concessions from the state.

## Context

- Primary archival collections include the BWM Annual Reports and Memorandum and Articles of Association (Chen Voon Fee Resource Centre, Kuala Lumpur), the Bukit China and Cheng Hoon Teng Temple Records Collection (Centre for Malaysian Chinese Studies, Kuala Lumpur), PHT Newsletters, Aliran Monthly, and Utusan Konsumer, supplemented by interviews with Lim Teck Ghee, Khoo Salma Nasution, Ahmad Sarji, and Siti Zuraina Abdul Majid.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in extending the existing scholarship on Melaka (Cartier, 1997) and Penang (Jenkins, 2008; Nagata, 2010; Cheng et al., 2014) to include Kuala Lumpur and the BWM, and in providing the first integrated account of how the three cities' heritage movements interacted with and reinforced one another over four decades.
