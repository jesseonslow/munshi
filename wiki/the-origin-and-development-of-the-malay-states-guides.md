# The origin and development of the Malay States Guides

Abdul Karim bin Bagoo, writing in 1962, traces the institutional evolution of the Malay States Guides from their origins as a small body of Indian sepoys in Larut, Perak, in 1873 through their disbandment in Aden in 1919. The article argues that the Guides emerged organically from the practical security demands of colonial Perak—first as an ad hoc police force, then as a professional military regiment—and that their eventual dissolution was driven by a convergence of political, military, and administrative factors rather than any single cause.

## Summary

Bagoo's article is a comprehensive institutional history that follows a single security force through four distinct phases: the Larut Police and Resident's Guard (1874–1876), the Perak Armed Police (1877–1884), the First Battalion Perak Sikhs (1884–1896), and the Malay States Guides (1896–1919). The central historical problem he addresses is how a colonial security force evolved from an improvised police unit into a professional military regiment, and why that regiment was ultimately dissolved. The narrative is shaped by the practical challenges of governing a rapidly developing tin-mining state whose Chinese population grew from 4,000 to 25,000 in a single year after the Pangkor Engagement, creating constant pressure on a small and often under-resourced force to maintain order, collect revenue, and project military authority simultaneously.

The article's core argument is that the separation of civil and military functions was the decisive structural transformation in the force's development. Under Swinburne and Walker, the Perak Armed Police and Perak Sikhs performed an ever-widening range of public services—fire brigade, registration of births and deaths, vehicle and dog registration, census-taking, and tax collection—alongside their core policing and military duties. By the 1890s this dual role had become unsustainable, and the Federation Agreement of 1896 provided the mechanism for splitting the force: the Guides took on purely military duties while the civil police function was absorbed into the FMS Police. Bagoo also explores the tension between military efficiency and political reliability, a tension that came to a head during World War I when the Guides' loyalty was questioned following the 5th Light Infantry mutiny and the Punjab disturbances of 1919.

The narrative is built almost entirely from administrative records: Straits Settlements despatches, Perak Annual Reports, the *Malayan Police Magazine*, and Hugh Low's Diary. Bagoo uses these sources to reconstruct the force's organisation, strength, deployment, and operational history in considerable detail, while also drawing on personal accounts (Swettenham, Walker, Abbott) to illuminate the human dimension of the force's development.

### Key Findings

- Captain Speedy enlisted 110 discharged sepoys at Calcutta in 1873 for Larut; by the end of 1874 the force had grown to 266 men, and the Chinese population of the Taiping area had surged from 4,000 to 25,000 in twelve months (pp. 51–53).
- In the Taiping riot of 3 October 1879, the Perak Armed Police killed 28 Chinese and wounded many others; Walker reported the men fired "with the coolness and precision of the steadiest, best trained and most disciplined troops" (p. 58).
- The force was renamed the First Battalion Perak Sikhs in 1884; under Walker its strength grew from 576 (1882) to 768 (1883), and in December 1883 Walker recruited 113 sepoys and 15 sowars from Fane's Horse in India (pp. 59–60).
- The Malay States Guides were formed on 1 September 1896 with 18 officers and 600 NCOs and men, organised into five infantry companies of 100, one artillery company of 50, and a depot company of 50; by 1904 the regiment numbered approximately 900 men (pp. 69–70).
- In their first engagement at Aden on 12 January 1916, the Guides lost 9 killed and 19 wounded while repulsing a Turkish infantry attack; they served continuously in the Aden Field Force from October 1915 until November 1918 (pp. 75–76).
- The regiment was disbanded in Aden at the end of 1919; men with more than ten years' service were pensioned, the rest received gratuities, and younger members could join the Indian Army or be absorbed into the FMS Police (p. 77).

### Conclusion

Bagoo concludes that the Guides' disbandment was the product of multiple converging pressures: the doubts cast on the regiment's reliability by the 1914–1915 loyalty incidents and the Punjab riots of 1919, the loss of its traditional recruiting ground in the Punjab, the chronic difficulty of attracting and retaining officers at the regiment's pay levels, and—most decisively—the political decision to raise a Malay Regiment, a proposal urged by the Sultan of Perak and the Yam-Tuan of Negri Sembilan since 1913 and finally implemented in 1933.

## Context

- The article is a revised version of Bagoo's B.A. (Honours) thesis from the University of Singapore, edited by C. Gibson-Hill and K.G. Tregonning; Bagoo himself held the rank of Sergeant (p. 51, n. 1).
- Primary sources include Straits Settlements despatches (S.S.Des.), Perak Annual Reports, the *Malayan Police Magazine* (1928–1929), Hugh Low's Diary, and documents from the Public Record Office, London. Historiographically, the article is one of the earliest dedicated institutional histories of a Malayan military force, predating the more comprehensive studies of the FMS Police and the Malay Regiment that followed in the 1970s and 1980s.
