# Some account of the independent native states of the Malay Peninsula, especially of the circumstances which led to the more intimate relations recently adopted towards some of them by the British Government

Frank A. Swettenham, a senior British colonial officer in the Straits Settlements, published this two-part account in 1880 to document the state of anarchy in the independent Malay states between 1871 and 1875 and to justify the British policy of installing Residents in Perak, Selangor, and Sungei Ujong. The overarching thesis is that the chronic piracy, succession disputes, and internal warfare that had rendered the western Malay states ungovernable left no viable alternative to direct British administrative intervention under the guise of advisory assistance.

## Summary

Swettenham's account is structured as a state-by-state survey of the independent Malay polities on the western coast of the Peninsula, with particular depth devoted to Perak and Selangor. The central historical problem he addresses is the collapse of indigenous governance: in Perak, the succession dispute between Raja Muda Abdullah and the Sumatran-born Bendahara Ismail, compounded by the Menti of Larut's ambition to break the hereditary line of succession, had produced a "War of Extermination" between the Si Kuan and Go Kuan Chinese factions that reduced Larut from a prosperous district of 20,000–30,000 inhabitants to a "wilderness" of pirates and stockades. In Selangor, the Viceroy Tunku Dia Udin's prolonged struggle against the outlawed Rajas Mahdi and Mahmud had similarly depopulated the Klang and Selangor districts, while the small inland states of Sungei Ujong and Rambau were locked in a perpetual cycle of river piracy and cattle-lifting that made the Linggi river impassable to trade.

The article's core argument is that the Pengkor Engagement of January 1874—brokered by Governor Sir Andrew Clarke—represented not an act of imperial aggression but a necessary and even requested response to conditions that threatened British subjects and the security of the Straits Settlements themselves. Swettenham emphasizes that the Resident system was proposed by the Malay rulers themselves: Abdullah had begged Governor Sir Harry Ord in 1872 for an officer to teach him governance, and Tunku Dia Udin and the Klana of Sungei Ujong each explicitly requested and offered to defray the costs of a British Resident. The narrative is informed by Swettenham's own eyewitness observations of Larut in January 1874, his direct participation in the Pengkor negotiations, and his subsequent administrative experience in the region, giving the account the character of a contemporary official's report rather than a retrospective analysis.

A secondary thread concerns the unresolved difficulties that persisted even after the initial pacification: Abdullah's personal incapacity and unpopularity, the continued opposition of Ismail and Jusof, the Menti's financial improprieties, and Raja Mahdi's intransigence over Klang. Swettenham uses these to argue that the Resident system, while successful in stopping piracy and restoring revenue, was insufficient for the "permanent well-being" of the states and that a more advanced policy would eventually be required.

### Key Findings

- Larut's revenue had fallen from approximately $200,000 per annum at the height of its prosperity to near nothing during the War of Extermination; by January 1875, under British supervision, it was producing $30,000 per month (p. 168, 180).
- The Pengkor Engagement of January 1874 formally recognized Abdullah as Sultan of Perak, conferred the title "Ex-Sultan" on Ismail, and established a British Resident to collect revenue and advise the Sultan; it also rectified the boundary between Province Wellesley and Krian and confirmed the Dindings and Pulau Pengkor as British territory (p. 182).
- Lukut's revenue collapsed from nearly $200,000 per annum under its last Raja to $5,000 per annum under his sons' administration, illustrating the fiscal consequences of internal misrule (p. 180).
- The Johor–Pahang boundary was fixed by arbitration in 1867 at the Indau river, with the right bank assigned to Johor and the left to Pahang, and seawards along the parallel of latitude 2° 39′ 20″ (p. 200).
- The Menti of Larut and Tunku Dia Udin of Selangor were each indebted to the extent of $300,000–$400,000 from their respective military campaigns, with no prospect of repayment absent years of peace (p. 174).
- The Klana of Sungei Ujong's attack on the Bandar's village of Kapayang in 1874 resulted in the flight of his entire force of 500 men at the mere appearance of Raja Mahmud, necessitating the dispatch of a small body of Straits troops to protect the Government officer (pp. 194–195).

### Conclusion

Swettenham's definitive historical takeaway is that the Resident system, while having achieved its immediate objectives of stopping piracy and restoring revenue, had reached a point of diminishing returns in Perak due to Abdullah's proven incapacity and the entrenched opposition of the Ulu Chiefs. He argues that the Government must "carefully re-consider Perak affairs" with a view to a "more advanced policy" that would ensure the permanent administration of the state, while cautioning that any such advance must not neglect the eastern states under Siamese protection, with whom friendly relations should be maintained.

## Context

- Swettenham was a serving British colonial officer in the Straits Settlements at the time of writing (the text is dated 1 June 1875); he had personally visited Larut in January 1874 and participated in the Pengkor negotiations, making this a contemporary administrative account rather than a detached scholarly study.
- The article was published in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society (Vol. 6, 1880), five years after the events it describes, and functions as both a justification of the Pengkor policy and a briefing document for future decision-makers on the trajectory of British involvement in the Malay states.
