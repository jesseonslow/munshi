# The “Che Wong” revisited. maps

Nathan Porath's 2011 article examines the *Hikayat Patani*—a six-book Malay manuscript composed between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries—as a mimetic text that encodes the kingdom of Patani's power relations with Ayutthaya and Johor within the hierarchical Indic political order of pre-modern Southeast Asia. The central thesis is that the text simultaneously affirms Patani's mimetic similarity with Ayutthaya (even while narrating rebellions against it) and fractures its resemblances with Johor at every narrative juncture, revealing that Patani's political prestige was derived in part from its relationship with the northern Siamese kingdom.

## Summary

Porath applies René Girard's theory of mimetic desire and Stephen Harrison's concept of "fracturing resemblances" to interpret the first book of the *Hikayat Patani*. He argues that the text's dual mimetic strategy—affirming kinship with Ayutthaya while denying kinship with Johor—reveals an unstated recognition that Patani gained prestige from its position within the Ayutthaya mandala, which gave it an elitist confidence vis-à-vis other Malay-speaking states to the south. The founding legends of the text, including the Encik Tani skin disease story and the king's conversion to Islam, function as social contract metaphors (following Jordaan and Josselin de Jong) that encode the conditions of political legitimacy: the kingdom's unity depended on its Islamic character, yet the converted king retained his Indic political identity and cosmic title (pp. 49–50).

The article then traces the two major rebellions against Ayutthaya. The first, under Sultan Mudhaffar Syah (c. 1564), is reinterpreted not as a secessionist act but as a failed usurpation within the Indic frame of mimetic kingdom-hood, set against the backdrop of the Burmese threat to Ayutthaya. Porath identifies a key Malay phrase—"dari pada sebuah negeri baiklah dua" (rather than one country, two is better)—as an indirect reference to the Ayutthaya–Burmese war and the fear that the two kingdoms would be merged into "one golden country" under Pegu's domination (pp. 55–58). The second rebellion (c. 1636) was triggered by the illegitimate accession of King Prasat Thong, whom the Patani queen Raja Ungu refused to recognize, rejecting her Thai royal title in favour of the Islamic title Paduka Syah Alam (pp. 60–61).

The article also addresses the political devolution of power from Patani's centre to the prime ministers of Sai and later to the Kelantanese dynasty, and the text's portrayal of Patani's decline under foreign rule as a decline in cultural civility. Porath cross-references the *hikayat*'s claims with van Vliet's dispatches, the Ayutthaya chronicles, the *Sejarah Melayu*, and the Javanese poem *Desawarnana* to test its historical reliability and situate it within the broader political geography of the peninsula (pp. 51–53).

### Key Findings

- The *Hikayat Patani* manuscript was written in 1839 and comprises six books composed at different periods between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries, following the end of the original founding dynasty (p. 47).
- Sultan Mudhaffar Syah's attack on Ayutthaya (c. 1564) is confirmed by the Luang Prasoert chronicle, which records the sultan riding one of King Cakkraphat's white elephants in the palace plaza immediately after the Burmese king departed for Pegu (p. 56).
- King Prasat Thong mobilized 60,000 men for the punitive expedition against Patani in the 1630s, headed by the Governor of Ligor, Okya Kalahom, the Pra Klang, and Rabisit, a Muslim official; the king also requested armed ships from the Dutch in Batavia (p. 61).
- Van Vliet's 1644 dispatch to Batavia reported that war was imminent between Patani and Johor and that the Johor raja's mother and other relatives in Patani were massacred; a subsequent dispatch noted a Patani envoy had arrived in Johor to settle the differences (p. 54).
- Bradley's revision dates the Kelantanese deposition of Raja Kuning to 1649 (not 1688 as Teeuw and Wyatt suggested), with the King of Kelantan installing his own son in 1651 with Ayutthaya's support (p. 52).
- The *Desawarnana* poem (completed 1365) mentions Langkasuka and Sai as separate political entities but does not mention Patani, suggesting the kingdom may not yet have been founded at that date (p. 51).

### Conclusion

Porath's definitive historical takeaway is that the *Hikayat Patani*, far from being a secessionist or ethno-nationalist text, is a political charter that affirms Patani's mimetic kinship with Ayutthaya as a precondition for its viability as a *kerajaan*. The text's refusal to moralize against Ayutthaya—even after decades of warfare—reveals that the scribes understood the kingdom's political identity as fundamentally embedded in the Indic hierarchical order shared with Siam. The ethnic identity question is raised only in relation to Johor, and solely to fracture resemblances and maintain internal political differentiation within the Malay-speaking world.

## Context

- Primary sources include the 1839 *Hikayat Patani* manuscript (Teeuw and Wyatt 1970 edition), van Vliet's *Siam* (Baker et al. 2005), the Royal Chronicles of Ayutthaya (Cushman 2006), and the *Desawarnana* Javanese poem (Robson 1997).
- The article contributes to the historiography by applying Girardian mimesis and Harrison's "fracturing resemblances" to Malay political texts, offering a structural alternative to ethnicist readings of the *hikayat* that have dominated recent scholarship on Patani's relationship with Siam.
