# The flora of Mount Ophir

H. N. Ridley's "The Flora of Mount Ophir" (1901) is the first comprehensive botanical account of the isolated mountain range in Malacca, focusing on the characteristic upper flora between 3,000 and 4,000 feet altitude. Drawing on his own two expeditions and the collections of Griffith, Maingay, Hullett, and Derry, Ridley argues that the Ophir flora is composed of three distinct elements—Malayan lowland, alpine, and a remarkable Australian component—that together point to a complex geological and climatic history for the peninsula.

## Summary

Ridley situates his work within a tradition of naturalist exploration stretching back to Griffith's 1845 ascent, the first extensive botanical collection on the mountain. He notes that earlier visitors such as Cuming, Lobb, Wallace, and Maingay all made collections but left no published botanical accounts, and that the *Flora of British India* frequently misattributed lowland plants to Ophir (p. 1–2). Ridley deliberately restricts his scope to the upper range, arguing that the lower woods differ little from similar altitudes elsewhere in the peninsula and that the upper flora is the truly distinctive element (p. 2). He describes the physical setting in detail: the ascent from Chabau through Bukit Besar to the Padang Batu rock-face, then onward to Gunong Tunduk and the summit of Gunong Ledang, noting the progressive transition from lowland forest to stunted mountain vegetation (pp. 2–4).

The core of the paper is Ridley's tripartite analysis of the Ophir flora. The first element is a Malayan lowland component of species that have ascended the hills, sometimes modified into peculiar forms. The second is an alpine element shared with other high ranges of the peninsula, including *Illicium*, *Rhododendron*, *Microtropis*, and *Burmannia disticha*. The third and most interesting element is Australian, represented by *Boeckia*, *Leptospermum*, *Tristania*, *Leucopogon*, *Dianella*, and four species of Cyperaceae in typically Australian genera (*Gahnia*, *Lepidosperma*, *Cladium*) (pp. 6–7). Ridley extends this observation beyond Ophir, listing typical Australian genera found along the peninsula's mountain ridges and sandy coasts, and proposes that these are relics of a flora that once grew on the sandy shores of a sea which washed the foot of the mountain before its denudation (pp. 7–8).

The species list that follows (pp. 8–28) documents the upper flora in taxonomic order, with several new species described: *Garcinia montana*, *Microtropis ophirensis*, *Argostemma hirta*, *A. equifolia*, and *Hedyotis flexuosa*. Ridley also records the extraordinary find of *Linaria alpina* on the summit, a plant previously unknown from any other part of tropical Asia (p. 6, 19). The paper closes with a brief account of animal life above Padang Batu, noting the scarcity of mammals, birds, and insects at altitude (pp. 4–5).

### Key Findings

- The Ophir upper flora contains a distinct Australian element including *Boeckia frutescens*, *Leptospermum amboinense*, *Tristania merguensis*, *Leucopogon malayanus*, and four Cyperaceae in Australian genera (*Gahnia*, *Lepidosperma*, *Cladium*), all absent from the intervening lowland forest (pp. 6–7).
- *Linaria alpina*, collected by Hullett on the summit, is the only record of this genus from tropical Asia and has never been found before or since (pp. 6, 19).
- Five new species are described from the mountain: *Garcinia montana*, *Microtropis ophirensis*, *Argostemma hirta*, *A. equifolia*, and *Hedyotis flexuosa* (pp. 8–16).
- Several species are recorded as endemic to Mount Ophir, including *Pygeum brevifolium*, *Pygeum griffithii*, *Hedyotis maingayi*, *Dilymocarpus semitorta*, *D. longipes*, *Geostachys elegans*, *Gastrochilus scaphochlamys*, *Cladium maingayi*, *Ischaemum feildingianum*, and *Polypodium malaccanum* (pp. 11–28).
- Animal life above Padang Batu is notably sparse: only tiger tracks, deer footprints, a few birds, and limited invertebrate fauna were observed, with elephants and wild dogs apparently extinct from the range (pp. 4–5).
- The flora of Gunong Mering, ascended by Ridley and Hervey in 1892, is essentially the same as that of Gunong Ledang but differs from the highlands of Perak and Selangor, more closely resembling Kedah Peak (p. 5).

### Conclusion

Ridley's definitive takeaway is that the Australian element in the Ophir flora—concentrated on the upper ridges and sandy coasts but entirely absent from the lowland forest between—constitutes a relict community. He proposes that the western peninsula underwent enormous denudation in geologically recent times, reducing what was once a continuous highland chain to the isolated hills now visible, and that these Australian-affiliated plants are survivors of a coastal or sandy-habitat flora that once extended across the region when a sea washed the foot of Ophir (pp. 5–8).

## Context

- The paper was published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, the primary venue for natural history scholarship in the Straits Settlements during the colonial period, and reflects the MBRAS's role in documenting the region's biodiversity.
- Ridley's collections from two expeditions to Mount Ophir are preserved in the Herbarium of the Botanic Gardens, Singapore, alongside those of Griffith, Maingay, Hullett, and Derry (p. 2).
