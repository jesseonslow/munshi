# The flora of Mount Ophir

John Anderson, a Malay Translator in the Penang Civil Service, composed *Political and Commercial Considerations* in late 1823 and submitted it to the Governor-in-Council in September 1824, arguing that the Siamese conquest of Kedah was an unjustifiable usurpation of an independent state and that the British bore a binding obligation to restore its exiled monarch. The work was published under government authority in 1824 in an edition of one hundred copies, and its 1962 facsimile reprint, introduced by J. S. Bastin, simultaneously serves as a corrective to the long-accepted narrative—originating with J. R. Logan—that the book had been suppressed by order of the East India Company.

## Summary

Anderson's two-part work addresses the political crisis created by the Siamese seizure of Kedah in November 1821 and the subsequent subjugation of Perak. Part First constructs a legal-historical case that Kedah was never a Siamese vassal state but an independent kingdom whose triennial sending of the *Boonga Mas* (gold and silver flower) constituted a mere token of courtesy, analogous to Siam's own tribute to China. Anderson marshals the correspondence of Captain Francis Light from 1786, the cession documents, and the testimony of contemporary officials to demonstrate that the cession of Penang was made on the express condition of British protection against Siam, and that the Supreme Government's subsequent refusal to honour that commitment constituted a breach of faith. He draws on Vattel's *Law of Nations* to frame the argument in terms of international law, and on the political philosophy of Lord Wellesley and Sir John Malcolm to justify active British intervention against Siamese aggrandisement.

Part Second provides a detailed geographical and commercial survey of the tin-producing states on the western coast from Junk Ceylon to the Lingi River near Malacca, enumerating eighty-four rivers, describing village populations, recording tin output and prices, and documenting the boundaries and chiefdoms of each state. This section functions as both a commercial prospectus and a diplomatic reference, intended to inform future negotiations with Siam and to demonstrate the economic value of the peninsula to British trade.

Bastin's introduction (pp. 4–13) constitutes a significant piece of historiographical detective work. He demonstrates that the suppression story, repeated by Logan in 1854 and accepted by all subsequent commentators including L. A. Mills, rests on the unverified testimony of defence counsel at the 1840 trial of Tengku Mohamed Saad. Anderson himself categorically denied any government recall in an 1841 statement in the *Asiatic Journal*, and Bastin identifies at least ten surviving copies across libraries in London, Oxford, The Hague, Singapore, and Taiping—far more than the single copy Logan claimed had escaped destruction (pp. 11–13).

### Key Findings

- Only one hundred copies were printed in 1824; the distribution list allocated 11 to the Court of Directors, 10 to the Supreme Government, 1 to proceedings appendices, 4 to the Governor and Council, and 33 to the Secretary's Office (p. 7).
- The Siamese fleet entered the Kedah river on 12 November 1821; the Bindahara and Laksamana were killed in the ensuing melee, and the King fled on elephants to the Prye River, arriving at British territory after five days (pp. 41–44).
- The average tin export from Penang in the seven years preceding 1822 was 16,300 piculs per annum; the price had risen from 18 dollars per picul to 22–23 dollars by 1824 (pp. 136, 160).
- Junk Ceylon's tin ore assayed at 64.5 per cent fine tin, compared with 53.5 per cent for Penang ore; the island was capable of producing approximately 500 tons annually (pp. 160, 308–309).
- Perak's population was estimated at 100,000 men (excluding women and children) in 1818; its tin output had reached 9,000 piculs in the late 1790s before the Dutch monopoly reduced exports to approximately 5,000 piculs (pp. 223–224).
- The King of Kedah's cession of Penang in 1786 was accompanied by a demand for 30,000 dollars per annum in compensation; the Supreme Government ultimately offered only 10,000 dollars for seven or eight years, which the King rejected (pp. 95, 112).

### Conclusion

Anderson's definitive argument is that the British Government, by accepting Penang on the condition of protecting Kedah and then refusing to honour that commitment, had created the very vulnerability it now sought to avoid; the Siamese conquest was not the enforcement of a legitimate tributary right but an act of aggression against a sovereign ally, and only decisive British intervention could restore the balance of power in the Straits and secure the commercial prosperity of the Settlements.

## Context

- Bastin's introduction draws on the Straits Settlements Records (India Office Library, London), the Burney Papers (Royal Commonwealth Society, London), the *Board's Collections* (India Office Library), and the trial transcript of Tengku Mohamed Saad (1840) to reconstruct the publication history and demolish the suppression myth.
- The reprint's historiographical contribution lies in making accessible a primary source that shaped Fullerton's anti-Siamese policy of 1824 and informed the Burney–Low treaties of 1826, while Bastin's bibliographical survey (identifying ten extant copies) corrects a persistent error in the secondary literature.
