# The menagerie at the Botanic Gardens

H. N. Ridley, Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, published this extensive account of the Gardens' menagerie in 1906, three years after its abolition by government order in 1903. Writing from the position of the man who had overseen the collection through its most productive decades, Ridley's overarching argument is that Singapore possessed unique climatic and economic advantages for maintaining a tropical zoological collection, and that its suppression represented a significant loss to science and public education.

## Summary

Ridley frames the menagerie's history within the broader institutional trajectory of the Botanic Gardens, which passed from the Agri-Horticultural Society to government control in 1874. The zoological component, initially managed by Mr. Krohn, grew from a modest collection of donated animals into a representative assemblage of Malay fauna by the 1890s, sustained almost entirely by donations and a modest annual expenditure drawn from the Gardens Vote. The collection's abolition in 1903 followed a failed attempt to secure government funding for improved enclosures, a decision Ridley implicitly criticises by demonstrating the collection's scientific value and public appeal.

The bulk of the article is a species-by-species account of animals kept, organised taxonomically from primates through birds to reptiles and amphibians. Ridley draws on his own direct observation and keeper records to document breeding successes, causes of death, behavioural observations, and the practical challenges of tropical captivity. A recurring theme is the argument against anthropomorphic assumptions about animal welfare: Ridley marshals numerous anecdotes—pelicans refusing to leave their enclosure, lovebirds dying in ornamental cages, civets requiring darkness—to demonstrate that animals in the Gardens generally thrived and that their preferences often contradicted European expectations. He also records several firsts in captive breeding, including the jackal, the kijang, the napu, hybrid *Macacus* crosses, and the green viper, none of which he believes had previously been achieved elsewhere.

The article also serves as a record of the collection's losses and the practical difficulties of tropical zoology: the expense of big cats (a tiger costing approximately fifty dollars monthly in food), the vulnerability of marsupials to the damp climate, the prevalence of distemper in canids, and the constant threat of escape or predation within the enclosures themselves.

### Key Findings

- The menagerie was formally abolished by government order in 1903 after an estimate for improved enclosures was rejected as too expensive; the collection had operated without dedicated government funding since 1881, with annual costs of approximately £100–£150 at its peak (pp. 133–134).
- Notable firsts in captive breeding achieved at the Gardens included the Indian jackal (*Cyon aureus*, pair presented 1895, producing five pups), the kijang (*Cervulus muntjac*, a pair producing eight young), the napu (*Tragulus napu*), hybrid *Macacus cynomolgus* × *M. nemestrinus* (first produced 1895), and the green viper (*Lachesis wagleri*, producing 5–9 live young per litter) (pp. 134, 140, 154–155, 164, 187).
- A tiger's monthly food cost was approximately fifty dollars; the tigress presented by M. Wise in 1895 lived in the Gardens until 1903, when she was sold (p. 149).
- The first *Macacus* hybrid (male K'ra × female Berok, born 1895) was transferred to the London Zoological Gardens in 1905 and was still alive at eleven years old in fine condition (p. 140).
- A python approximately 22 feet long was observed to fast for six months after consuming three chickens, then ate a pariah dog which sustained it for nine months; large pythons over 20 feet typically fed only once every six to nine months (p. 189).
- The Agri-Horticultural Society, which founded the Gardens in 1859, transferred its property to the Government in 1874; in 1878 the larger animals were disposed of to the Calcutta Zoological Gardens, retaining only birds, monkeys, and small animals (p. 133).

### Conclusion

Ridley's definitive takeaway is that Singapore was uniquely suited to maintain a tropical zoological collection at minimal cost, that the menagerie had achieved scientific results (particularly in captive breeding) unattained elsewhere, and that its abolition was a short-sighted administrative decision that deprived both the colony and the wider scientific community of a valuable resource. The article functions simultaneously as a eulogy for the lost collection and a practical handbook for tropical animal husbandry.

## Context

- Ridley wrote as the sitting Director of the Botanic Gardens (1887–1913) and the de facto custodian of the menagerie; the article is therefore a first-person institutional memoir rather than an independent scholarly assessment.
- The piece draws on Ridley's own observation, keeper records, and correspondence with donors and government officials; no external archival collections are cited, and the article's historiographical value lies primarily in preserving operational details of a collection that no longer exists.
