# The grasses and sedges of the Malay Peninsula

Dr W. Linehan's 1950 article examines a unique tortoise-shell-shaped tin ingot bearing a faint inscription, found in a tin mine at Kuala Dipang in the Kinta District of Perak and donated to the Perak Museum in 1885. The piece argues that such ingots were not currency but sacred objects cast to sanctify the commencement of new undertakings—mines or houses—and that the inscription, likely a *mantra* in Burmese or Mon script, represents a vestige of earlier ritual practices that persisted in modified form well into the colonial period.

## Summary

The article centres on a single artefact: a tortoise-shell (*kura-kura*) shaped tin ingot weighing 1 lb. 14¼ ozs, found at Kuala Dipang and presented to the Perak Museum by A. Hale, Inspector of Mines, in 1885. Its label described the inscription as "Siamese," but Weera Patana, the Siamese priest at the Sakya Muni Buddha Gaya Temple in Singapore, identified the characters as Burmese and the text as a *mantra*—a prayer with magical properties. Dr Coedes, who examined a cast and photograph, concurred that it was very likely a *mantra* and suggested the script could be either Burmese or Mon. Linehan treats this as the only known inscribed example of its type, and the only one whose inscription is neither Malay nor Chinese.

Linehan's broader argument concerns the cultural and ritual significance of animal-shaped tin ingots in the Malay world. Drawing on Laidlaw's 1904 account of Haji Mat Arshat's testimony, he explains that such ingots were known as *sulong relau* ("the eldest-born of the smelting-furnace") or *sulong kelian* ("the eldest-born of the mines"), were cast in duplicate, and were used in ceremonies attending the erection of the *tiang seri* (the main pillar of a house) before being bequeathed as heirlooms. He situates the tortoise symbol within a comparative framework: an avatar of Vishnu in Indian tradition, a symbol of longevity in Chinese tradition, and a vessel of magical potency in Malay practice. The *mantra* inscribed on the ingot, he argues, conferred the sacred properties that made it suitable for these ritual purposes.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to correcting errors in the literature. Linehan demonstrates that Laidlaw misidentified the tortoise-shell shape as *jongkong* (boat), a confusion that subsequently misled Mrs A. Hingston Quiggin in her 1949 *A Survey of Primitive Money*, who attributed the sacred and heirloom qualities of the *kura-kura* type to the *jongkong* type. He also challenges Temple's view that animal-shaped ingots served as currency, arguing instead that they were produced only for special ceremonial occasions and that their pure tin content and careful preservation reflect their sacred rather than commercial function.

### Key Findings

- The ingot weighs 1 lb. 14¼ ozs and shows no signs of deterioration, indicating a pure tin content (p. 1).
- It was donated to the Perak Museum by A. Hale, Inspector of Mines, Perak, in 1885 (p. 1).
- The inscription is identified as a *mantra* in Burmese or Mon script; it is the only known inscribed tortoise-shell ingot and the only one not inscribed in Malay or Chinese (pp. 1, 4).
- The date of manufacture cannot be later than 1826, the year James Low expelled Siamese invaders from Kampar, the district in which the find was made (p. 5).
- Haji Mat Arshat (1904) testified that such ingots were called *sulong relau* or *sulong kelian*, cast in duplicate, and used in *tiang seri* ceremonies before being passed down as heirlooms (pp. 2–3).
- In Pahang, during the month of Muharram, the four great chiefs presented tin wrought into tortoise or other animal shapes at the Ruler's court in Pekan (p. 2).

### Conclusion

Linehan concludes that tortoise-shell tin ingots were sacred objects made to commemorate and sanctify the commencement of new ventures, originally bearing a *mantra* that conferred magical properties. Over time, as the language, alphabet, racial character, and religion of the miners changed, the inscriptions were dropped, but the essential ritual significance of the form persisted into the modern era. The Kuala Dipang ingot, with its surviving inscription, is a rare material witness to that earlier phase.

## Context

- The article draws on the Perak Museum collection, Laidlaw's unpublished observations (1904), Temple's *Obsolete Tin Currency of the Federated Malay States* (1914), and Doyle's *Tin Mining in Larut* (1879).
- Historiographically, the piece corrects a persistent misidentification in the tin-currency literature (the *kura-kura*/ *jongkong* confusion) and provides a ritual-interpretive framework for animal-shaped ingots that complements the purely economic analyses of Temple and Quiggin.
