# Islam

Islam in the Malay Peninsula and Borneo constitutes one of the most extensively documented processes of religious transformation in Southeast Asian history, spanning from the earliest Arab trading contacts of the ninth and tenth centuries to the contemporary institutionalisation of *sharia*-based governance in the modern Malaysian state. The faith arrived not as a single missionary wave but through a slow, multi-generational process driven by maritime commerce, intermarriage, royal conversion, and the political leverage of Muslim courts, with the succession of great trading ports—Kedah, Pasai, Malacca, Aceh—serving as relay stations for its advance [[1, pp. 17–21]](#ref-1). Its regional prevalence is now near-total among the Malay population, yet the historical record reveals a complex palimpsest of successive faiths—animist, Hindu, Buddhist, and Islamic—each stratum preserved beneath the next [[2, pp. 3–5]](#ref-2). The economic, cultural, and political significance of Islam in the region is such that it has become, in the words of one mid-twentieth-century observer, less a creed than a "quasi-political force" and "militant brotherhood" to which the Malay is proud to belong [[2, pp. 3–5]](#ref-2).

### Coming to Southeast Asia

The earliest evidence for Muslim presence in the region is commercial rather than devotional. G.R. Tibbetts, drawing on Arab geographical texts including the *Akhbar al-Sin wa'l-Hind* (c. 851) and Ibn Khurdadhbih's *Kitab al-masalik wa'l mamalik* (c. 846–886), demonstrated that Arab and Persian merchants from the Gulf region dominated the maritime trade between the Middle East and the Far East from the rise of Islam through the Mongol period, with the trade peaking in the tenth century [[3, pp. 1–45]](#ref-3). A conservative estimate places the maximum extent of Arab trading activity in the archipelago at the longitude of 110°E, with no evidence of regular commerce with the Moluccas, the Gulf of Siam, or the interior of Borneo [[3, p. 36]](#ref-3). Two Abbasid coins discovered at Kedah, one clearly dated A.H. 234 (A.D. 848), provide material confirmation of Arab commercial presence at Kalah during the period described in the textual sources [[3, p. 33]](#ref-3).

A critical turning point was the massacre of foreign merchants in Canton in 878 during the Huang Ch'ao rebellion, which effectively severed the direct maritime link between the Gulf and China and caused Kalah (Kedah) to become the entrepôt where Muslim and Chinese vessels met [[3, pp. 19–20]](#ref-3). Tibbetts argues that the exodus of merchants from Canton led to the formation of permanent Muslim trading colonies in South-East Asia, particularly at Kalah, Lamuri, and the capital of the Maharaja of Zabaj (Sri Vijaya) [[3, pp. 19–20]](#ref-3). The Leran tombstone in eastern Java, bearing the date 475 or 495 A.H. (1082 or 1101 CE), remains the only definite archaeological proof of Muslims in Java before the fourteenth century, though whether the carver was Arab, Persian, or a local convert remains uncertain [[3, pp. 35]](#ref-3).

The question of the precise origin of the first Muslim missions to the Archipelago has generated sustained scholarly debate. G.E. Marrison (1951) challenged the prevailing Dutch theory that Islam came from Gujarat, arguing instead for a Coromandel Coast origin in southern India. His decisive evidence was chronological and institutional: Gujarat was not a Muslim polity until Alauddin Khilji subdued it in 1297, the very year Malik al-Saleh of Pasai died, and Marco Polo's 1293 description of Cambay presents it as a Hindu kingdom [[4, pp. 32–33]](#ref-4). More tellingly, the characteristic *madhab* of Malay Islam is the Shafi school, which matches the tradition of southern Indian Muslim communities but is alien to Gujarat, where the Hanafi school or Shia affiliation predominates [[4, p. 36]](#ref-4). Marrison further identified the "Ma'abri" of the *Sejarah Melayu* conversion narrative with the Arabic name for the Coromandel Coast [[4, p. 31]](#ref-4).

R.O. Winstedt's 1917 survey established the broader political and commercial framework for conversion, demonstrating that the adoption of Islam was a consequence of the economic and diplomatic position of Muslim trading communities rather than a purely spiritual phenomenon [[1, pp. 17–175]](#ref-1). He traced the pattern repeating at each major port: the marriage of Merah Silu to a Muslim princess at Pasai, the accession of Sultan Muhammad Shah at Malacca, and the conquest of Kedah by Aceh under Sultan Iskandar Muda [[1, pp. 18–21]](#ref-1). Chinese records confirm the Malays of Malacca had become Muslim by 1409 A.D., and Sultan Muhammad Shah, the first Muslim prince of Malacca, reigned 1403–1414 A.D. [[1, p. 19]](#ref-1).

In Borneo, Tom Harrisson (1972) challenged the conventional dating of c. 1500 A.D. for the Brunei Sultanate, arguing that three independent indigenous sources converge on a significantly earlier process of Islamisation beginning in the fourteenth century [[5, pp. 10–20]](#ref-5). The Idahan manuscript from Madai, Sabah, records a first Muslim convert "Abdullah" dated to 1408 A.D.; applying a 25-year generational model to the Brunei chronicles and Pigafetta's 1521 account of Sultan Bolkiah yields a first Muslim sultan's accession at c. 1400–1430 A.D.; and the Iban *tusut* genealogy tracing 24 generations back to Abang Musa yields a date of approximately 1380 A.D. for his "trading to Brunei" [[5, pp. 12–18]](#ref-5). Harrisson's provisional summary places possible first strong Islamic impacts in West Borneo at c. 1325–1350, with the sultanate and court converted extensively by c. 1400–1430 [[5, p. 15]](#ref-5).

The introduction of Islam into Champa, the Austronesian kingdom on the central Vietnamese coast, followed a distinct trajectory. Pierre-Yves Manguin (1985) demonstrated that Champa's conversion was neither a sudden event nor the product of a single vector, but the cumulative result of Middle Eastern merchant colonies, Javanese-Islamic cultural transmission, and ultimately Malay immigration [[6, pp. 1–28]](#ref-6). Two Arabic inscriptions from the Champa coast, one dated 21 November 1039 marking the tomb of Abu Kamil in pure Fatimid Kufic, constitute the earliest tangible proof of a Middle Eastern Muslim community there, though their precise findspot remains uncertain [[6, p. 1]](#ref-6). The decisive evidence for actual conversion comes from the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: by 1595, Iberian documents report "many Mahometans" and "many mosques" coexisting with Hindu temples, while the king remained a "gentile"; by 1676, M. Mahot reports that "the King and one part of the Kingdom are Mahometans," and a 1680 letter from the Champa sovereign to Batavia bears the title *Paduka Seri Sultan* [[6, pp. 8–10]](#ref-6).

### Islamic Law, Theology, and the Transmission of Knowledge

The transmission of Islamic knowledge in the Malay world was driven by teacher–pupil networks rather than by commercial or royal channels. Muhamad Ali (2006) demonstrated that in Kelantan, local *ulama* (Islamic scholars) were the primary agents of Islamisation, tracing how scholarly networks linking the state to Mecca, Egypt, Patani, and South Asia produced generations of teachers who disseminated Islamic learning through *surau* (village prayer halls), *pondok* (semi-independent residential schools), and *madrasah* (formal Islamic schools) [[7, pp. 39–58]](#ref-7). The *pondok* system, traced to Haji Abd. Samad (Tok Pulai Chondong) in 1820, was hierarchical, orally oriented, and rooted in memorisation of classical Arabic texts [[7, p. 6]](#ref-7). By 1973, Kelantan had 54 *pondok* with approximately 3,152 pupils, demonstrating the survival of the tradition alongside *madrasah* institutions [[7, p. 14]](#ref-7).

The early twentieth century brought two major transformations. The establishment of the Majelis Ugama dan Isti'adat Melayu in 1915 marked the bureaucratisation of Islamic education, creating a state-recognised class of *ulama* and *imam* and founding the Madrasah Muhammadiah, which offered a modernist curriculum in Malay, English, and Arabic [[7, pp. 10]](#ref-7). Reformist ideas from Egypt (Muhammad Abduh, Rashid Rida) and India (Shah Waliyullah al-Dahlawi, Darul Uloom Deoband) entered Kelantan through returning graduates such as Tok Kenali, who reportedly had about 300 students from across the peninsula, Sumatra, Patani, and Cambodia [[7, p. 9]](#ref-7). By the 1950s, the boundary between religious education and political engagement dissolved, culminating in the formation of PAS in 1951 and its electoral victories in Kelantan [[7, p. 17]](#ref-7).

In the modern Malaysian state, the institutionalisation of Islamic law has taken a distinct form. Ahmad Fauzi Abdul Hamid (2019) traces the progressive "shariaization" of Malay-Muslim identity—the institutionalisation of *sharia*-based values, norms, and categories into the discourse and practice of Malay religio-cultural life [[8, pp. 1–18]](#ref-8). The Federal Constitution of 1957 deliberately confined *sharia* courts to state-level jurisdiction with strict limits on their punitive powers (the "3-6-5 safeguards" capping sentences at three years, six canings, and RM5,000 in fines), but successive political pressures from the 1980s Islamic resurgence onward steadily eroded these boundaries [[8, pp. 52–54]](#ref-8). The pivotal moment came with PAS's 1990 takeover of Kelantan, which provoked UMNO into a competitive Islamisation drive that expanded *sharia* criminal offences enactments and centralised Islamic administration under the federal government through JAKIM [[8, pp. 55–56]](#ref-8). A December 2005 Merdeka Centre survey found that 72.7% of Muslims identified "Muslim" as their primary identity, far exceeding "Malaysian" (14.4%) and "Malay" (12.5%), while 57.3% supported *hudud* [[8, p. 63]](#ref-8).

### Mysticism and the Sufi Tradition

The Sufi mystical tradition in the Malay world represents one of the most intellectually rich and politically consequential dimensions of Islamic history in the region. A.H. Johns (1955) demonstrated that Indonesian mystical speculation was not a passive reception of Islamic doctrine but an active, creative engagement shaped by direct contact with Indian and Middle Eastern sources [[9, pp. 70–77]](#ref-9). The central philosophical divergence he identifies is between the *Wujudiyya* (pantheists), who held that each emanation in the Neo-Platonic chain was an aspect of God Himself, and the orthodox position, exemplified by al-Raniri, which maintained that emanations were merely reflections of the divine essence cast into non-being, illustrated by the analogy of a single candle reflected in multiple mirrors [[9, pp. 73–74]](#ref-9).

Johns' 1957 study of an anonymous collection of eighteen Sufi tracts in archaic Malay, composed at Aceh in the first half of the seventeenth century, provides the most detailed doctrinal evidence for the *Wujudiyya* position [[10, pp. 1–111]](#ref-10). The tracts employ a seven-grade emanation schema (*Ahadiyya*, *Wahda*, *Wahidiyya*, followed by the World of Spirits, the World of Ideas, the World of Bodies, and the World of Man) built upon the principle that all being is one; God's attributes and acts are identical with His Essence, and the visible world is merely a set of aspects under which the One reveals itself [[10, pp. 20–22]](#ref-10). The practical path to union with God is through *dhikr* (recitation), *murakabah* (observation), *tawajjuh* (concentration), and *mushahadah* (contemplation) [[10, pp. 27–28]](#ref-10). The seven-grade system itself appears to be of Indian origin, first crystallised in Fadl Allah's *Tuhfa al-Mursala ila'l-Nabi* (d. 1620), on which a commentary was written expressly for the people of Aceh [[10, p. 32]](#ref-10).

The political fortunes of heterodox mysticism in the region closely mirrored developments in the Mughal Empire. Johns shows that the execution of Dara Shikoh in 1658 and the demolition of Hindu temples from 1633 in India paralleled the persecution of pantheist Sufis in Aceh after the death of Iskandar Muda in 1636 [[9, p. 73]](#ref-9). Al-Raniri, a Gujerati-born polemicist who arrived in Aceh in 1637, called the *Wujudiyya* "arch-heretics," had their books burnt, and their followers executed; 'Abdul 'l-Ra'uf (d. c. 1690), by contrast, urged tolerance, warning that false accusations of heresy would "come back on ourselves" [[9, pp. 73–74]](#ref-9). Winstedt (1923) had earlier laid bare the Neo-Platonic scaffolding beneath both the heretical and orthodox positions, showing that both ultimately inherit Plotinus's triad of Absolute, *nous*, and *psyche*, transmitted to the Muslim world through the "Theology of Aristotle," diverging only on whether the emanation is a necessary unfolding of Being itself or a free act of creation [[11, pp. 314–315]](#ref-11).

The interaction between Islam and pre-existing belief systems produced a distinctive syncretic culture. Wilkinson (1957) documented that Malay demonology is composed of distinct historical strata: a Muslim upper layer (Persian and Arab *jinn*), a Hindu middle layer (Siva, Kala, Sri, Indra, Ganesha), and a primeval Indonesian base (the four Spirits of the Sea and the "Black Youth" of the land), the latter representing the gods of an aboriginal race [[2, pp. 13–14]](#ref-2). The *pawang* (wizard) is the lineal representative of the old priests of Siva; his office is hereditary, he possesses regalia, and he appeals directly to Betara Guru, yet the word *pawang* itself predates Hinduism and belongs to a still older creed [[2, pp. 38–39]](#ref-2). Wilkinson's concluding assessment is that Malay ceremonial is "a museum of dead customs kept up for the benefit of the dead": the Malay does not exchange old practices for new but adds the new to the old, preserving each as a mere formality to appease the ghostly majesty of ancient lawgivers [[2, p. 74]](#ref-2).

Among the Orang Asli, the impact of Islam has been comparatively limited. Carey (1970) noted that only about 200 Orang Asli had been genuinely converted to Islam in the preceding century, a small figure when compared to Christian and Bahá'í converts [[12, p. 156]](#ref-12). The practical demands of Islam—circumcision, dietary restrictions, five daily prayers in Arabic, and fasting—present genuine obstacles for a people whose lifestyle does not accommodate rigid schedules [[12, p. 156]](#ref-12).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Islam reveals a clear evolution in scholarly priorities across nearly a century. The earliest contributions, led by Winstedt's landmark 1917 survey, were fundamentally political and commercial in orientation, treating the adoption of Islam as a consequence of the economic and diplomatic position of Muslim trading communities [[1, pp. 17–175]](#ref-1). Winstedt's work, produced within the administrative-intellectual culture of the colonial service, drew on the Society's own Malay manuscript collection and on Dutch archaeological and linguistic publications, situating it within the collaborative Anglo-Dutch scholarly network of the period [[1]](#ref-1).

The 1950s marked a decisive shift toward theological and philosophical analysis. Johns' two studies of 1955 and 1957, the latter originating as the first doctoral thesis on Indonesian Islamic mysticism written for an English university, established the Sufi tradition as a subject of rigorous philological and doctrinal investigation [[10, pp. 3–4]](#ref-10). Tibbetts' 1957 synthesis of Arab and Chinese primary sources on pre-modern maritime trade represented a parallel effort to reconstruct the commercial history of the Indian Ocean from textual evidence, drawing heavily on the earlier French scholarship of G. Ferrand and the German editions of Hirth and Rockhill [[3]](#ref-3). Marrison's 1951 article, though shorter, was historiographically significant as a direct challenge to the Dutch scholarly consensus and an anticipation of the later shift toward taking Malay literary sources seriously as historical evidence [[4]](#ref-4).

The 1970s and 1980s saw the expansion of geographic scope and methodological pluralism. Harrisson's 1972 article on Borneo challenged the D.G.E. Hall consensus of c. 1500 A.D. by triangulating three independent indigenous source types and arguing for the methodological legitimacy of genealogical folklore in Bornean historiography where formal documentation is absent [[5]](#ref-5). Manguin's 1985 study of Champa, originally published in the *Bulletin de l'École française d'Extrême-Orient*, extended the analysis to the Vietnamese coast and emphasised Champa's proper belonging to the maritime network of Nusantara rather than to the Vietnamese or "Indochinese" sphere [[6]](#ref-6). Wilkinson's 1957 monograph, though ethnographic in method, provided the most comprehensive account of the layered religious beliefs of the Peninsular Malay, building on the foundational literature of W.W. Skeat and Snouck Hurgronje [[2]](#ref-2).

The most recent contribution, Ahmad Fauzi Abdul Hamid's 2019 article, marks a transition from historical reconstruction to contemporary political analysis, examining the institutionalisation of *sharia*-based governance and its consequences for national identity and inter-religious relations [[8]](#ref-8). The cumulative arc of the Society's literature thus moves from the commercial and political history of conversion, through the theological and philosophical analysis of Sufi doctrine, to the ethnographic documentation of syncretic practice, and finally to the critical examination of Islam's role in the modern Malaysian state.
