# Malaya in the Wu-pei-chih charts

F. A. Swettenham, a senior British colonial administrator, published this detailed travel journal in 1885, recording his sea-to-sea crossing of the Malay Peninsula from the Bernam River on the Straits of Malacca coast to the Pahang River on the China Sea coast. The account documents the first such crossing by a European from any point north of the Muar River, and serves simultaneously as a geographical survey, an administrative assessment of the Sultanate of Pahang, and a polemic for British intervention in a state Swettenham considered misgoverned.

## Summary

The journal traces a 402-mile route beginning at Kuala Bernam, ascending the Bernam and Slim rivers, crossing the main range of mountains (the true backbone of the Peninsula) via a newly traced trunk-road, and descending the Lipis and Pahang rivers to Pekan and the river mouth. Swettenham's party included Sikhs, Malay coolies, and European companions (Giles, Lister, and others), and the narrative is organised as a day-by-day diary with precise elevations, temperatures, distances, and compass bearings recorded throughout. The crossing of the watershed at Batu Gajah (3,150 feet above sea level) is described in considerable topographical detail, noting the granite geology, the drainage patterns on both flanks, and the identification of the source streams of the Pahang system.

The Pahang portion of the journal shifts from pure travelogue to political and economic commentary. Swettenham records the tax system (the *hasil banchi*, the *serah*, export duties, and the gold monopoly), the near-absence of Chinese settlement (attributed to arbitrary expropriation by chiefs), the drought conditions, and the personal governance style of the Yam Tuan (Sultan) and his Orang Besar. He interviews multiple chiefs—Toh Bakar, Imam Prang, Toh Gajah, the Orang Kaya Lipis—and consistently finds the same complaints: excessive and arbitrary taxation, absence of settled law, debt-slavery, and forced labour. The account of the Jeram Besu rapid, the caves at Kota Kelanggi, and the reception at Pekan (including the Yam Tuan's illness and the reconciliation with his brother, the Raja Muda) provide vivid ethnographic and administrative detail.

The journal closes with a programme of infrastructure: a 130-mile road from Johor Bahru to Pekan (estimated at under $150,000), a town at the Temelin-Pahang confluence, and a continuation of the existing road from Kuala Lumpur down the Pahang side of the range to Bentong. Swettenham argues that Pahang's resources—rice, gold, silk, mats, cattle—are being wasted under the current regime and that only British administrative control can unlock them.

### Key Findings

- Total measured distance of the crossing: 402 miles by water and land, against 170 miles as the crow flies from Kuala Bernam (3°50′S) to Kuala Pahang (3°44½′S) (p. 34).
- The watershed gap at Batu Gajah stands at 3,150 feet above sea level; the Briseh River source is a "very tiny rill" only a few feet from the first stream feeding the Pahang system (p. 4).
- At least three deaths from cholera occurred among the party's Malay coolies during the journey; one Bernam man died at Permatang Linggi, a girl left at Batu Talam died overnight, and a third was left too sick to move at Kuala Semantan (pp. 7, 10, 12–13).
- The Chinese population of Pekan numbered approximately 80; the total for the entire State was estimated at two to three hundred, which Swettenham cites as proof of the intolerability of the tax system (p. 31).
- The Yam Tuan's principal house at Pekan cost $25,000 to build; the "bazaar" consisted of forty to fifty hovels in two small rows (p. 31).
- Swettenham's proposed road from Johor Bahru to Pekan: 130 miles, first-class bridle-road constructible in eighteen months for less than $150,000, convertible at any time to a cart-road, tramway, or light railroad (p. 36).

### Conclusion

Swettenham's definitive historical takeaway is that Pahang, despite possessing the longest navigable river on either coast of the Peninsula, excellent soil, and considerable mineral wealth, is held in economic stagnation by the arbitrary and extractive governance of its Malay ruling class. He frames the state as a resource-rich territory whose potential can only be realised under British administrative oversight, and he presents his journey as both a geographical first and an empirical brief for the extension of colonial control to the east coast.

## Context

- Swettenham was a senior official of the Straits Settlements and a close associate of Sir Hugh Low; this journal was composed in the immediate aftermath of the 1888 British intervention in Pahang (though the text is dated 1885, it was published in the JMBRAS in a context where the case for intervention was being actively argued). His administrative perspective shapes every observation: the people are assessed for their productivity, the chiefs for their competence, and the geography for its commercial potential.
- The journal draws on no archival collection in the modern sense; it is a field document based on direct observation, interview, and time-and-compass survey. Its historiographical value lies in providing a contemporary, on-the-ground account of Pahang's internal administration at the precise moment when British policy was shifting from non-interference to active control.
