# Malay and Cham relations with the kingdom of Cambodia during and after the French protectorate (1863–2000

Virginia Matheson Hooker's obituary and appreciation of O. W. Wolters (1915–2000), published in 2001 in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, traces the life and intellectual legacy of one of the most original scholars of Southeast Asian history. The piece moves from Wolters' formative years as a Malayan Civil Service officer during the Emergency to his second career as a professor at Cornell University, arguing that his distinctive methodological approach—combining close textual analysis with historical imagination—transformed the study of early Southeast Asian history and the concept of the region itself.

## Summary

Hooker presents Wolters not merely as a prolific author but as a scholar whose work was shaped by lived experience. His seven years in the Malayan Civil Service (1938–1957), including internment at Changi, post-war labour dispute resolution, and district-level work on Chinese resettlement during the Emergency, instilled in him a conviction that historical explanations must sustain the test of practical application. This pragmatic sensibility carried through to his academic work, where he insisted on grounding interpretive claims in the minutiae of primary sources—Chinese trade records, Malay chronicles, Vietnamese annals—rather than in abstract theoretical frameworks.

The obituary highlights three major phases of Wolters' scholarship. First, his doctoral research on Srivijaya (*Early Indonesian Commerce*, 1967) reconstructed the commercial foundations of the maritime kingdom from early Chinese sources, identifying specific plants, resins, and trade patterns that others had overlooked. Second, *The Fall of Srivijaya in Malay History* (1970) applied methods of literary criticism to the *Sejarah Melayu*, treating it as a serious text product of its own time and traditions rather than a repository of factual data to be extracted. Third, *History, Culture, and Region in Southeast Asian Perspectives* (1982; revised 1999) crystallized his concept of "localization"—the process by which external cultural influences (Indian, Chinese, Islamic) were absorbed and transformed by local societies—into a framework for understanding Southeast Asia as a region defined by intra-regional relationships rather than by its position between India and China.

Hooker's own contribution to the piece, "On Translating a Pause," extends Wolters' methodological insights into the domain of literary analysis. Drawing on Bakhtin's concept of dialogic criticism, she demonstrates how the "pause" functions as a literary device in Southeast Asian texts to alert readers to shifts in meaning, and introduces the notion of an "invisible text"—the underlying belief system (Buddhist, Islamic, or cosmological) that saturates a work without being explicitly stated. Her final section describes Wolters' last, unfinished project: an experimental historical novel on Tran Vietnam written through character dialogue, inspired by Bakhtin, which he abandoned due to ill health shortly before his death.

### Key Findings

- Wolters served in the Malayan Civil Service from 1938 to 1957, holding posts including DO Batang Padang, DO Larut and Matang (Taiping), and Director of Psychological Warfare; he was awarded the OBE in 1952 for bravery after being ambushed twice in Pahang (pp. 2–5).
- His doctoral thesis, submitted to SOAS in 1962 and published as *Early Indonesian Commerce* in 1967, argued that "the expansion in trade at that time was an indigenous and not an Indian achievement" (p. 6).
- *The Fall of Srivijaya in Malay History* (1970) was the first major Western scholarly work to apply figurative and literary-critical methods to the *Sejarah Melayu*, a text other Western scholars "had been unable to penetrate" (p. 7).
- *History, Culture, and Region in Southeast Asian Perspectives* was first published in 1982 by the Institute of Southeast Asian Studies, Singapore, and revised in 1999 with a Postscript longer than the entire original text (pp. 9, 14).
- Wolters received the Distinguished Scholarship Award from the American Association of Asian Studies in 1990, the highest honour the association confers (p. 5).
- His final work, an unfinished historical novel on Tran Vietnam using Bakhtin's dialogic method, was abandoned after he fell ill following a holiday in Ireland in late 2000 (p. 17).

### Conclusion

Hooker's definitive takeaway is that Wolters' lasting contribution lies not in any single argument but in a methodological stance: the insistence that Southeast Asian texts be read as products of their own imaginative universes, that historical explanation must be testable against practical reality, and that regional studies remain vital precisely because they are grounded in detailed local knowledge rather than subsumed under global frameworks. His caution against scholarly introspection—urging engagement with scholars of other parts of the world—encapsulates the eclecticism and generosity that characterized his work.

## Context

- This obituary was written by Virginia Matheson Hooker, a fellow scholar of Southeast Asian literary and historical traditions, and includes her own analytical paper ("On Translating a Pause") as a tribute to Wolters' influence on her work.
- The piece draws on personal correspondence, family recollections (from Euteen Wolters and son-in-law Graham Gillespie), and contemporary accounts from fellow colonial officers (G. E. D. Lewis, F. Spencer Chapman) to reconstruct Wolters' administrative career alongside his scholarly output.
