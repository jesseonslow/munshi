# Malay and Cham relations with the kingdom of Cambodia during and after the French protectorate (1863–2000

Mohamed Zain bin Musa's 2001 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* traces the political, military, and social role of the Malay-Cham Muslim community in Cambodia from the establishment of the French protectorate in 1863 through the post-Khmer Rouge era of the 1990s. Drawing on Khmer chronicles, Cham manuscripts, French colonial archives, and oral tradition, the article argues that the Malay-Cham have functioned as a loyal yet distinctively identified community whose relationship with the Khmer state has been defined by mutual service, religious difference, and periodic political crisis.

## Summary

The article first establishes the deep historical roots of the Malay and Cham in Cambodia, documenting Cham arrivals driven by successive waves of Vietnamese *Nam-tien* expansion (1471, 1692–3, and 1835) and Malay migration for trade and Islamic missionary purposes from the fourteenth century onward (pp. 3–6). By the nineteenth century, the two groups had merged into a single community known to the Khmer as *Cam-Jva* (Cham-Chvea), later reclassified as *Khmer Islam*, numbering approximately 25,599 in 1874 and an estimated 200,000 by the 1990s (p. 2). Their settlements concentrated along the Mekong and Tonle Sap rivers, in Kompong Cham, Pursat, Battambang, and Kampot (pp. 6–7).

During the French protectorate, the Malay-Cham proved militarily decisive in two critical episodes: the 1864 Achar Sva rebellion and the 1865–67 Pou Kombo uprising. In both cases, the community's leader, the *Samdach*, organized Malay-Cham contingents to defend King Norodom and the French-backed order, even as the community harboured private reservations about the king's unpopularity (pp. 8–11). The French colonial administration came to regard the Malay-Cham as the most reliable military asset available, with Briere de l'Isle maintaining a standing Malay troop at Oudong specifically because "only the Malay-Cham people, under the leadership of the Samdach, were trustworthy" (p. 10).

After independence in 1953, the community faced systematic erasure of its ethnic identity, being officially designated merely as "Khmer-Islam" rather than recognized as a distinct people (p. 11). The formation of FULRO in 1964 and the creation of the 5th Special Infantry Brigade under General Les Kosem during the Lon Nol regime (1970–5) represented a partial restoration of communal standing, but the Khmer Rouge period (1975–79) brought targeted persecution on the basis of both religious affiliation and political association with the Lon Nol government (pp. 12–14). In the post-1979 period, the community rebuilt its religious infrastructure and regained political representation under the Hun Sen government (p. 15).

### Key Findings

- The Cham diaspora into Cambodia was a direct consequence of Vietnamese *Nam-tien* expansion, with major waves in 1471 (fall of Vijaya), 1692–3 (annexation of Phanrang), and 1835 (annexation of Panduranga); the Malays arrived primarily for commerce and Islamic missionary work (pp. 4–6).
- During the 1864 Achar Sva rebellion, the *Samdach* informed the French representative Doudart de Lagree that the community did not support Norodom's coronation in Bangkok, revealing a complex political calculus beneath the surface of loyalty (p. 8).
- In the Pou Kombo affair (1865–67), approximately 2,000 Malays from Chaudoc initially joined the rebels, while the *Samdach* organized a loyalist contingent of roughly 100 Malays from Kompong Luong to defend Oudong; the loyalist Malays alone held the palace when Khmer troops abandoned it on 17 December 1866 (pp. 9–10).
- The 5th BIS (Brigade d'Infanterie Speciale), composed entirely of Khmer-Islam soldiers including Cham from Phanri and Phanrang, was commanded by General Les Kosem and deployed to the worst insurgency zones during the 1970–75 civil war (p. 13).
- The Khmer Rouge regime targeted the Malay-Cham for both their religious practice (Islam being declared the "worst enemy" of the state) and their political association with Lon Nol; the intensity of repression varied by area but followed a centrally organized pattern (p. 14).
- Post-1979, the community rebuilt its religious institutions, beginning with the Madrasa Hafiz Al-Quran in Trea district (Kompong Cham province) with approximately 500 students, and expanded diplomatic ties with Muslim countries, particularly Malaysia (p. 15).

### Conclusion

Musa's definitive takeaway is that the Malay-Cham community's identity in Cambodia has been sustained by two recurring dynamics: an enduring sense of Cambodia as home, and an irreducible religious distinctiveness (Islam) that prevented full assimilation into the Khmer majority. The community's history is one of loyal service to successive regimes—Khmer kings, French administrators, Sihanouk, Lon Nol, and Hun Sen—tempered by periodic political dissent and, most severely, by the genocidal targeting of their faith under the Khmer Rouge. Their post-1979 recovery, marked by the reconstruction of mosques, madrasas, and political representation, confirms what Musa identifies as the community's core strength: Islam as the unifying factor that guarantees collective identity and social cohesion (p. 15).

## Context

- The article draws heavily on French colonial archival material held at the Archives nationales d'outre-mer (ANOM) in Aix-en-Provence, particularly the correspondence of Doudart de Lagree, Briere de l'Isle, and Pottier, as well as Cham manuscripts (e.g., CM 39(36)) that document the community's own historical narrative (pp. 8–10).
- The piece was published in the context of growing academic and diplomatic attention to Muslim minority rights in Southeast Asia, coinciding with the 1999 IKIM seminar in Kuala Lumpur and the post-Civil War reconstruction of Cambodia under the UN Transitional Authority (UNTAC) and its successors.
