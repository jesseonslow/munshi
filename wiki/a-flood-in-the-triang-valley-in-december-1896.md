# A flood in the Triang valley in December 1896

Arthur Keyser, Collector and Magistrate of Jelebu, composed this account in late December 1896, originally published in the *Negri Sembilan Government Gazette* (26 February 1897) and reprinted in JMBRAS in 2016 as part of the "Documents from Malaysian History" series. Set in the lower Triang Valley of colonial Negri Sembilan, the letter provides a rare first-hand administrative record of a major riverine flood and the emergency relief operations it triggered. Its principal value lies in capturing the scale of the disaster, the logistical dilemmas of colonial riverine administration, and the lived experience of affected communities through the eyes of the district officer responsible for their welfare.

## Summary

The document is a formal letter from Keyser to the British Resident, Negri Sembilan, reporting on the effects of the December 1896 floods in the lower Triang Valley and the measures taken for relief. The central administrative dilemma Keyser confronts is one of access: the land route to the affected village of Jerang was completely submerged, and local chiefs assured him that the river rapids were impassable by the small dug-out boats available. Despite this, he authorised two expert boatmen to attempt the river journey with rice, salt, and tobacco, accepting the risk because the alternative was to abandon the population at Jerang and the unreported village of Juntai to their fate.

The narrative then shifts to Keyser's own river journey, which reveals the flood's physical transformation of the landscape. At Pasir Panggil, an island that had existed "so long as anyone in Jelebu can remember" had vanished entirely; permanent landmarks, old tree trunks, and tanjongs had been erased or reconfigured. At Juntai, water still stood knee-deep around houses, and the local chief, Dato' Pagang Leesut, described the terrifying roar of enormous trees carried by the current. At Jerang itself, Keyser found no loss of life but a community in acute distress, with the police having used a single leaky dug-out to rescue women and children to the one nearby hill.

The letter closes with Keyser's return by the now-fordable land route, a journey of waist-deep wading that confirmed the extraordinary endurance of the two Malay messengers who had first swum the flooded path to seek help. He records that padi crops had largely survived, that relief goods were distributed with recipients' names recorded for repayment after harvest, and that the absence of boats in the village would be remedied in future.

### Key Findings

- The flood was first reported on 14 December 1896 by a Malay constable and a companion from Jerang who swam for hours to reach Kuala Klawang, reporting water four feet high inside the Police Station building (p. 155).
- At Jerang, water marks in the Police Station stood at four feet; on the return land route, water had passed through trees at heights of 15 feet (pp. 157–158).
- The island at Pasir Panggil, a permanent geographical feature, was entirely destroyed; new islands were created and old tanjongs broken (p. 156).
- Jerang contained approximately 25 widely scattered houses; all houses recently built for tuba fishing had disappeared, and an immense tree trunk floated on the site of the Resident-General's house (p. 157).
- No loss of life was recorded at Jerang, Juntai, or downstream in Pahang, though a man tending his padi nearly drowned and a woman and her children sat on a cracking roof for a night and a day (p. 157).
- An elderly resident recalled a flood of even greater magnitude approximately 14 years earlier, i.e. c. 1882 (p. 158).

### Conclusion

Keyser's account presents the December 1896 flood as a catastrophic but ultimately survivable event, its severity underscored by the complete erasure of long-standing geographical features and the near-total isolation of the affected communities. The letter functions as both a relief report and an implicit justification of the colonial administration's promptness, while the absence of any fatalities and the survival of padi crops allow Keyser to frame the episode as a managed crisis rather than a disaster.

## Context

- Keyser held the colonial appointment of Collector and Magistrate of Jelebu; the letter is a formal dispatch to the British Resident, reflecting the hierarchical reporting structure of the Straits Settlements-era colonial administration in the Malay states (p. 155).
- The document was reprinted in JMBRAS under the "Documents from Malaysian History" series, which aims to recover primary administrative sources for scholarly use; no additional archival research or historiographical argument is offered beyond the original text and three explanatory footnotes (pp. 155, 158).
