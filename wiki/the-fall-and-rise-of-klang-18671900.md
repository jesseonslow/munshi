# The fall and rise of Klang, 1867–1900

Lee Boon Thong's 2006 article traces the origins and first two decades of Petaling Jaya, Malaysia's first new town, arguing that what began as a squatter resettlement scheme in the immediate post-war period was rapidly transformed by middle-class demand, strategic infrastructure investment, and private capital into a thriving satellite city. The study draws on colonial administrative records, town planning documents, and aerial photography to reconstruct how a rubber estate on the south-western fringe of Kuala Lumpur became a model of planned urban development in Southeast Asia.

## Summary

The article situates Petaling Jaya's creation within the demographic crisis that beset Kuala Lumpur after the Second World War. The city's population surged from 175,000 in 1947 to 220,000 within five years, driven by rural-to-urban migration prompted by the communist insurgency in the countryside. Housing construction had already failed to keep pace before the war, and the Japanese Occupation had filled designated temporary housing zones with uncontrolled squatter settlements. By 1953, over 21,000 families occupied more than 14,000 squatter shacks, and the colonial administration faced both a humanitarian and a security imperative to resettle these communities, who were perceived as potential sources of communist agitation.

The Federal Town Planning Department conceived a new town modelled on the British New Towns movement, selecting a site approximately 10 km south-west of Kuala Lumpur on former rubber and mining land. The plan incorporated residential neighbourhood units of 400 houses each, an industrial zone with rail access, a commercial and administrative core, and public open spaces. However, the initial implementation in 1953 was far more rudimentary than the conceptual plan suggested: timber houses, unpaved roads, shared community water taps, and a government correspondence that frankly described the settlement as "largely a Kuala Lumpur slum clearance scheme." The industrial zone, which was to provide employment and economic self-sufficiency, proved disappointing in its early years due to water shortages and competition from closer sites.

The transformation from squatter camp to middle-class suburb was driven by several converging factors. The completion of the Federal Highway through the town in 1957 dramatically improved accessibility for car-owning commuters working in Kuala Lumpur. The Petaling Jaya Authority's aggressive marketing of residential lots at very low prices, combined with the entry of private developers and the Malaya Borneo Building Society's mortgage products, shifted the housing stock from 60-year-leasehold timber shacks to 99-year-leasehold brick bungalows. The Pioneer Industries Ordinance of 1958 and the First Malaya Five-Year Plan catalysed industrial growth, while the establishment of the University of Malaya campus nearby in 1959 added further demand. By the late 1950s, the new commercial centre around the Federal Government offices had eclipsed the original "Old Town," and Petaling Jaya had shed its squatter-resettlement identity entirely.

### Key Findings

- By 1953, 21,851 families occupied 14,089 squatter shacks in the Kuala Lumpur area; the Federal Government allocated an emergency fund of $3.1 million for the new town (pp. 3–5).
- The initial land acquisition totalled 1,437 ha, comprising 486 ha of former rubber estate, 554 ha of rubber land, and 397 ha of ex-mining land; the first 1,300 lots in Sections 1 and 2 were sold at a premium of $200 per lot with 60-year leaseholds and $50 annual quit rent (pp. 7–9).
- The Petaling Jaya Authority Ordinance (Ordinance 36) was gazetted on 11 November 1954, granting powers to acquire land and develop the area as a "satellite town" (p. 11).
- By the end of 1957, the town contained approximately 3,193 houses, 100 shops, and 28 operating factories; the population had grown from roughly 10,590 in early 1955 to 16,600 (pp. 12–14).
- Industrial lots in Section 51 were priced at 25 cents per square foot; by 1960 all lots were sold and the area had to be expanded to Section 51A. By 1966, 264 factories had been established, creating 10,000 jobs (pp. 12–16).
- By 1974, Petaling Jaya contained 13,592 dwelling units; by 1980 it had 97,208 homes over 97.2 sq km with a population approaching half a million, and was granted city status on 20 June 2006 (pp. 1, 16–20).

### Conclusion

Lee Boon Thong concludes that Petaling Jaya's original purpose as a squatter resettlement scheme was quickly superseded by the demands of an emerging middle class attracted by good planning, accessibility, and quality housing. The town's strategic position between the historic Klang Road and Damansara Road corridors, combined with the Federal Highway and railway access, made it an ideal commuter suburb. The article ultimately presents Petaling Jaya as a case study in how a colonial-era emergency measure, when combined with post-independence industrial policy and private capital, could produce a self-sustaining urban centre that exceeded its original population target of 70,000 within two decades.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on District Office Kuala Lumpur administrative files (DOKL 1951–61), Selangor State Secretariat correspondence, Colonial Office papers (CO 717/201, CO 717/178, CO 1030/279), and RAF aerial photography from 1954, supplemented by the 1955 Concannon town planning report and the 1967 McGee and McTaggart socio-economic survey.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Malaysian urban planning by documenting the formative phase of the Klang Valley's metropolitan growth and by demonstrating that Malaysia's new town tradition predates comparable developments elsewhere in Southeast Asia.
