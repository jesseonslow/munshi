# The fall and rise of Klang, 1867–1900

J.M. Gullick's 2006 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* traces the economic and physical transformation of Klang from a declining tin-transhipment port in the 1870s to a revitalised agricultural centre by 1900, arguing that the town's "rise" was driven not by its historic role as a port but by the drainage of surrounding swamp land and the improvement of communications, particularly the railway (pp. 1–2).

## Summary

Klang occupied a strategically defensible position on a ridge above coastal marshes, at the head of a narrow, winding river channel that did not give direct access to the open Straits of Malacca (pp. 2–3). Its economy was entirely dependent on the tin trade flowing from the mines around Kuala Lumpur, which Raja Abdullah had opened in 1857. The civil war of 1867–73, in which Raja Mahdi expelled Abdullah and was himself defeated by Tunku Kudin in 1870, devastated the town. The subsequent collapse of the world tin price in 1875 reduced Klang's house count from 400 to 50, and the relocation of the state government to Kuala Lumpur in 1880 removed what little administrative vitality remained (pp. 5–6, 10).

The railway, constructed between 1883 and 1886 at a cost of $700,000, injected capital into the local economy and stimulated the building of 30 brick shophouses by 1885 (pp. 7, 10–11). The Connaught Bridge of 1890, with its cast-iron screw-pile piers driven 48 to 72 feet into soft mud, connected the line to the town on the south bank (p. 7). Yet the port itself remained awkward: steamers had to ram their bows into the opposite bank to turn, and a one-way tidal system was imposed on river traffic (p. 8). The decision in 1894 to build Port Swettenham at the estuary, following Spence Moss's 1884 analysis, effectively ended Klang's future as a significant port, though the town's population recovered to 3,576 by 1901 (pp. 10, 12).

The more consequential development was agricultural. The drainage of the coastal flats, initially for coffee, attracted European planters, Javanese settlers, and Chinese smallholders to the Klang district in the 1890s. Haji Tahir's pioneering coffee estate, the Stephensons' pepper venture at Beverlac, and the land auctions of 1889–95 all fed a speculative boom that ended in the "Klang fiasco" of 1895, when it became clear that peaty soils were unsuitable for coffee (pp. 17–22). Despite this failure, the infrastructure of roads and drains laid down during the boom prepared the ground for the rubber plantations of the following decade.

### Key Findings

- Klang's house count fell from 400 in 1872 to 50 in 1878 during the tin-price slump, then recovered to 293 houses and 3,576 inhabitants by the 1901 census (pp. 5, 10–12).
- The Connaught Bridge (1890) cost approximately $100,000; its screw-pile piers were driven into mud at depths of 48–72 feet, and one cylinder "made a sudden drop of 14 feet" and disappeared entirely (p. 7).
- The railway outlay of $700,000 over three years (1883–86) was 1.5 times the average annual state revenue of Selangor for that period (p. 10, n. 48).
- Port Swettenham, built at the estuary in 1894, cost $1.1 million exclusive of the railway link; Spence Moss's 1884 site analysis shaped port policy for nearly a century (p. 10).
- The "Klang fiasco" of 1895 resulted from the government's failure to distinguish between clay alluvial and peaty soils in auction particulars, leading to abandoned plantations and a damaging reputation for Selangor land (pp. 21–22).
- By 1894, 500 Chinese and 20 Malay fishermen worked at Pulau Ketam using the *sungkor* push-net for prawns, representing a distinct local industry separate from the agricultural boom (p. 22).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that Klang's second life as an agricultural centre, rather than its first as a tin port, determined its modern character. The town's "rise" was a by-product of infrastructure investment—railway, bridge, roads, and drainage—undertaken primarily to serve the tin economy and the new port, but which incidentally made the surrounding swamp land productive. The coffee boom and its bust were a transitional phase; the real legacy was the drained, road-linked landscape that would support rubber cultivation from the 1900s onward (pp. 1, 22).
