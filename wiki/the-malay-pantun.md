# The Malay pantun

H. Overbeck, a German-born linguist and collector of Malay literature serving in the Straits Settlements, published this comparative study in 1922 in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society. The article investigates whether the Malay pantun—a quatrain in which the first couplet presents a veiled image and the second delivers the substantive thought—is a uniquely Malay form or has structural analogues among neighbouring peoples and in related literary traditions. Overbeck's central thesis is that the pantun's characteristically loose connection between its two couplets aligns it more closely with Chinese poetry than with the Indian sloka, where the illustrative image must strictly correspond to the moral that follows.

## Summary

Overbeck begins by surveying the literary traditions of the Malay Archipelago's immediate neighbours. Javanese poetry, he argues, bears no resemblance to the Malay pantun: it employs ten or more distinct metres with four to twelve lines of varying structure, and no equivalent of the pantun quatrain exists in its written literature. The Sundanese tradition, however, yields two relevant parallels. The Sundanese *pantun* is a narrative form—half sung, half recited by a bard to the accompaniment of a *tarawangsa* or *kachapi*—drawn from legend and history, and it bears resemblance to the Malay *penglipor lara* tale. More significantly, the Sundanese *sindir* is a system of pun-based veiled expressions so deeply embedded in daily conversation that, as Pleyte observed, an animated colloquy seldom passes without their use. Overbeck reproduces Pleyte's analysis of a courting *sindir* in which "a long net lying in the ship" resolves through a chain of synonym substitutions into "a marriageable maiden," demonstrating a mechanism of veiled meaning structurally analogous to the pantun's first couplet.

Turning to South and East Asia, Overbeck examines the Pali *Dhammapada* and the Sanskrit *sloka*—a four-line verse of eight syllables per line, only four of which are fixed in quantity. In both traditions the first couplet presents a natural image whose meaning is applied in the second, but the connection is always explicit and logical, often marked by the correlatives *vatha* ("as") and *evam* ("thus"). Tamil *niti-slokas* follow the same pattern. Chinese poetry, by contrast, exhibits precisely the feature that distinguishes the Malay pantun: the introductory image (the *fu* technique in the *Shi Jing*) is frequently only loosely or impressionistically connected to the thought it introduces. Overbeck quotes several stanzas from the *Book of Odes*—the green robe and yellow lining, the noisy blue-bottles on the hazels, the fish under the willow-bough—and argues that their relationship to the subsequent lines is as tenuous as that between the first and second couplets of a Malay pantun. He further notes a shared idiom between Chinese and Malay: the use of classifiers, the predominance of co-ordination over subordination, and the frequent deployment of the possessive suffix and demonstrative pronouns, all of which point to a possible Chinese influence on the pantun's structural looseness.

The article's most original contribution is its empirical demonstration of how the pantun actually functions in performance. Overbeck presents numerous pairs of quatrains in which the second couplet is identical or nearly identical while the first couplet varies, and vice versa. In one extended series—the "Anak ayam turun" (chickens descending) sequence—the first couplet follows a fixed numerical countdown while the second couplet shifts between versions, sometimes narrating a shipwreck, sometimes a lover's petition. He cites the passage from the *Pelayaran Abdullah* in which the author states that the first two lines of a pantun "have no meaning, they merely serve to carry the rhyme," and corroborates this with the testimony of a Sumatran *munshi* who declared the first couplets meaningless. The practical implication is that the pantun-singer fixes the second couplet in memory and improvises the first on the spot, drawing from a repertory of rhyme-equivalents and stock phrases.

### Key Findings

- The Sanskrit *sloka* consists of four lines of eight syllables each, only four of which are fixed in quantity (long or short at the author's option); in the *Ramayana* and *Dhammapada*, the first couplet always presents a clear, logical illustration of the moral in the second (pp. 8–9).
- The Sundanese *sindir* operates through a chain of synonym substitutions: "hento salisung gardu" resolves via the rhyme-word *sahkokol* to *sakongkol* ("gossiping"), yielding the injunction "no gossip!"—a mechanism structurally parallel to the pantun's veiled first couplet (pp. 5–6).
- In the *Alif-ba-ta* series of quatrains (procured from a Chinese collector, probably of Malaccan origin), the word *pantun* or *sa-pantun* is used in the position where modern pantuns employ *laksana* or *sa-umpama*, suggesting that the term *pantun* may originally have designated the quatrain form itself before the *seloka* (in which the same thought runs through all four lines) was distinguished from it (pp. 11–12).
- The *Pelayaran Abdullah* states explicitly: "the first two lines have no meaning, they merely serve to carry the rhyme" (*menjadi pasang-nya sahaja*); a Sumatran *munshi* confirmed this, declaring the first couplets meaningless (p. 17).
- In the "Anak ayam turun" series, the first couplet follows a fixed countdown (eleven chickens descend, one dies, ten remain; ten descend, one dies, nine remain; etc.) while the second couplet varies between versions—sometimes narrating a shipwreck at Pulau Sembilan, sometimes a lover's petition to become a captain—demonstrating that the first couplet is the stable element and the second is interchangeable (pp. 27–28).
- Overbeck identifies shared idiom features between Chinese and Malay that are absent in Javanese and Sundanese: classifiers (*ekor*, *buah*), the predominance of co-ordination over subordination, and the high frequency of the possessive suffix *-nya* and demonstrative pronouns *ini*, *itu*, *pun*—features he attributes to possible Chinese influence on the pantun's loose couplet connection (p. 16).

### Conclusion

Overbeck concludes that the Malay pantun is not a closed, uniquely Malay form but one whose structural principle—the loose, often impressionistic link between an introductory image and a substantive thought—finds its closest parallel in Chinese poetry rather than in the Indian *sloka*, where the illustration is always strict and logical. The pantun's first couplet functions primarily as a rhyme vehicle, a fact confirmed by both literary testimony and the empirical evidence of variant versions, and its characteristic looseness may owe something to the deep structural affinities between Malay and Chinese idiom.

## Context

- Overbeck was a German-born scholar employed in the Straits Settlements who collected Malay literary texts and contributed to the JSBRAS; his access to native informants (the Sumatran *munshi*, the pantun-singers whose extemporisations he observed) reflects the colonial-era practice of European administrators and residents conducting linguistic fieldwork as a sideline to their administrative duties.
- The article draws on Pleyte's editions of Sundanese *pantun* (published in the *Tijdschrift voor Indische Taal, Land en Volkenkunde*, 1906–1911), Winstedt's preface to *Pantun Melayu*, and de Hollander's grammatical works, situating it within the early twentieth-century Dutch and British scholarly apparatus for the study of Malay-language literature.
