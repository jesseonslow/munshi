# The structure of the economy of Kedah, 1879–1905

H. G. Quaritch Wales, a British archaeologist who had excavated approximately thirty sites in Kedah during the 1930s and 1940s, published this reconsideration in 1970 to reassess his "historical conclusions" three decades after his original fieldwork. The article's central thesis is that the Malay Peninsula functioned as a critical relay in the eastward spread of Indianized culture from South India to Sumatra and beyond, a role that Wales defends against the challenge posed by A. Lamb, who had argued that the Peninsula was effectively "missed out" in the early Indianization process. Wales marshals Chinese textual sources, epigraphic evidence, and—most decisively—art-historical analysis of Buddhist and Hindu sculpture to demonstrate that intensive Indian settlement and local artistic production occurred in the Peninsula from at least the fifth century onward.

## Summary

Wales opens by distinguishing between conclusions that rest directly on his archaeological excavations and those that were merely expressions of opinion on historical geography, the latter having been compromised by the speculative wave triggered by J. L. Moens' 1937 monograph on Srivijaya. He acknowledges that his earlier substitution of Chaiya for Palembang as the Srivijayan capital was untenable, but insists that the cultural interpretation of the material remains essentially valid. The bulk of the article is devoted to rebutting Lamb's 1961 paper, in which Lamb had declared that the Isthmian and Kedah sites "need not be an essential stepping stone in the eastward spread of Indianization" and had explicitly refused to integrate literary evidence with archaeology. Wales considers this methodological separation fatal to Lamb's position, arguing that the archaeologist cannot ignore textual evidence bearing directly on the subject.

Wales then constructs his case on three pillars. First, Chinese sources from the third to sixth centuries describe a populous Indian colony at Tun-sun (with five hundred merchant families and over a thousand Brahmans), close dynastic ties between Lankasuka and India, and a general intensity of Indian presence unmatched in contemporary Java, Champa, or Cambodia. Second, the epigraphic record—including the fifth-century Buddhist inscriptions of Bukit Meriam and Province Wellesley, and the sixth-century monastic rules of Wat Maheyang near Ligor—points to established Indianized communities rather than transient visitors. Third, and most importantly, the art-historical work of Pierre Dupont and A. B. Griswold demonstrates that the composite character of Peninsular Buddhist sculpture (blending Amaravati, Ceylonese, Gupta, and Post-Gupta elements) is precisely what one would expect from a local school trained under successive waves of Indian masters, not from mere importation of finished objects. Griswold's detailed analysis of the bronze Buddha from Kedah site 16A, the Pengkalan Ipoh image, and the Wieng Sra relief shows a Peninsular workshop flourishing before the end of the sixth century with workshops on both sides of the present Siam-Malaya border.

Wales extends this argument to the Hindu-Pallava phase, examining the mitred Visnu group from Takuapa and the Isthmian region, the Siva temple foundations at Kedah sites 4, 5, and 6, and the Mahayanist temple at Bukit Batu Pahat (site 8). He resolves the long-standing debate over the nine-chambered foundation caskets by identifying their origin in the Ceylonese *yantra-gala* tradition, thereby refuting Lamb's attempt to use them as evidence of Javanese cultural influence. His principal modification of earlier views is a deeper appreciation of how earlier waves of Indian influence persist within composite styles—a phenomenon he terms "limited originality"—which distinguishes the western zone of Greater India (Peninsula, Sumatra, Ceylon, Dvaravati) from the eastern zone (Champa, Cambodia, Java) where local genius produced true artistic evolution.

### Key Findings

- Chinese sources record 500 Indian merchant families and over 1,000 Brahmans resident in Tun-sun (third century), with local people giving their daughters in marriage to the Brahmans (p. 5).
- The Wat Maheyang inscription near Ligor, dated no later than the sixth century, prescribes domestic discipline for a monastery, proving an established Buddhist community in the Isthmian region (p. 9).
- The large granite Buddha of Bukit Seguntang, Palembang, must have been manufactured locally (granite being available on neighbouring Bangka), with estimates of its date ranging from the second to the sixth century A.D. (pp. 10–11).
- Griswold's art-historical analysis dates the bronze Buddha from Kedah site 16A to the sixth century, identifying a composite Ceylonese-Post-Gupta heritage produced by a local Peninsular school (p. 12).
- Two Arab coins found at Kedah site 14 are dated 234 A.H. (848 A.D.), confirming active trade connections at that date (p. 30).
- The fragmentary Lubo Tua Tamil inscription in Sumatra is dated 1088 A.D. and mentions a South Indian merchant corporation, attesting the continuation of direct South Indian commercial relations well into the eleventh century (p. 25).

### Conclusion

Wales concludes that the Malay Peninsula was an integral link in the chain of heavily Indianized territory stretching from the Mon country of Burma to Sumatra, acting as a relay for Indian cultural influences during the early centuries of the Christian era. The composite character of its art—where earlier waves survive within later acculturation without being fully absorbed—reflects a process of "limited originality" that distinguishes the western zone of Greater India from the eastern zone, where local genius ultimately produced genuinely original artistic traditions. The paucity of impressive Srivijayan architecture, he argues, should be attributed not to commercial preoccupation but to the very intensity of Indianization, which left little room for independent creative development.

## Context

- Wales was a British colonial-era archaeologist whose original excavations in Kedah (1935–1940) were conducted under the auspices of the Federated Malay States government; his 1940 publication *Archaeological Researches on Ancient Indian Colonization in Malaya* remains the foundational monograph on the Bujang Valley sites.
- The article draws on the collections and findings of the University of Malaya's post-war excavation programme (particularly A. Lamb's work at Bukit Batu Pahat), the Bangkok National Museum, and the Wat Matchemawat collection at Songkhla, and represents a significant historiographical intervention in the debate over the Peninsula's role in South-east Asian Indianization that was then being reshaped by Paul Wheatley's *The Golden Khersonese* (1961) and O. W. Wolters' *Early Indonesian Commerce* (1967).
