# Alfred Russel Wallace

Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913) was a Victorian naturalist and commercial collector whose eight-year expedition through the Malay Archipelago (1854–1862) yielded 125,660 natural history specimens, a body of work that has long been attributed to him as a solitary figure of scientific genius [[1]](#ref-1). Recent scholarship, however, has substantially revised this portrait, revealing Wallace as a quintessential Victorian collector-naturalist operating within the economic and social conditions of mid-nineteenth-century colonial Southeast Asia, whose celebrated collection was assembled with the indispensable labour of a vast network of local people, hired assistants, and European collaborators [[1]](#ref-1). His work in the region—spanning Sarawak, the Moluccas, New Guinea, and Borneo—placed him at the centre of a complex web of collecting, identification, and publication that linked Welsh industrialists, Cambridge naturalists, and Malay island communities in a shared enterprise of natural history [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3), [[1]](#ref-1).

## Early Career and the Welsh Connection

Wallace's formative years in Wales established the personal and intellectual networks that would later underpin his Bornean work. While employed as a land surveyor in Neath, he became familiar with the Dillwyn family, whose natural history collections and connections to the British Association for the Advancement of Science provided him with an early platform in the scientific community [[2]](#ref-2). His credentials were sufficient to supply a list of Coleoptera for *Materials for a Flora and Fauna of Swansea*, a work presented to every delegate at the British Association's 1848 meeting in Swansea [[2]](#ref-2). This early association with the Dillwyns proved consequential: in August 1853, Wallace attended a meeting in Swansea where he examined, alongside Hugh Edwin Strickland, the collection of birds, mammals, reptiles, and possibly shells and rocks that James Motley had sent from Labuan [[2]](#ref-2). This encounter, which had been overlooked by previous scholars including R. Elwyn Hughes, represents a previously unrecognised point of contact between Wallace and the Labuan specimen network that would later feed into the publication of *Contributions to the Natural History of Labuan* [[2, p. 117]](#ref-2).

The 1853 Swansea meeting also brought Wallace into proximity with Strickland, who by then held the position of Assistant Reader in Geology at Oxford and was known for his concentration on the systemisation of biological nomenclature [[2]](#ref-2). Strickland's death shortly thereafter—killed in August 1853 when he sidestepped a slowly approaching train on a railway cutting and was struck by a faster one he did not see—removed a key figure from the network of identification and description that Wallace's own work would later depend upon [[2]](#ref-2). The specific name *Copsychus stricklandii*, assigned to the White-crowned Shama in Motley and Dillwyn's 1855 publication, commemorates this loss: Dillwyn had written to Strickland in January 1853 announcing that he had named a new bird in his honour, a gesture that predated the geologist's death by several months [[2, p. 117]](#ref-2).

Wallace's relationship with James Motley, the other principal collector in 1850s Borneo, was one of proximity without direct encounter. Both men lived close to Swansea from 1845 to 1848, and both were present in Singapore during April, May, and September 1854, yet a letter from Motley to Wallace dated 1858, held in the British Library (Add. 46435 ff. 2–3), proves that they corresponded but never met in person [1, p. 117, n. 17]. The reasons for this missed encounter remain unexplained, though Laverty suggests that political circumstances may have been a factor [[2, p. 117]](#ref-2).

## The Malay Archipelago Expedition and Its Human Infrastructure

Wallace's collecting expedition in the Malay Archipelago, spanning 1854 to 1862, has traditionally been narrated as the achievement of a lone naturalist of exceptional ability. Van Wyhe's 2018 study challenges this hagiographic framing, arguing that Wallace was a commercial collector-naturalist whose work was fundamentally a product of colonial-era labour networks and purchasing-power asymmetries [[1]](#ref-1). The primary sources—particularly the day-by-day journals and specimen notebooks with their marginal annotations attributing finds to named individuals—provide the evidential basis for estimating that at least 1,200 local people, possibly twice that number, aided Wallace in some capacity, in addition to more than 100 Europeans, bringing the minimum total to approximately 1,300 people [[1, p. 67]](#ref-1).

The two most prominent assistants in this network were Charles Allen and Ali, each of whose contributions has been the subject of dedicated reconstruction. Allen, a young Englishman who departed England in March 1854 at the age of fourteen (not sixteen, as Wallace claimed in his autobiography), served as Wallace's collecting assistant during two separate periods: 1854–56 and 1860–62 [[4]](#ref-4). During a single collecting period between 4 August and 18 October 1855 at Simunjan, Sarawak, Allen collected 6,198 insect individuals, averaging just over one hundred per day excluding Sundays [[4, p. 5]](#ref-4). His independent 1860–62 campaign across the Moluccas and New Guinea—encompassing Ambon, Seram, Misool, Ternate, Halmahera, Morotai, New Guinea, Buru, the Sula Islands, Flores, and Solor—produced 28,438 specimens, amounting to roughly a quarter of Wallace's total collection [[4]](#ref-4). Van Wyhe's extrapolation from monthly output suggests that Allen's total contribution across both periods of employment may have reached 48,000 specimens [[1, pp. 63–64]](#ref-1). Despite this scale, all specimens preserved in British museums are credited to Wallace alone, and no species bears Allen's name [[4]](#ref-4).

Ali, the Malay assistant, presents a different but equally significant case. First appearing in Wallace's records in December 1855 at Sarawak as a personal servant and cook, Ali gradually transformed into Wallace's chief collecting assistant, only beginning to shoot and skin birds in Lombok in mid-1856 and becoming Wallace's "head man" by the Aru Islands expedition of 1857 [[3, pp. 4–7]](#ref-3). His most celebrated achievement was the discovery of Wallace's Standard Wing (*Semioptera wallacii*) on the island of Batchian on 24 October 1858, a new genus of Bird of Paradise that Wallace described as "a great prize" and a "completely new form" [[3, pp. 14–15]](#ref-3). Van Wyhe and Drawhorn estimate that Ali collected roughly 5,150 of Wallace's 8,050 bird specimens, making him the expedition's principal ornithological collector [[3, p. 24]](#ref-3). His wages, reconstructed for the first time by the authors, amounted to approximately f.9 per month—equivalent to 5 Singapore dollars or 13s. 4d.—placing him at the lowest tier of colonial labour, comparable to the ranks of the Singapore Police and native military corps [[3, pp. 24–25]](#ref-3).

The broader human infrastructure extended well beyond these two figures. At Si Munjon in Sarawak, Wallace paid a cent per insect to local Dyak and Chinese workmen, and approximately 63 people are estimated to have been directly involved in orangutan locating, hunting, and retrieval [[1, pp. 46–47]](#ref-1). The Goram island voyage alone required just over 300 men serving as boat crews across multiple vessels [[1, p. 59]](#ref-1). The costs of this labour were not without human consequence: the assistant Jumaat, a Ternate man, died of dysentery at Dorey in New Guinea—the only one of Wallace's assistants known to have died in his service [[1, p. 56]](#ref-1).

## Financial Networks and Colonial Context

The economic dimensions of Wallace's collecting enterprise have received increasing scholarly attention. Wallace's financial accounts, preserved in Notebook 5 at the Natural History Museum, London, record total payments to Allen of 4,480.64 guilders for specimens and 702.07 guilders for travel expenses, totalling 5,182.71 guilders (approximately £500 at the time) [[4, p. 17]](#ref-4). Allen's New Guinea collection alone—comprising 22 birds of paradise, 468 birds, 4,166 beetles, and 843 butterflies and moths—was valued at 1,221.38 guilders [[4, p. 13]](#ref-4). These figures illustrate the commercial character of the enterprise: Wallace was not merely a scientist but a buyer of specimens, operating within a market in which local knowledge and labour were converted into natural history collections for European institutions.

The colonial context in which this work was conducted shaped both its possibilities and its limitations. Wallace's early years in the region brought him into contact with the political structures of Sarawak, where Rajah James Brooke's authority governed the territory. A letter from Brooke dated 1 April 1854, informing Wallace that "I shall be glad to forward your wishes and to see you at Sarawak," marks the beginning of a relationship that facilitated Wallace's access to the interior [1, p. 117, n. 17]. The broader political environment also affected Wallace's movements: his presence in Singapore in 1854 coincided with the inquiry into Brooke's conduct, an event in which Motley had volunteered to appear as a witness and been subjected to three days of hostile questioning [1, p. 114, n. 9].

## Legacy and Documentation

The historiography of Wallace's Malay Archipelago work has undergone significant revision in recent decades, driven by the digitisation of archival collections and the recovery of previously overlooked sources. Laverty's 2013 article demonstrated how the interplay of Motley, Dillwyn, and Strickland as part-time collector, sponsor, and authority in the publication of *Copsychus stricklandii* is contrasted by Wallace, who combined all three roles [[2, p. 117]](#ref-2). The 1853 Swansea meeting, which linked Wallace to the Labuan specimen network, had been overlooked by earlier scholars, and its identification fills a gap in the understanding of how Wallace's Bornean knowledge was constructed [1, p. 117, n. 17].

Rookmaaker and van Wyhe's 2012 reconstruction of Charles Allen's life represents a major intervention in Wallace studies, correcting the record on the scale of Allen's independent collecting and providing the first comprehensive biography of a figure who had previously been reduced to a few lines in Wallace's autobiography [[4]](#ref-4). The authors draw on Wallace's financial notebooks, Singapore newspaper obituaries, Straits Directories, and church baptismal records to demonstrate that Allen's contribution was far more substantial than previously acknowledged [[4]](#ref-4). The irony that Wallace's own map of the Malay Archipelago includes a line for "Mr. Allen's routes" that is incomplete and partly obscured by Wallace's own travels underscores the erasure of the assistant's role in the standard narrative [[4]](#ref-4).

Van Wyhe and Drawhorn's 2015 article on Ali extends this corrective impulse to the non-European participants in colonial science, arguing that Ali was far more than a passive servant but a skilled hunter, a knowledgeable informant, and the discoverer of at least one major new species [[3]](#ref-3). The reconstruction of Ali's wages and his estimated contribution of 5,150 bird specimens challenges the assumption that Wallace's ornithological collection was his personal achievement [[3, p. 24]](#ref-3). The fact that Ali was last recorded in 1907 by the American zoologist Thomas Barbour in Ternate, where he identified himself as "Ali Wallace," and that the only known photograph of him survives in the Natural History Museum, London (WP/6/8/19), testifies to the fragility of the documentary record for non-European participants in colonial science [[3, pp. 26–27]](#ref-3).

Van Wyhe's 2018 synthesis brings these threads together, arguing that the persistent framing of Wallace as "the greatest field biologist of the nineteenth century" is anachronistic and obscures the collective, labour-intensive character of the work [[1]](#ref-1). The primary sources—voyage journals, field notebooks, and correspondence, all searchable via Wallace Online—provide the evidential basis for a location-by-location reconstruction that incorporates sub-contractors whose contributions had previously been undercounted or overlooked [[1]](#ref-1). The archival collections underpinning this scholarship include the Wallace Collection at the Natural History Museum, London, the Linnean Society of London manuscripts, the Strickland Correspondence at Cambridge University Museum of Zoology, the Dillwyn Diaries at Swansea University, and the British Library holdings, together forming a distributed archive that only recent digitisation has made fully accessible [[2, p. 118]](#ref-2), [[4, pp. 50–51]](#ref-4), [[1]](#ref-1).
