# Chinese exorcists

M. R. Fernando's 2005 article challenges the long-held assumption that the Dutch colonial archives of Melaka were lost without trace, demonstrating that a vast corpus of VOC records covering 1641–1824 survives in the National Archives of the Netherlands, the British Library, and the National Archives of Indonesia. The article is a survey of these holdings and their implications for the political, economic, and social history of the Malay Peninsula.

## Summary

The article addresses a persistent historiographical problem: Malaysian scholars have long worked under the belief that virtually all Dutch records produced at the Melaka *comptoir* vanished, leaving them to rely on British colonial narratives and nineteenth-century Malay chronicles for the period 1641–1824. Fernando identifies three assumptions underpinning this belief — that only a small portion of Melaka's papers reached Batavia or Amsterdam, that those papers covered only matters of vital importance to the Company and were irrelevant to indigenous history, and that the local *comptoir* archive was destroyed. He demonstrates that all three are erroneous.

Fernando surveys the two principal holdings in detail. The first is the series of letters and papers from Melaka to Batavia, preserved in the VOC archives in The Hague, which covers the full duration of Dutch rule from 1641 to 1795. These include the governor's annual correspondence, the *dagh register*, Political Council resolutions, diplomatic letters with Malay rulers, shipping lists, and governors' memoirs. The second holding is the local *comptoir* archive, comprising thirty-eight categories of papers from the 1740s to the mid-1820s, discovered in the Melaka courthouse and now held in the British Library. This collection includes Court of Justice records, wills and testaments, protocol records of business transactions, Orphan Chamber accounts, and church registers. Fernando illustrates the richness of these sources through detailed case studies drawn from murder trials, slave transactions, and commercial partnerships.

A central argument of the article is that these records fundamentally revise two conventional narratives. First, the claim that Dutch control over the Straits of Melaka imposed hegemony over the Malay kingdoms is shown to be an overstatement; the Malay rulers successfully circumvented disadvantageous treaties and maintained a relationship of mutual, if uneasy, benefit. Second, the assertion that VOC trade monopoly policies impoverished the indigenous population is contradicted by evidence of a vibrant local economy, with Melaka serving as the centre of a regional commercial network well into the 1790s.

### Key Findings

- Virtually all papers Melaka sent to Batavia from 1641 to 1795 are preserved in the VOC archives (*Overgekomen brieven en papieren uit Indie aan de Heren XVII en de kamer Amsterdam*, folio volumes 1053–3987) in the National Archives of the Netherlands (p. 4).
- The local *comptoir* archive, comprising thirty-eight categories of papers covering the 1740s to the mid-1820s, was discovered in the Melaka courthouse, deposited in the India Record Office Library in 1927, and is now held in the British Library (pp. 9–10).
- The Melaka harbour master's shipping list (*syahbandar* register) covers 1682 to 1792 and is described as the most complete and detailed of its kind for any port in Asia in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (p. 7).
- The Court of Justice records include a register of civil and criminal cases from 1760 to 1825, with detailed proceedings of thirty-six civil cases (1773–1825) and criminal cases spanning 1749 to 1825 (pp. 11–12).
- The protocol records (British Library R/9/22/1 to R/9/22/75) document business transactions including the sale of a Batak slave girl for 100 reals in 1786, a brigantine of 150 *last* sold for Rxd. 6,000 in 1787, and a debt of Rxd. 6,520 acknowledged by Chinese traders in 1786 (pp. 18–19).
- The baptismal register of the Dutch Reformed Church in Melaka is complete from 1642 onwards, while the marriage and burial registers are lost (p. 20).

### Conclusion

Fernando's definitive historical takeaway is that the "lost archives of Melaka" are a misnomer: the core of the *comptoir*'s correspondence with Batavia survives intact, and the greater portion of the local administrative records from the 1740s onward has been preserved. The total neglect of these records by Malaysian historians has produced a patently misleading view of an important phase in the history of the Malay world, and their patient study demands a radical revision of both the political and economic narratives of the Dutch period.

## Context

- Primary archival collections explored: the VOC archives in The National Archives of the Netherlands (The Hague), the Dutch Records from Malacca in the British Library (formerly India Record Office), and the Residency Archives of Riau in the National Archives of Indonesia.
- Historiographical contribution: the article reopens the debate initiated by F. R. J. Verhoeven (1964) and provides a comprehensive guide to the extant holdings, encouraging Malaysian scholars to engage directly with Dutch-language primary sources rather than relying on the tradition of British colonial historiography.
