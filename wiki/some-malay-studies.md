# Some Malay studies

R.J. Wilkinson, a senior British colonial administrator (C.M.G.) with deep personal access to the Perak court, published this sweeping cultural survey in 1932, drawing on Malay chronicles, inscriptions, and direct interviews with Sultan Idris of Perak to trace the layered origins of Malay kingship, religion, and belief from Proto-Malayan animism through Indian and Islamic accretions to the living traditions of the 1930s. The overarching thesis is that Malay culture is a palimpsest: each successive civilisation (Indian, Buddhist, Islamic) has been grafted onto an indigenous Proto-Malayan substrate without erasing it, so that the "shadows of the past" remain visible in every ritual, from the coronation drum to the village shaman's rice-field altar.

## Summary

Wilkinson structures his argument around the question of how a people he describes as "listless and backward" in their Proto-Malayan phase produced the sophisticated court culture of the Malay sultanates. He rejects racial essentialism—insisting that "a Malay is a man of Malay speech and habits and not a somatological formula"—and instead traces cultural transmission through trade, intermarriage, and selective adoption. The Proto-Malays, he argues, were not a fixed "racial element" but a cultural type whose members were absorbed into higher civilisations as foreign settlers (first Deccan Indian, then Indo-Chinese, then Arab, Chinese, and Bugis) brought new scripts, religions, and political forms. The economic motive for this contact was the jungle produce that only nomad-hunters could locate: gums, dammars, rhinoceros-horn, gold-dust, and ivory.

The middle sections of the essay dissect the machinery of Malay kingship. Wilkinson reconstructs the Alexander legend's journey from a Pseudo-Callisthenes romance of the second or third century B.C. through Persian, Arabic, and Malay adaptations to show how Malay dynasties manufactured a Macedonian-Persian pedigree. He then examines the *daulat* (royal sanctity), the *nobat* (State Band), and the *kebesaran* (regalia) as institutions that fused pre-Islamic Hindu-Buddhist ritual with Islamic formulae. The *nobat*, he demonstrates, is a Persian *nau-bat* ("nine items") combined with an Indian installation-drum (*genderang tabal*), and its instruments carry graded degrees of holiness tied to the Sultan's physical presence. The regalia—originally a planter's chopper, a piece of haunted wood, and a wooden pike—were seized by the first Palembang prince to confirm his claim by conquest, then progressively augmented over twelve centuries.

The final sections turn to the "World Invisible": the shaman (*pawang*), the mystic (*sufi*), the demonology of *hantu*, the multiple conceptions of the soul, and the were-tiger. Wilkinson's method throughout is to strip away the mythological surface to reveal the practical or historical kernel beneath. The *pawang*'s "divination" for planting time is actually a solar calendar encoded in phenological observation; the *Sultan Muda*'s seances were a state-sanctioned system of spiritual diplomacy with the tutelary spirits of the land; the *pelesit* (vampire) is a conflation of at least three distinct beliefs (a corpse-sucker, a headless spirit, and a severed-head phantom) that have merged in popular usage.

### Key Findings

- The Kota Kapur inscription (A.D. 686) is in South Indian script and Sanskrit-Malay, recording a Sri Vijaya *datu*'s Hinayana Buddhist faith; the next dated inscriptions (A.D. 775, 778, 782) shift to North Indian script, Sanskrit only, and Mahayana Buddhism under a *maharaja* of the "Shailendra" line, indicating a dynastic convulsion between 732 and 775 A.D. (pp. 77–78).
- The Buitenzorg inscription (c. 375 A.D.), the oldest in Java, shows two carved footprints identified as those of Vishnu belonging to Purnawarnam, "reigning prince of the City of Taruma"—the etymological root of the Malay royal address "footprints" (*kaki*) still used in diplomatic correspondence (p. 79).
- The *Sultan Muda* of Perak received a fixed fee of $100 for his spiritual services; at the historic seance of August 1875 at Batak Rabit, the spirits predicted that a ghost at Kuala Perak would "wreck Mr. Birch's launch and drown him in the river"—Birch was in fact speared and sabred three months later (pp. 94–97).
- The ideal *nobat* comprises eight instruments (four "haunted," four unhaunted) plus the conductor, totalling nine items (*nau-bat*); the Perak Royal Band had shrunk to five instruments by the 1930s, while the Selangor band of seven was made in Perak on the model of the Perak *nobat* in use approximately 170 years earlier (pp. 82–83).
- Cranial indices cited: Kubu 79, Kenaboi Mantra 76, Minangkabau Malay 80–82.18, Selangor Malay 81, South Perak Malay 82.3, East Coast Malay 83.6—Wilkinson uses these to argue that the higher index of the "civilised" Malay reflects Indo-Chinese admixture rather than an indigenous Proto-Malayan type (p. 68).
- The three oldest Malay regalia—the *churek simandang-kini* (a planter's chopper), the *kamat*-wood seal, and the *lembuara* spear (a wooden pike)—are identified as Proto-Malayan heirlooms seized by the first Palembang prince to confirm his right to the throne by conquest, roughly twelve centuries before Wilkinson's writing (pp. 86–87).

### Conclusion

Wilkinson's definitive takeaway is that Malay civilisation is not a single inheritance but a continuous process of cultural layering in which each new element—Indian, Buddhist, Islamic, Persian—was absorbed without displacing what came before. The "shadows of the past" he observes in Perak's coronation ritual are not survivals in the pejorative sense but living strata: the Islamic *daulat* formula sits atop the Sanskrit *chiri*, which sits atop the Proto-Malayan spirit-cult of the *Sultan Muda*, all of which remain operative simultaneously in the mind of the Malay subject. The essay's final implicit argument is that to understand any single Malay institution in isolation is to miss the point; its meaning resides in the palimpsest.

## Context

- Wilkinson was a C.M.G. and senior colonial administrator who served in Perak; his direct access to Sultan Idris, the Dato' Sri Diraja, and the Perak regalia (which he was permitted to examine, copy, and even learn the State Secret from) gives the *daulat*, *nobat*, and *kebesaran* sections an evidential quality unavailable to most outside writers (p. 131).
- The article draws heavily on the *Papers on Malay Subjects* (P.M.S.) series published by the Federated Malay States government, on Skeat's *Malay Magic* (1900), and on Winstedt's *Shaman, Saiva and Sufi* (1925), positioning itself as a synthetic work that integrates administrative, literary, and ethnographic sources into a single narrative of Malay cultural development.
