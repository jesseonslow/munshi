# William George Stirling (1887–1951): civil servant and artist

## Abstract

William George Stirling joined the Malayan Civil Service in 1909 and worked in the Monopolies and Customs Services Department and the Chinese Protectorate. Stirling was a member of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society and contributed six articles to the society’s journal. He wrote and illustrated several books on Malaya and illustrated an edition of the *Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam* published in Singapore by the Lotus Library in 1918. After retiring from the civil service in 1932, he returned to England where he developed a reputation as a painter and sculptor. This brief account of his life is excerpted from the author’s paper entitled “William George Stirling (1887–1951): A Tale of Two Rubaiyats”, which is available through the following website: http://www.bobforrestweb.co.uk/The_Rubaiyat/N_and_Q/W_G_Stirling/W_G_Stirling.htm.

## Summary

This article reconstructs the life of William George Stirling (1887–1951), a British civil servant in colonial Malaya who simultaneously developed a significant body of artistic work documenting Chinese and Malay life. Stirling joined the Malayan Civil Service in 1909 and served in the Monopolies and Customs Services Department (with duties related to the opium trade) before transferring to the Chinese Protectorate, where he served as Assistant Protector of Chinese in Singapore from 1921 to 1931. His administrative work placed him at the intersection of colonial governance and the criminal underworld of Chinese secret societies, a world he documented both in official capacity and through his own publications and illustrations.

The narrative is built from a wide range of primary sources: the 1891 census, ship manifests, electoral rolls, probate records, contemporary newspaper reports, and Stirling's own books and journal articles. These sources allow the author to trace not only Stirling's colonial career but also his post-retirement life in England, where he became a sculptor exhibiting at the Royal Academy and a forensic scientist who invented an optical instrument for document examination. The article thus bridges two phases of a single life that are rarely considered together: the colonial administrator-artist in Singapore and the retired sculptor and forensic expert in London.

A recurring thread is the tension between Stirling's official role and his artistic sensibility. His work in the Chinese Protectorate gave him access to secret society rituals, opium dens, and the daily lives of Chinese immigrants that he rendered in silhouette drawings and watercolours. His 1918 illustrated edition of the *Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam*, published in Singapore, is a particularly striking example of this fusion, replacing Omar's wine with opium and his tavern with the opium den. The article positions Stirling as a figure whose colonial-era contributions—particularly his secret society collection now in the National Museum of Singapore—deserve greater scholarly attention.

### Key Findings

- Stirling was born on 25 August 1887 at Sysonby House, Melton Mowbray; his father was a retired major in the Royal Horse Guards and his mother the daughter of Henry Westenra, 3rd Baron Rossmore (p. 136).
- In December 1926, a Chinese tin miner stabbed Stirling in the thigh during an interview at the Chinese Protectorate; the man's wife was critically injured (p. 136).
- He published four illustrated books during his Malayan career: *Shadows in the Malay Peninsula* (1910), *Opium Smoking among the Chinese* (1913), *John Chinaman* (1914), and *Shadows on a Malayan Screen* (1926) (pp. 136–138).
- He illustrated a limited edition of 1,000 copies of the *Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam* (FitzGerald's first edition text), printed by Fraser & Neave and published by Lotus Library in Singapore around 1918 (p. 138).
- His collection of Chinese secret society artefacts, amassed during his Protectorate service, is housed in the National Museum of Singapore and was the subject of Irene Lim's 1999 monograph (p. 139).
- After retiring in 1932, he exhibited sculpture at the Royal Academy in 1935 and at the Wertheim Gallery in 1937, and from c. 1934 collaborated with Dr Edmund Locard in Lyons on forensic document examination, inventing a device called the Synchrisiscope (pp. 140–141).

### Conclusion

Forrest presents Stirling as a remarkably versatile figure whose legacy spans colonial administration, artistic documentation of Chinese and Malay popular culture, and post-retirement work in sculpture and forensic science. The article's implicit argument is that Stirling's significance extends well beyond his civil service role: his artistic output provides a valuable visual record of early twentieth-century Chinese life in the Straits Settlements, and his secret society collection remains a key resource for scholars of Chinese diaspora history.
