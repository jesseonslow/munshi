# Tanah Rata and the development of the Cameron Highlands, 1925–2030

## Abstract

The Cameron Highlands is named after William Cameron, a surveyor working for the British colonial administration who explored the region in the mid-1880s. In 1931 a township site was selected for Tanah Rata, with designated areas for public buildings, residential use, the armed forces, and recreation. Planning for the town was based in part on the garden city movement, whose supporters included Charles Compton Reade, the first town planner of the Federated Malay States. Economically, the town depends on income from agriculture and from tourism, both of which are expanding and creating environmental problems.

## Summary

The article traces the planning and development of the Cameron Highlands from its colonial origins as a hill station through post-independence expansion to projected developments in 2030. Weebers frames the Cameron Highlands within the broader British imperial pattern of hill station development in tropical colonies, where cooler highland climates were sought for health and recreation. The core argument is that the town of Tanah Rata was deliberately designed using garden city principles and modelled on Baguio in the Philippines, and that this colonial planning legacy created a template difficult to reconcile with the post-independence shift toward mass tourism and agricultural expansion. The article draws on National Archives material including annual reports, budget proposals, development guidelines, memoranda, and plans covering 1960–2030 to show how successive planning documents reveal conflicting priorities between economic growth and environmental sustainability.

The central dilemma Weebers identifies is the tension between the Cameron Highlands' economic dependence on agriculture and tourism and the environmental degradation both activities produce. As flat, gently sloping land became exhausted, new development moved onto steep slopes, triggering soil erosion, siltation, landslides, and flash floods. The article argues that no single masterplan has yet resolved this tension, and that the 2016–2030 local plan, while rhetorically committed to sustainability, contains projects that environmental groups warn will cause severe ecological damage.

### Key Findings

- The 1931 re-zoning plan for the Northern Highlands designated specific areas for agriculture (Area A), armed services (Area D), a park (Area E), viewpoints (Area F), general administration (Area G), recreation and golf (Area H), schools and churches (Area I), a Brinchang Valley Reserve (Area J), and waterworks and catchment areas (Area K) (p. 107).
- Agriculture is the largest land use in the Cameron Highlands at 5,705 hectares, followed by forestry; approximately 80 per cent of tourists are Malaysians (p. 108).
- The 1929 preliminary general zoning plan, chaired by Charles Compton Reade following his official visit to Baguio, divided the highlands into Southern Highlands and Cameron Highlands, with the latter subdivided into service, village, administrative, residential, and recreation zones (p. 106).
- The town of Tanah Rata sits at an altitude of 1,432 metres in the Bertam Valley and was modelled on Baguio, whose layout was designed by Daniel H. Burnham as a compact garden city for 25,000–30,000 people (pp. 105–106).
- The 2016–2030 local plan proposes an aerial tram between Kuala Terla and Habu or an alternative road from Tringkap to Habu crossing the Mentigi Forest Reserve, both of which would damage environmentally sensitive areas including the Habu water catchment and Robinson Falls (p. 109).
- The Cameron Highlands Development Plan for 2003–15 called for increases in agro-tourism, eco-tourism, and cultural and heritage tourism, marking a shift from the 1960 report, which made no mention of agriculture or agricultural tourism (p. 108).

### Conclusion

Weebers concludes that the Cameron Highlands requires an ecologically appropriate masterplan that balances tourism, recreation, scientific and educational functions, and agricultural development while preserving the region's distinctive environmental features. The colonial-era design, with its emphasis on a limited, health-oriented visitor base and British-style landscape, has been overwhelmed by post-independence population growth and commercial expansion, and without such a plan the region faces environmental catastrophe.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: National Archives of Malaysia (Kuala Lumpur), including annual reports, budget proposals, development guidelines, memoranda, minutes, and plans covering 1960–2030; Federated Malay States Survey maps (c.10/e.1); Federal Council Papers (Nos. 13 of 1925, 15 of 1925, 19 of 1929).
- Historiographical contribution: The article situates the Cameron Highlands within the comparative literature on Asian hill stations (Freeman, Crossette, Aiken) and connects colonial garden city planning theory (Howard, Reade) to its practical application in Malaya, bridging planning history and environmental history.
