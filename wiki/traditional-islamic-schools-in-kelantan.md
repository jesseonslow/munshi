# Traditional Islamic schools in Kelantan

The document is a traditional Malay folktale, "The Rich Man, the Poor Man and the Way the Pelandok Squared the Score," published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, No. 48 (June 1907), pp. 91–96. It presents a trickster narrative in which the pelandok (porcupine), a well-known figure in Malay folklore, resolves an intractable legal dispute between a wealthy merchant and a poor man through a piece of lateral logic. The tale is provided in both an English translation and in a northern Malay dialect, likely Kelantanese, reflecting the colonial-era practice of recording and translating local oral traditions for scholarly consumption.

## Summary

The narrative centres on a legal dilemma that the raja (king) cannot adjudicate: a wealthy merchant sues a poor man and his wife for having "devoured" the flavour of all food ever cooked in the merchant's household, claiming that the smell of frying, baking, stewing, and broiling was consumed by the poor couple, who eat only when they detect cooking aromas drifting from the merchant's house. The merchant demands a thousand dollars in compensation. The raja, unable to decide, issues a proclamation offering the position of grand vizier to anyone who can settle the case (pp. 91–92).

No human answers the proclamation, but the pelandok—a trickster animal figure in Malay folklore—steps forward, accepting the king's threat of death if he fails. In court, the pelandok establishes that the poor man has never entered the merchant's house or garden, only that the smell of cooking reached his nostrils. He then borrows a thousand dollars from the king, places a state curtain between the two parties, and has the poor man count the money aloud behind the curtain while the merchant listens on the other side. When the merchant demands the physical currency, the pelandok delivers his verdict: the poor man took the flavour by smell alone, and the merchant has now received his payment by hearing alone—the two are equivalent modes of sensory receipt, and the account is settled (pp. 93–94).

The tale functions as a piece of popular jurisprudence, encoding a community's sense of justice and the limits of property claims over intangible things like scent. The dialect version (pp. 95–96) preserves the story in its oral register, with characteristic northern Malay phonological features, suggesting it was recorded from a local informant rather than composed in literary Malay.

### Key Findings

- The merchant's claimed loss is quantified at exactly one thousand dollars (English version) or one thousand *ringgit* (Malay version), a round figure typical of folk narrative rather than a specific historical claim (p. 93).
- The raja's proclamation offers the position of *menteri* (grand vizier) as reward for resolving the case, indicating the story's setting within a Malay courtly political structure (p. 92).
- The pelandok is addressed as "Salam di Rimba" (Greeting of the Forest), a title marking him as a forest-dwelling supernatural or semi-supernatural agent distinct from human society (p. 93).
- The Malay text uses orthography and vocabulary consistent with a northern Malay dialect (e.g., *běrbahu* for "smelling," *chakap* for "able to," *měnděngar* for "to listen"), pointing to a Kelantanese or adjacent regional source (pp. 95–96).
- The logical structure of the resolution rests on a symmetry argument: smell (olfactory receipt) is equated with hearing (auditory receipt), both being non-physical, non-possessive modes of sensory contact, thereby negating the merchant's property claim over an intangible (p. 94).

### Conclusion

The tale is a piece of popular legal philosophy in which the trickster pelandok exposes the absurdity of a property claim over an intangible by mirroring the merchant's own logic back at him. Its publication in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1907, in both English and dialect Malay, reflects the colonial-era ethnographic impulse to document and translate local oral traditions, preserving a narrative that encodes community norms about justice, poverty, and the limits of wealth-based entitlement.

## Context

- The document was published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (later the Malaysian Branch), a colonial-era scholarly periodical that routinely featured translated folktales, proverbs, and dialect texts as part of its ethnographic mission. The dual-language presentation (English translation alongside the original dialect) is characteristic of the journal's editorial practice for oral literature.
- The dialect text (pp. 95–96) appears to be a direct transcription of a spoken performance, preserving colloquial morphology and phonology that would have been of interest to colonial linguists and ethnographers documenting regional Malay varieties.
