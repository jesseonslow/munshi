# Traditional Islamic schools in Kelantan

Robert L. Winzeler's 1975 article documents the structure, social function, and political entanglement of traditional Islamic boarding schools (*pondoks*) in Kelantan, a northeastern Malaysian state distinguished by an unusually strong popular commitment to Islam. Drawing on fieldwork conducted in 1966–67 and 1971, Winzeler argues that *pondoks* are not merely educational institutions but the foundational social and political units through which Kelantanese Islamic identity has been produced, sustained, and contested in the face of modernization and party politics.

## Summary

Winzeler situates Kelantan's Islamic distinctiveness in its demographic and historical particularities. Unlike much of the Malay Peninsula, Kelantan was not populated by migrants from Sumatra or other parts of Indonesia; its population derives largely from the Patani region of southern Thailand, an early centre of Islamic spread in Southeast Asia. The Thai conquest and subjugation of Patani in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries drove successive waves of Malay refugees into Kelantan, carrying with them religious traditions and institutional forms that took root in the Kelantan plain (pp. 89–90). The *pondok*—a religious boarding school in which male students reside and study under a *tuan guru*—is the institutional expression of this heritage, concentrated in the plain and particularly in the sector nearest the Thai border.

The article provides a detailed ethnographic account of *pondok* life. Physically, a *pondok* comprises student huts (small one-room structures of wood, bamboo, and palm thatch), one or more *madrassah* buildings for teaching and prayer, and the houses of the *guru* and other residents. The *guru* is the indispensable centre of the institution: the *pondok* develops around him and frequently does not survive his death. His reputation rests on acknowledged mastery of Islamic scripture (increasingly requiring Arabic literacy and study in Mecca), personal piety and conduct, and in some cases reputed mystical powers (*berkat*). Beyond the student body, established *pondoks* function as communities housing widowed and elderly people, former students, and their families, creating a local religious community with its own rhythms of communal prayer, *meratib* chanting, and festival observance (pp. 91–94).

Winzeler then turns to the pressures of modernization and politics. The expansion of government Malay schools and state-run "Arabic schools" (*sekolah Arab*) has drawn students away from traditional *pondoks*, which are in relative decline. Simultaneously, the rise of the Pan Malayan Islamic Party (PMIP) in the 1950s intertwined *pondok* communities with party politics, as *pondoks* served as effective bases for local political organization and *gurus* became partisan figures. Winzeler closes with three case studies of contemporary *pondoks* that illustrate varying degrees of adaptation, from a small village school that has also established an Arabic school, to a large interior *pondok* that has integrated a full Arabic school curriculum within its traditional structure, to the state's largest *pondok* (approximately 500 students, 800 total residents, eight acres) which maintains a purely traditional curriculum while benefiting from the pre-education its students receive in Arabic schools (pp. 99–103).

### Key Findings

- The Kelantan State Religious Council estimated 55 *pondok* schools in the state, though the true number in local usage is higher; the largest have upwards of 500 students, multiple teachers, and occupy five to eight acres of land (p. 91).
- No tuition fees are charged; students are maintained by their parents who supply food, and periodic contributions are made at harvest time and major holidays. A student at the largest *pondok* studied by Winzeler paid only 70 sen per month for kerosene and building upkeep (pp. 92, 102).
- Among the most important *gurus*—those with the largest *pondoks* and widest followings—nearly all are literate in Arabic and have completed lengthy study in Mecca, a practice established by the earlier decades of the twentieth century (p. 97).
- The PMIP held control of the Kelantan state government from 1959 to 1974, and party strength was concentrated in areas surrounding important *pondoks*, which served as effective bases of local political operation (pp. 89, 99).
- The largest *pondok* documented (approximately 500 students, 400 buildings, eight acres) charges a stated purpose of training a vigorous Malay *ulama* and offers two courses: an eleven-subject "lower certificate" programme of about four years, and a fourteen-subject advanced course requiring ten years or more (pp. 101–103).
- One large interior *pondok* (170 resident students, 15 families, five acres) represents the most thorough-going attempt to combine a traditional *pondok* with a certified Arabic school, with nine Religious Council-certified *Ustadz* teaching a regular curriculum in a dozen classrooms alongside the traditional evening *madrassah* instruction (p. 101).

### Conclusion

Winzeler's definitive takeaway is that the *pondok* system in Kelantan is undergoing a structural transformation driven by the expansion of formal education and the demands of a modern state bureaucracy that requires government-recognized degrees for employment, including in religious administration. While the traditional *pondok* is in relative decline, it has not disappeared; rather, it persists as a site of moral and spiritual formation and as a training ground for religious leadership, adapting by either incorporating Arabic school curricula or by positioning itself as a complement to the formal system. The *guru* remains the irreplaceable centre of this institution, and the *pondok* community—encompassing students, the elderly, and families—continues to function as a distinctive social formation that no other type of school or institution in Kelantan replicates.

## Context

- Winzeler's fieldwork was conducted in the Pasir Mas district of Kelantan, where several of the state's most important *pondoks* are located; his broader research on Kelantanese society was published in W. R. Roff (ed.), *Kelantan: Religion, Society and Politics in a Malay State* (Kuala Lumpur, 1973) (p. 89).
- The article contributes to a broader comparative literature on Islamic boarding schools in Southeast Asia, particularly the Indonesian *pesantren* tradition documented by Geertz, Snouck Hurgronje, and others, with which the Kelantanese *pondok* is explicitly compared (p. 89, n. 2).
