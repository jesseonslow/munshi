# Malays in the Indochinese Peninsula: The Rise and Fall of a 'Tuan' in Precolonial Mainland Southeast Asia

## Abstract

This paper features the military and political fortunes of a man of Malay origin and called Tuen Phaow/Toan Phu/Tuan Pha. Vietnamese, Cam and Khmer historical sources record his activity in the Indochinese Peninsula from 1786 to 1820. After crowning himself king in the region south of today’s Central Vietnam highlands, he fought the Vietnamese (1796–97) and then fled, only to reappear a decade later at the Khmer royal court. Promoted to the highest position, he was then used by the Vietnamese imperial court to further their influence over Cambodian politics. His spectacular ascension abruptly ended in 1820 when he was put to death. Celebrated by some as a hero, reviled as a traitor or loathed as a ruthless brute by others, this complex and unique character exemplifies patterns of mobility and political opportunism in mainland Southeast Asian precolonial politics.

## Summary

This article reconstructs the political and military career of a man of Malay origin—known as Tuen Phaow in Cam, Toan Phu or Đong Phu in Vietnamese, and Tuan Pha in Khmer—who operated across the Indochinese Peninsula from 1786 to 1820. Weber's central argument is that this figure exemplifies the patterns of mobility, political opportunism, and shifting alliances that characterised precolonial mainland Southeast Asian politics, and that his case deconstructs the rigid ethnic boundaries assumed by nationalist historiographies. The article is structured around two major phases: Tuen Phaow's self-proclamation as king in the highlands of southern Panduranga and his subsequent uprising against Nguyễn Phúc Ánh's forces, followed by his reappearance at the Khmer royal court where he was instrumentalised by the Vietnamese imperial court to extend its influence over Cambodia.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to analysing how Tuen Phaow constructed and performed royal legitimacy by drawing on Malay kingship traditions—self-awarding Sanskrit-derived titles, building a palace, demanding prostration, issuing edicts proclaiming supernatural prowess, and grounding his authority in an orthodox Islamic framework distinct from the syncretic Islam practised by the Bani communities of Panduranga. Weber argues that these performances were not merely cosmetic but were essential to the attraction of followers across ethnic lines, relying on the Malay political currency of *nama* (reputation) and *daulat* (sacral power).

The article's evidential basis rests on three distinct source traditions that Weber reads in parallel: Vietnamese official records (*Đại Nam Thực Lục*, *Gia Định Thành Thông Chí*, *Quốc Triều Chánh Biên*, *Minh Mệnh Chính Yếu*), Cam versified narratives (*ariya*) preserved in EFEO and Société Asiatique manuscripts, and the Khmer chronicle *Sastra Voat Kroch* by the Venerable Batum Baramey Pich. Weber treats these not as contradictory accounts of different individuals but as "snapshots" reflecting different facets of a complex personality at different times and places, each shaped by the political interests of its producers.

### Key Findings

- Tuen Phaow was active from 1786 (first mentioned in the *Đại Nam Thực Lục*) until his execution in 1820, spanning two distinct political phases separated by approximately a decade of disappearance (pp. 44–45).
- His 1796–97 uprising was crushed by a combined force of 1,600 men under General Phan Tiến Hoàng and the Company of Siamese Soldiers; his followers surrendered while he fled into the Jarai and Radhe highlands (p. 13).
- By 1812 he had reappeared at the court of King Ang Chan as a "close friend" of the king, and was summoned to Huế to receive imperial orders from the Vietnamese court, which explicitly framed his rehabilitation as conditional on his service to Vietnamese interests in Cambodia (pp. 14–15).
- In 1819 he was appointed *Chavea Ta-la-ha* (first minister), a position equivalent to viceroy or regent during Ang Chan's reign, and was placed at the head of 5,000 Cambodian men for the construction of the Vinh Te Canal, which was supervised by 5,000 workers and 500 Vietnamese soldiers (pp. 16–17).
- His execution in 1820 followed the suppression of the Ke revolt in Prey Veng; he was accused of using troops "against the people" and of massacring Buddhist monks and civilians indiscriminately during the pacification, as well as of having coaxed the Khmer officials Noren Tol and Ke Preal into supporting the revolt (pp. 17–18).
- Cam narratives identify his army as composed of Churu, Raglai, Koho, Rhade, lowland Cam, and Malays of Cambodia (*kur jawa*), with the latter wearing distinctive black tunics, large trousers, and brown turbans dyed with a mixture of leaves and pond mud (pp. 7–8).

### Conclusion

Weber concludes that Tuen Phaow/Toan Phu/Tuan Pha was an exceptional character whose case demonstrates the exceptional mobility of people in precolonial Southeast Asia and the depth of cross-ethnic political cooperation that transcended the boundaries assumed by modern nationalist discourses. The irreconcilable portraits offered by the three source traditions—hero in Cam narratives, cunning opportunist in Vietnamese official records, and ruthless brute in Pich's Khmer chronicle—should be understood as partial reflections of a single complex individual rather than evidence of multiple persons, and together they illuminate the intricate web of dependency, co-dependency, and shifting loyalties that defined the region's politics in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: Cam *ariya* manuscripts held at the École Française d'Extrême-Orient (Paris) and the Société Asiatique de Paris; Vietnamese official histories published by Nhà Xuất Bản Giáo Dục (Hanoi) and Nhà Xuất Bản Thuận Hóa (Huế); the Khmer *Sastra Voat Kroch* as translated by Khin Sok (2002).
- Historiographical contribution: The article synthesises three previously studied-in-isolation source traditions (Vietnamese, Cam, Khmer) into a single biographical narrative, extending Weber's earlier work on Malay involvement in precolonial mainland Southeast Asia (Weber 2021, JMBRAS 94.1) and building on Inrasara's Cam-language publications and his own 2003 French translation of the Cam narratives.
