# Manggeng and Datu Merpati

A.H. Hill's 1955 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines two interrelated Bornean origin legends—Manggeng, the Land Dayak ancestor who became a territorial chief in Java, and Datu Merpati, the Javanese nobleman who settled in Sarawak—and argues that both are historical figures whose lives oral tradition has distorted and partially conflated. Set against the backdrop of Majapahit's decline and the spread of Islam across the Indonesian archipelago in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, Hill's central thesis is that the core narratives contain "a great measure of historical truth" despite their mythological embellishments (p. 154).

## Summary

Hill reconstructs the Manggeng legend by comparing three printed versions: Denison's 1875 account from Senah, Boland and Harrisson's 1949 collection from Emperoh Gerong, and Father P. Howes's synthesis of many oral tellings from the Sarawak River (Simpang Kiri) area. The basic story has two parts: Manggeng flees his home after striking his young son Jawih, reaches Java, rids a village of marauding tigers, passes trials of strength imposed by the local Rajah, and assumes the throne; years later his son finds him, is recognized by a scar on his forehead, and returns to Borneo with gifts (pp. 148–150). Hill notes that the story is known with only minor variations across a small, racially homogeneous area of western Borneo, and that no close parallels exist in the folk-tales of neighbouring countries—a point he considers significant for its historicity (p. 152).

The article then broadens its scope to situate both legends within the political and religious transformations of the period. Hill draws on Javanese written sources (the *Nagarakretagama* of 1365, Raffles's *History of Java*), Portuguese accounts (Pires, Pigafetta), and Brunei annals to establish that Majapahit's influence over Borneo was brief and that the empire's fragmentation created conditions under which a skilled stranger could establish a petty chieftaincy in west Java (pp. 154–156). He connects the Datu Merpati legend to the Brunei *Salasilah* account of Alak Bertata's abduction of a Johore princess, and to the broader pattern of foreign-origin claims made by Borneo polities recorded in Rajah James Brook's journal (pp. 153, 156). Material evidence—gold ornaments with Majapahit-period patterns found across Malaysia, and the "Manggeng kendi" (an earthenware drinking vessel of probable Indian provenance)—is assessed as corroborating the legends' claims of Javanese contact (pp. 157–158).

### Key Findings

- Three printed versions of the Manggeng legend exist: Denison (1875), Boland & Harrisson (1949), and Fr. Howes's synthesis; all collected from the same general area near Pengkalan Empat (p. 148).
- Generation counts place Manggeng at approximately 16 generations before 1955 (at 25 years per generation, c. 1500–1550) and Datu Merpati at 14–16 generations back (c. 1450–1475) (pp. 154, 158).
- Krom's revised dating of Majapahit's fall to c. 1500 (rather than Raffles's 1475) is adopted; the Portuguese still found a Hindu ruler in Bantam in 1511 (pp. 154–155).
- Magellan's party reached Brunei in 1521; Pigafetta's description of Sultan Bolkiah's court (brick wall, 56 bronze cannon, silk-dressed attendants) is identified as the first Muslim ruler of Brunei, a contemporary of Angka Wijaya (p. 156).
- Gold ornaments with patterns characteristic of the Majapahit period have been found across Malaysia, including flexible gold armlets excavated at Fort Canning Hill, Singapore, in 1928 (p. 157).
- No close parallels to the Manggeng narrative (departure for Java, tiger-slaying, trials of strength, son's discovery) have been found in the folk-tales of any neighbouring country (p. 152).

### Conclusion

Hill concludes that Manggeng and Datu Merpati are historical figures whose lives tradition has distorted and to some extent confused. Manggeng became a territorial chief somewhere in west Java at about the time Islam had established itself as the dominant religion, say 1500–1525; Datu Merpati, himself a convert to Islam, founded a colony of Malays in Sarawak probably between 1450 and 1475 (p. 158).

## Context

- Primary sources include Fr. Howes's unpublished oral tradition collections from the Land Dayaks of the First Division, Sarawak; Javanese texts cited in Raffles's *History of Java*; the *Nagarakretagama* (1365); Portuguese accounts by Pires (1515) and Pigafetta (1521); and the Brunei *Salasilah* annals.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in cross-referencing Dayak oral tradition with Javanese and Portuguese written sources to date and historicize Bornean origin legends, a method that prefigures later work on the historicity of Southeast Asian mythological narratives.
