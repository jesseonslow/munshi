# Carlos Cuarteron. In Tregonning, K.G. Two notes on Church history in Sabah

Rev. A. Antonissen, a Mill Hill Father, contributed this note (written in late 1957, published in 1966) to K.G. Tregonning's composite piece on church history in Sabah. The article traces the life of Carlos Cuarteron—a former Spanish smuggler and slave-runner who, after a near-fatal typhoon in 1849, converted to Catholicism and became the first Prefect Apostolic of North Borneo and Labuan—and follows the Catholic mission's development from that founding moment through the Japanese occupation to the mid-1950s. The overarching thesis is that Cuarteron's personal redemption established the institutional foundation upon which the entire Catholic presence in North Borneo was built.

## Summary

The article opens with a brief note on the Borneo Basel Selfestablished Church, the first entirely indigenous Protestant church in North Borneo, before turning to its central subject. Antonissen narrates Cuarteron's transformation from a Cadiz-born captain engaged in smuggling and the slave trade in the 1830s–40s to a Roman Catholic priest. The pivotal moment is a typhoon in 1849, during which Cuarteron, fearing for his soul, vowed to dedicate his life and fortune to liberating Christian slaves from Brunei. He sold his ship, entered the College of the Propaganda Fide in Rome in 1850, was ordained in 1855, and was immediately appointed Prefect Apostolic of North Borneo and Labuan.

The narrative then follows the practical difficulties of the early mission. Two Milan Fathers arrived in Labuan in April 1857 but departed for Hong Kong in 1860, leaving Cuarteron to work alone for eighteen years in what Antonissen describes as a "very poor life" of penance and good work in the Borneo jungle. British authorities in Labuan suspected him of being a Spanish spy. He died in Cadiz in 1878, and the Prefecture passed to the Mill Hill Fathers (St. Joseph's Foreign Missionary Society) in 1881, with Msgr. Jackson as the new Prefect Apostolic.

The final section charts the mission's institutional growth: the opening of the first school at Sandakan in 1883, the arrival of the first Sisters in North Borneo in 1891, the 1927 division of the Prefecture, and the catastrophic disruption of the Japanese occupation, during which eight Tyrolese Fathers were taken to Tenom and never seen again. The article closes with 1957 statistics demonstrating the scale of the mission Antonissen himself served within.

### Key Findings

- Cuarteron was born at Cadiz in 1816; he made his vow during a typhoon in 1849, entered the Propaganda Fide College in 1850, was ordained in 1855, and was appointed Prefect Apostolic of North Borneo and Labuan in September 1855 (pp. 168–169).
- The two Milan Fathers who assisted Cuarteron arrived in Labuan in April 1857 and left for Hong Kong in 1860, leaving him to work alone until 1878 (p. 169).
- The first Catholic school in North Borneo opened at Sandakan on 24 July 1883 with 5 boys; by February 1888 the *BNB Herald* recorded 12 enrolled pupils of nine different ethnic origins (p. 170).
- The first Sisters arrived in North Borneo on 5 November 1891, opening St. Mary's Convent, Sandakan (p. 170).
- During the Japanese occupation, eight Tyrolese Fathers (including the Prefect Apostolic) were rounded up after the Allied landing on Labuan and taken to Japanese Headquarters in Tenom; they were never seen again (p. 171).
- By December 1957 the Vicariate Apostolic of North Borneo reported 23,867 Catholics, 45 primary schools (6,879 pupils), 8 secondary schools (807 pupils), 45 priests, and 37 Sisters (p. 171).

### Conclusion

Antonissen presents Cuarteron as the singular founding figure of the Catholic Church in North Borneo, whose personal act of penance—abandoning a life of piracy and slavery—generated an institutional legacy that survived his death, the loss of his treasure to Roman financial troubles, the Japanese occupation, and the internment of missionaries, ultimately producing a vicariate of nearly 24,000 Catholics by 1957.

## Context

- The article was written by a serving Mill Hill Father and published within Tregonning's composite piece; it draws on mission records, the *BNB Herald*, and personal knowledge of the community rather than a systematic archival study.
- The 1957 statistics and the account of the eight missing Tyrolese Fathers represent the most concrete empirical contributions; the Cuarteron narrative itself relies on traditional hagiographical sources and British administrative reports (noted by Tregonning's marginal comment on the spy suspicion).
