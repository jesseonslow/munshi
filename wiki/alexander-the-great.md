# Alexander the Great

Alexander the Great, the Macedonian king whose conquests extended from Greece to the Indian subcontinent, has left a remarkable afterlife in the literary and folkloric traditions of the Malay world. In the Malay and Indonesian archives, his figure survives not as a historical portrait but as a legendary hero whose name, deeds, and lineage were absorbed into local chronicles, romances, and cosmological narratives. The two principal scholarly treatments of this phenomenon in the Society's literature are the works of R. O. Winstedt, whose 1938 study of the Malay romance of Alexander and his 1940 note on the Mount Meru and Chula legends together constitute the foundational documentation of the subject in this field [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2).

## The Malay Romance of Alexander

The most substantial body of evidence for Alexander's reception in the Malay world is the Malay romance bearing his name, a text whose date, authorship, and contents were examined by R. O. Winstedt in a detailed study published in 1938 [[1]](#ref-1). Winstedt's article, spanning twenty-three pages, addresses the manuscript tradition of the work, identifying and describing new manuscripts that had come to light, and situating the romance within the broader context of Southeast Asian literary culture [[1, pp. 1–23]](#ref-1). The study treats the text as a literary artefact in its own right, concerned with questions of composition, transmission, and the relationship between the Malay version and its antecedents in other linguistic traditions [[1]](#ref-1).

The existence of a dedicated Malay romance of Alexander testifies to the depth of his penetration into the region's narrative imagination. That a substantial prose work should have been composed in Malay, and that multiple manuscripts of it should have survived, indicates that the Alexander legend was not a marginal or passing curiosity but a subject of sustained literary interest [[1]](#ref-1). Winstedt's identification of new manuscripts further suggests that the text circulated widely enough to generate a manuscript tradition of some size, a fact that speaks to its popularity among readers and copyists in the Malay-speaking world [[1, pp. 1–23]](#ref-1).

## The Mount Meru and Chula Legends

Winstedt's 1940 note, though brief, opens a distinct line of inquiry into the ways in which the Alexander legend became entangled with other elements of Malay and Indonesian folklore, specifically the Mount Meru and Chula legends [[2]](#ref-2). The note examines the etymological and legendary connections between Alexander the Great, the sacred mountain *Mahameru* (the cosmic axis in Hindu mythology), and the Chula raids, which appear in Malay chronicles as a historical or semi-historical episode [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2).

The Mount Meru connection is traced through Arrian's account of Alexander's Indian campaign, in which the Macedonian king encountered the Nysaeans, a people who claimed descent from worshippers of Dionysus [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2). A neighbouring summit was called Meru, a name that Hinduism applied to many mountains, with *Mahameru* serving as the pivot of the universe in Hindu cosmology [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2). Winstedt draws on E. R. Bevan's explanation in *The Cambridge History of India* (1922) to establish the link between this geographical reference and the name *Mahameru* as it appears in the "Malay Annals" and in Sumatran folk-lore [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2). The implication is that the Alexander legend, in its transmission to the Malay world, became fused with pre-existing cosmological and geographical concepts, the mountain that bore Alexander's name in the Greek sources being identified with the sacred mountain of Hindu and local tradition [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2).

The Chula element presents a parallel case of legendary absorption. In the Malay legend, a descendant of Alexander marries a daughter of Demang Lebar Daun, whose son-in-law had among his ancestors Raja Suran and Raja Chulan [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2). These figures are generally identified as folk-lore reminiscences of the Chula raids of the eleventh century, a historical episode that had itself become mythologised in local tradition [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2). Winstedt supports this identification by citing L. D. Barnett's note on a Ceylonese prince whose name appears in Pali as *Mahachuli*, *chulika*, and *chula*, and in Sinhalese as *Mahasilu*, thereby establishing a linguistic bridge between the Chula/Merah Silu variants found in Malay chronicles such as the "Chronicles of Pasai" and a plausible historical referent [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2).

The significance of these connections lies in the way they demonstrate that the Alexander legend did not arrive in the Malay world as a self-contained narrative but was integrated into existing mythological and historical frameworks. The figure of Alexander became a node in a network of associations that included sacred geography, dynastic legitimacy, and the memory of foreign incursions [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2). His legendary descendants were woven into the genealogies of local rulers, and his geographical encounters were mapped onto the cosmological landscape of Hindu and Buddhist tradition [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2).

## Legacy and Documentation

The documentation of Alexander the Great in the Society's literature is concentrated in the work of a single scholar, R. O. Winstedt, whose two contributions — the 1938 study of the Malay romance and the 1940 note on the Mount Meru and Chula legends — together provide the essential scholarly foundation for the subject [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2). Winstedt's approach is characterised by a close attention to manuscript evidence, etymological analysis, and the comparative method, drawing on Greek sources such as Arrian, Sanskrit and Pali linguistic material, and Malay chronicles to construct a coherent picture of how the Alexander legend was transformed in its passage to Southeast Asia [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2).

The 1938 study, with its focus on the date, authorship, contents, and new manuscripts of the Malay romance, represents a contribution to the field of Malay literary studies as much as to the history of the Alexander legend [[1, pp. 1–23]](#ref-1). By identifying and describing previously unrecorded manuscripts, Winstedt expanded the evidential base for understanding the text's transmission and reception [[1]](#ref-1). The 1940 note, though shorter, extends the inquiry beyond the literary text into the domain of folk-lore and comparative mythology, demonstrating how the Alexander legend interacted with other narrative traditions in the Malay and Indonesian worlds [[2, p. 153]](#ref-2).

Together, these two works establish that the Alexander legend in the Malay world is a subject of considerable complexity, involving questions of literary history, linguistic etymology, comparative mythology, and the reception of foreign narratives in local cultural contexts [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2). The records attest to a tradition of substantial depth, in which a Greek historical figure was transformed into a legendary ancestor, a cosmological reference point, and a literary hero, and in which the processes of that transformation can be traced through manuscript evidence, chronicle entries, and folk-lore narratives [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2).
