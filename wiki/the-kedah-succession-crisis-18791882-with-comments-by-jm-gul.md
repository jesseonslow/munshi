# The Kedah succession crisis 1879–1882 {with comments by J.M. Gullick

Kobkua Suwannathat-Pian's 1989 article examines the Kedah succession crisis of 1879–1882, a period of intense intra-dynastic conflict following the death of Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin II, arguing that Siamese suzerain intervention was the decisive mechanism that prevented the state from descending into armed civil war and that the episode exemplifies the constructive dimension of the post-1842 tributary relationship between Bangkok and Kedah.

## Summary

The article traces two successive succession crises in Kedah, separated by the brief and troubled reign of Sultan Zainal Rashid II (1879–1881). When Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin II died in June 1879 without naming a successor—a practice consistent with the post-1842 norm that required Siamese approval for any new ruler—conflict erupted between Tengku Dhiauddin, the deceased sultan's elder brother and father-in-law of the eldest son by the second consort, and Tengku Yaakob, the Raja Muda who championed the claim of Tengku Abdul Hamid, the eldest son by the senior consort. The Siamese commissioner Phraya Montri-suriyawong mediated the initial standoff, and King Chulalongkorn resolved it by appointing Tengku Zainal Rashid as sultan, Tengku Abdul Hamid as Raja Muda, and both uncles as equal co-regents. The arrangement collapsed almost immediately: Dhiauddin isolated the young sultan from his council, the co-regents quarrelled openly, and Zainal Rashid died in September 1881, triggering a second and more dangerous crisis.

The 1881–82 crisis was more complex because Dhiauddin now pressed his own claim to the throne, threatened to support his younger brother Tengku Kassim as an alternative, and ultimately demanded the partition of Kedah (Kubang Pasu to be separated as his personal fief) as a condition of accepting Tengku Abdul Hamid's appointment. Chulalongkorn, after extensive correspondence with his senior adviser Somdet Chao Phraya, rejected the partition plan and all of Dhiauddin's concessions, appointed Tengku Abdul Hamid as sultan, abolished the co-regency, and reduced the two uncles to the status of senior advisers. The article draws heavily on Thai archival correspondence—particularly the King's own letters to Somdet Chao Phraya and the Kalahom—to reconstruct the reasoning behind Bangkok's decisions, revealing Chulalongkorn's personal regard for Ahmad Tajuddin II and his determination to preserve that ruler's line in power.

The aftermath, spanning from 1882 to the late 1880s, involved a protracted dispute over the uncles' annual allowances, which Dhiauddin and Tengku Yusof used as leverage to signal their non-cooperation. The conflict gradually lost force and effectively ended with Dhiauddin's death in 1909, when Sultan Abdul Hamid arranged a full royal burial at Kuala Langgar.

### Key Findings

- Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin II died on 22 June 1879; his son Sultan Zainal Rashid II died on 22 September 1881, a reign of barely two years (p. 85).
- Chulalongkorn's official despatch announcing Tengku Abdul Hamid's appointment as Phraya Zaiburi was dated Thursday of the 13th waxing moon, the fourth moon, 1882, and explicitly abolished the co-regency, vesting full authority in the new sultan (p. 93).
- Between 1886 and 1888, Tengku Dhiauddin and Tengku Yusof sent 23 letters and appeals to Bangkok regarding their allowances; Sultan Abdul Hamid responded with 14 despatches. Bangkok's final ruling fixed their annual allowances at $9,600 (Dhiauddin) and $4,800 (Yusof) for the period they declined to serve the state (p. 95).
- During the recession period, the Sultan had agreed to pay reduced monthly allowances of $800 (Dhiauddin) and $400 (Yusof), which were subsequently cut entirely in 1883 before being partially restored under Bangkok's instruction (pp. 94–95).
- Somdet Chao Phraya's partition proposal—dividing Kubang Pasu from Kedah and assigning it to Tengku Yaakob while giving the remainder to Tengku Dhiauddin—was rejected by Chulalongkorn on the grounds that it would create border disputes, invite European interference, and alienate Tengku Yaakob, who had explicitly stated he desired no part of Kedah but would uphold his nephew's right to rule (pp. 84, 101–104).
- Tengku Dhiauddin's final petition demanded the partition of Kuala Muda as a separate tributary state under his own governance, an additional income beyond his existing padi tax, and a permanent residence and income for Tengku Yusof; all were refused within one week (pp. 91–93).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian concludes that the succession crisis of 1879–1882 stands as the clearest demonstration of the stabilising function of Siamese suzerainty over Kedah in the post-1842 period. Without Bangkok's firm exercise of its right to nominate and recognise the ruler, the intra-dynastic rivalries—particularly Dhiauddin's ambition—could have plunged the state into the kind of armed conflict that had characterised Kedah's eighteenth-century history. The episode confirmed that the recognised sultan held an unassailable position, and that the tributary framework, far from being merely extractive, had become the guarantor of political order in Kedah.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on Thai archival sources held in Bangkok (R.5 series files of the Kalahom and the Foreign Affairs ministry) and on the published correspondence of Somdet Chao Phraya Borom Maha Sisuriyawong (Natwud Suthisongkhram, 1973), making the Siamese administrative perspective accessible to readers who cannot consult the Thai-language originals.
- J.M. Gullick's appended commentary ("A Kedah Viewpoint") provides an important historiographical counterweight, arguing that Chulalongkorn's choices were constrained by local Malay political realities and the memory of the 1821–1842 period of Siamese direct rule, and that the King effectively followed the late sultan's own will regarding the order of succession among his sons.
