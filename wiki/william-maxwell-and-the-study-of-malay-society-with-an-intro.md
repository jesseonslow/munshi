# William Maxwell and the study of Malay society (with an introduction by Khoo Kay Kim

J.M. Gullick's 1991 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 64, No. 261) is a comprehensive biographical and scholarly assessment of Sir William Maxwell (1846–1897), the Straits Settlements administrator who, in the absence of any professional academic establishment in Malaya, became the pioneering figure in the systematic study of Malay society, language, and history during the 1870s and 1880s. Gullick's central argument is that Maxwell's unique combination of legal training, deep immersion in Malay village life, multilingual competence, and voracious reading made him the first "first-hand observer with scholastic knowledge" in Malayan studies — a role that no university-trained scholar could fill until well after his death.

## Summary

Gullick traces Maxwell's career from his arrival in Malaya at age 18 as clerk to his father, the Recorder of Penang, through his service as magistrate, Assistant Resident of Perak under Hugh Low, Commissioner of Lands/Titles in the Straits Settlements, Resident of Selangor, and finally Colonial Secretary and acting Governor of the Straits Settlements before his transfer to West Africa, where he died in 1897 at 51. The article is not merely a career narrative; it uses Maxwell's official positions as the lens through which his scholarly output is understood. His courtroom experience in Province Wellesley and Melaka gave him direct knowledge of Malay village disputes, Islamic law, and land tenure; his time in Perak under Low provided access to traditional Malay society at multiple levels; and his Residency in Selangor allowed him to implement the land reforms that became the foundation of modern Malayan land law.

The article devotes substantial attention to Maxwell's role as editor of the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (1883–1887), a period when the Society was struggling with declining membership, lack of premises, and a dearth of contributors. Maxwell kept the Journal alive through his own prolific contributions, the introduction of *Notes and Queries*, the publication of Malay texts in Jawi, and the cultivation of contacts with French and Dutch scholars. He also initiated the Society's map of the Malay Peninsula, the reprinting of the *Hikayat Abdullah*, and the ambitious (if ultimately flawed) project of republishing earlier scholarly papers on the region under the editorship of Dr. Reinhold Rost.

A significant portion of the article examines the bitter controversy between Maxwell and Swettenham over Malay land tenure, particularly Maxwell's theory that the Malay Ruler had a customary right to a tithe of agricultural produce. Gullick is candid that this theory "did not stand up to scrutiny of the evidence" and that Maxwell was "confusing a government tax with the Ruler's obligation to collect the tithe due from Muslims as a charitable due — the familiar zakat" (p. 19). Yet the controversy produced a valuable body of documentary evidence, and the underlying principle of periodic revision of quit-rent was retained in the FMS Land Code of 1896 and ultimately in the National Land Code of 1965.

### Key Findings

- Maxwell's *Manual of the Malay Language* (1882) included an introductory essay on "the Sanskrit element in Malay" that tabulated Malay words across themes (commerce, cosmology, religion, political office) with their Sanskrit, Sundanese, Batak, and Bugis equivalents; at his death in 1897, contemporaries singled out this essay as "the most memorable of his works" (p. 16).
- His bequest to the Royal Asiatic Society in London comprised 105 items of Malay books and manuscripts, including the *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Hikayat Abdullah*, the *Taj al-Salatin*, and the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* in two volumes; in several cases he borrowed originals from Malay owners to have copies made, then returned the originals so they remained in Malaya (p. 17).
- The Selangor Land Code of 1890, drafted under Maxwell as Resident, laid the foundations of the modern National Land Code; R. H. Hickling, who drafted the 1965 National Land Code, confirmed that "three quarters of a century after Maxwell produced his Selangor Land Code, its basic principles had been preserved in the current land legislation" (p. 14, n. 19).
- Maxwell's collection of 301 Malay proverbs (published in *JSBRAS* 1–3, 1878–79) was followed by a further 272 from French and Dutch sources (*JSBRAS* 11, 1883); subsequent collections, including C. C. Brown's *Malay Sayings*, drew on Maxwell's work as a principal source (p. 17, n. 39).
- The "Nisero" affair of 1883–85, in which Maxwell was sent as envoy to negotiate the release of 30 shipwrecked crew members held by the Raja of Tenom in Aceh, resulted in a Dutch payment of $38,000 as compensation; Maxwell was awarded a CMG and £500 by the British government (pp. 10–14).
- Maxwell's Society membership figures show the Journal's recovery under his editorship: membership fell from 153 to 111 by 1882, then rose to 157 by 1891 (p. 21).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that Maxwell's legacy to Malay studies is immense and varied, and that the Society owes him "a debt of gratitude" (p. 29). Maxwell was the pioneer who bridged the gulf between armchair anthropologists and field observers, and whose work — from linguistic analysis to land tenure to oral literature — established the empirical foundation upon which later professional scholarship was built. His flaws, particularly his autocratic temperament and his indefensible tithe theory, do not diminish the high standard of scholarship that characterizes his broader output.

## Context

- The article draws on Maxwell's official papers in the Colonial Office records (CO 273 series), Parliamentary Papers (including C.3429 of 1882 on slavery and the 1884–85 volumes on the "Nisero"), the Royal Asiatic Society's Maxwell bequest (105 items), SOAS manuscripts (MS 46941–46946), and the RCS/BAM papers at the Royal Commonwealth Society Library.
- Historiographically, the article positions Maxwell as the first figure in Malayan studies to combine systematic field observation with comparative linguistic and historical scholarship, predating the professionalization of the discipline at universities. It also corrects several biographical errors in earlier accounts (e.g., Isabella Bird's claim that Maxwell was educated at Oxford) and provides the first comprehensive inventory of his published and unpublished writings on Malayan subjects (Appendix B).
