# Golden flowers

Alastair Lamb's 1959 report, based on four field visits to Kedah in 1957–1958 and the University of Malaya Archaeological Society's February 1958 expedition, revisits and amplifies the pioneering excavations of H.G.Q. Wales at the ancient temple sites of central Kedah. Lamb's overarching argument is twofold: that Wales's documentation was too sketchy to serve as a reliable foundation for future scholarship, and that Wales's attribution of the Kedah temples to direct Pallava (South Indian) colonisation rests on methodologically unsound reasoning about "associated finds."

## Summary

Lamb's central focus is Site 8, a granite temple on the west bank of the Sungei Batu Pahat near Merbok, which he identifies as the most extensive and best-preserved of all the Kedah sites Wales investigated. Through fresh excavation and measurement, Lamb establishes the temple's full extent—terrace walls, a long porch (mandapam), a substantial plinth, a sanctuary with a ring of socles, and a levelled platform behind—revealing a structure far larger than Wales's brief description suggested. Most significantly, the expedition uncovered an intact, unopened granite casket in a niche in the sanctuary's rear wall, which Lamb identifies as almost certainly a third nine-chambered reliquary from the site.

The article's most substantial intellectual contribution is its critique of Wales's dating and cultural attribution methodology. Lamb draws on Stutterheim's 1939 argument that the chandi ritual and its associated nine-chambered reliquaries originated in pre-Hindu Java rather than in India, thereby undermining Wales's claim that these objects prove direct Pallava colonisation of Kedah. Lamb further demonstrates that Wales's dating of Sites 4 through 9 to the sixth or seventh century relies on a small number of portable objects—a miniature bronze shrine roof, a Ganesa figure, a bronze trident fragment, and a few ceramic shards—whose connection to the architecture they are supposed to date is tenuous at best. The implication is that the Kedah temples may show less direct Indian influence and more insular South East Asian (Javanese or Srivijayan) affinity than Wales allowed.

Lamb also surveys several other sites briefly: Site 7 on Bukit Gajah Mati, where a modern bungalow has been built over earlier laterite foundations; Site 18 on the Sungei Bujang, where celadon and glazed pottery suggest a port; Bukit Meriam (Site 26), where a socle with a triangular mortise was found in a nearby village; and Site 28, where celadon dishes fished from the Muda River are now in the possession of the Prime Minister.

### Key Findings

- Site 8's plinth measures approximately 36 feet square and 6 feet high, comprising 16–18 courses of granite bricks; the sanctuary measures about 20 feet square at its base with walls 5 feet thick (pp. 218–219).
- An intact granite casket, slightly over 7 inches cube with its lid in place, was discovered *in situ* in a niche in the centre of the sanctuary's rear (north-west) wall; it is almost certainly a nine-chambered reliquary matching the fragments Wales found at the same site (pp. 221–222).
- Five distinct types of socle (A through E) were identified at Site 8, each reserved for a specific architectural function; Type E socles contain a round depression at the base of their mortise in which Wales reported a small jewel was deposited (pp. 223–224).
- The total distance from the first terrace wall to the rear platform exceeds 300 feet, making Site 8 the most extensive of the Kedah sites (p. 216).
- Comparative analysis of Dravidian and Javanese temple proportions suggests the original roof height from plinth to finial was between 40 and 80 feet (p. 226).
- At Site 18, fragments of celadon and a bluish glazed bowl with fluted decoration were recovered from a shallow pit abundant in pottery and small tiles, supporting the interpretation of the location as a port (p. 231).

### Conclusion

Lamb's definitive takeaway is that the dating and cultural attribution of the Kedah temples—particularly the claim of direct Pallava colonisation in the seventh or eighth century—remains unproven and rests on an insecure evidential basis. Until the casket at Site 8 is opened and its contents examined, and until the porch, terraces, and rear platform are fully excavated, the architectural and historical problems posed by these structures cannot be resolved with confidence.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the field records and publications of H.G.Q. Wales (JMBRAS 18, 1940; 20, 1947) and on Stutterheim's *Studies in Indonesian Archaeology* (1956), positioning itself as a corrective to Wales's interpretive framework.
- Lamb's work represents an early example of systematic re-investigation of colonial-era archaeological sites in Malaya, introducing more rigorous measurement, typological classification of architectural elements, and critical engagement with the provenance of portable finds as dating evidence.
