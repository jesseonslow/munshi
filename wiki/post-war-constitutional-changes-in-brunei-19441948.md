# Post-war constitutional changes in Brunei 1944―1948

Ooi Keat Gin's 1990 article examines the education policies and programmes of the British Colonial Government in Sarawak from 1946 to 1963, arguing that the Colonial Office pursued two interlocking objectives: closing the wide educational disparity between native and Chinese populations, and transforming the plural, racially segmented school systems inherited from the Brooke era into a unified national system that would foster a common Sarawak identity in preparation for self-government.

## Summary

The article traces how the Colonial Government, which assumed administration of Sarawak upon the cession from the Brookes in July 1946, confronted a deeply unequal educational landscape. Under the Brooke Rajahs, the Chinese community had developed an extensive self-funded vernacular school system while the native races—Malays, Dayaks, and other indigenous peoples—had received comparatively minimal provision, with illiteracy rates among indigenous groups reaching 93–98 per cent by the 1947 census (pp. 40–41). The Colonial Government's response was a comprehensive programme that combined expansion of native primary schooling in the rural outstations with the deliberate dismantling of communal education at the secondary level through the Conversion Plan, which mandated English as the medium of instruction in all secondary schools (pp. 48–49).

The narrative is organised around the practical mechanisms through which these objectives were pursued: self-help methods for school construction, the Grant Code (Regulations) of 1956 which shifted financing responsibility to the government, the establishment of the Batu Lintang Teacher Training Centre in 1948, the Group Headmaster Scheme, Schools Broadcasting, and the introduction of common syllabuses, Sarawak-centric textbooks, and civics instruction (pp. 42–50). A significant portion of the article is devoted to the responses of different communities to these policies. The Malay community's initial apathy gave way to greater engagement, though the Cession controversy of 1946–47 caused a temporary setback when mass teacher resignations closed a third of Government Malay schools (pp. 54–55). The Chinese response was more complex: while the English-educated majority was broadly supportive, the Chinese-educated minority, influenced by the Clandestine Communist Organization, mounted vocal opposition to the Conversion Plan, which the author characterises as stage-managed Communist agitation rather than genuine community sentiment (pp. 57–59).

Primary sources from the Sarawak State Archives, Colonial Office records, Council Negri debates, Education Department triennial surveys, and mission society papers underpin the analysis. The author draws on both official government documents and the internal records of the Anglican and Roman Catholic missions to present a multi-perspective account of policy implementation and community reception.

### Key Findings

- By 1937, Chinese student enrolment (11,279) was more than five times that of all native groups combined (2,721), and the 1947 census recorded illiteracy rates of 98 per cent among Sea Dayaks and "Other Indigenous" peoples versus 66 per cent among the Chinese (pp. 40–41).
- The Grant Code (Regulations) of 1956 made the government responsible for all approved recurrent expenditure of aided schools, with capital expenditure shared equally; native student enrolment rose from 15,121 in 1954 to 57,700 (estimated) by 1961, while total recurrent education expenditure increased from $1,042,137 to $10,907,492 over the same period (pp. 43–44).
- The Cession controversy triggered the resignation of 87 out of 152 Malay teachers and the closure of 20 out of 62 Government Malay schools; all 56 student teachers at the Sarawak Malay Teachers Training College also ceased their studies in protest (p. 54).
- Eleven out of sixteen Chinese Middle Schools accepted the Conversion Plan; the remaining five ceased to receive government aid from 1 April 1962 when they opted to retain Kuo-yu as their medium of instruction (p. 59).
- Schools Broadcasting, which began in 1959 with assistance from The Asia Foundation and the Australian, New Zealand, and Japanese governments, reached over 60,000 children in 770 schools by the end of 1960 (p. 46).
- By September 1962, natives comprised only 18.33 per cent of secondary school enrolment against 80.36 per cent for the Chinese, and approximately 70 per cent of non-Malay indigenous children of school age had yet to receive any formal education (pp. 60–61).

### Conclusion

Ooi concludes that while the Colonial Government made substantial progress in expanding educational access and establishing the framework of a national education system, the fundamental objective of achieving educational parity between native and Chinese populations remained unfulfilled on the eve of Malaysia's formation in 1963. The disparity at the secondary level was particularly acute, and the author stresses that lasting improvement in native education would require not only greater financial investment but a "mental revolution" in indigenous attitudes towards formal schooling—a change that could not be imposed from above (p. 61).

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Sarawak State Archives, Colonial Office records at the Public Record Office in London, Rhodes House (Oxford) collections including the papers of M.G. Dickson, R.A. Bewsher, and J.A. Young, and the internal records of the Anglican Borneo Mission Association (*The Chronicle*).
- The article contributes to the historiography of colonial education policy in Borneo by situating Sarawak's experience within the broader framework of British decolonisation and the preparation of protectorates for self-government, while also documenting the role of Communist subversion in shaping educational controversies during the Cold War period.
