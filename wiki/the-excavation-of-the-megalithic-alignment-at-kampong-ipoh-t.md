# The excavation of the megalithic alignment at Kampong Ipoh, Tampin, Negri Sembilan: a note. Adi Haji Taha and Abdul Jalil Osman

Adi Haji Taha and Abdul Jalil Osman (1982) report the first systematic excavation of a megalithic alignment in Peninsular Malaysia, conducted at Kampong Ipoh, Tampin, Negeri Sembilan, in late 1981 and early 1982. Their central finding is that the site, long believed by local communities to be an ancestral burial ground, yielded no skeletal remains within the alignment itself, thereby providing the first negative evidence to contradict the widespread *batu hidup* tradition.

## Summary

The article addresses a persistent gap in Malayan prehistoric archaeology: the near-total absence of published excavation data on the peninsula's megalithic alignments. Taha and Osman frame the problem in two parts. First, the scholarly literature is thin—Sheppard's 1962 monograph remains the most substantial treatment, and even that work merely lists the Kampong Ipoh alignment without detailed study. Second, the local belief system surrounding the *batu hidup*—stones held to be sacred, sometimes dangerous, and treated as ancestral graves—has made excavation politically and culturally difficult. The authors describe how the site was only made accessible because a proposed road through a housing scheme threatened to destroy it, and the Museums Department seized the opportunity to excavate before the stones were relocated (pp. 75–76).

The excavation methodology followed standard archaeological procedure: three initial cuttings across the mound were expanded to cover the entire barrow, with the stones left standing until the end. The deposit was vertically homogenous, making stratigraphic identification difficult, and the soil structure differed between the western and eastern halves of the mound. The authors are candid about the limited positive results—ceramics (mainly later-period blue-and-white Chinese and Islamic ware) and a few buried stone slabs were the principal finds. The stone slabs, found more than a metre below the present surface, offer a tentative clue that the alignment was erected when the ground level was significantly lower, implying considerable antiquity, though no absolute date could be determined (pp. 76–77).

The article's significance lies less in its positive artefactual yield than in what the excavation did not find. The absence of human remains within the alignment area directly challenges the local tradition that these stones mark ancestral graves. The authors acknowledge that confirmation requires excavation of additional sites in Alor Gajah and Kuala Pilah, which they note the Museums Department intended to undertake (p. 81).

### Key Findings

- The Kampong Ipoh alignment measured 48 metres in total length and contained 79 stones, of which 63 exhibited the characteristic features of normal menhirs; the western half held 54 stones (mostly in pairs) on a 10-metre-wide mound, while the eastern half had fewer stones on a 4-metre-wide mound (p. 75).
- Excavation reached a depth of approximately 4 metres in the western half (to the water table) and was shallower in the eastern half; no skeletal remains were found within the megalithic area proper (pp. 76, 81).
- A single human skeleton was recovered just outside the mound on the westernmost edge, near the main road; the skull was slightly damaged and the burial was attributed to the Japanese occupation period (1941–1945) (p. 81).
- The bulk of recovered artefacts consisted of later-period blue-and-white Chinese and Islamic ceramics with floral motifs, occurring from the surface down to approximately 165 cm; the authors suggest these may represent ritual offerings by local communities (p. 76).
- Stone slabs were found buried more than one metre below the present earth surface, indicating the alignment was erected when the ground level was at least one metre lower than at the time of excavation (pp. 76–77).
- Most menhirs were buried no more than 40 cm into the ground regardless of size; some tall stones were buried to only 20 cm, while a few small stones had three-quarters of their length underground (p. 81).

### Conclusion

Taha and Osman conclude that the excavation, though unproductive in terms of positive dating evidence, provided "negative evidence which is of great significance" by demonstrating for the first time that a megalithic alignment in Peninsular Malaysia is not a burial site. This finding directly contradicts the deeply held local tradition that the *batu hidup* are ancestral grave markers, and the authors call for further excavations at other alignments to confirm and extend the result (p. 81).

## Context

- The study draws on the Museums Department's field records and the authors' direct excavation experience; no external archival collections are cited beyond the existing published literature (Sheppard, Chandran, Evans, Harrisson, Loofs).
- Historiographically, the article marks the first time a Peninsular megalithic alignment was subjected to systematic archaeological excavation, filling a gap that Harrisson (1962) had identified as the only viable path toward clarifying the function and antiquity of these monuments.
