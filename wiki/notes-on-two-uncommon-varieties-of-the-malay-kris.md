# Notes on two uncommon varieties of the Malay kris

G.B. Gardner, a British resident in Malaya for twenty years, published this short note in 1933 describing two exceptionally rare varieties of the Malay kris—the *Keris Majapahit* and the *Keris Pichit*—drawing on his personal collection, museum specimens, and extensive Malay oral testimony to argue that these weapons are ancient relics from a period when iron was scarce and its working was shrouded in ritual secrecy.

## Summary

Gardner's central problem is one of scarcity and verification. Having spent two decades in the Malay Peninsula, he had encountered only a handful of specimens of each type, making it impossible to establish a firm typology or to confirm the extraordinary powers attributed to them in popular belief. His method is essentially comparative: he examines the physical characteristics of the few specimens available to him, cross-references them with the folklore he has collected from Malay informants, and then attempts to reconcile the contradictory stories into a coherent historical explanation.

The *Keris Majapahit* is described as a small, slender, straight-bladed weapon of black iron, with the blade and hilt forged as a single piece and damascene (*pamur*) visible on both. The hilt takes the form of a seated figure wearing a head-dress, which from the rear resembles a cobra reared to strike. The *Keris Pichit* is distinguished by round depressions on either side of the blade, as if the metal had been pinched between fingertips while red-hot; Gardner notes that the *pamur* pattern runs continuously through these marks, indicating they were formed during the forging process rather than applied afterwards. He rejects the suggestion of Professor Balfour at the Pitt Rivers Museum that the *Majapahit* were currency bars, arguing their form clearly indicates a weapon.

Gardner's interpretive framework draws on Raffles' *History of Java*, which records a king of Majapahit who could shape red-hot iron with his bare hands, and on the testimony of the Dutch archaeologist Dr. van Stein-Callenfels, who dated such weapons to the earliest Iron Age in Java. Gardner synthesises these sources with the Malay oral tradition to propose that the "magic" stories are residual memories of a period when iron was so rare and valuable that smiths used ritual and secrecy to protect their monopoly on the craft. The dedication of a spirit (*hantu*) to the blade, the association with a snake deity, and the claims of self-acting power are, in his view, layered accretions of belief built upon a genuine technological mystery.

### Key Findings

- In twenty years of residence in Malaya, Gardner identified only eight *Keris Majapahit* and three *Keris Pichit* in total; of the Majapahit, four were in the Singapore Museum (two of which he judged to be later Chinese copies), one belonged to a lady in Malacca, one was in the Pitt Rivers Museum at Oxford, and two were in his own possession (p. 178).
- Dr. van Stein-Callenfels, the Dutch archaeologist, dated the *Keris Majapahit* to the earliest Iron Age in Java, approximately the 4th century A.D., while acknowledging that "scarcely anything is known" about them (p. 180).
- The *Keris Pichit* finger-marks are formed during manufacture, as the *pamur* (damascene) pattern follows continuously through the depressions, ruling out the possibility that they were applied after forging (p. 179).
- Raffles' *History of Java* records that a king of Majapahit could squeeze red-hot iron into kris shape with his bare hands, and that smiths in Majapahit were granted land and slaves in recognition of their importance (p. 181).
- Majapahit destroyed Singapore c. 1360 and was itself overthrown by a combination of Muslim states in the 14th century, which would place the weapons at over 600 years old by Gardner's reckoning (p. 180).
- Gardner records a legend of a kris in the Taiping Museum that allegedly leaves its case at night, kills someone, cleans itself, and returns before morning—a story he could not verify (p. 181).

### Conclusion

Gardner's definitive takeaway is that the *Keris Majapahit* and *Keris Pichit* are genuine ancient weapons from a period when iron was rare and its working was a closely guarded, ritualised craft, and that the elaborate folklore surrounding them—poison, spirits, self-acting combat, fire-drawing—is best understood as a cultural overlay on a real technological mystery rather than as literal fact. He remains open to further evidence and explicitly invites readers to contribute information on dates, provenance, and additional specimens.

## Context

- Gardner was a British colonial administrator in Malaya; his access to specimens came through his official position and personal collecting, and his informants were predominantly Malay. His tone is that of a sympathetic but sceptical outsider, explicitly aware of the risk of "starting new stories" by suggesting interpretations to his informants (p. 180).
- The article relies on no formal archival collection; its evidential basis is personal observation of a very small number of objects, oral testimony, and secondary references to Raffles and van Stein-Callenfels. Its historiographical value lies in preserving a snapshot of Malay oral tradition regarding these weapons at a time when the specimens themselves were still scattered across private collections and museums.
