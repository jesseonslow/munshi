# Sea Dyak religion

J. Perham's 1884 article "Sea Dyak Religion (Continued)" completes a two-part ethnographic study of the religious life of the Sea Dyaks of Sarawak, published in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society. Writing from within the colonial administrative framework of White Rajah Sarawak, Perham documents the burial rites, eschatological beliefs, and marriage customs of these Bornean highland peoples, arguing that their afterlife observances constitute a remarkably elaborate system of reciprocal obligation between the living and the dead that exceeds those of most other known societies.

## Summary

The article opens with a brief account of Sea Dyak marriage, centred on the *Mlah pinang* (splitting the betel-nut) divination, before turning to its principal subject: the complex ritual architecture surrounding death. Perham draws on direct observation of burials, wailing ceremonies, and the *Garei antu* (Festival of Departed Spirits) to reconstruct the Sea Dyak eschatological worldview. The narrative is organised not chronologically but thematically, moving from the moment of death through interment, post-burial observances (*pana*, *sumping*), the great festival, and finally the popular cosmology of the afterworld.

A central argument runs throughout: the Sea Dyak conception of the dead is neither purely fearful nor purely affectionate but a fusion of both. The dead are *antu*—spirits capable of harm and of benefit—and the elaborate ritual cycle exists to manage this dual relationship. Perham shows how each ceremony addresses a specific anxiety: the wailer's song ensures the soul reaches Hades; the *pana* feeds it; the *sumping* with head-hunting trophies restores its dignity; and the *Garei antu* formally releases it from all claims on the living. The professional wailer (*manang*) is the indispensable agent in nearly every stage, her incantations serving as the vehicle by which food, messages, and even the dead themselves are transmitted between worlds.

Perham also records the popular cosmology that undergirds these rites: the borderland house of the Bird Buhut, the Bridge of Fear, the Hill of Fire (the sole suggestion of moral retribution), and the ultimate fate of successive deaths ending in dissolution into air, fog, or unnamed jungle plants. The article closes with the folk tale of *Kadawa*, a cock-fighter who wanders into deathland and returns only to learn his wife has died, illustrating in narrative form the permeability of the boundary between the two worlds.

### Key Findings

- The professional wailer's song for the dead takes approximately twelve hours to perform and narrates the spirit of the winds journeying to Hades to fetch the departed soul, which is then carried off by a boat that kills all neighbouring fish in its wake (pp. 289–290).
- The *Garei antu* (Festival of Departed Spirits) is held at a variable interval of one to three or four years after death, with preparations lasting weeks or months and taxing the household's resources severely enough to diminish the following year's paddy crop (pp. 295, 299).
- The *baiya* (grave goods) includes personal necessaries buried with the body (rice, plates, betel-nut mixture, money) and surface deposits (spears, gongs, jars, gongs broken to prevent theft); a burial without *baiya* is equated with "the burial of a dog" (p. 291).
- The *sumping* observance, which conveyed head-hunting trophies to the dead to restore their standing in Hades, could under ancient custom not be performed until a new enemy's head had been obtained; by 1884 it was "very seldom" carried out in its full form under colonial administration (p. 295).
- The "drinking the bamboo"—in which an elderly guest of noted bravery or wealth consumes *tuak* reserved as the sacred portion of Hades—represents the climactic moment of intercommunion between the living and the dead at the *Garei antu* (p. 298).
- The Sea Dyak eschatology includes a "Hill of Fire" through which evil souls are compelled to pass while good souls are led around by an easy path, which Perham identifies as the only instance he encountered suggesting future retribution for wrongdoing (p. 299).

### Conclusion

Perham's definitive takeaway is that the Sea Dyak afterlife is neither a gloomy Tartarus nor a blessed Elysium but a straightforward continuation of present conditions in a new sphere—complete with paddy farming, social inequality, and mutual aid—yet bounded by an ultimate annihilation through successive deaths (three or seven, depending on the informant) ending in absorption into air, fog, or unrecognised jungle plants. The extraordinary elaboration of their eschatological observances, Perham suggests, reflects not a capacity for imagining endless life but a practical system for managing the perceived needs of the dead and the reciprocal obligations of the living.

## Context

- Perham was a British colonial administrator in Sarawak under the Brooke regime; the article is framed by his direct observational access to Sea Dyak communities and his familiarity with local Malay terminology, which he annotates for the metropolitan readership.
- The article draws on no named archival collection; its evidential basis is entirely field observation and oral testimony gathered in Sarawak during the early 1880s, making it one of the earliest systematic ethnographic records of Sea Dyak funerary religion.
