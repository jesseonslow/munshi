# A romanized version of the Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai

R. O. Winstedt's 1917 article traces the diffusion of Islam across the Malay Peninsula and Archipelago from the earliest Arab trading contacts at Kedah in the tenth century through the establishment of Muslim polities in Java, Sumatra, Borneo, and the Moluccas by the seventeenth century. The overarching thesis is that Islam spread through a combination of commercial contact, royal conversion, and the deliberate missionary activity of Arab and Indian (particularly Gujarati and Hadhrami) scholars, with each successive trading port—Kedah, Pasai, Malacca, Acheen—serving as a relay point for the faith's advance.

## Summary

Winstedt constructs a narrative of religious diffusion anchored in the succession of great trading ports. He begins with Arab traders at Kedah around 915 A.D. and follows the centre of gravity as it shifts to Pasai (where Sultan Maliku's-Salleh converted in the late thirteenth century), then to Malacca (where Sultan Muhammad Shah, the first Muslim ruler, ascended in 1403), and finally to Acheen after the Portuguese fall of Malacca in 1511. The article draws heavily on the *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai*, the *Kedah Annals*, and the *Bustanu's-salatin* to trace royal conversions and the transmission of religious texts between courts.

A distinctive feature of the piece is its attention to the material and cultural texture of the conversion process. Winstedt discusses the importation of tomb-stones from Cambay and other Indian centres, the presence of foreign merchandise (Kashmiri shawls, Meccan robes, Khorassan steel) in Malay folk-tales, and the social friction between missionaries and local elites, illustrated by two vivid anecdotes from the *Sejarah Melayu* involving the Arab teacher Sadar Jahan and the courtiers Sri Rama and Tun Mai. These episodes reveal that conversion was neither smooth nor uniform, and that linguistic and cultural barriers posed real obstacles to the missionary enterprise.

The article extends beyond the Peninsula to cover Java (the fall of Majapahit in 1478 and the rise of Demak), the spread from Acheen through Minangkabau to Lampung, the conversion of Borneo and the Celebes in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and the Islamisation of the Moluccas. Winstedt closes by pointing to the genealogies of ruling families across the archipelago as a further source for tracing the religion's spread.

### Key Findings

- Arab traders reached Kedah by 915 A.D.; Marco Polo found Muslims at Perlak but not yet at Samudra-Pasai in 1292 A.D. (p. 2).
- Sultan Maliku's-Salleh of Pasai died in 1297 A.D.; Muhammadan tomb-stones at Pasai dated 1407 A.D. have been identified by J. P. Magnette as originating from Cambay, India (pp. 2–3).
- Sultan Muhammad Shah, the first Muslim ruler of Malacca, reigned 1403–1414 A.D.; Chinese records confirm the Malays of Malacca were Muslim by 1409 A.D. (p. 3).
- The tomb-stone of Sultan Mansur Shah (d. 1475) bears an Arabic inscription dated 880 A.H.; the stone of Shaikh Ahmad Makhtar Ramali at Pangkalan Kempas is dated A.H. 872 (1467 A.D.) (p. 4).
- The *Bustanu's-salatin* and *Siratu'l-mustakim* were composed by Shaikh Nuru'd-din ar-Raniri of Gujerat at Acheen in the 1630s; the *Kedah Annals* record that copies of these works were sent to Kedah on the occasion of its conversion (p. 5).
- The Hindu kingdom of Majapahit fell in 1478 A.D.; the first Arab missionary to Java, Maulana Malik Ibrahim, died at Grisek in 1419 A.D. (p. 5).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that the Islamisation of the Malay world was a gradual, multi-century process driven by the commercial and political centrality of successive port-states, in which royal conversion acted as the catalyst for wider adoption. The faith did not arrive as a single wave but propagated through a chain of trading nodes, each of which absorbed and re-transmitted Islamic culture to its neighbours, with Acheen serving as the final and most powerful engine of diffusion in the post-Malaccan era.

## Context

- The article draws on the *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai* (as published in J.R.A.S. S.B. No. 66), the *Kedah Annals* (J.R.A.S. S.B. No. 72), the *Bustanu's-salatin*, and archaeological evidence from Pasai, Malacca, and Pangkalan Kempas.
- Winstedt's study contributes to the historiography of Islamic expansion in Southeast Asia by integrating textual, epigraphic, and archaeological evidence into a single chronological narrative, and by foregrounding the role of Indian (Gujarati and Hadhrami) missionaries alongside Arab ones—a point that was not always emphasised in earlier accounts.
