# Acheh, commonly called Acheen

G.P. Tolson's 1880 article in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* is a descriptive compilation of notes on the Sultanate of Acheh (Aceh), covering its geography, political subdivisions, dynastic history, and material culture as observed by a European visitor. The piece functions as a practical guide for traders and administrators, blending ethnographic observation with political geography, and draws on earlier European sources—Valentyn, Crawfurd, Anderson, and Veth—alongside the author's own field experience.

## Summary

Tolson's account is organized around a political-geographic framework, dividing Acheh into four zones: the East Coast, the North-East Coast, the West Coast, and Acheh Besar (Acheen Proper). Each zone is enumerated with its constituent states and ruling families, revealing a patchwork of petty principalities whose rulers were largely self-made immigrants from older centres such as Pidir, Gighen, and Tclok Semoy. The only ruler Tolson identifies as possessing genuine royal blood is the Tunku Maharaja of Tclok Semawei, who had formerly acted as the Sultan's *Wakil* collecting tribute from the East Coast states. This fragmentation, he argues, was the product of the Sultanate's long decline from its zenith under Iskander Muda (r. 1606–1641), when Acheh's dominion extended across the entire East Coast and, as late as 1652, the whole West Coast including Padang (pp. 40–42).

The article devotes considerable attention to the internal administrative structure of Acheh Besar, describing the three *Sagis* (XXV, XXVI, and XXII Mukims), their subdivision into *Mukims* and villages, and the hereditary council of four *Hulubalangs* that had governed until the reign of the last three Sultans. Tolson notes that this council had been abolished, leaving the Sultan to rule without formal advisers beyond court favourites, with the *Tuku Kali* (High Priest) wielding decisive influence in the selection of new rulers (pp. 44–45). The coronation system, centred on four recognised *Misjids*, is described as a safeguard against the invalidation of a succession should the primary site fall to an enemy.

The ethnographic portions of the article—covering dress, weapons, housing, diet, labour division, agriculture, and military practice—are written in the frank, often unflattering idiom of a colonial observer. Tolson characterises the Achinese as "more turbulent, more piratical, more treacherous" than the Sumatran Malay, and describes the rulers' practice of keeping *sedalis* (boy-favourites aged eight to twelve) trained as *Bayaderes* (p. 47). Yet he also acknowledges their considerable military skill, noting their ability to construct entrenched positions, rifle pits, and bomb-proof underground tunnels (p. 49).

### Key Findings

- The Sultanate of Acheh reached its zenith under Iskander Muda (r. 1606–1641); by 1652 it still controlled the entire West Coast including Padang, but had subsequently fragmented into numerous independent petty states (pp. 41–42).
- The Arabic dynasty, beginning with Sultan Mahmud Shah (r. 1760–1781), produced a genealogical line extending through Sultan Mantsur Shah (r. 1838–1870) and Sultan Mahmud Shah (r. 1870–1874), with multiple surviving *Tunku* princes listed as of 1880 (p. 43).
- The hereditary council of four *Hulubalangs*—Maharaja Mangku Bumi, Maharaja Mangku Besi, Perdana Menti, and Laksamana Panglima Dalam—had been abolished by the time of the last three Sultans, concentrating power in the Sultan and the *Tuku Kali* (p. 45).
- The population was generally accepted at approximately 1.5 million, composed of a mixture of Hindu, Malay, and aboriginal hill-tribe stock, with Tamil, Arabic, and Nias minorities concentrated at coastal ports (p. 46).
- Pepper was the chief export of the East and West Coasts; the North-East Coast produced betel and tobacco; Acheh Besar produced little for export, its people being "more commercial" and content with *sawah* cultivation (p. 48).
- The universal currency was the Carolus dollar (*ringgit* "Meriam") and Straits copper; earlier the currency had consisted of small gold *derhams* and tiny lead half-cent pieces (p. 49).

### Conclusion

Tolson's definitive historical takeaway is that Acheh's political disintegration was the natural consequence of a once-powerful centralised Sultanate losing its capacity to enforce tribute and military discipline over distant provinces, a process accelerated by the Portuguese wars in the Malayan Peninsula which consumed the Sultan's attention and resources. The result, by 1880, was a collection of autonomous petty states whose rulers owed no real allegiance to the nominal sovereign at Kota Raja, a condition that would soon be exploited by the Dutch in their eventual conquest of the Sultanate.

## Context

- Tolson writes as a British colonial observer—likely a trader or administrative official based in the Straits Settlements—whose account is framed as a practical reference for fellow Europeans dealing with Acheh. His ethnographic judgments (e.g., the Achinese as "the greater blackguard of the two") reflect the unapologetic racial hierarchies of Victorian colonial discourse (p. 46).
- The article draws on Valentyn (1688), Crawfurd, Anderson, and Veth for historical context, and on Van Ophuyzen's transcription of the *Undang Undang* of Menangkabau for the etymological and dynastic claims, but its primary value lies in Tolson's own direct observation of the political and social landscape as it stood in the early 1870s.
