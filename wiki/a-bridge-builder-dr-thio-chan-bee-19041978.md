# A bridge-builder. Dr Thio Chan Bee (1904–1978)

Noel Ong's 1997 biographical study of Dr Thio Chan Bee (1904–1978), published in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, traces the life of a Straits Chinese educator and politician who, inspired by his Christian faith and the Moral Re-Armament movement, devoted himself to constructing inter-racial and inter-communal bridges in Singapore and Malaya from the 1920s through the 1970s. Set against the backdrop of colonial rule, Japanese occupation, the Malayan Union controversy, and the turbulent path to independence, the article argues that Thio's consistent commitment to cooperation over confrontation made him a pivotal though ultimately marginalised figure in the region's political development.

## Summary

The article reconstructs Thio's life across four broad phases: his formative years as a teacher at Anglo-Chinese School (ACS) in Singapore; his entry into politics as a nominated Legislative Councillor and later elected Assemblyman; his peripheral role in the mass-politics era after 1955; and his final decade as Education Secretary for Methodist schools in Malaysia and as the founder of the Association for the Promotion of Higher Education in Malaysia (APHEM). Ong draws heavily on Thio's private papers, his autobiography *Extraordinary Adventures of an Ordinary Man* (1977), and oral interviews to present a portrait of a man whose personal philosophy—shaped by childhood hardship, total Christian commitment in 1934, and the Moral Re-Armament movement—consistently oriented him toward mediation, consensus, and service to the less privileged.

The central historical problem the article addresses is how a non-confrontational, morally driven individual could operate effectively within the escalating racial and class politics of post-war Southeast Asia. Ong shows that Thio was most successful during the 1947–1955 period, when the political environment still favoured cooperation within the colonial framework. His mediation between Tan Cheng Lock and Dato Onn bin Ja'afar, his advocacy for educational reform, housing, and the Central Provident Fund, and his refusal to support a teachers' strike all illustrate a consistent pattern: Thio sought to build institutional and interpersonal bridges rather than mobilise mass opposition. After 1955, however, the rise of the People's Action Party and the demands of mass politics rendered his approach increasingly anachronistic, and he was progressively eclipsed.

The article also explores the tension between Thio's vision and the structural constraints of the era. His dream of a multi-racial college at Genting Highlands, conceived in the context of the New Economic Policy's affirmative action for Malays, was ultimately defeated by political opposition from UMNO Youth and insufficient funding. Ong uses this episode to illustrate the broader pattern of Thio's life: a man whose ideals were consistently at odds with the political realities of his time, yet whose groundwork—particularly in education and in the institutional memory of inter-racial cooperation—outlived his personal political relevance.

### Key Findings

- Thio was appointed the first Asian principal of ACS in 1952, a position not held by an Asian in the school's 66-year history, and served until his retirement in 1960 (p. 78).
- The pivotal inter-racial meeting of 29 December 1948, convened at Dato Onn's house in Johore Bahru with ten Chinese and ten Malay leaders, directly led to the formation of the Malayan Chinese Association (MCA) and the Communities Liaison Committee, which in turn influenced the liberalisation of citizenship requirements in the 1952 Ordinance (pp. 83–84).
- The Singapore Polytechnic Ordinance was passed on 27 October 1954, establishing the institution as an autonomous body with Thio as a member of its eight-person Board of Governors; the Central Provident Fund scheme, which Thio first raised in the Legislative Council in June 1948, came into effect in 1955 (pp. 86–88).
- In the April 1955 elections, the Progressive Party won only four of twenty-two seats and Thio lost his Whampoa constituency to the Singapore Labour Front's Chew Swee Kee; in 1959, the PAP captured 43 of 51 seats while the SPA won only four of thirty-nine, though Thio himself won Tanglin by a small margin (pp. 89–91).
- The Genting Highlands College project, with a projected expenditure of M$2.6–3 million, attracted only M$1.5 million in donations; the completed science block and 120 acres of land were ultimately sold to the Malaysian Ministry of Education for M$7 million (p. 95).
- Thio was awarded the Commander of the British Empire (CBE) in June 1955, the honorary Doctor of Laws from the University of Malaya in 1961, and the Johan Mangku Negara (JMN) from the Malaysian federal government on 5 August 1965 (pp. 89, 94, 96).

### Conclusion

Ong's definitive takeaway is that Thio's legacy lies not in political power but in the institutional and moral groundwork he laid for inter-racial cooperation and educational development. His contributions to the CPF, the Singapore Polytechnic, the 1948 Tan–Onn reconciliation, and the Methodist school system have been largely forgotten in the national narrative, yet the seeds he sowed—particularly through APHEM—continued to bear fruit after his death in 1978, with the Genting Highlands College project resuscitated in the late 1990s. Ong frames Thio's life through the metaphor of the bridge-builder who constructs not for himself but for those who will follow, a figure whose quiet consistency in an age of political upheaval represents an alternative model of leadership that history has not adequately recognised.

## Context

- The study is primarily based on the private papers of Thio Chan Bee, loaned by his daughter Dr Eunice Thio, supplemented by official records, oral interviews, and secondary sources; it originated as an Honours Year exercise in the Department of History, National University of Singapore (1995) (p. 71, n. 1).
- Historiographically, the article contributes a biographical case study to the literature on non-confrontational political actors in post-war Southeast Asia, filling a gap in the English-language record on Straits Chinese figures who operated at the intersection of education, religion, and politics during the transition from colonialism to independence.
