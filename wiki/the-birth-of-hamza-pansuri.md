# The birth of Hamza Pansuri

L.F. Brakel's 1979 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 52, No. 235, pp. 73–98) presents a series of philological and interpretive notes on the 16th-century Acehnese mystic Hamza Pansuri, arguing that Hamza should be understood simultaneously as an imitator of Persian mystical literature and an innovator of the Malay literary tradition. The piece functions as prolegomena to a critical edition Brakel intended to produce, addressing technical problems he considered too specialized for a general monograph.

## Summary

The article opens by identifying the target of Hamza's polemics against yogic practices—breath control, concentration on the fontanel (*ubun-ubun*), and visualization of divine light—as the *pranayama* ritual still practised among Balinese Brahman priests. Brakel draws on Weck's ethnographic work on Balinese medicine, Soebadio's edition of a Balinese theological text, and Worsley's Javano-Balinese literary sources to demonstrate that the specific body-localisation practices Hamza attacks correspond precisely to Balinese Siva-worship rituals, in which the fontanel is regarded as the seat of Siva and the divine glow radiates from the forehead (pp. 73–77). From this, Brakel infers that pranayama or something very similar was still known in 16th-century Aceh, and that Allah was worshipped in a manner highly reminiscent of the Siva cult (p. 76).

The middle sections turn to literary-critical questions. Brakel analyses the recurring double-use of the rhyme-word *zahir* in Hamza's *sya'ir*, arguing it reflects a phonemic distinction between /z/ and /l/ realizations of the same Arabic root still operative in 16th-century Malay, with *zahir* carrying the sense "external" and *lahir* the sense "to be born" (pp. 77–79). He then examines the *taxallos*—the poet's self-naming in the final stanza, borrowed from the Persian *gazal* tradition—showing how this device serves both as a biographical key to Hamza's inner mystical experience and as a philological tool for authenticating his genuine verses against those of epigones who violate a negative structural rule: in Hamza's authentic poetry, Arabic-derived rhyme-words preserve their original vowel quantity, whereas his imitators consistently fail to do so (pp. 79–81). A further section on punning demonstrates how Hamza exploits homonymy (*Lahut/laut*, *Mansur/Pansur*, *Adamu/ada*) to express the mystical identity of phenomenal and transcendental realms, extending a technique inherited from Arabic and Persian sources into purely Malay linguistic territory (pp. 82–84).

The article also tackles a notoriously difficult passage in the *Kitab al-Muntahi* concerning the Pasai saying on *kufr* and *kufu'*, offering a new interpretation that reads "hidden" (*tertutup*) as a necessary precondition for mystical encounter rather than an obstacle to be removed. Brakel argues the passage has been misread by Voorhoeve and al-Attas because scholars followed ar-Raniri's selective quotation, which truncated the context before the crucial analogy of seed and tree and the reference to al-Hallaj's "Ana'l-Haqq" (pp. 85–88). He closes with a survey of Barus's historical importance as a cosmopolitan trading port attested in Greek, Indian, Jewish, Chinese, European, and Western Muslim sources from the 2nd to the 16th century, and a preliminary analysis of Hamza's imagery, identifying the source of his water-cycle simile in Fakhruddin Iraqi's *Lama'at* and arguing that the "internal landscape" of his imagery—torch-lit nights, cotton *kain*, fringed umbrellas, coconut and shell—is unmistakably Indonesian (pp. 89–96).

### Key Findings

- The *pranayama* practices Hamza attacks (breath drawn into the brain, divine glow localised in the fontanel, concentration between the eyebrows) correspond precisely to rituals described by Weck in Balinese Brahman practice and by Soebadio in a Balinese theological text, indicating the survival of Hindu elements in 16th-century Acehnese culture (pp. 74–76).
- A Tamil inscription from 1088 (reign of Cola emperor Kulottunga I, 1070–1120) discovered at Lobu Tuwa, Barus, evidences a permanent Tamil colony with stone-engraving artisans in the town (p. 89).
- Tome Pires (c. 1515) describes Barus as "the key to the land of the Menangkabau" and the principal Sumatran port for the export of gold, silk, benzoin, and camphor, with Gujarati traders visiting annually (pp. 90–91).
- The water-cycle simile in *Kitab Sarab al-'Asiqin* (*ta'ayyun awwal* = sea; waves; vapour; clouds; rain; river; sea) is an adaptation of a passage in Fakhruddin Iraqi's *Lama'at* (third *Lama'e*, p. 380 in Nafsi's 1956 edition), which Hamza modified to map onto the six emanations of his theological system (pp. 92–94).
- The Javanese translation of the *Kitab al-Muntahi* passage (Cod. Or. 7736, with variants from Cod. Or. 5716) confirms that the text continues beyond the point where ar-Raniri's quotation breaks off, containing the seed-and-tree analogy and the explicit reference to al-Hallaj's "Ana'l-Haqq" (pp. 88–89).
- Brakel identifies a negative structural rule in Hamza's genuine poetry: when rhyme-words are borrowed from Arabic, the rhyming vowels must preserve the same quantity as in the original; his epigones (Hasan Pansuri, 'Abdu'l-Jamal) consistently violate this rule, providing a criterion for textual authentication (pp. 80–81).

### Conclusion

Brakel's definitive historical takeaway is that Hamza Pansuri occupies a unique dual position in the history of Muslim letters: he is an imitator when viewed from the broader Islamic literary tradition (drawing on Persian Sufi imagery, Arabic punning conventions, and the *taxallos* device), yet an innovator within the Malay literary field, where he extended these techniques into indigenous linguistic and cultural territory. Hamza was able "almost singlehandedly, to mediate between the world of western Islam and Indonesia, to fuse the strange and the familiar and to integrate Indonesia into the general realm of Muslim, mystical poetry, without making it lose its 'rasa', its own taste and colour" (p. 96).

## Context

- Primary archival sources: MS M.L. 82 (Jakarta), the most reliable manuscript of Hamza's poetry; Cod. Or. 7736 and Cod. Or. 5716 (Leiden), Javanese translations of the *Kitab al-Muntahi*; Doorenbos' 1933 edition as the baseline text for collation.
- Historiographical contribution: The article corrects misreadings by Kraemer (1921), Voorhoeve (1955), and al-Attas (1970), and proposes new interpretive frameworks—Balinese yoga as the target of Hamza's polemics, the *taxallos* as a philological authentication tool, and the phonemic z/l distinction as an explanation for apparent metrical irregularities—while positioning itself as groundwork for a future critical edition.
