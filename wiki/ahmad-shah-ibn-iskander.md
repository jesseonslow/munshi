# Ahmad Shah ibn Iskander

Ahmad Shah ibn Iskandar, known by the title *Yang di-Pertuan Raja Sakti* (Supreme Holy Ruler), was a Minangkabau claimant to the rulership of that region who emerged in the 1680s as the central figure of the late 17th-century anti-*kafir* (infidel) movement in the Indonesian Archipelago. Claiming descent from Iskandar Dzu'l Karnain (Alexander the Great) and presenting himself as a saint destined to lead a holy war against the Dutch, he assembled a substantial following across Sumatra and Java before his activities shifted from religious insurgency to local political adventuring in the 1690s. Dutch colonial sources, preoccupied with commerce and political control, recorded him merely as a "troublesome pirate" in the Java Sea, reducing a complex movement to a single disruptive individual [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1).

## Origins and the Anti-Kafir Context

The circumstances that produced Ahmad Shah were an inevitable sequel to expanding Dutch influence following the capture of Malacca in 1641. The Dutch East India Company's treaties with west Sumatra in 1663, Macassar in 1667, Mataram in 1677, and Bantam in 1684 virtually ended the political independence of these areas, yet indigenous resistance persisted through non-compliance and the channeling of grievances into Islamic unity generated by anti-*kafir* propaganda from the old Muslim centres of Bantam, Giri, and Hitu [[1, pp. 48–49]](#ref-1). Islam's role in anti-colonial movements had early beginnings: Portuguese monopoly efforts in the 16th century provoked violent Islamic reaction in the Moluccas, and in the 17th century, religious propaganda under the patronage of Sunan Giri spearheaded anti-Dutch reaction [[1, p. 49]](#ref-1).

Ahmad Shah's actual background before his appearance at Billiton in 1685 is difficult to establish, and even his real name remains uncertain. He was sometimes referred to as Raja Ibrahim but was known more popularly as Paduka Ahmad Shah or Ahmad Shah ibn Iskandar [[1, p. 53]](#ref-1). All accounts agree that he came from the ruling house of Minangkabau, and it is very likely that he was one of the many contenders for the throne after the death in 1674 of the Minangkabau ruler of the same name, though there is insufficient evidence to prove he was either the ex-ruler's son or his maternal nephew [[1, p. 54]](#ref-1). His claim to holy descent from Iskandar Dzu'l Karnain drew upon a common tradition of legend shared by the rulers of Palembang, Malacca, Aceh, and Deli, whose royal houses all claimed direct or indirect lineage from Pagar-Ruyung [[1, pp. 53–54]](#ref-1).

## The Campaign of 1685–1687

Ahmad Shah first appeared at Billiton in 1685 with five *perahu* (vessels) and 200 men, selecting the island as a staging point because of its popularity as a base for marauders and its connections with Java through Palembang and Jambi [[1, p. 54]](#ref-1). He rapidly won the sympathy of Pangeran Aria of Palembang and the ruler of Jambi, assembling a fleet of approximately 300 vessels and a force nearly 4,000 strong, composed mainly of Macassarese and Minangkabaus with some recruits from Riau-Johore and Jambi [[1, p. 55]](#ref-1). From Billiton he wrote letters to Siam, Aceh, west Sumatra, Borneo, and Mataram, testifying that he was commissioned by God to expel the Dutch and appealing for assistance [[1, p. 55]](#ref-1).

The most significant response came from Amangkurat II of Mataram, who was anxious to cast off the Dutch stranglehold on his internal politics. The *Susuhunan* and his chief minister, Pangeran 'Dipati Amirang Kasuma, addressed Ahmad Shah as "grandfather" in intercepted letters, acknowledging him as the *Yang di-Pertuan Raja Sakti* and attributing to him regal claims, religious connections, and magical powers [[1, pp. 55–56]](#ref-1). The *Susuhunan*'s credulous acceptance can be understood through the importance of the Alexander legend in Javanese historiography, where the wizard "Sekandar" had been taken on by Senapati, the founder of Mataram, as companion, councillor, and guardian spirit [[1, p. 56]](#ref-1).

The Dutch, alarmed by these developments, assembled a combined fleet with Bantamese forces. Departing Batavia on 1 June 1686, the expedition comprised five Dutch vessels and eight *penchalang* (a type of war vessel) with a total crew of 60 soldiers and about 400 sailors, joined by 50 Bantamese *penchalang* manned by nearly 1,000 Bantamese, Bugis, and Balinese [[1, p. 57]](#ref-1). The campaign failed to locate Ahmad Shah's fleet, which had dispersed into three directions. Pursuing him up the Tulangbawang river in the Lampungs, the forces found only abandoned vessels and evacuated villages, the rebels employing superior guerrilla tactics with the evident cooperation of locals who withheld all information [[1, pp. 57–58]](#ref-1). Ahmad Shah eventually dashed into the hill-country with his remaining followers of about 300 [[1, p. 58]](#ref-1).

## Engagement with the British and Decline

Ahmad Shah crossed the Barisan highlands and arrived in March 1687 at the British settlement of Bengkulen, where the inhabitants received him with great rejoicing as a saint, presumably due to the popular belief that the founder of the ruling family of Sungai Lemau came from the ruling house of Minangkabau [[1, p. 58]](#ref-1). The British, initially wary of his anti-*kafir* background, soon found him amenable to an anti-Dutch alliance and allowed him to mint his own coinage [[1, p. 59]](#ref-1). He was rumoured to have been bribed with 1,000 *rijksdaalders* for assistance against the Bantamese pepper monopoly at Silebar, though no English Company record confirms this [[1, p. 59]](#ref-1). In January 1688, with the support of about 1,000 Bugis, Macassarese, and some English, he launched an attack on the Bantamese at Silebar, killing Pangeran Bandar, three *kiyai* (religious officials), and five lesser chiefs [[1, p. 60]](#ref-1).

Relations with the British deteriorated when Ahmad Shah proved more interested in local politics than in promoting commercial interests. By March 1689 they broke their connections with him, burned his *kampong* (village), and drove him out of Bengkulen [[1, p. 60]](#ref-1). He subsequently became entrenched in the internal politics of Sumatra, taking a leading role in the civil war at Manjuta before being forced to take refuge in Palembang in late 1689 [[1, pp. 60–61]](#ref-1). An English official who visited Padang in 1691 reported that the *Raja Sakti* had died in an encounter with Company forces and his head had been exhibited on a spike, but there is proof that he was active in Jambi for at least another five years; the British had presumably been mistaken through the false identification of a dead man [[1, p. 61]](#ref-1).

At the beginning of 1691 Ahmad Shah appeared in Jambi with a force nearly 7,000 strong, including about 2,000 armed men, claiming support from the ruler of Minangkabau. In 1694 he returned with 400 men, and in January 1695 he was last recorded at Bukit Silantuan, Sunai, in the upper reaches of the Batang Hari, after which there is no further record of him [[1, pp. 61–62]](#ref-1).

## Legacy and Documentation

Kathirithamby-Wells' 1970 study represents an early post-colonial attempt to reinterpret Indonesian resistance through indigenous religious and cultural frameworks rather than the Dutch colonial lens of "unrest" and "piracy," and it explicitly positions itself as a preliminary study inviting further research [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1). The article draws primarily on Dutch VOC archival sources, including the *Daghregister* and De Jonge's *De opkomst van het Nederlandsch gezag in Oost-Indie*, supplemented by English East India Company *Sumatra Factory Records* and secondary works by Veth, MacLeod, De Graaf, Schrieke, and Snouck Hurgronje [[1, pp. 48–63]](#ref-1).

The assessment of Ahmad Shah's significance is nuanced. There is little to disprove the Dutch Company's allegation that he was an impostor and political adventurer, and the sincerity of his claims as leader of the anti-Dutch movement is questionable in light of his later activities in Sumatra [[1, p. 63]](#ref-1). He did not emerge from the movement either as a political and military hero, as did Trunajaya and Surapati, or as a religious figure of veneration, like Shaikh Yusuf [[1, p. 63]](#ref-1). Nonetheless, for about a decade he fired the imagination of the leaders of the late 17th-century anti-*kafir* movement as a symbol of indigenous unity. In a situation where local rivalries precluded the ready acknowledgement of a leader from any specific region or political sphere, it was still possible to accept a personality whose credentials, however dubious, were drawn from the common Indonesian heritage of legend, history, and religion [[1, p. 63]](#ref-1).
