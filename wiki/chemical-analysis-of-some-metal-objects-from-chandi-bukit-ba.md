# Chemical analysis of some metal objects from Chandi Bukit Batu Pahat, Kedah: suggested origin and date

G. N. Appell's 1968 article examines the social organization of the Rungus, a Dusun-speaking cognatic group in the Kudat District of Sabah, Malaysia, applying a theoretical framework that distinguishes "social isolates" (entities with system-specific jural or ritual personality) from universal social units such as aggregates and collectivities. The central argument is that the Rungus domestic family and village function as both jural and ritual isolates, while the long-house is predominantly a jural and ritual collectivity, with a single exceptional ceremony in which it briefly assumes the status of a ritual isolate.

## Summary

Appell's purpose is to develop analytical procedures for identifying which social groupings in a given society possess independent jural and ritual personality—that is, which can enter into relations with other entities (human or spiritual) as a corporate unit rather than merely as an aggregate of individuals. He applies this framework to three major Rungus social groupings: the domestic family, the village, and the long-house. The Rungus practice swidden agriculture (rice, maize, manioc), invest surpluses in nondepreciating prestige goods such as gongs and brassware, and maintain a religion organized around fertility, curing, and property accumulation, with pigs and chickens sacrificed to spirits in all three domains (pp. 193–194).

The domestic family emerges as the most fully developed social isolate in both the jural and religious realms. It holds property rights as a unit over accumulated goods (*indopuan do nongkob*), contracts debts that survive the death of the male founder, pays and receives bride-prices and fines as a corporate body, and enters into ritual relations with spirits as a single entity—illness caused by an offended spirit may strike any family member, and the entire family must be ritually cleansed together (pp. 194–196). The village also qualifies as a jural and ritual isolate, though its personality is less developed: it holds residual ownership over bounded territory, its headman may sue trespassers for brassware or burn their swiddens, and it holds a corporate renewal ceremony roughly every decade that closes the territory to nonmembers and imposes post-ceremonial restrictions (illicit sex, sale of seeds) whose violation invalidates the ceremony for all (pp. 196–197).

The long-house, by contrast, possesses no corporately held property, no formal leader, and no specific office for initiating jural or ritual action. It functions as a jural and ritual collectivity: when a ritual delict is committed against its members (a cut ladder, a false death-implication, a corpse carried past), any skilled litigator may sue on behalf of the others, and the resulting chicken sacrifice cleanses all member families simultaneously, yet the restitution belongs to the suing family, not to the long-house as a unit (pp. 197–200). The single exception is the ceremony for the *labut* spirits, which afflict a long-house when a family that cut timbers for an apartment fails to join on. Here the long-house is treated as a ritual isolate—the *monginan* (first builder) represents it before the spirit world, and the pig's head is given to him to keep—yet it remains only a jural collectivity, since no specific jurally recognized representative initiates the litigation (pp. 200–201).

### Key Findings

- The Rungus domestic family is a fully developed jural and ritual isolate: it contracts debts, pays bride-prices and fines as a unit, and enters spirit relations as a single entity whose members share ritual jeopardy collectively (pp. 194–196).
- The village holds residual ownership over explicitly bounded territory and conducts a corporate renewal ceremony approximately every decade; violation of post-ceremonial restrictions (illicit intercourse, removal of seeds for three years) invalidates the ceremony for the entire village and triggers a suit for a pig (pp. 196–197).
- The long-house has no corporately held property, no formal leader, and no specific office for jural or ritual representation; it functions as a jural and ritual collectivity in which any skilled litigator may sue on behalf of co-members (pp. 197–198).
- The *labut* ceremony is the sole instance in which the long-house acts as a ritual isolate: the *monginan* represents the long-house before the spirit world, the pig's head is retained by him, and no stranger may enter for three days; yet the long-house remains a jural collectivity because no specific jurally recognized representative initiates the action (pp. 200–201).
- Agricultural surpluses are invested in gongs, brassware, and ceramic ware described as nondepreciating but income-producing; these goods constitute the family's accumulated assets (*indopuan do nongkob*) and are the medium of bride-price, fines, and ritual restitution (pp. 194–195).
- The Rungus kinship terminology is of Eskimo type, with cousins sometimes equated with siblings depending on social situation and level of contrast (p. 194).

### Conclusion

Appell's definitive takeaway is that the Rungus social system exhibits a clear hierarchy of corporate personality: the domestic family and village are both jural and ritual isolates with well-developed corporate rights and duties, while the long-house is a collectivity whose social nature is recognized but whose corporate personality is minimal and situational. The unusual case of the *labut* ceremony—where the long-house is a ritual isolate but not a jural isolate—illustrates that jural and ritual corporateness need not co-occur, and that the rarity of the ceremony explains why the long-house has never developed a corresponding jural personality.

## Context

- The article draws on Appell's own ethnographic fieldwork among the Rungus of Kudat District, Sabah, as documented in his 1965 Ph.D. dissertation at The Australian National University and his earlier publications on Rungus residence patterns and long-house architecture.
- Theoretically, the piece contributes to the anthropological debate on social structure by proposing a typology (isolate, aggregate, collectivity) that allows precise description of the degree to which a social grouping is recognized as a corporate entity within a given system, building on Oliver (1958) and Derham (1958).
