# Dr. Brandstetter’s Malayo-Polynesian researches: an appreciation

C. O. Blagden, a British colonial administrator in the Straits Settlements, published this appreciation in 1905 to draw English-speaking readers' attention to the Malayo-Polynesian researches of Professor Brandstetter of Lucerne, whom he identifies as the most rigorous Malayan scholar in Europe outside the Netherlands. The article serves as a guided introduction to Brandstetter's German-language monographs, arguing that his strictly comparative linguistic methods—particularly his pairing of Tagalog and Malagasy—reveal the deep unity and prehistoric civilizational level of the Malayo-Polynesian language family in ways that English-language scholarship had largely overlooked.

## Summary

Blagden's central purpose is to bridge a gap in English-language awareness of continental European Malayan studies. He argues that English scholars have been persistently to their own disadvantage in ignoring Dutch and German authorities, and that Brandstetter's work—though trained in the Dutch school—is purely original and accessible to readers with a fair knowledge of German. The article is not itself a research study but a critical appreciation designed to direct readers toward the primary monographs.

The substantive core of the piece concerns Brandstetter's two comparative studies on the relationship between Tagalog (or Tagal) and Malagasy. Blagden uses these to demonstrate that Malay, far from being the "normal type" of a Malayan language as Crawfurd had maintained, is in many respects a simplified and reduced representative of the family. He illustrates this with the verb for "writing" (Malay *surat*, Tagalog *sulat*, Malagasy *soratra*), showing how Tagalog and Malagasy preserve a full system of agglutination—prefixes, suffixes, infixes, and reduplication—whereas Malay has reduced it to a mere remnant. The comparison of shared vocabulary between these two geographically distant languages, Blagden argues, points to a common ancestral form older than Malay in several cases.

Beyond pure philology, Blagden draws out the anthropological significance of Brandstetter's comparative vocabulary. The shared words for marine life, sailing equipment, agricultural products, metalwork, domestic implements, and social institutions allow a reconstruction of the material and social civilization of the primitive Malayo-Polynesian ancestors before their dispersal. He also briefly touches on Brandstetter's translation of the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* and his essay on the Malay aesthetic sense in pre-nineteenth-century literature, the latter of which Blagden considers a genuinely original contribution to understanding the Malay mind.

### Key Findings

- Brandstetter's two key comparative studies are *Die Beziehungen des Malagasy zum Malayischen* (1893, 43 pp.) and *Tagalen und Madecassen* (1902, 85 pp.), which together establish the interconnection of Tagalog and Malagasy and illuminate the family's past development (p. 212).
- The Malay verb *surat* ("writing") corresponds to Tagalog *sulat* and Malagasy *soratra*; the full conjugation tables Brandstetter provides show Tagalog and Malagasy retaining active and passive forms with prefixes, suffixes, infixes, and reduplication, while Malay has lost most of this agglutinating apparatus (pp. 212–213).
- Comparative vocabulary indicates the primitive Malayo-Polynesian ancestors were at home on tropical seas, with common words for sea, shore, crocodile, prawn, ray, and dugong; they used sails and fishing hooks (p. 214).
- The same vocabulary reveals acquaintance with rice, yams, bamboo, cocoanut, and screw-palm; knowledge of iron and apparently silver; tools including knives and files; houses with walls and roofs; pots, dishes, spoons, and mortars with pestles; and garments of cloth (p. 214).
- Social vocabulary includes terms for buying, borrowing, debt, payment, and differences of social rank (chiefs to whom personal respect was due), and the institution of taboo appears to have already existed (p. 214).
- The *Hikayat Hang Tuah* is dated by Brandstetter to probably the early part of the 17th century and is characterized as a recognized specimen of the best type of Malay classical prose literature (p. 215).

### Conclusion

Blagden's definitive takeaway is that Brandstetter has earned a place among the distinguished Malayan scholars—van der Tuuk, Kern, Niemann, and Brandes—and that his work, though rooted in the Dutch school, is purely original. The broader argument is one of scholarly accessibility: English readers who ignore continental European Malayan studies do so to their own disadvantage, and Brandstetter's German-language publications offer a viable entry point for those who cannot read Dutch.

## Context

- Blagden was a British colonial official in the Straits Settlements; his framing reflects the administrative perspective of a colonial administrator seeking to connect English-speaking readers to European scholarly traditions they had neglected.
- The article draws on Brandstetter's published German monographs (First and Second Series of *Malayo-Polynesische Forschungen*) and references to prior JSBRAS issues (No. 17, No. 39) as its evidential base; its historiographical contribution is primarily one of reception and dissemination rather than original research.
