# Tom Harrisson’s unpublished legacy on Niah

Barbara Harrisson's 1977 article, published in the Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, inventories and assesses the vast body of unpublished papers, field notes, and correspondence left by her late husband, the archaeologist and civil servant Tom Harrisson, following his sudden death in January 1976. Writing from a position of intimate familiarity with the Niah Caves research programme in Sarawak, she argues that the materials constitute a critical but fragile legacy for Bornean prehistory, and proposes a structured, regionally-led path for their recovery and publication.

## Summary

The article addresses a practical and scholarly dilemma: Tom Harrisson had deposited his Niah papers in regional museums upon his retirement from government service in 1967, meaning that any attempt to preserve, order, or publish them requires institutional co-operation and the navigation of professional and political sensitivities. Harrisson identifies three distinct categories of unpublished material—government reporting, personal reporting, and site reporting—and describes their physical form, location, and condition. The government files, which include correspondence with the Borneo Shell Companies and the Gulbenkian Foundation, are essential for understanding the political and economic constraints under which Niah archaeology operated. The personal reporting, largely handwritten in exercise books at the Niah base camp between 1958 and 1963, represents Tom's reflective thinking intended as an anchor for a monograph he never completed. The site reporting, distributed across the Great Cave, Lobang Kain Hitam, and Lobang Jeragan, contains the descriptive and analytical data that would form the empirical core of any future publication.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to the question of bias in evaluating Tom's archaeological competence. Harrisson responds to William Solheim's characterisation of "faulty excavation method" as an "unquestioned major weakness" by arguing that Tom's multi-disciplinary, ecologically-oriented approach was a product of its time and context, and that his work should be assessed against the standards and resources available to an expatriate civil servant operating without university support in Sarawak. She recounts Tom's distinctive working style—his possessiveness over Niah, his use of "rude manners" as a professional tool, and his insistence that archaeology was a vehicle for broader environmental and ecological questions rather than an end in itself. The Brunei rebellion of 1962–63, which pulled Tom into civil service duties and effectively ended his direct fieldwork at Niah, is identified as the pivotal event that prevented him from completing his monographic report.

Harrison also raises the specific problem of the deep skull of Niah, a Pleistocene human specimen that Tom treated as a Leitfossil for the evolution of modern man. She notes an apparent discrepancy of some five thousand years between Tom's references to the skull's date in 1972 and 1976, suggesting that his correction may have stemmed from new interpretive approaches he was unable to document before his death. The article closes with a practical proposal: a person or consortium with academic overview, government support, and the ability to critically edit and write should be tasked with recovering the files, separating biographical from archaeological content, and publishing the latter site by site, with regional initiative taking priority from the outset.

### Key Findings

- Tom Harrisson died in January 1976 (obituary in *The London Times*, 21 January 1976), leaving unpublished memoranda, papers, notes, articles, and letters distributed across institutional and private files (p. 41).
- The deep skull of Niah, described by Brothwell (1960) as an Upper Pleistocene human skull, shows an apparent dating discrepancy of approximately five thousand years between Tom's references in 1972 and 1976, a correction he may not have been able to document before his death (p. 50).
- The Brunei rebellion of 1962–63, which spilled into Sarawak, removed Tom from active archaeological fieldwork at Niah; thereafter, excavations were carried out by staff and associates, with Barbara Harrisson responsible for fieldwork until Tom's retirement in 1967 (pp. 44–45).
- The Lobang Jeragan ceramic analysis, undertaken by Germaine Woodfield in Miri, remained entirely unpublished due to an unresolved argument between Woodfield and Tom over her interpretations; the material continues to be held in the Sarawak Museum (p. 49).
- The Lobang Kain Hitam files and photographic prints were transferred to the Brunei Museum in 1976–77 for a planned joint write-up with Tom, while all artifacts and original film remained in Sarawak (p. 49).
- External funding for Niah research came from the Borneo Shell Companies and the Gulbenkian Foundation of Lisbon, administered through a government-ordered committee; a burst of publications by multiple authors between 1960 and 1967 reflects this concentrated support (p. 46).

### Conclusion

Harrison's definitive takeaway is that Niah's scientific potential is at risk of being lost unless a concerted, regionally-led effort is made to recover and publish Tom's unpublished legacy. She rejects the framing of Tom's work as fundamentally flawed, insisting instead that it should be presented as a contribution to Bornean and Southeast Asian prehistory, evaluated against the standards and constraints of its time. The task is urgent, difficult, and must proceed gradually—site by site, file by file—with priority given to regional initiative and inter-disciplinary co-operation.

## Context

- The primary archival collections discussed are those held by the Sarawak Museum (Kuching) and the Brunei Museum, including exercise books, loose-file assemblages, photographic archives, and government correspondence files deposited by Tom upon his retirement in 1967.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its rare insider account of the political, economic, and personal dimensions of colonial-era archaeological practice in Borneo, and in its explicit methodological proposal for the recovery of a significant but fragmented research legacy.
