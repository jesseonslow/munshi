# The social organization of the mining community in Malaya during the depression 1929–1933

A. Azmi Abdul Khalid's 1992 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines how the Great Depression (1929–1933) restructured the social fabric of the tin-mining community in the Kinta Valley, Perak, arguing that the interplay of collapsing tin prices, the tin-restriction scheme, restricted immigration, and the colonial government's ad hoc repatriation policy produced a cascade of coping strategies—vegetable gardening, *dulang* washing, and ore theft—that exposed the fundamental weaknesses in both industrial and governmental labour management.

## Summary

Khalid opens by confronting a methodological problem that has long plagued the historiography of Chinese unemployment in Malaya: the actual number of unemployed tin-mine workers during the slump was never firmly established, and repatriation statistics cannot be reliably mapped onto mining-sector unemployment because repatriated Chinese were not categorized by occupation. The author draws on J. V. Cowgill's detailed 1932 note on Chinese repatriation in Perak to demonstrate how the government's stated policy of repatriating only "decrepit" Chinese was progressively abandoned in practice, with able-bodied workers, whole families, and even recent arrivals queuing for free passage to China. This erosion of policy discipline, Khalid argues, was driven by the combined pressure of low tin prices, controlled production, and the resulting retrenchment of the mining labour force.

The article then traces the alternative livelihoods that unemployed workers pursued. Vegetable gardening, promoted by the Kinta Unemployment Committee, initially attracted enthusiasm but collapsed under market oversaturation and rising railway freight charges. The *dulang* (pan-washing) method of recovering tin ore from streams became a significant refuge, though it was dominated by Chinese pass-holders and riddled with abuse—pass-selling, ore theft from mines, and fraudulent renewal applications. Khalid documents the Perak State Council's 1931 proposal to issue free *dulang* passes to Malay women, the Senior Warden of Mines' objections, and the eventual compromise of 30 initial passes (capped at 100). A parallel episode involving 21 Indian women in Menglembu petitioning for passes in 1932 illustrates the multi-ethnic dimensions of mining-community distress.

The final section addresses the escalating problem of tin-ore theft by unemployed coolies in the Batu Gajah area, which intensified from late 1931 through early 1933. Khalid engages with Ricardo Godoy's concept of "social protest" but ultimately declines to theorize the phenomenon in radical labour-movement terms, instead concluding that the depression exposed the internal contradictions of a capitalist mining industry whose management of labour was both reactive and structurally inadequate.

### Key Findings

- The FMS government repatriated approximately 50,494 Chinese between 1930 and 1932; Perak alone accounted for 2,340 repatriations in the second half of 1930, compared with 899 in Selangor and 273 in Negri Sembilan (pp. 85–87).
- The Aliens' Ordinance, which finally terminated Chinese immigration to the Malay Peninsula, was enforced on 1 January 1933, despite the Governor of the Straits Settlements urging cessation as early as October 1931 (pp. 92–93).
- In Pahang in 1931, of 309 *dulang* passes issued, only 13 went to Malays while 200 were allocated to Chinese; during the restriction period the monthly ore limit was capped at 20 *kati*, down from an average production of approximately 24 *kati* between 1927 and 1930 (pp. 95–96).
- Chinese *dulang* washers in the last quarter of 1930 were alleged to have sold approximately 1,100 *pikul* of tin ore above the permitted limit; pass-selling generated income of $3.00–$5.50 per month for the original holder (pp. 95–96).
- In the Ipoh area, T.O.L. fees collected from Chinese vegetable growers rose to $11,000 for January–May 1932, up from $10,000 for the same period in 1931, indicating that not all market gardeners were destitute (p. 89).
- In one documented incident in 1932, approximately 80–100 people gathered within five days to work in shifts on an unused section of Kay Yew Tin Mines Ltd's land, threatening the mine's watchmen with *parang* when warned to stop (p. 98).

### Conclusion

Khalid's definitive takeaway is that the depression laid bare the structural fragility of the colonial mining economy's labour-management apparatus. The government's step-by-step, *ad hoc* approach—maintaining that the situation was "merely industrial" and posed "no menace to the public peace" even as the army was deployed to strategic locations in the Kinta Valley—revealed a fundamental disconnect between administrative rhetoric and on-the-ground reality. The social disorganization that followed was not the product of a single policy failure but of the cumulative interaction of price collapse, production restriction, immigration control, and the absence of any coherent welfare framework for a labour force that had been imported for a single extractive purpose and left without alternative when that purpose contracted.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the Mines Department Batu Gajah (MDBG) files and CO 273/572 (Colonial Office records on Chinese Affairs, FMS), supplemented by Parmer's *Colonial Labor Policy and Administration* (1960) and Loh Koh Wah's *Beyond the Tin Mines* (1988).
- Historiographically, the piece extends the labour-history framework established by Parmer by shifting focus from policy formulation to the lived social organization of the mining community, and by incorporating Malay and Indian experiences alongside the dominant Chinese narrative.
