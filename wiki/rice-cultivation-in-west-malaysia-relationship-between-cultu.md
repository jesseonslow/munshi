# Rice cultivation in West Malaysia: relationship between culture history, customary practices and recent developments

James C. Jackson published this article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1972, during the early phase of Malaysia's aggressive push toward rice self-sufficiency through double cropping and high-yielding varieties. His central thesis is that the regional variations in customary rice-farming practices across West Malaysia are not merely adaptive responses to local soils, rainfall, or topography but are fundamentally products of culture history—the different directions, times, and vectors through which wet-rice techniques entered each part of the peninsula—and that these cultural roots have direct consequences for how receptively different regions will absorb the new agricultural technologies being promoted by government.

## Summary

Jackson challenges two prevailing assumptions in the literature on Malayan rice farming: first, that the basic pattern of cultivation is essentially the same everywhere and can be described by a single "fairly representative" cycle of operations; and second, that rice farming is a static, "traditional" element in contrast to the dynamic commercial sector. He demonstrates that every stage of the farming cycle—land preparation, nursery type, transplanting, harvesting, threshing, and winnowing—exhibits significant regional variation, and that these variations correlate with the historical pathways by which wet-rice cultivation reached each area. The northwest and northeast were early hearths of lowland rice, but the techniques came from different directions (Thai versus Sumatran influences), and the inland valleys of the western states were pioneered by Minangkabau migrants from the fifteenth century onward, carrying with them a distinct set of practices that persisted for centuries.

The article then provides a region-by-region survey of customary practices, covering the northwestern plains, the northeast, the inland valleys, the Perak Valley, the Malacca coastal plain, central Pahang, Krian, Tanjong Karang, and Sungei Manik. Jackson draws on a mix of early colonial-era descriptions (Grey's 1894 account of Kuala Kangsar, Blagden's 1893 observations on Malacca, Cant's 1888 notes on Pahang), mid-century agricultural surveys, and his own field observations to show that these systems are not frozen relics but have undergone cumulative modification. The practical stakes of this argument become clear in the final section, where he examines the uneven reception of double cropping and new varieties across regions. The degree of acceptance varies dramatically: Tanjong Karang and Krian show rapid adoption, while central Pahang and parts of Trengganu remain largely resistant. Jackson attributes these differences not to farmer laziness or ignorance but to the specific cultural configurations of each local farming system, including dietary preferences for particular rice varieties, the integration of cattle grazing into the fallow cycle, and the disruption that a second crop imposes on the ritual calendar of the village.

### Key Findings

- In 1969, 225,000 acres of riceland in West Malaysia were planted with an off-season crop—nearly a quarter of total rice acreage and almost five times the area so used in 1962 (p. 96).
- The Muda Irrigation Project, largely completed by the early 1970s, was designed to provide irrigation for double cropping of 261,500 acres in coastal Kedah and Perlis; the Kemubu Irrigation Project in Kelantan was planned for 47,000 acres with completion targeted for 1974 (pp. 80–83).
- In Krian, the number of locally-owned tractors rose from one in 1965 to thirty-seven after the 1967 off-season harvest, alongside a marked increase in fertilizer use (p. 91).
- In the Trengganu River Pumping Scheme, double cropping ranged from only 94 to 370 acres across 1964–1966 despite an assured irrigation supply for approximately 3,600 acres, illustrating the gap between technical feasibility and cultural receptivity (p. 83).
- Kedah's double-cropped area grew from 820 acres in 1961 to 18,650 acres in 1969, while in central Pahang (Temerloh District) no off-season padi was planted at all in 1969 despite 3,700 acres being double cropped elsewhere in the state (pp. 80, 90).
- The floating seed-bed (*semaian rakit*) and multiple transplanting system, introduced to Krian by Banjarese settlers in the third quarter of the nineteenth century, was retained tenaciously by Malay farmers in Krian and Tanjong Karang even after they recognized it required more seed and gave poorer germination rates than the single-transplanting methods used by their Chinese neighbours (pp. 91–92).

### Conclusion

Jackson's definitive takeaway is that the national rice problem cannot be solved by a uniform technical prescription applied across all regions. The cultural heterogeneity of West Malaysia's rice-farming systems means that the same package of innovations—double cropping, new varieties, chemical fertilizers, mechanized ploughing—will be received with radically different degrees of success in different places. He warns that the current trajectory, which intensifies the gap between rapidly modernizing areas like Tanjong Karang and Province Wellesley and relatively stagnant areas like central Pahang, risks solving the national production target while creating new and serious imbalances in rural Malay incomes, with the less receptive regions destined to become the problem areas of the future.

## Context

- Jackson draws extensively on published colonial-era and mid-century agricultural surveys (notably the 1939 "Padi Planting Methods in Malaya" report, Dobby's geographical studies, and Robert Ho's monograph on central Pahang), government irrigation department reports, and his own field observations; no single archival collection dominates the study.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in reframing regional variation in farming technique as a product of culture history rather than environmental determinism, and in demonstrating through documented case studies that "traditional" systems have always been dynamic—a corrective to the static model that had dominated both colonial administrative thinking and early academic writing on Malayan agriculture.
