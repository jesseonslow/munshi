# The Nanhai trade: a study of the early history of Chinese trade in the South China Sea

W. W. Skeat's 1898 article presents first-hand ethnographic observations of Malay magical ceremonies conducted in the Malacca Peninsula, recorded during his residence in the region. The overarching thesis is that these rituals, far from being "mere childish folly," constitute a coherent system of sympathetic magic intelligible through the comparative framework of James Frazer's *Golden Bough*, and that they represent a syncretic fusion of indigenous animism with Islamic formulae.

## Summary

Skeat documents several major ceremonial complexes with the meticulous detail of an eyewitness. The most extensively described is the padi (rice) ceremony, in which a pawang removes the "rice child" (*anak padi*) from a selected sheaf, swaddles it in cloth, places it in a basket-cradle with symbolic objects (an egg, a jungle-nut, an iron nail, a shell), and carries it home under an umbrella where it is treated with the same taboos as a newborn human infant for three days. Skeat interprets this through Frazer's principle that "any effect can be produced by imitating it": the sheaf is the rice-mother, and the first seven ears are her child, whose proper nurturing ensures the prosperity of the harvest. The sugar-cane stem planted in the sheaf functions as a fertility pole analogous to the European May-pole, and the iron nail serves as a charm against evil spirits (pp. 3–15).

The second major complex is a healing ceremony in which a male pawang, after being "shampooed" in incense smoke, enters a state of possession by a tiger spirit. In this state he rises on all fours, growls, licks the patient's body from head to foot "exactly as a tigress might lick down her cub," lets blood from his own arm over the patient, and engages in an imaginary combat with the disease-spirit using a kris and an areca blossom spike (pp. 20–23). Skeat records a partial glossary of the *bhasa hantu* (spirit language) used during the trance, noting cognates with New Zealand Māori terms (e.g., *demit* for "child" compared to Māori *tamaita*), which he flags as potentially significant for comparative linguistics (p. 23).

The remaining sections cover the *lanchang* (spirit-boat) ceremony for expelling illness by sending the disease adrift on the sea, and the elaborate offerings made at fishing stations (*kelong*) to water spirits, including the suspension of trays laden with rice, fowl, cakes, betel, and lit tapers from mangrove trees and wooden tripods on sandbanks (pp. 26–41). Throughout, Skeat transcribes the full Malay charms and prayers, many of which open with Islamic invocations (*Bismillah*, references to Muhammad, Ali, and the Prophet) grafted onto indigenous spirit-addresses, illustrating the deep syncretism of the practice.

### Key Findings

- The padi ceremony's "cradle" contained exactly seven symbolic items: a strip of white cloth, a piece of partly coloured thread (*benang pancharona*), an egg, a hard jungle-nut (*buah kras*), a shell called *krang*, a long iron nail, and five *hasta* of red cloth for slinging (p. 3).
- The "Ten Prayers" (*doa sapuloh*) recited by the pawang during the rice ceremony invoke Allah, Muhammad, the five daily prayers, the *Pancha Indra*, and seven named prophets (Isa, Karun, Musa, Daud, Karun) in a single unbroken breath (p. 6).
- The healing ceremony's spirit-language glossary records 15 terms with one noted cognate to New Zealand Māori: *demit* (child) compared to *tamaita* (p. 23).
- The fishing-station offering tray contained 28 small woven receptacles (14 *katupat* and 14 *lepat*), each set comprising seven sorts of cooked and seven sorts of uncooked food, plus five portions each of parched rice, washed rice, and five coloured rices (green, red, blue, black, saffron), five portions of fowl (cooked and raw), five cakes, five betel chews, five cigarettes, and five stones (pp. 30–31).
- The *tepong tawar* (magic flour) brush for the padi ceremony was composed of exactly seven leaves: *sapenoh*, *sapanggil*, *jenjuang merah*, *gandarusa*, *pulut-pulut*, *selaguri*, and *sambau dara*, bound with *terap* bark and *ribu-ribu* (p. 3).
- The 1891 census showed that in Rembau (Negri Sembilan), native women outnumbered native men, and not one per cent of native customary landholdings were registered as the property of men, reflecting the matrilineal inheritance customs traced to intermarriage between the Waris tribe and the Sakai (p. 42).

### Conclusion

Skeat's definitive takeaway is that Malay magical ceremonies are not superstitious nonsense but a rational (if pre-scientific) system of sympathetic magic in which every gesture, object, and utterance has a specific causal logic. By demonstrating that the rice-child is a deliberate imitation of human childbirth, that the tiger-lick is a homoeopathic transfer of vitality, and that the spirit-boat is a concrete vehicle for abstract disease, Skeat argues these practices deserve scholarly respect as "part and parcel of an old-world religion" rather than dismissal as primitive folly (pp. 14–15).

## Context

- Skeat was a senior colonial administrator and linguist in the Straits Settlements; his authority as an "eye-witness" is central to the article's evidential value, and he explicitly frames the work as preliminary notes intended to "lighten" the task of future researchers (p. 2).
- The article draws on no archival collections but on direct observation and elicitation from local pawangs; its historiographical contribution lies in providing one of the earliest systematic English-language transcriptions of Malay ritual language and in applying Frazerian comparative methodology to the Malay world.
