# The Chinese in Malaya and China’s politics, 1895–1911

Stephen Leong's 1977 article traces the emergence of political consciousness among the Chinese in Malaya between the Sino-Japanese War of 1894–95 and the Xinhai Revolution of 1911, arguing that the *hua-ch'iao* shifted from decades of economic preoccupation to active participation in their motherland's politics through the catalytic presence of Chinese national leaders in the region. The study demonstrates how both the reformist and revolutionary movements competed for the allegiance of the overseas Chinese, with the revolutionaries ultimately prevailing as the Manchu dynasty's inability to deliver constitutional reform discredited the moderate path (pp. 7–19).

## Summary

Leong's central concern is explaining how a population that had emigrated largely to escape China's political turmoil and economic hardship became, within roughly fifteen years, a significant political force in the nationalist revolution. The article identifies three interlocking mechanisms: the shock of China's defeat by Japan, which shattered the overseas Chinese's assumption of their homeland's invulnerability; the physical presence of Chinese political leaders in Malaya, who brought the motherland's problems into immediate proximity; and the external nationalist incidents of 1905 and 1908, which provided additional fuel for patriotic sentiment beyond domestic Chinese politics (pp. 16–18).

The narrative is structured around the rivalry between K'ang Yu-wei's constitutional reformists and Sun Yat-sen's revolutionaries for the loyalty of the *hua-ch'iao*. Leong argues that the reformists initially held the advantage because their program of evolutionary change was more palatable to a community that, while disillusioned with Manchu neglect, harboured no deep hatred for the ruling house. The revolutionaries' strategy of mass mobilisation—through party branches, secret society networks, public lectures, and nationalist literature—gradually eroded this advantage, particularly after the Manchu court's failure to deliver a constitution and the reformists' perceived impotence (pp. 17–18).

Primary sources inform the narrative at multiple levels: colonial administrative correspondence (notably Colonial Office files on the *Acme* affair and the Japanese boycott), contemporary Chinese-language newspapers such as the *Thien Nan Shin Pau*, *Jit Shin Pau*, and *Chong Shing Yit Pao*, and the published memoirs and biographies of key figures including Song Ong Siang, Teo Eng Hock, and Khoo Seok Wan. Leong draws heavily on Yen Ching-huang's unpublished 1968 doctoral dissertation and Wang Gungwu's 1953 thesis, both of which relied on interviews with aging participants and their biographers, giving the study a distinctly oral-history character in its source base (pp. 7–14).

### Key Findings

- The Chinese population in the Straits Settlements and Federated Malay States numbered 584,036 in 1901, with Singapore (164,681), Perak (151,192), and Selangor (109,598) holding the largest communities (p. 7).
- Khoo Seok Wan donated S$250,000 to K'ang Yu-wei's failed Hankow Uprising of August 1900, and the Malayan overseas Chinese contributed S$47,660 toward the Huang-hua Kang Uprising of 1911 (pp. 7, 12).
- The Singapore branch of the T'ung Meng Hui was established in April 1906 at the Wan Ching Yuan (Teo Eng Hock's former private mansion) on Balastier Road, with Tan Chor Lam as chairman and Teo Eng Hock as vice-chairman (pp. 8–9).
- Following the Wuchang Revolt of October 1911, Penang immediately cabled S$20,000 to China, Ipoh collected S$8,000, and the Hokkien community in Singapore cabled a further S$20,000 to the Fukien Province Security Fund-Raising Committee (pp. 13–14).
- The anti-American boycott of 1905 was forcibly suppressed when the Secretary of Chinese Affairs, W.D. Barnes, warned Chinese dock workers that refusal to repair the American vessel *Acme* would result in arrest and deportation to China (p. 14).
- Leong estimates that by 1911, at least 25 per cent of the adult Chinese population in Malaya (approximately 902,574 total) had been aroused to nationalist sentiment by the Revolution (p. 19).

### Conclusion

Leong's definitive takeaway is that the overseas Chinese in Malaya did not spontaneously become nationalists but were progressively conditioned into political engagement through a combination of external shocks, the physical presence of Chinese leaders who required their financial and moral support, and the demonstrable failure of the reformist path. The revolutionaries succeeded not because the *hua-ch'iao* initially favoured violent overthrow, but because the Manchu dynasty's continued refusal to grant meaningful constitutional reform made the reformists' programme untenable, leaving revolution as the only viable alternative for a community that had already developed a sense of national identity (p. 18).

## Context

- The study draws substantially on unpublished theses (Yen Ching-huang, ANU 1968; Wang Gungwu, University of Malaya 1953) and Colonial Office correspondence (CO 273/310/2198; CO 273/337/22519; CO 273/352/2210), alongside Chinese-language periodicals and biographical memoirs.
- Historiographically, the article represents an early attempt to synthesise the reformist and revolutionary narratives into a single account of overseas Chinese political development, and it remains notable for its use of interview-based oral history with participants and their biographers, a method that has since been supplemented by more extensive archival work.
