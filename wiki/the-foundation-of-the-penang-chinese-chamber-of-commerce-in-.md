# The foundation of the Penang Chinese Chamber of Commerce in 1903: Protecting Chinese business interests in the two states

Kaori Shinozaki's 2006 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* re-examines the founding of the Penang Chinese Chamber of Commerce (PCCC) in 1903, arguing against the prevailing historiographical assumption that such institutions were products of Ching Government mobilization. Drawing on Chinese-language newspapers, colonial correspondence, and bankruptcy reports, Shinozaki demonstrates that the PCCC was autonomously initiated by Penang's leading Chinese merchants to address specific commercial abuses and to create formal channels of influence over both the Straits Settlements Government and the Ching Government simultaneously.

## Summary

The article challenges the conventional narrative, advanced by scholars such as Zhuang (1989), that Chinese Chambers of Commerce in Southeast Asia were established at the direction of the Ching Government to mobilize overseas Chinese capital and political support. Shinozaki's central evidence is the proceedings of the founding meeting on 27 June 1903 at the Chinese Town Hall, convened by Yeoh Paik Tatt, which identified two specific "evil trade customs"—the practice of "copper payment" (giving 20–30 per cent in copper coins as de facto payment) and the unreliability of "Credit Books" and "Payment Books" that could be destroyed by fraudulent parties. No reference to the Ching Government appears in these proceedings; the initiative was entirely local and practical (pp. 45–46).

The second major argument concerns the PCCC's relationship with the Straits Settlements Government. Shinozaki shows that the PCCC was designed as a self-regulatory body that allowed Chinese merchants to supervise their own accounting practices without government interference, while simultaneously creating a formal channel to petition the colonial administration. The pepper trade crisis of November 1903, triggered by the Currency Ordinance that banned British and Mexican dollars, illustrates this dual function: the PCCC organized petitions demanding government intervention to protect the Aceh pepper trade, framing Chinese commercial interests as inseparable from the interests of the British Empire (pp. 50–52). The PCCC's rules explicitly provided for electing a representative to the Legislative Council, a privilege the European Penang Chamber of Commerce already enjoyed, though this was not granted until 1923 (pp. 48–49).

The third argument addresses the PCCC's later registration with the Ching Government's Bureau of Commerce in January 1907. Shinozaki draws on the parallel cases of the Singapore and Selangor Chinese Chambers of Commerce to show that registration was motivated by the practical problem of debtors absconding to China, where Straits Settlements legal authority could not reach, and by the need for protection certificates for Chinese merchants returning to their home provinces. The *Lat Pao* and *Penang Sin Poe* provide vivid contemporary testimony to the dangers returnees faced from local officials and scoundrels in China (pp. 53–56).

### Key Findings

- The founding meeting of 27 June 1903 was convened by Yeoh Paik Tatt and attended by leading Chinese merchants, miners, and traders; the resolution to form the PCCC was carried unanimously with no reference to the Ching Government (p. 45).
- The PCCC's rules (Clause 27) provided for electing a nominee to the Legislative Council; a petition to the Secretary of State for the Colonies on 31 January 1908 cited the disparity of four members representing 4,000 Europeans versus one member representing 175,000 Chinese in Singapore, and two members for 1,300 Europeans in Penang with none for 100,000 Chinese (p. 49).
- The *Pegu* seizure of 2 November 1903 involved over 48,000 British and Mexican coins; the PCCC petitioned for the export of 1,000,000 Straits dollars to Aceh, and the Government ultimately permitted the exportation of no more than $500,000 from Penang to Aceh between February and September 1904 (pp. 50–52).
- Bankruptcy records for 1891–1906 show that Chinese constituted the largest ethnic group among bankrupts in both Singapore and Penang, with roughly one in three or four absconding; in Penang specifically, Chinese absconders numbered between 0 and 7 per year over this period (p. 54, Table 1).
- The PCCC changed its Chinese name from "Bing Cheng Hua Ren Shang Wu Ju" to "Bing Lang Yu Zhong Hua Shang Wu Zong Hui" and registered with the Bureau of Commerce of the Ching Government on 14 January 1907, receiving an official seal in February 1907 (p. 52).
- The 1895 Bankruptcy Ordinance Amendment Bill was opposed by a joint petition from Chinese in Penang, Singapore, and Malacca bearing 238 signatures, arguing that detention powers could be misused to settle private accounts (p. 47).

### Conclusion

Shinozaki concludes that the PCCC functioned as a meaningful institution in three simultaneous capacities: a self-regulatory body for business transactions among Chinese merchants, a pressure group and communication channel toward the Straits Settlements Government, and a node in the transnational network of Chambers of Commerce linking Penang to China. The Chinese in Penang, as "people crossing borders," did not reject the nation-state system but committed themselves to it, seeking recognition as integral members of each state and the right to influence policy through formal channels—an approach that would be inconceivable under the modern nation-state system where allegiance is expected to be singular (pp. 56–57).

## Context

- Primary sources include the Chinese-language newspapers *Lat Pao* (Singapore) and *Penang Sin Poe*, the English-language *Straits Echo* and *Pinang Gazette and Straits Chronicle*, the annual *Report on the Working of 'The Bankruptcy Ordinance 1888'* (1891–1906), Colonial Office records (CO273 series), and the *Commercial Gazette* of the Ching Government's Bureau of Commerce.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in shifting the analytical frame from the Ching Government as the driving force behind overseas Chinese institutional formation to the autonomous agency of Chinese merchants operating across multiple sovereign jurisdictions in the pre-nation-state era.
