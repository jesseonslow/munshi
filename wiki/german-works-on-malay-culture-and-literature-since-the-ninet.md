# German works on Malay culture and literature since the nineteenth century

Arndt Graf, writing in 2007, surveys the full arc of German-language scholarship on Malay culture and literature from the early nineteenth century to the present, arguing that these works followed clearly distinguishable periods shaped by Cold War politics, Malaysian nation-building, and a gradual but decisive shift of academic agency from European to Malaysian institutions.

## Summary

Graf's article is a historiographical survey that maps the evolution of German-language engagement with Malay literature across roughly two centuries. The central problem he addresses is one of periodization: how and why the focus of German Malay studies shifted from traditional genres (pantun, folk tales, court chronicles) to modern Malaysian literature, and how geopolitical forces—particularly the Cold War division of Germany and Malaysia's own post-colonial trajectory—determined which genres were studied, by whom, and in which institutional settings. The pre-war tradition, anchored by Hans Overbeck's translations of the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* and the *Sejarah Melayu*, established a German image of Malay culture as fundamentally a culture of folk and court literature that persisted well into the 1950s.

The Cold War introduced a sharp ideological bifurcation. In East Germany, socialist genre theory privileged the "low genres" of the common people, resulting in a sustained programme of folk-tale translations (Kahlo, Nevermann, Schebesta) that had no parallel in West Germany. In West Germany, by contrast, the post-1969 period of Malaysian stability catalysed a turn toward classical court literature as a "post-colonial era gesture of recognition," followed by an increasingly robust engagement with modern Malaysian literary production centred at Hamburg, Frankfurt, and Cologne. Graf draws on institutional histories, publication records, and the careers of individual scholars to trace how these shifts occurred, emphasizing the role of key figures such as Otto Karow, Irene Hilgers-Hesse, Rainer Carle, and Bernd Nothofer as institutional gatekeepers.

The final phase of the article examines a reversal of the traditional power dynamic: from the 1990s onward, Malaysian institutions—Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka, ATMA, IKON, and the European Chair of Malay Studies in Leiden—began to set the agenda for international Malay studies, organizing conferences, funding translations, and critically re-examining Western Orientalist approaches. Graf frames this as a post-Orientalist deconstruction carried out from the Malaysian side, marking a qualitative break from the earlier pattern in which European scholars defined the terms of engagement.

### Key Findings

- The earliest German engagement with Malay literature dates to Adelbert von Chamisso's translation and adaptation of Malay *pantun* into German in the early nineteenth century, predating any formal academic discipline (p. 51).
- Hans Overbeck's translations of the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* (1922) and *Malaiische Chronik* (1927) were reprinted in West Germany in 1975, 1976, and 1986, demonstrating the long shelf-life of pre-war German Malay literary scholarship (pp. 53–54).
- East German scholar Gerhard Kahlo's anthology *Bidasari* (1958) was translated into Russian and republished in Moscow in 1967, illustrating the circulation of Malay folk literature within the socialist bloc (p. 53).
- E. U. Kratz produced the first German PhD dissertation on Malay literature from Malaysia under Otto Karow's supervision at Frankfurt; he later became Professor at SOAS, London, and remains one of the principal German contributors to the field (pp. 53–54).
- In 2004, Kratz and Anwar Ridhwan of Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka co-published *Hati Mesra: Pantun Melayu Sebelum 1914*, featuring Overbeck's pre-World War I pantun collection, symbolizing the full circle of Malaysian–German literary exchange (p. 59).
- By 2003, German academic publications on Malay literature were concentrated in only four universities—Cologne, Bonn, Frankfurt, and Hamburg—reflecting a marked contraction of the field following the Asian financial crisis and German university budget cuts (p. 58).

### Conclusion

Graf's definitive takeaway is that Malaysian–German literary relations, while having experienced a significant flowering between 1969 and 2003, face a structurally diminished future. The convergence of Malaysia's post-1997 financial constraints on Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka and the parallel economic retrenchment within the German university system has produced a "marked reduction in activities" in the field. Graf closes on a deliberately sober note: fifty years after the burgeoning of German–Malaysian diplomatic relations, the prospects for enhancing those relations in the literary field "look less than buoyant" (p. 60).

## Context

- The article draws primarily on publication records, institutional histories of German universities (Hamburg, Frankfurt, Cologne, Bonn, Jena), and the documented careers of individual scholars rather than on a single archival collection. Its historiographical contribution lies in providing the first comprehensive periodization of German-language Malay literary studies as a continuous field, bridging the gap between Warnk's earlier work on the pre-1945 period and the contemporary scene.
