# Malay culture and society

Malay culture and society, as documented in the scholarly literature of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, constitutes a layered civilisational formation spanning the Malay Peninsula and the Riau Archipelago, characterised by the successive superimposition of indigenous animistic, Hindu-Buddhist, and Islamic traditions without the displacement of earlier strata. The resulting culture operates as what R.J. Wilkinson described as "a sort of museum of ancient customs — an ill-kept and ill-designed museum in which no exhibit is dated, labelled or explained" [[1, p. 74]](#ref-1). From the wet-rice *kampong* (village) of the northern river valleys to the hierarchical courts of the sultanates, from the elaborate *adab* (propriety) governing interpersonal conduct to the political logic of *pandai* (equivocal management) that sustained village governance, Malay society presents a system of remarkable internal coherence whose components—linguistic, ceremonial, economic, and political—have been the subject of sustained scholarly investigation from the 1930s to the present.

## The Layered Origins of Malay Civilisation

The foundational thesis of Malay cultural studies, as articulated by Wilkinson in his landmark 1932 survey, is that Malay civilisation is a palimpsest in which each successive foreign influence—Deccan Indian, Indo-Chinese, Arab, Chinese, Bugis—has been grafted onto an indigenous Proto-Malayan substrate without erasing it [[2]](#ref-2). Wilkinson rejected racial essentialism, insisting that "a Malay is a man of Malay speech and habits and not a somatological formula," and instead traced cultural transmission through trade, intermarriage, and selective adoption [[2]](#ref-2). The economic motive for this contact was the jungle produce that only nomad-hunters could locate: gums, dammars, rhinoceros-horn, gold-dust, and ivory [[2]](#ref-2).

The epigraphic record confirms the early Indianisation of the peninsula. The Kota Kapur inscription (A.D. 686), in South Indian script and Sanskrit-Malay, records a Sri Vijaya *datu*'s Hinayana Buddhist faith; the next dated inscriptions (A.D. 775, 778, 782) shift to North Indian script, Sanskrit only, and Mahayana Buddhism under a *maharaja* of the "Shailendra" line, indicating a dynastic convulsion between 732 and 775 A.D. [[2, pp. 77–78]](#ref-2). The Buitenzorg inscription (c. 375 A.D.), the oldest in Java, shows two carved footprints identified as those of Vishnu belonging to Purnawarnam, "reigning prince of the City of Taruma"—the etymological root of the Malay royal address "footprints" (*kaki*) still used in diplomatic correspondence [[2, p. 79]](#ref-2).

The machinery of Malay kingship itself bears the marks of this accretion. Wilkinson reconstructed the Alexander legend's journey from a Pseudo-Callisthenes romance of the second or third century B.C. through Persian, Arabic, and Malay adaptations to show how Malay dynasties manufactured a Macedonian-Persian pedigree [[2]](#ref-2). The *daulat* (royal sanctity), the *nobat* (State Band), and the *kebesaran* (regalia) functioned as institutions that fused pre-Islamic Hindu-Buddhist ritual with Islamic formulae [[2]](#ref-2). The *nobat*, Wilkinson demonstrated, is a Persian *nau-bat* ("nine items") combined with an Indian installation-drum (*genderang tabal*), and its instruments carry graded degrees of holiness tied to the Sultan's physical presence [[2]](#ref-2). The ideal *nobat* comprises eight instruments (four "haunted," four unhaunted) plus the conductor, totalling nine items; the Perak Royal Band had shrunk to five instruments by the 1930s, while the Selangor band of seven was made in Perak on the model of the Perak *nobat* in use approximately 170 years earlier [[2, pp. 82–83]](#ref-2). The three oldest Malay regalia—the *churek simandang-kini* (a planter's chopper), the *kamat*-wood seal, and the *lembuara* spear (a wooden pike)—are identified as Proto-Malayan heirlooms seized by the first Palembang prince to confirm his right to the throne by conquest, roughly twelve centuries before Wilkinson's writing [[2, pp. 86–87]](#ref-2).

The "World Invisible" of Malay belief—shamanism, mysticism, demonology—likewise exhibits stratification. Wilkinson's method was to strip away the mythological surface to reveal the practical or historical kernel beneath: the *pawang*'s (shaman's) "divination" for planting time is actually a solar calendar encoded in phenological observation; the *Sultan Muda*'s seances were a state-sanctioned system of spiritual diplomacy with the tutelary spirits of the land; the *pelesit* (vampire) is a conflation of at least three distinct beliefs that have merged in popular usage [[2]](#ref-2). Wilkinson's 1957 synthesis extended this analysis, demonstrating that Malay demonology is composed of distinct strata: a Muslim upper layer (jinn, Persian and Arab spirits), a Hindu middle layer (Siva, Kala, Sri, Indra, Ganesha, the *raksasa* ogres), and a primeval Indonesian base layer including the Four Great Spirits of the Sea and the "Black Youth" (*jin hitam*), the latter probably a deity of the aboriginal race [[1, pp. 13–14]](#ref-1). The *semangat* (spirit of life) is not a soul in the Christian or Islamic sense but a vital force common to all animate and inanimate things; its most clearly documented survival is in the rice-soul ceremonies, where the reaper uses a specially shaped knife (*tuai*) to avoid alarming the "bird of life" in the seed-rice [[1, pp. 25–26]](#ref-1).

## Settlement, Economy, and the Traditional Malay World

Zaharah binti Hj. Mahmud's 1970 study addressed a fundamental gap in peninsular historical geography: the near-total absence of specific information on settlement types, economic activities, and their distribution during the "traditional" centuries, defined as the span between the termination of the Indianised millenia and the onset of widespread European colonisation (approximately 10th–18th centuries A.D.) [[3]](#ref-3). Her overarching thesis is that these centuries were not a historical "limbo" but a period of active and continuous development of wet rice cultivation and the accompanying social, economic, and political institutions that constitute the "traditional" Malay way of life [[3]](#ref-3).

The core analytical framework identifies multiple sources of influence on the development of traditional settlement: Indianisation (which introduced wet rice techniques, particularly in the northern river valleys of Kedah, Kelantan, and Trengganu), Thai political and cultural influence, the arrival of Islam, the indigenous pre-sedentary agricultural heritage visible in the *kampong* plot, and Indonesian immigration from the 15th century onwards [[3]](#ref-3). The *Nagarakertagama* (1365) lists Pahang, Johore, Muar, Langkasuka, Kelantan, Trengganu, Dungun, Temasik, Sungei Ujong, Kelang, and Kedah as dependencies of Majapahit, confirming that lower-valley settlement was an accomplished fact by the 14th century [[3, p. 93]](#ref-3). A Chinese account by Tschau Jou Kua (1225) mentions rice as an abundant product of the lower Kelantan, lower Trengganu, and lower Pahang rivers, with tin, pepper, and gold as export commodities—evidence that these settlements were already operating within the traditional economy by the 13th century [[3, p. 98]](#ref-3).

Environmental factors constrained settlement unevenly. The presence of peat soils, brackish water, and formidable swamp forests limited development in states like Selangor, Melaka, and parts of Perak, while the northern states, with their earlier exposure to Indianisation and more favourable natural drainage, accumulated larger traditional populations [[3]](#ref-3). The 1835 population estimates reflect this gradient: Kedah and Kelantan at 50,000 each, Pahang at 40,000, Perak at 35,000, Trengganu at 31,000, Negri Sembilan at 27,000, Johore at 25,000, and Selangor at only 12,000 [[3, p. 94]](#ref-3). The Tanjong Karang Drainage and Irrigation Scheme in Selangor brought 48,763 acres into wet rice cultivation in the early 20th century, accounting for almost the entirety of the state's wet rice area—meaning the only major wet rice region in present-day Selangor has a completely non-traditional origin [[3, p. 105]](#ref-3). Mahmud further challenged the assumption that the Melaka Sultanate represented the epitome of traditional Malay civilisation, arguing that Melaka's rise was fundamentally a commercial phenomenon unrelated to the development of traditional settlement in its immediate hinterland [[3]](#ref-3).

The adequacy of indigenous agricultural technique was confirmed by the 1907 Krian experiment, in which Banjarese native methods yielded 366 *gantang* per acre (the best result), while European interventions—bone manure, seed selection by weight, and Ceylon seed importation—produced markedly inferior crops of 128, 164, and negligible yields respectively [[1, pp. 64–65]](#ref-1).

## Social Structure, Leadership, and the Architecture of *Adab*

The social architecture of Malay society is governed by a system of *adab*—propriety, etiquette, and moral conduct—that Zainal Abidin bin Ahmad documented systematically in 1950 as the first English-language codification of Malay social manners [[4]](#ref-4). Ahmad treated manners not as mere politeness but as a moral and social system in which the body itself is a text: the head is the noblest part and must be covered, the feet the lowest, the right hand the clean and the left the unclean [[4]](#ref-4). Each physical gesture—hand-joining, bowed walking, the *bersila* squat for men and *bertimpoh* for women—encodes a relationship of respect, deference, or equality [[4]](#ref-4). The linguistic dimension is equally central: the pronoun system (*saya*, *aku*, *hamba*, *patek*, *beta*) precisely calibrates the social distance between speaker and addressee, from the *patek* used before a Sultan down to the *aku-engkau* reserved for intimate childhood friends or family seniors addressing juniors [[4]](#ref-4).

Dato' Muhammad Ghazzali, a senior courtier of Kelantan with thirty years of service to the royal family, provided the definitive insider's account of court language and etiquette in 1933 [[5]](#ref-5). The formal address of the Sultan is *Duli Yang Maha Mulia Sultan*; the Crown Prince is *Yang Teramat Mulia Raja Muda*; the Chief Minister (if non-royal) is *Yang Teramat Hormat Dato' Mentri* [[5, pp. 273–274]](#ref-5). The third-person pronoun *dia* is never applied to royalty, being replaced by the suffix *-nya* [[5, pp. 273–274]](#ref-5). State colours are strictly assigned: white for the royal standard, yellow for royalty, green for the Bendahara (Chief Minister), black for the Temenggong (Keeper of the Peace), and red for the Laksamana (military commander) [[5, p. 278]](#ref-5). The palace audience hall (*balai*) is divided into four tiers: a dais for royalty, a first *tapakan* for ministers and Dato's, a second for gentry, and the ground floor for the peasantry [[5, p. 279]](#ref-5). At a royal marriage, the *Ikat Tangan* contribution is fixed by rank: the Crown Prince pays $100, the Chief Minister $70, other chiefs $50, and a rich merchant approximately $25 [[5, p. 284]](#ref-5).

At the village level, the distribution of power and influence has been the subject of sustained ethnographic investigation. K.O.L. Burridge's 1957 study of a Johore village demonstrated that traditional kin-based and religious structures successfully absorbed newer political institutions—particularly the Local Council and national political parties—rather than being displaced by them [[6]](#ref-6). The governing logic of village management is the concept of *pandai*: the deliberate avoidance of positive decision-making, the playing of rivals against each other, and the maintenance of the status quo as a positive value [[6]](#ref-6). Burridge traced this to the historical position of Malays between more powerful political entities, and showed how it is reinforced by kin obligations, Islamic norms, and the proverbial culture that relieves individuals of the burden of novel decision-making [[6]](#ref-6). The Local Council, formed in 1953, "slid neatly into the existing managerial structure": its Malay members are all agnates or closely allied to the dominant group [[6]](#ref-6).

S. Husin Ali's 1968 comparative study of rural leadership in Pahang and Johore demonstrated that the transition from traditional to modern leadership is neither complete nor uniform [[7]](#ref-7). The *penghulu* retains the formal trappings of the old hereditary village head but now functions as a salaried government servant whose authority derives from the District Office rather than the *sultan* [[7]](#ref-7). New dominant leaders—school teachers and wealthy landlords—have risen not by displacing the traditional categories but by occupying the key posts in the proliferating committees and organisations that the modern administrative and political order has introduced into the village [[7]](#ref-7). In Kerdau, 7 teachers held 10 of 53 committee posts; in Bagan, 10 teachers held 13 of 56 posts, despite constituting only 10.8% and 10.1% of household heads respectively [[7, pp. 134–135]](#ref-7). In the urban Malay settlement of Kampung Baru, Kuala Lumpur, the *penghulu* was virtually unknown to residents; dominant leaders were English-educated government or ex-government servants with monthly incomes of $300–$700 [[7, pp. 138–141]](#ref-7).

The persistence of sub-ethnic identity within the homogenised category of "Malay" has also been documented. Nur Aisyah Kotarumalos's 2019 ethnographic study of a Bugis *kampong* in Pontian, Johore, revealed that approximately 500,000 Bugis Malays reside throughout Malaysia, with around 200,000 in Johore [[8, p. 80]](#ref-8). The 1911 census recorded 3,779 Bugis immigrants at Kukup; by 1921 this had nearly doubled to 5,500 [[8, pp. 80–81]](#ref-8). Bugis identity is mediated through narratives of the past, specifically the story of five aristocratic Bugis brothers who played a foundational role in the Johor Sultanate [[8]](#ref-8). Noble Bugis preserve detailed genealogical and migration narratives transmitted orally across generations, while non-nobles exhibit what Carsten terms "genealogical amnesia" regarding their own family histories but remain attached to the communal story of the five brothers [[8]](#ref-8).

## Manuscript Culture and the Material Record

The material record of Malay culture is preserved in a substantial body of manuscripts and early printed books. A. Kohar Rony's documentation of the Library of Congress collection, acquired largely through the agency of American missionary Alfred North during the Wilkes Exploring Expedition of 1838–1842, demonstrates that the collection's value lies not in its size but in its uniqueness and the specific historical window it opens onto the first half of the nineteenth century [[9, p. 33]](#ref-9). The collection comprises fourteen Malay codices and one bound volume containing forty-seven letters of official correspondence from rulers in Brunei, Johore-Pahang, Kelantan, Riau, Siak, Trengganu, Palembang, and Cambodia, addressed to Colonel William Farquhar, British Resident in Singapore, dated between 1812 and 1822 [[9, pp. 35–38]](#ref-9). The earliest printed title in the collection is a 1677 Oxford imprint of the Four Gospels and Acts of the Apostles translated into Malay, unique in its testimony to Malay as the *lingua franca* of the seventeenth-century Malay world [[9, p. 40]](#ref-9). The first printing press in the region was established at Prince of Wales Island, Penang, in 1806, followed by Malacca in 1815 and Singapore in 1822; the first Arabic-script imprints appeared in 1817 [[9, p. 39]](#ref-9). Two copies of the *Sejarah Melayu* held at the Library are the only extant copies of these particular imprints worldwide [[9, p. 44]](#ref-9).

## Research and Documentation

The scholarly investigation of Malay culture and society within the Society's journal literature reveals a clear evolution in methodology, perspective, and institutional context. The earliest major contributions—Wilkinson's 1932 and 1957 studies—were produced by British colonial administrators with direct access to royal courts and regalia, and they established the synthetic, comparative framework that dominated the field for decades [[2]](#ref-2), [[1]](#ref-1). Wilkinson's 1932 article drew heavily on the *Papers on Malay Subjects* series published by the Federated Malay States government, on Skeat's *Malay Magic* (1900), and on Winstedt's *Shaman, Saiva and Sufi* (1925), positioning itself as a synthetic work integrating administrative, literary, and ethnographic sources [[2]](#ref-2). His 1957 volume, published in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, represents one of the last major pre-independence ethnographic syntheses of Malay culture produced by a British colonial administrator-scholar [[1]](#ref-1).

The 1950s and 1960s marked a shift toward indigenous and post-colonial perspectives. Zainal Abidin bin Ahmad's 1950 article on Malay manners and etiquette, written by a Malay scholar for a mixed colonial and indigenous readership during the early phase of the Malayan Emergency, gave the text both ethnographic authority and a subtle apologetic function, presenting Malay *adab* as a civilised and sophisticated system comparable to European codes of conduct [[4]](#ref-4). Dato' Muhammad Ghazzali's 1933 court manual, though earlier in date, similarly represented an insider's account written for an external audience [[5]](#ref-5). The post-independence period saw the emergence of systematic sociological and historical-geographical approaches: Husin Ali's 1968 study of rural leadership constituted the first systematic treatment of Malay rural leadership as a distinct subject [[7]](#ref-7), while Mahmud's 1970 article contributed to the emerging field of historical geography by attempting to reconstruct past cultural landscapes from fragmentary evidence [[3]](#ref-3).

The late 20th and early 21st centuries have witnessed a further diversification. Roff's 2012 memoir on fieldwork methodology illustrates the intersection of ethnographic immersion and archival discovery in the production of historical scholarship, documenting how his six weeks in Kampung Jawa and two months in Kampung Baru proved indispensable to the archival discoveries that underpinned *The Origins of Malay Nationalism* [[10]](#ref-10). Kotarumalos's 2019 study of Bugis identity in Johore represents the current frontier of "Other Malays" scholarship, challenging the homogeneity of the Malay category and demonstrating how sub-ethnic persistence operates under conditions of state-driven homogenisation [[8]](#ref-8). The Library of Congress documentation by Rony (reprinted 2020) extends the material-culture dimension, providing the first systematic discussion of early Malay printed books and their significance for the history of printing in the archipelago [[9]](#ref-9).

Across this entire trajectory, a persistent tension runs between the desire to document Malay culture as a coherent, self-sustaining system and the recognition that it is a dynamic, stratified formation continuously reshaped by external contact, internal differentiation, and the pressures of modernisation. The records attest to a field that has moved from the synthetic colonial survey to the specialised, methodologically diverse scholarship of the present, while retaining the foundational insight—first articulated by Wilkinson—that Malay civilisation is best understood not as a fixed essence but as a process of continuous accretion and selective adaptation.
