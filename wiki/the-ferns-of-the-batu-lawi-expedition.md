# The ferns of the Batu Lawi expedition

E.B. Copeland's "The ferns of the Batu Lawi expedition" (1912) is a brief taxonomic appendix to the larger multi-author account of the 1911 expedition to Mount Batu Lawi in the Limbang district of Sarawak, in which Copeland served as the botanical collector. The paper catalogues the fern species gathered during the expedition's transit through the upper Limbang, Madihit, and mountainous interior regions, providing a preliminary flora list for an area previously almost entirely unknown to Western science.

## Summary

Copeland's contribution is a concise list of ferns collected during the expedition's roughly two-and-a-half-month journey from Kuching to the Baram district, with the bulk of collecting concentrated in the upper Limbang and Madihit valleys and on the slopes of Mount Batu Lawi itself. The paper addresses the paucity of botanical knowledge for this remote interior region, where, as the main narrative notes, only three Europeans had reached the Kuala Madihit in the preceding twenty years (p. 2). The ferns are listed with their localities, and the collection, though small in absolute terms owing to the expedition's hurried character and the difficulty of the terrain, yielded several species new to science or new to the known range of Sarawak.

The paper is embedded within a larger collaborative publication that includes contributions on entomology, ornithology, and general ethnography by Moulton, Ridley, Smith, Griffini, and Laidlaw. Copeland's section is proportionately brief—spanning only two pages—reflecting both the limited time available for botanical collecting and the fact that the expedition's primary objective was geographical and ethnographic rather than purely scientific. The ferns recorded represent the montane and lowland rainforest assemblages of the Limbang–Madihit watershed, an area of considerable elevational range from sea level to over 5,000 feet.

### Key Findings

- The expedition collected ferns across a wide elevational gradient, from the lowland Limbang River (sea level) to the upper slopes of Batu Lawi (approximately 5,660 ft.) and the intervening Madihit valley (p. 33–35).
- The collecting period was severely constrained: of the 40 days from Limbang to Claudetown, 31 were spent in actual travelling, with only five occasions on which the party slept two consecutive nights in the same place (p. 56).
- The Madihit district was so sparsely populated that the party spent 13 days between Penribut's house and Batu Lawi without encountering any native habitation, limiting opportunities for resupply and extended collecting (p. 56).
- The expedition's general health was poor, with fever, colds, and stomach disorders prevalent among the native carriers in the cold, damp mountain conditions, further reducing collecting capacity (p. 57).
- The paper forms part of a multi-disciplinary publication in JSBRAS Vol. 63 (1912), which also includes entomological, ornithological, and ethnographic appendices by the other expedition members.

### Conclusion

Copeland's fern list, though modest in scope, constitutes one of the earliest recorded botanical inventories for the upper Limbang and Madihit regions of Sarawak, an area of considerable topographic and ecological diversity that remained largely unexplored by Western naturalists until this expedition. Its value lies less in the number of species recorded than in establishing a baseline flora for a region that, as Moulton's main narrative emphasizes, had seen only a handful of European visitors in the preceding half-century.

## Context

- The paper draws on field collections made during the May–July 1911 expedition, the first European party to reach the vicinity of Mount Batu Lawi since Sir Spenser St. John's 1858 journey (p. 1).
- The study contributes to the early botanical mapping of Sarawak's interior highlands, a region whose flora was only beginning to be characterized in the first decade of the twentieth century.
