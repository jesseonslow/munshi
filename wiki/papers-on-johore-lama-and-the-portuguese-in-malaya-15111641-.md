# Papers on Johore Lama and the Portuguese in Malaya (1511–1641). C.A. Gibson-Hill {and} G. de G. Sieveking

A. M. S.'s memoir of Captain Francis Light, published in 1895 on the centenary of Light's death, reconstructs the life and administrative career of the founder of Penang from 1771 to 1794. Drawing on Light's own diary, despatches, and the official records of the East India Company, the author presents Light as a man of singular purpose and probity who established a thriving mixed settlement against considerable institutional indifference from Calcutta. The memoir also engages the long-running historiographical dispute over whether the Raja of Kedah had the legal competence to cede Penang, siding with J. R. Logan's view that Kedah's independence was the accepted position in 1786.

## Summary

The memoir traces Light's career from his first correspondence with Warren Hastings in 1771 proposing a repairing harbour in the Straits, through the abandoned Junk Ceylon scheme of 1780–81, to the successful landing at Penang in July 1786. The author emphasises the practical difficulties of the early years—piracy, disease, Dutch hostility at Malacca, and the jealousy of Kedah—against which Light maintained the settlement with a force of 100 native marines, 30 lascars, and 15 European artillerymen (p. 2). A central theme is Light's persistent but largely unsuccessful campaign to secure a proper judicial and administrative framework from the Bengal Government, which regarded the settlement with suspicion and offered little material support (pp. 4–5).

The second major thread is the Kedah suzerainty controversy. The author marshals Colonel Low's own admissions in Logan's Journal to demonstrate that the Siamese claim rested solely on the triennial *bunga mas*, a token of inferior pretensions rather than evidence of effective sovereignty, and that the question was never raised during the original negotiations of 1786 (pp. 9–12). The author also corrects the persistent myth, repeated even in Balfour's *Encyclopædia of India*, that Penang was given as a marriage portion for Light's wife, identifying her instead as Martina Rozells, a Portuguese Christian of the Roman Catholic Mission at Kedah (pp. 12–13).

The memoir closes with an assessment of Light's land-grant policy, arguing that the perpetuity grants he issued were the only viable strategy for a new colony and that the blame for the restrictive five-year lease resolution of 1794 lay with his superiors, not with him (pp. 14–15).

### Key Findings

- Light landed at Pulau Tikus on 15 July 1786 and took formal possession of the island on 11 August 1786, with a force of 100 native marines, 30 lascars, and 15 European artillerymen (p. 2).
- The settlement's population reached approximately 10,000 by the end of 1789 and over 20,000 by 1795, of whom the Chinese numbered not much over 3,000 (p. 4).
- Light's 1791 victory at Kuala Pry saw his force of 400 men disperse a Kedah force that had swelled to over 8,000 Malays (p. 7).
- The first *ad valorem* customs duty of 2 per cent on tin, pepper, and betel-nut was levied in 1801, producing $13,076 in that year (p. 14).
- The Governor-General in Council rescinded the restrictive land-grant resolution of 1 August 1794 on 22 August 1796, restoring perpetuity grants (p. 15).
- Light's salary as Superintendent was Rs. 1,000 per month, which he found insufficient to justify abandoning his merchant activities (p. 7).

### Conclusion

The author's definitive takeaway is that Light's success was attributable to personal qualities—singleness of purpose, administrative insight, and an honourable refusal to exploit his dual position as Superintendent and principal merchant—rather than to institutional support, which was minimal and often hostile. The memoir positions Light as the worthy forerunner of Raffles, whose Singapore settlement of 1819 followed "very similar lines" (p. 8).

## Context

- The memoir was written for the centenary of Light's death (21 October 1894) and draws on Light's diary (printed in Logan's Journal, Vol. III), his despatches (Logan's Journal, Vols. IV–V), and the Court of Directors' despatch of September 1805 (Logan's Journal, Vol. V).
- The author takes a clear side in the Kedah suzerainty controversy, aligning with J. R. Logan against Colonel Low, and explicitly notes that Low was "an avowed partisan" writing from a later political context (p. 9).
