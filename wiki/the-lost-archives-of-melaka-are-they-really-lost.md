# The lost archives of Melaka: are they really lost?

P. J. Rivers, in this 2005 article for the Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, re-examines the Malay geographical terms *negeri di-bawah angin* ("below the wind") and *negeri atas angin* ("above the wind") against the backdrop of pre-modern maritime trade in the Straits of Malacca. His overarching thesis is that British scholars, following the Dutch Valentyn (1726) and William Marsden (1812), erroneously restricted *atas angin* to western countries alone, when it in fact denoted all lands to the north of the Malay heartland—both westerly and easterly—and that a corrupted form of the Malay *ka-atas* may have contributed to the European conflation of Marco Polo's Central Asian "Cathay" with coastal China.

## Summary

Rivers traces the paper trail of these Malay wind-direction terms from their first European recording by the Portuguese historian De Barros in 1553 through successive British and Dutch interpretations. De Barros, writing from Portugal but drawing on local knowledge, identified two distinct maritime zones converging on Singapore: the Bay of Bengal to the west and the "great gulf" of the China Sea to the east, with the Malays occupying the leeward position relative to both. Valentyn, writing on the spot in Malacca in 1726, reversed De Barros's orientation by anchoring the terms to the Persian *zirbad* ("leeward") and the Indian Ocean monsoon system, thereby casting the Malays as "easterlings" and all westerners as "westerlings." Marsden's 1812 *Dictionary* cemented this Western-only reading for over eighty years, and it was inherited by Crawfurd, Yule and Burnell, Maxwell, Wilkinson, and Winstedt.

Rivers dismantles this consensus on several grounds. First, he demonstrates that two separate wind-reference systems were in operation simultaneously: a "Sulu" system for the South China Sea (where Borneo and the Malay heartland were both "below the wind") and a "Persian" system for the western Indian Ocean. Both systems converged on the Straits of Malacca as the meeting point of monsoons, making the Malays simultaneously "easterlings" to Indian Ocean traders and "westerlings" to China Sea traders. Second, he shows that the assumption of one-way monsoon traffic is incorrect for the Bay of Bengal, where ships sailed both ways on the same wind; only in the South China Sea did the strict one-way pattern hold. Third, he argues that the modern explanation linking *di-bawah angin* to the typhoon belt is anachronistic, since the term predates any charting of typhoon patterns and the first recorded typhoon at Sandakan did not occur until 1904.

The article's most speculative but original contribution concerns the etymology of "Cathay." Rivers notes that Eredia, the Portuguese Cosmographer-Major writing in Malacca around 1613, used "Atay" and "Cathay" interchangeably and placed them in Manchuria on his maps, not on the Chinese coast. He suggests that the Malay *ka-atas* ("to the north/above") in a corrupted form—*Kataia*—may have provided a phonetic resonance that helped early European geographers shift Marco Polo's inland Cathay to the coastal regions of northern China, a "rectification" that Rivers argues was neither immediate nor correct.

### Key Findings

- De Barros (1553) recorded the terms with *de-bawah angin* = western and *atas angin* = eastern, relative to Singapore; Valentyn (1726) reversed this, and Marsden (1812) formally dismissed De Barros as "not locally acquainted with the country" (pp. 1–2, 24–25).
- The Persian *zirbad* ("leeward") appears in a 1442 reference around the Persian Gulf, where people arrived "from the countries of Tchin (China), Java, Bengal, the cities of Zirbad," predating any Malay usage by over a century (p. 5).
- The first typhoon recorded at Sandakan, Sabah, was on 31 October 1904, demonstrating that the "below the typhoon belt" explanation for *negeri di-bawah angin* is a modern anachronism (p. 27).
- Eredia's three maps (c. 1613) place "Cathay" in Manchuria or west of China, never as a synonym for coastal China; only one map shows a coastline, belonging to a separate "Atay" on the Sea of Japan (pp. 17–20).
- Linschoten (1598) still described Cathay as "lying westward from China" and "bordering upon Persia," while the shift to northern China is credited to the Jesuit Martin Martini's *Atlas Novus Sinensis* (1655) and Valentyn some seventy years later (pp. 15–16).
- The Royal Navy's East Indies and China Stations were divided at the northern end of the Straits of Malacca, mirroring the ancient maritime boundary between the Indian Ocean and the China Sea (p. 6).

### Conclusion

Rivers concludes that the Malay wind-direction terms were a relative, nautical expression rooted in the position of the Straits of Malacca as the point where monsoon systems met, not a fixed geographical or meteorological classification. The British scholarly consensus that *atas angin* meant exclusively "western" was a misreading introduced by Valentyn and perpetuated by Marsden, and the further identification of "Cathay" with coastal China was a cartographic error that may have been facilitated by the phonetic similarity between the Malay *ka-atas* and the European "Cathay."

## Context

- The article draws heavily on primary sources held in MBRAS reprint collections: Marsden's *Dictionary* (1812), Crawfurd's *Descriptive Dictionary* (1856), Yule and Burnell's *Hobson-Jobson* (1886), and J. V. Mills's edition of Eredia's *Description of Malacca* (1930/1997), along with the *Sejarah Melayu* in C. C. Brown's translation (1970).
- Historiographically, the piece challenges the entrenched Marsden–Valentyn orthodoxy that dominated Malay linguistic and geographical scholarship from the early nineteenth century through the mid-twentieth, offering a reintegration of the South China Sea trading system into the analysis of these terms.
