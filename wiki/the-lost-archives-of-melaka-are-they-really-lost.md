# The lost archives of Melaka: are they really lost?

M.R. Fernando's 2005 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* challenges the long-standing assumption that the archival records of the Dutch Melaka *comptoir* (1641–1824) have been lost without trace. Drawing on a detailed survey of holdings in the VOC archives at The Hague and the Dutch Records from Malacca collection in the British Library, Fernando demonstrates that a vast corpus of documents covering the political, economic, and social affairs of the Malay Peninsula survives and remains largely untapped by Malaysian historians.

## Summary

The claim that Melaka's Dutch archives are lost rests on three assumptions Fernando systematically dismantles: that only a small portion of Melaka's papers reached Batavia or Amsterdam; that those papers covered only matters of vital importance to the Company and were irrelevant to indigenous history; and that the local *comptoir* archive perished entirely (pp. 2–3). In reality, virtually all papers Melaka dispatched to Batavia from 1641 to 1795 are preserved in the *Overgekomen brieven en papieren uit Indie aan de Heren XVII en de kamer Amsterdam* series of the VOC archives in the national archives of the Netherlands (p. 4). These include the governor's letters, the *dagh register* (daily journal), resolution books of the Political Council, instructions to envoys, memoirs of outgoing governors, account books, and—most notably—the harbour master's shipping list, which Fernando identifies as the most complete and detailed register of its kind for any port in Asia during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (pp. 5–7).

The second major body of evidence is the local archive of the Melaka *comptoir*, which Fernando shows was not lost but simply neglected after the British took over in 1824. The papers languished in damp cellars of old buildings in Melaka until a large consignment was discovered in the storage of the Melaka courthouse and deposited in the India Office Library in 1927; following the 1982 reorganization of British libraries, they are now held in the British Library (p. 10). This collection, comprising thirty-eight categories of papers covering the period from the 1740s to the mid-1820s, includes the proceedings of the Political Council (1764–1795), the registers and detailed proceedings of the Court of Justice (1756–1825), wills and testaments (1685–1822), protocol records of business transactions, estate papers, and records of the Orphan Chamber (pp. 10–11, 16–19). Fernando illustrates the richness of these records through extended case studies of criminal trials involving slaves, Malay villagers, and Javanese migrants, as well as commercial documents revealing cross-ethnic partnerships, shipbuilding, and credit arrangements (pp. 11–19).

Fernando argues that the evidence in these archives demands a radical revision of two dominant narratives in Malaysian historiography. First, the conventional view that Dutch control from 1641 imposed a trade monopoly that stifled indigenous economic activity is contradicted by the shipping list and commercial papers, which depict a vibrant local economy well into the 1790s (pp. 8–9). Second, the image of Dutch political hegemony over the Malay kingdoms is overstated; the correspondence between Melaka and Malay rulers reveals an uneasy but often amicable relationship in which the rulers successfully circumvented disadvantageous treaties (pp. 8–9).

### Key Findings

- Virtually all papers Melaka sent to Batavia from 1641 to 1795 survive in the VOC archives at The Hague, forming the kernel of the *comptoir*'s documentary record (p. 4).
- The Melaka harbour master's shipping list (1682–1792) records all European and indigenous vessels passing through the Straits, providing the only source on individual traders operating in the western Malay Archipelago and lending itself to quantitative economic and social history (p. 7).
- The local archive of the Melaka *comptoir* (1740s–mid-1820s), comprising thirty-eight categories of papers, was discovered in the Melaka courthouse and is now held in the British Library under the shelfmark R/9 (pp. 10–11).
- Court records from 1749 to 1825 document crimes ranging from petty theft to wilful murder across all ethnic groups, revealing a litigious, multi-ethnic urban society in which inhabitants were thoroughly familiar with Dutch legal procedures (pp. 11–16).
- Protocol records and wills demonstrate a high degree of cross-ethnic economic relations, including Chinese-Malay partnerships, a thriving slave trade drawing on supplies from Minangkabau, and a local shipbuilding industry (pp. 16–19).
- The correspondence between Melaka and Malay rulers shows that the VOC was unable to impose its will on indigenous polities with impunity; Malay rulers protested against and circumvented disadvantageous treaties, and relations were more often amicable than hostile (pp. 8–9).

### Conclusion

Fernando's definitive takeaway is that the "lost archives" narrative is not merely incomplete but actively misleading, and that its persistence in Malaysian historiography has led scholars to rely on British colonial writings and nineteenth-century Malay chronicles rather than on the rich contemporary Dutch record. The surviving documents—both in The Hague and in the British Library—provide an indispensable foundation for reconstructing the political, economic, and social history of the Malay Peninsula from the 1640s to the 1820s, and their total neglect would produce "a patently misleading view of an important phase in the history of the Malay world" (p. 20).

## Context

- The article was prompted by the continued influence of F.R.J. Verhoeven's 1964 claim in the same journal that the Melaka archives had "vanished without leaving a trace," a view Fernando identifies as the root of the problem (p. 1).
- The British Library's Dutch Records from Malacca collection (R/9) was inventorized by I.A. Baxter in 1983, and the VOC archives were reorganized in the late 1970s under the catalogue edited by Meilink-Roelofsz, Raben, and Spijkerman (1992), both of which facilitated Fernando's survey (pp. 4, 10).
