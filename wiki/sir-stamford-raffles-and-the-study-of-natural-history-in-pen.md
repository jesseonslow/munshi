# Sir Stamford Raffles and the study of natural history in Penang, Singapore and Indonesia

Lee Kam Hing and Ahmat Adam resolve a long-standing historiographical puzzle about the Acehnese Order of the Golden Sword conferred on Sir Stamford Raffles, drawing on a previously unknown Malay letter from Sultan Jauhar Alam Syah dated 27 April 1811 to demonstrate that the award was a legitimate diplomatic exchange rather than the trivial or suspicious affair that some contemporaries suspected. Published in 1990 in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, the article situates Raffles' engagement with Aceh within the broader context of British strategic and commercial interests in the Strait of Malacca during the Napoleonic Wars.

## Summary

The article addresses questions first raised by C. A. Gibson-Hill in 1956, who noted that Raffles' biographers had entirely omitted the Acehnese decoration from their accounts despite Raffles himself including it in his 1817 coat of arms. Gibson-Hill had speculated about the circumstances of the award but found no documentary evidence. The authors now demonstrate that a Malay letter from Sultan Jauhar Alam Syah, discovered in a British attic and translated by Ahmat Adam, explicitly confers the title *Sri Paduka Orangkaya Berpedang Emas* ("His Excellency the Nobleman with the Golden Sword") on Raffles. The letter was delivered by the envoy Tengku Nakhoda Abdul Rahman when Raffles was in Melaka preparing for the Java expedition, and a copy was transmitted to Lord Minto as Enclosure No. 7 to Raffles' despatch of 11 June 1811, now held in the Raffles-Minto Collection at the India Office Library (pp. 77–79).

The authors reconstruct the diplomatic context that made the award meaningful. The Sultan had been aggrieved by the 1808 seizure of his ship the *Hydroos* by Penang authorities, and was under suspicion of harbouring French naval interests in Acehnese ports. Raffles' letter of early 1811, which briefed the Sultan on impending British military moves against the French in Java, must have appeared to Jauhar Alam Syah as a signal of restored British goodwill and a possible avenue for redress. The Golden Sword was thus a gesture of reciprocal goodwill, likely exchanged for presents of gunpowder and firearms Raffles had already sent (pp. 80–81).

The article then explores why Raffles, who received more prestigious decorations from Javanese rulers, singled out the Acehnese award for his coat of arms. The authors argue it was the first such honour he received from any monarch (his British knighthood came only in 1816), and that Raffles, as a student of regional history, appreciated Aceh's distinguished past as the first Asian power to sign a treaty with the English East India Company in 1602. The inclusion also reflected Raffles' firm political stance in favour of Jauhar Alam Syah's legitimacy against the rival claim of Syed Hussein Aidid of Penang, a position that culminated in Raffles' decisive support for the Sultan during the 1819 Acehnese succession mission (pp. 82–85).

### Key Findings

- The Sultan's letter of 27 April 1811 (Jawi script, 1 folio, 47 cm × 57.6 cm) explicitly confers the title *Sri Paduka Orangkaya Berpedang Emas* on Raffles and was delivered by Tengku Nakhoda Abdul Rahman to Melaka (pp. 77, 86–88).
- Raffles' despatch to Lord Minto of 11 June 1811 included the Sultan's letter as Enclosure No. 7, meaning the award was formally communicated to the Governor-General in Bengal (pp. 78–79).
- The *Hydroos*, a ship purchased by the Sultan but registered in a Danish citizen's name, was seized and sold by Penang authorities in 1808 as enemy property during the Anglo-Danish war; the cargo was later returned but the ship itself was not (pp. 80, 83).
- In 1814 the three Panglima Sagis declared the overthrow of Jauhar Alam Syah and invited Syed Hussein Aidid of Penang to the Acehnese throne; Raffles' 1819 mission ultimately resolved the succession in the Sultan's favour (pp. 82–83, 85).
- The Penang government obtained short-term loans from Syed Hussein of $50,000 at 12% interest in July 1815 and a further $30,000 in August 1816, illustrating his commercial entanglement with the colonial administration (p. 82, n. 10).
- Raffles proposed in 1810 that the British take over collection of Acehnese port duties, supported by a force of 100 sepoys, with costs borne by the Acehnese — a scheme that was never implemented (p. 83).

### Conclusion

The authors conclude that the Order of the Golden Sword was a proper diplomatic exchange that should have been public knowledge in Bengal and Penang through Raffles' despatch to Minto, and that Raffles' attachment to the decoration reflected both its status as his first monarchical honour and his consistent political commitment to Jauhar Alam Syah's legitimacy — a commitment that bore fruit in the 1819 treaty ending the Acehnese civil war.

## Context

- The primary archival sources are the Raffles-Minto Collection (Vol. 6) in the India Office Library, London, and the Straits Settlements Factory Records (Vols. 31, 50, 56, 180) in the same collection.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in resolving Gibson-Hill's 1956 speculative exercise with a newly discovered primary document, and in reframing Raffles' Acehnese diplomacy as a coherent strategic programme rather than an isolated curiosity.
