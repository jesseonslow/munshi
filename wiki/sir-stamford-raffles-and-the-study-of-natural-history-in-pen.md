# Sir Stamford Raffles and the study of natural history in Penang, Singapore and Indonesia

In this 1990 article published in the Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, John Bastin examines the neglected scientific career of Sir Stamford Raffles across Penang, Singapore, and the Indonesian archipelago. The paper argues that contrary to popular belief, Raffles was an active naturalist prior to his time in Java, dedicating significant resources to specimen collection and the patronage of professional scientists throughout his administrative tenure. Bastin contends that while political history often overshadows these efforts, Raffles’s contributions to zoology and botany were foundational to British scientific institutions in the region.

## Summary

Bastin establishes that Raffles’s enthusiasm for natural history predated his famous association with Dr. Thomas Horsfield in Java, citing accounts from his Malay scribe Abdullah bin Abdul Kadir Munshi regarding Raffles’s employment of collectors in Melaka and Penang (p. 1–2). During his tenure in Java, Raffles shifted from personal collecting to patronage, securing Horsfield’s vast collections for the East India Company Museum and facilitating correspondence with Sir Joseph Banks in London (p. 6–8). Upon moving to west Sumatra, Raffles intensified his personal involvement, employing Dr. Joseph Arnold and later William Jack, whose botanical expertise complemented Raffles’s zoological ambitions (p. 10–12).

The narrative details the complex dynamics of Raffles’s relationships with other naturalists, including the French duo Diard and Duvaucel, whose collections were seized following a financial dispute to bolster British holdings (p. 15–16). A pivotal moment occurred during the voyage home aboard the ship *Fame*, where a fire destroyed nearly all living animals and thousands of drawings, forcing Raffles to recreate records from memory upon arrival in England (p. 18–19). Despite these losses, Raffles successfully repatriated preserved specimens and utilized his accumulated knowledge to establish the Zoological Society of London before his death in 1826 (p. 20–21).

### Key Findings

*   Raffles employed four men specifically to search for insects, shells, and wild animals during his service in Melaka between 1810 and 1811 (p. 1–2).
*   The giant flower *Rafflesia arnoldi* was discovered by Dr. Joseph Arnold in May 1818, though Robert Brown assigned the generic name to honor Raffles (p. 11–12).
*   William Jack authored the descriptions for the ‘Descriptive Catalogue’ of zoological materials seized from French naturalists, though Raffles published it under his own name (p. 16).
*   The ship *Fame* fire on 2 February 1824 consumed approximately one-third of the vessel’s cargo, including a living tapir and domesticated tigers (p. 18–19).
*   Raffles allocated land on Government Hill in Singapore for a Botanical Garden in November 1822, placing it under the charge of Dr. William Montgomerie (p. 17).
*   Sir Humphry Davy and Raffles founded the Zoological Society of London in 1826, with Raffles serving as its first President (p. 21).

### Conclusion

Bastin concludes that Raffles’s legacy extends beyond his political administration of Southeast Asia into the realm of scientific institution-building, particularly through the Zoological Society of London. Although the physical destruction of his collections on the *Fame* represents a significant historical tragedy, his strategic patronage of naturalists and dedication to documentation ensured that his scientific influence persisted in European academia. Ultimately, the article repositions Raffles not merely as a colonial administrator, but as a committed amateur naturalist whose efforts laid groundwork for modern biological understanding in the region.

## Context

*   Colonial Role: Natural history collections were instrumental in asserting British imperial knowledge and resource potential in the East Indies.
*   Archival Collections: Surviving drawings and specimens are held in the India Office Library and Records, London, and the Linnean Society.
