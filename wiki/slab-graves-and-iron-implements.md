# Slab-graves and iron implements

R.O. Winstedt, writing in 1941 as a senior colonial administrator and amateur archaeologist, synthesizes a decade of scattered reports on megalithic slab-graves across Perak, Sumatra, and Java to evaluate competing hypotheses about their builders. His overarching thesis is that the origin of these graves—whether Indian, Indo-Chinese, or otherwise—remains genuinely unresolved, and that the existing evidence, while suggestive, is insufficient to commit to any single cultural attribution.

## Summary

Winstedt's article functions as a critical review and synthesis of the principal published accounts of slab-graves in the Malay world and western Indonesia. He draws on Evans' descriptions of five Perak graves at Sungkai and the Bernam, Collings' finds at Slim and Sungai Kruit, van der Hoop's excavations in South Sumatra and Java, and the more recent interpretive papers by Braddell and Wilkinson. The material evidence is consistent across sites: no skeletal remains, coarse pottery fragments, glass and stone beads, and iron implements—socketed tools in Malaya, adze-type implements in Java. The absence of gold in any grave is noted as a significant negative datum.

The interpretive debate Winstedt addresses centres on two main rival hypotheses. Braddell argued the Perak graves lay on a route to Pahang gold-mines and that the Bernam was Ptolemy's Khryscanas, implying Indian miners; Winstedt concedes the gold argument but notes the total absence of gold in the graves undermines it. Wilkinson proposed an Indo-Chinese (Mon) origin, linking the graves to the same cultural sphere that produced the bronze drums. Winstedt raises several objections: the absence of bronze in the Malayan graves (whereas bronze drums are well attested), the fact that only one megalithic burial vault is known from Indo-China itself, and the linguistic argument that the relevant Mon words entered local speech only in a later wave.

Winstedt then turns to the Indian hypothesis, which Wilkinson had dismissed on the grounds that Indians practised cremation and did not export iron. Winstedt counters by citing Cumming's survey of South Indian Iron Age burial sites, which include pottery cists, sarcophagi, funeral urns, grinding stones, iron weapons, bronze articles, cornelian and crystal beads, and buffalo figures—a profile that overlaps considerably with the slab-grave assemblages. He notes that iron reached southern India by approximately 500 B.C., removing any *a priori* chronological objection. Yet he stops short of endorsing the Indian hypothesis, acknowledging that the Bernam site's later Hindu and Buddhist remains merely suggest earlier Indian contact without proving it.

### Key Findings

- Five Perak slab-graves (Sungkai and Bernam) yielded socketed iron tools, coarse pottery, and no skeletal remains; the Changkat Mantri grave uniquely contained a cross-hatched stone bark-pounder, a small bronze bowl, and three cornelian beads (p. 93).
- H.C. Beck dated the bead assemblages from the Slim and Sungai Kruit graves to between 1 and 400 A.D., based on their similarity to Kuala Selinsing and Johore beads (p. 94).
- Nine slab-graves were identified between Pagar 'Alam and Tanjong Sakti in South Sumatra; one at Tegoerwangi contained a gold pin and over 100 beads, while a second held 1,372 tiny ochre, white, and red beads plus eight bronze fragments (p. 94).
- The Wanasari slab-grave in Java contained 35 superimposed bodies, thousands of glass beads (including three long cornelian beads of a type also found in Malaya), woven patterns preserved in oxidised iron, and iron adze-type implements—none of the socketed type found in Malaya (p. 95).
- No gold was recovered from any of the slab-graves in Malaya, Sumatra, or Java, a fact Winstedt treats as a serious difficulty for Braddell's gold-mining hypothesis (p. 95).
- The only authenticated Indo-Chinese bronze found in Malaya is a drum-head of the type Callenfels allocated to 400–300 B.C., recovered from the Tembeling River (p. 97, n.).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is one of disciplined agnosticism: the slab-grave problem cannot be solved with the evidence then available. He favours neither the Indian nor the Indo-Chinese hypothesis, noting that the bronze drums' provenance from Indo-China does not automatically extend to the grave-builders, and that the Indian Iron Age burial record, while suggestive, has not been sufficiently documented for rigorous comparison. He calls for further excavation, more systematic bead typology, and the tracing of socketed iron tool distributions, while cautioning that multiple megalithic cultures and multiple sources of bronze may have operated in the region simultaneously.

## Context

- Winstedt was a senior colonial administrator (K.B.E., C.M.G.) and the principal editor of the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*; his article reflects the amateur-antiquarian tradition of colonial-era Malayan archaeology, relying on published museum reports rather than personal fieldwork.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its attempt to integrate the scattered Dutch, British, and Straits Settlements literature on megalithic graves into a single comparative framework, and in its explicit methodological caution against premature cultural attribution—a stance that anticipated later, more rigorous approaches to South and Southeast Asian prehistory.
