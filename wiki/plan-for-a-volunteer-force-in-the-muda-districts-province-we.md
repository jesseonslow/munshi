# Plan for a volunteer force in the Muda districts, Province Wellesley, submitted to government by the late J.R. Logan in 1867

J.R. Logan, a long-resident European in Province Wellesley, submitted this memorandum to the Lieutenant-Governor in August 1867 proposing a system of organized village volunteer police to counter gang robbery and secret-society violence in the under-policed Muda and Kreh districts. The document, published posthumously in the *Journal of the Siam Society* in 1885, is accompanied by the Lieutenant-Governor's approving response and a prefatory note by the late Resident Councillor H. Man, and it stands as one of the most detailed contemporary accounts of Malay village society, colonial policing failures, and the social pathology of the Straits Settlements interior.

## Summary

Logan's memorandum opens with a catalogue of recent violent crimes in his own neighbourhood—Chinese shopkeepers murdered, Malay houses burned with bamboo spikes laid along escape routes, and armed gangs of twenty to seventy men moving through populated villages with impunity. He attributes the failure of existing security arrangements to three structural deficiencies: the paid police force is too small and too distant from the interior villages; the Malay villagers, though numerous and armed, lack concert and leadership to resist organized bands; and the existing *Penghulu Besar* (Police Inspectors) have appointed unqualified local headmen without government sanction, creating a confused and unaccountable chain of command. During the recent Penang disturbances, a requisition for 300 Malay volunteers was answered by only about 120, and even those arrived with a *Penghulu* for every seven men rather than the intended structure of village headmen holding their posts while a senior officer led the detachment.

The core of the document is a fifteen-point plan for a volunteer police organized at the *kampong* (hamlet) level, with groups of adjacent *kampongs* forming *Dairahs* under a *Penghulu Besar*. The system is deliberately kept small-scale and village-based: Logan argues explicitly against the concentration of power in district-level Malay chiefs, whom he considers prone to corruption, alliance with religious leaders, and domination over populations they do not personally know. He insists that the system must be "totally disconnected with the mukims, mosques and jumahas" to prevent religious organizations from acquiring a political instrument. The plan includes a gong-and-drum alarm signal system modelled on Javanese police practice, the licensing of arms, the appointment of *Penghulus* as constables under Section 21 of the Police Act of 1856, and a proposed international commission with the Raja of Kedah for cross-river cooperation on fugitive offenders and cattle theft.

In a second, longer letter of 22 November 1867, Logan expands the argument well beyond policing. He contends that the volunteer system is the vehicle for a broader programme of "gradual education" of the Malay population: teaching them their legal rights and obligations, introducing sanitary knowledge, replacing the Arabo-Persian script with Roman characters, and eventually establishing village schools. He devotes considerable space to exposing the abuses of the *Kali* (unlicensed Islamic judges) who exercise de facto jurisdiction over marriage and divorce, extracting fees and in some cases exploiting women in their custody, and to the parasitical class of Malay intermediaries who profit from the ignorance of villagers in land transactions and litigation. His argument is that all these evils "arise from one source, ignorance," and that the village organization provides the administrative channel through which practical legal and sanitary knowledge can be disseminated.

### Key Findings

- The total number of male adults enrolled in the volunteer rolls was 3,663, representing an estimated general population of approximately 20,000 in the Muda and Kreh districts (p. 196).
- During the Penang disturbances, a requisition for 300 Malay volunteers was answered by only about 120 men, who arrived with a *Penghulu* for every seven men rather than the intended command structure (p. 175).
- The plan proposed dividing larger villages into *kampongs* of 20 to 30 houses each, with each *kampong* having a *Katua Besar*, *Katua Kechil*, and *Kweang* (messenger), and groups of adjacent *kampongs* forming *Dairahs* of 100 to 150 adult males under a *Penghulu Besar* and *Penghulu Muda* (pp. 176–177, 182).
- The *Penghulus* and *Katuas* were to be appointed as "additional constables" under Section 21 of the Police Act of 1856, serving without pay but with exemption from rates, and were to be furnished with muskets, swords, and gongs by Government (pp. 176–177, 179).
- Logan proposed a standing international commission with the *Penghulu Besar* at Kota (on the Kedah side of the Muda) for the arrest of fugitive criminals, regulation of ferries, prevention of smuggling and cattle trespass, and recovery of debts across the frontier (p. 178).
- The Lieutenant-Governor Anson approved the plan in part on 30 August 1867, agreeing that *Penghulus* might be appointed under Section 21 of the Police Act and permitted to carry arms, but noted that Government had no spare arms to supply and that expected changes in the Police Force might render the volunteers unnecessary (p. 178).

### Conclusion

Logan's definitive historical takeaway is that the village-level volunteer police is not merely a stopgap security measure but the foundational instrument for the long-term integration of the Malay rural population into the colonial legal and administrative order. He argues that the system will simultaneously deter crime, create a direct channel of communication between Government and the population, check the political influence of religious leaders and the *jumahas*, and provide the administrative framework through which practical legal, sanitary, and eventually literary education can be introduced to a population he considers "excessively ignorant" and systematically exploited by intermediaries.

## Context

- The document draws on Logan's five years of continuous residence in the Muda districts and his professional experience as a lawyer, giving it the character of an informed practitioner's memorandum rather than a detached policy paper. His ethnographic observations on the Malay, Jawi-pakan, Baba, and Samsum populations (pp. 198–201) were originally prepared at the request of the local government for transmission to the Government of India.
- The publication in 1885, eighteen years after Logan's death, was prefaced by H. Man, who had intended to implement the plan himself but was removed from office by the transfer of the Straits Settlements from Indian to Colonial Office control in 1867. Man's note confirms that the document was printed but not published in Penang in 1863, and that it represents a lost opportunity for administrative reform.
