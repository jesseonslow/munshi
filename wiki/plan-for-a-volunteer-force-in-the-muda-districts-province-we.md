# Plan for a volunteer force in the Muda districts, Province Wellesley, submitted to government by the late J.R. Logan in 1867

J.R. Logan, a long-resident lawyer and ethnographer in Province Wellesley, submitted this memorandum to the Lieutenant-Governor in August 1867, proposing a village-based volunteer police system for the Muda and Kreh districts to counter gang robbery, Chinese secret society violence, and the chronic inability of the regular police to protect scattered rural populations. Published posthumously in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1885, the document also includes the approving response of Lieutenant-Governor A.E.H. Anson and a later endorsement by Colonel H. Man, former Resident Councillor of Penang.

## Summary

The Muda and Kreh districts of Province Wellesley (the northern mainland portion of the Straits Settlements, now part of Penang) were in a state of chronic insecurity by the mid-1860s. Gangs of armed Malay robbers, often led by *Panglimas* credited with supernatural invulnerability, plundered villages with impunity; Chinese shopkeepers, recently disarmed by the police, were left entirely defenceless; and the existing *Penghulu* system—headmen appointed without government authority and lacking any coordinated arrangement—proved useless when tested, as demonstrated when a requisition for 300 Malay volunteers during the Penang disturbances yielded only about 120 men, accompanied by a disproportionate number of *Penghulu Mukims* (p. 174–175). Logan, who had resided in the district for five years and held leasehold interests there, argued that the rural population was "paralysed and helpless" on each recurring outbreak because it lacked both the legal knowledge and the organizational structure to act in concert (p. 185).

Logan's plan divided larger villages into *kampongs* of 20 to 30 houses, each headed by a *Katua Besar*, a *Katua Kechil*, and a *Kweang* (messenger), with groups of adjacent *kampongs* forming *Dairahs* under a *Penghulu Besar* and *Penghulu Muda* (p. 176–177). The *Penghulus* would be appointed Constables under Section 21 of the Police Act of 1856, furnished with muskets and swords by government, and given written appointments under the seal of the Lieutenant-Governor. A system of alarm signals using gongs and wooden *tong-tong* drums (modelled on the Java police) would coordinate a "Hue and Cry" response across *kampongs* (p. 177). Critically, Logan insisted the system be "totally disconnected with the mukims (parishes), mosques and jumahas" to prevent religious leaders from acquiring a powerful parallel organization (p. 176). He also proposed a standing international commission with the Raja of Kedah's *Penghulu Besar* at Kota to manage cross-border crime, cattle trespass, and ferry regulation (p. 178).

In his extended memorandum of 22 November 1867, Logan elaborated the broader rationale: the village organization would serve as a channel for government communication with a population that otherwise knew "Kampani" only as a vague mythical entity, would gradually educate the Malays in their legal rights and obligations, and would check the corrupt jurisdiction of the *Kalis* (unlicensed religious judges) who exercised compulsory authority over divorce and maintenance cases with no legal foundation (pp. 186–193). He estimated the enrolled male adult population at 3,663, representing a total population of approximately 20,000 (p. 196–197). Anson approved the plan in part on 30 August 1867, and Logan subsequently secured formal appointments from the Commissioner of Police by 15 October 1867 (pp. 178–179).

### Key Findings

- The existing *Penghulu* system was structurally defective: headmen were appointed by *Penghulu Besar* without government authority, many were "of bad reputation, or unable to write," and they had no systematic arrangements for united action; during the Penang disturbances, a call for 300 men produced only about 120, with a *Penghulu* for every seven men (p. 174–175).
- Logan's plan enrolled 3,663 male adults across the Muda and Kreh districts, representing an estimated total population of 20,000, with both married and unmarried women holding land and property rights (p. 196–197).
- The *Kali* (religious judge) system was identified as a major source of oppression: Kalis exercised compulsory jurisdiction over divorce and maintenance with no legal authority, charged substantial fees, and were widely accused of corruption, including pandered to their own sons and friends; one case involved a Kali demanding $10 to return a wife, which the husband noted was "as much as I paid for her twenty years ago when she was a virgin" (pp. 190–192).
- Logan documented systematic exploitation of ignorant Malay landholders: at one auction, lots he had purchased for $25 (trees alone) were knocked down to a colluding buyer for approximately $4 (land and trees), and the elaborate statutory protections against over-valuation "each [turned] into a weapon of exaction in the hands of an unscrupulous bill collector" (p. 189–190).
- The plan explicitly excluded the *jumahas* (religious congregations) and *mukims* (parish territories) from the organizational structure to prevent religious leaders from building a "too powerful an organization" that would exert "a strong social pressure to bear on the villagers in the interest of a stricter and more fanatical observance of Mahomedanism" (p. 176).
- Logan proposed a standing international commission with the Kedah-side *Penghulu Besar* at Kota to regulate ferries, prevent passage of criminals and stolen property, arrest fugitives, and prevent smuggling and cattle trespass along the shared river boundary (p. 178).

### Conclusion

Logan's definitive historical takeaway is that the root cause of the district's ills—gang robbery, religious factionalism, exploitation by unscrupulous intermediaries, and the paralysis of the rural population—was ignorance, and that a village-level organizational structure, directly accountable to the Lieutenant-Governor and elected by the villagers themselves, was the single instrument capable of simultaneously providing security, establishing a channel of government communication, and initiating a gradual process of legal and practical education. He framed the volunteer police not as a temporary expedient but as a permanent feature of good governance that would be gradually curtailed only as regular police, courts, and schools became adequate (p. 197).

## Context

- The document was printed but not formally published in Penang in 1863 (per the editor's note) and appeared in the *JSBRAS* in 1885, two years after Logan's death; Colonel Yule's tribute describes him as having carried "to his too early tomb a vaster knowledge of the races and regions of the Indian Archipelago than any one else is likely to accumulate in our day" (p. 202).
- The plan was submitted during the transition of the Straits Settlements from Indian to Colonial Office administration (1867), a moment of institutional uncertainty that Logan and Man both reference as affecting the continuity of reform efforts (pp. 184, 202).
