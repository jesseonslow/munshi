# Government in Sarawak under Charles Brooke

Otto C. Doering III's 1966 article examines the administrative system of Sarawak during the reign of the second White Rajah, Charles Brooke (r. 1868–1888), arguing that Brooke's governance was not a simple top-down colonial imposition but a complex, compromise-driven system that depended fundamentally on the maintenance of native authority structures and the personal qualities of individual European officers.

## Summary

The article's central concern is to move beyond the romanticized narrative of James Brooke's founding and to analyze how Charles Brooke actually governed. Doering draws heavily on Brooke's own published writings—*Ten Years in Sarawak* (1866) and *Queries: Past, Present & Future* (1907)—to reconstruct the Rajah's theoretical framework for colonial rule. Brooke's philosophy was grounded in what Doering terms the "native principle": governance should start from existing conditions, weed out harmful practices while preserving beneficial ones, and develop institutions organically rather than importing Western models. This was both a humanitarian conviction and a practical necessity given the limited European resources available to the Raj. Brooke explicitly rejected the idea that councils or institutions alone could ensure good government, insisting instead that the character and personal immersion of individual rulers and officers were decisive.

Doering then turns to the actual administrative machinery, using Sarawak Gazette outstation reports (1880–1883) and civil service lists to demonstrate that the formal structure of four divisions, each with a Divisional Resident, was far less rigid in practice than official descriptions suggested. The term "residency" was used loosely to denote important district stations rather than coherent administrative units, and European officers were thinly spread across the territory. The District Officer's role was all-encompassing—judicial, fiscal, military, and diplomatic—and his success depended less on formal authority than on personal skill, local knowledge, and the good will of the community. At the community level, the Tuah Kampong and Pengulu systems represented not a faithful preservation of pre-existing native institutions but a colonial compromise that created new hybrid officials with responsibilities foreign to their traditional roles.

The article's most significant analytical contribution is its treatment of the relationship between European and native power. Doering argues against the assumption that native influence simply declined as Brooke authority consolidated. Instead, there was a geographical shift: the Kuching Datus became increasingly ceremonial and institutionalized, while real governing power at the district and community levels remained in the hands of Native Officers and local leaders. The system was not a clean three-tiered hierarchy but a web of compromises, with control from the center varying proportionally with communication distance and the Rajah's personal opinion of each officer.

### Key Findings

- The Supreme Council, founded in 1855 at Earl Grey's suggestion, operated under a rule that European members should never outnumber native ones; by the turn of the century it was considered the sole legislative authority in Sarawak (pp. 99–100).
- The Council Negri, created by Charles Brooke in 1865, comprised thirty to fifty members and met roughly every three years; Brooke waited until its 1870 session to be officially proclaimed Rajah following his uncle's death in 1868 (p. 100).
- The Committee of Administration first governed Sarawak in 1873 for a period of eighteen months during the Rajah's absence, its actions carrying the same force as the Rajah's until his return (pp. 100–101).
- In the 1880s, Doering estimates approximately twenty-five to thirty European officers in the entire Sarawak Civil Service, with a third based in Kuching; at stations such as Betong, Pusa, Tuban, Kabong, Sarebas, Balleh, and Kanowit, the entire staff was native (p. 102).
- The opium farm was the government's greatest single source of revenue at one point and remained very important in the 1880s; the Captain China controlled gambling, pawn-broking, and some monopolies within strictly regulated Chinese settlements (p. 105).
- Tuah Kampongs were initially allowed to retain ten percent of taxes collected as remuneration, a practice later changed to a salary system to reduce the temptation for extortion (pp. 104–105).

### Conclusion

Doering's definitive takeaway is that Brooke rule was neither the benevolent paternalism its contemporaries portrayed nor the efficient colonial machine later historians sometimes assumed. It was a system of "paternalistic totalitarianism" sustained by an unavoidable native content—both in the form of Native Officers exercising real governing functions and in the structural dependence on tacit native consent that limited European manpower made inevitable. The Rajah's authority did not percolate downward in an orderly fashion; rather, administration at every level was a matter of compromise and expediency, with irregularities tolerated by the center in exchange for local cooperation.

## Context

- Primary archival sources include the Sarawak Gazette (Vols. X–XIII, 1880–1883) for outstation reports and personnel data, the Sarawak Civil Service List (1925), and Charles Brooke's Letter Books held in the Sarawak Museum Archives.
- The article contributes to Sarawak historiography by shifting analytical focus from the romantic founding narrative of James Brooke to the administrative mechanics of Charles Brooke's reign, and by using outstation-level evidence to challenge the simplified three-tiered model of Brooke governance that prevailed in earlier secondary literature.
