# The introduction of Islam into Champa

Bishop Hose, with scholarly notes by G. F. Hose, published this Malay-language romance in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1911, presenting a translation of the *Hikayat Saif-al-Yezan*—an Arabic-origin tale set in a mythologised pre-Islamic Yemen and Ethiopia that narrates the destined rise of a hero who converts to the faith of Abraham and conquers the lands of Absha and Sudan. The work functions as both a literary text and a vehicle for documenting the transmission of Arabic romance traditions into the Malay world.

## Summary

The *Hikayat Saif-al-Yezan* is a lengthy Malay adaptation of an Arabic romance, attributed within the text to "Sheikh Abu-al-Maali." The narrative opens with King Teba Zu-al-Yezan of Yemen, whose vizier Yasrib foresees the coming of a prophet from the Hashimite line and accordingly founds Medina Yasrib in preparation for the Prophet Muhammad's arrival (pp. 3–9). The king's grandson—initially named Wahash Alfalah, later revealed as Saif-al-Yezan ("Sword of the Age")—is abandoned as an infant by his mother Kemrih, rescued by a hunter, and raised at the court of King Afrah in Absha (Ethiopia). A green mark on his cheek identifies him as the prophesied destroyer of Absha and Sudan, a destiny foretold through the curse of Noah upon his son Ham (pp. 22–24).

The central dramatic engine is the villainous vizier Sakerdion, who manipulates King Afrah into imposing impossible conditions on the hero's marriage to Seti Shameh: first the head of the bandit-king Sadon Zanji, then the sacred *Kitab Tarikh-al-Nil* (pp. 38–39, 58). The hero's quest for this text carries him across the Nile on the back of a dragon, into the city of King Malek al-Kamar, where he is imprisoned in the pit Hebab-al-Ghadlab and rescued by the jinn princess Akesah (pp. 62–81). He subsequently visits the palace of Ham, son of Noah, where he obtains a magical gold tablet and a sword, but disobeys the injunction not to uncover the sleeping giant's face, triggering a catastrophic escape (pp. 154–158). The narrative weaves together elements of the *Thousand and One Nights*, the Swan Maiden myth, and Islamic eschatology, with the hero's repeated conversions of enemies to the "religion of Ibrahim Khalil Allah" serving as its moral spine (pp. 61–62, 97–98).

G. F. Hose's extensive endnotes (pp. 246–252) provide the scholarly apparatus, identifying parallels with Lane's translation of the *Arabian Nights*, explaining Sabian star-worship, the *Sahif* of Abraham, and the four rivers of Eden, and situating the tale within the broader tradition of Arabic romances that circulated in manuscript and lithograph form in the Straits Settlements.

### Key Findings

- The text was composed in Arabic and translated into Malay; a lithograph edition was published at No. 37 Jalan Bali Lebar, Singapore, in 1894, and the author (Bishop Hose) could only obtain a second-hand copy of the second volume (p. 2).
- The hero's conversion formula is "Ishahadan la ilah illa Allah u ishahadat Ibrahim Khalil Allah"—a pre-Islamic formulation invoking Abraham rather than Muhammad, consistent with the story's setting before the Prophet's birth (pp. 61–62).
- The *Kitab Tarikh-al-Nil* is identified as having been written by Ham, son of Noah, and is worshipped by the people of King Malek al-Kamar's city; whoever possesses it rules over all Absha and Sudan (pp. 58, 247).
- The palace of Ham, son of Noah, contains a corpse twenty-one cubits long, from whose chest the hero extracts a gold tablet with a chain and a sword; the tablet summons the jinn Airodl when read aloud (pp. 154–156).
- The four rivers of Eden (Furat, Nil, Saihan, Jaihan) are said to emerge from beneath a tomb, with the last two reappearing at the city of Rome (p. 90).
- The text is dated to the 25th day of Shaban, 1287 AH (c. 1870 CE), indicating the Malay manuscript's composition date (p. 245).

### Conclusion

Bishop Hose presents the *Hikayat Saif-al-Yezan* as a specimen of the Arabic romance tradition as it survived in Malay translation, demonstrating how pre-Islamic prophetic legends, jinn mythology, and the Swan Maiden motif were synthesised into a narrative that ultimately serves an Islamic moral purpose. The tale's internal chronology—set before the Prophet Muhammad's birth yet invoking his name anachronistically—reveals the layered accretion of oral and written traditions that characterise the genre.

## Context

- Bishop Hose (1845–1918) served as Bishop of Malacca and was an active collector of Malay literary and ethnographic materials; his son G. F. Hose (1863–1936) was the colonial ethnographer of British North Borneo who prepared the scholarly notes. The publication reflects the late-Victorian colonial practice of documenting "native" literary heritage through the Royal Asiatic Society's journal.
- The primary source is a Malay manuscript of the *Hikayat Saif-al-Yezan*, with the 1894 Singapore lithograph edition as a reference point; no specific archival collection is identified beyond the author's personal possession of the text.
