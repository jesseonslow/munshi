# The Straits Settlements and the Malay Peninsula: address by Mr. J.A. Kruyt, delivered before the Indian Society

J.A. Kruyt, a Dutch consular official stationed in Penang, delivered this address before the Indian Society in 1895, offering a comprehensive survey of the Straits Settlements and the Malay Peninsula from the vantage point of a European colonial administrator observing British expansion in real time. His overarching thesis is that the British have achieved remarkable administrative and economic success in Malaya through a combination of free-trade policy, the Residential system, and the strategic enlistment of Chinese labour, and that the Dutch should study these methods to reform their own colonial governance.

## Summary

Kruyt's address functions as both a geographical and political survey and a comparative colonial critique. He traces the British presence from Francis Light's founding of Penang in 1786 through Raffles's establishment of Singapore in 1819, the creation of the Straits Settlements as a Crown Colony in 1867, and the progressive extension of British protection over the Malay states from 1874 onward. The narrative is organised around the transition from a policy of non-intervention to one of "active advice, assistance and control," a shift he attributes to the anarchy produced by Chinese *kongsi* warfare over tin-mining rights and the inability of Malay chiefs to maintain order. Kruyt draws on official dispatches, legislative council debates, and the published works of contemporaries such as Skinner, Dilke, and Weld to construct a picture of rapid material transformation: the replacement of armed travel by carriage roads and railways, the elimination of slavery, and the growth of state revenues from negligible sums to millions of dollars within two decades.

The second half of the address turns to administrative comparison. Kruyt describes the structure of the Straits government—its Governor, Legislative and Executive Councils, Municipal bodies, and the Residents in the Native States—and contrasts this with what he perceives as Dutch rigidity and centralisation. He praises the British practice of retaining officials in the same post for long periods (citing Sir Hugh Low's eleven-year residency in Perak), the informality of relations between officers and natives, and the commercial pragmatism that underlies all British policy. His concluding argument is explicitly reformist: the Dutch should adopt greater decentralisation, grant their colonial officials more independence, and recognise that the era of maintaining imperial power "by artificial means" has passed.

The discussion that follows the address, in which Kruyt answers questions on the Isthmus of Krah canal, the future of Pulau Weh as a port, the legal status of the Chinese, and the possibility of diverting Sumatran trade from Penang to Dutch ports, reveals the practical concerns of the Dutch colonial audience and Kruyt's consistent emphasis on cheap freight, commercial convenience, and the centrality of the Straits as a node in global shipping.

### Key Findings

- Singapore's population grew from 150 Malays in 1819 to 185,000 in 1891; its import and export trade rose from $12,000,000 in 1823 to $200,000,000 in 1890 (p. 35).
- Perak's state revenue increased from $226,000 in 1875 to $2,500,000 in 1891; the state held a cash surplus of $2,000,000 on 1 January 1891 after repaying all debts and war costs to Britain (p. 36).
- Tin output from the Straits in 1892 reached 636,000 pikuls, valued at approximately $24,000,000, representing more than two-thirds of world production (p. 36).
- The Treaty of Pangkor (25 January 1874) placed Perak under British protection and established the Residential system; the first Resident, Mr. Birch, was murdered at Pasir Sala in November 1875, prompting a punitive expedition and the Sultan's banishment (pp. 31–32).
- The total population of the British portion of the Peninsula in 1891 was approximately 1,000,000: 512,342 in the three Settlements and 518,644 in the Native States including Johor (p. 39).
- The Straits Colony's own revenue rose from approximately $1,500,000 in 1868 to $4,000,000 in 1890, with a surplus of $2,250,000 at the end of 1891 (p. 41).

### Conclusion

Kruyt's definitive historical takeaway is that British success in Malaya rests not on military force but on a coherent administrative philosophy—decentralised, commercially oriented, and willing to harness Chinese enterprise under a light regulatory hand—and that the Dutch, as the "second colonial power," must emulate this model or risk being outpaced. He frames the British achievement as a model of what "decentralisation" can accomplish when officials are given autonomy and long tenure, and he closes by urging the Dutch colonial establishment to abandon artificial centralisation and build "forts in the hearts of the Natives" rather than in the landscape.

## Context

- Kruyt wrote as the Dutch Consular Official in Penang, a role that gave him direct observational access to British administration; his address was commissioned by the Indian Society, a Dutch colonial professional body in the Netherlands East Indies, and was intended to inform Dutch policymakers about their closest imperial rival.
- The primary sources informing the narrative include British official dispatches, Legislative Council debates, the published works of J.R. Skinner and Sir Charles Dilke, and Kruyt's own consular experience; the piece contributes to the historiography of comparative colonial administration in the late nineteenth century by providing a rare Dutch insider's account of the British Residential system at the height of its consolidation.
