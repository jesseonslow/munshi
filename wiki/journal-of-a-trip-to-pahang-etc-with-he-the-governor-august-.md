# Journal of a trip to Pahang etc. with H.E. the Governor, August 17th to 27th, 1889

W. Davison, Secretary of the Raffles Library and Museum in Singapore, published this field journal in 1889, recording a ten-day voyage with the Governor of the Straits Settlements along the east coast of the Malay Peninsula from Pulau Tioman to the Rumpin River. The text functions simultaneously as a natural history collecting log and a brief ethnographic-economic survey of the Pahang and Kelantan littoral, offering a snapshot of colonial administrative presence and local livelihoods at a moment when the east coast was still largely unexplored by Western naturalists.

## Summary

The journal traces the Governor's inspection tour from Kwala Pahang southward through Pekan, Tringganu, Kelantan, the Cheranting River, and the Rumpin River, with Davison and Mr. Ridley (of the Raffles Museum) accompanying as collectors. The narrative is organised around the sequence of landings and the specimens obtained at each stop, but it is interspersed with observations on local economies, population characteristics, coinage, and landscape that give the text considerable value as a colonial-era ethnographic record. The author's primary interest is zoological—birds, insects, reptiles, and marine invertebrates—but his remarks on the contrast between Tringganu (a fishing and manufacturing town) and Kelantan (an agricultural settlement) reveal a comparative framework typical of late-Victorian colonial reporting.

The trip's most significant natural history yield came at the Rumpin River, where Davison identified the site as the best collecting ground of the entire voyage, obtaining six burrowing lizards (*Liolepis*), four tree swifts, and an aberrant cuckoo. A notable absence runs through the account: no land snakes were encountered at any stop, a fact Davison flags as remarkable. The journal also records the Governor's own collecting activity (oyster shells at Tringganu) and the administrative choreography of the tour—Dato's being dispatched ahead to arrange receptions, the Governor's movements being the organising principle around which scientific work was fitted.

As a document, the journal is modest in scope but valuable for its specificity. It captures a coastline in transition: primary forest felled within miles of Pekan, new police stations being erected at Rumpin, and a pumice-stone beach suggesting recent volcanic activity. The text preserves details of local material culture—salted limes drying in the sun, tin coinage, the segregation of Chinese quarters in Tringganu—that would otherwise be lost in the more formal dispatches of the period.

### Key Findings

- The Rumpin River (fifty miles south of Cheranting, twenty miles north of the Endau River boundary between Johor and Pahang) yielded the trip's richest haul: six *Liolepis* burrowing lizards, four tree swifts (*Dendrochelidon comatus* and *D. longipennis*), and a male-female pair of *Phaenicophaes erythronotus* showing aberrant iris colouration (p. 90).
- Tringganu's economy was dominated by fishing (a fleet of over a hundred boats, each crewed by eight to ten men, observed on the morning of arrival) and the manufacture of sarongs and krisses, while Kelantan was primarily agricultural, growing paddy, coconuts, Indian corn, tobacco, and croton (pp. 86–88).
- Kelantan's coinage consisted solely of the silver dollar and small circular tin coins (five hundred equal one dollar); copper cents and fractional silver were not accepted, unlike the Pahang coinage (p. 89).
- No land snakes were encountered at any of the seven stops visited; only a few sea snakes were seen, a fact Davison singles out as remarkable (p. 90).
- The beaches between Cheranting and Rumpin were covered with pumice-stone, in many places about six inches across, indicating a volcanic source (p. 90).
- The Raffles Museum's only specimen of Raffles' hawk owl (*Minox scutulata*) was recovered from a dead bird that had flown into a room and been discarded before Davison's arrival at Pekan (p. 86).

### Conclusion

Davison's journal is best understood as a working document of a colonial naturalist whose scientific collecting was parasitic upon the Governor's administrative itinerary. Its lasting value lies not in any single discovery but in the composite picture it preserves: a coastline where primary forest was giving way to secondary growth, where local economies were sharply differentiated by river, and where the machinery of colonial governance (Dato's, police stations, reception protocols) was still being extended into territory that Western science had barely touched.

## Context

- Davison held the position of Secretary of the Raffles Library and Museum, Singapore, and the trip was undertaken in his capacity as the Governor's scientific companion; the journal was written for the JSBRAS as a record of specimens acquired for the Museum's collections (p. 90).
- The text reflects the standard colonial administrative framing of the period, in which the Governor's tour served as the occasion for both governance and knowledge-production, and in which local populations are described in terms of their economic utility and racial admixture rather than their own agency.
