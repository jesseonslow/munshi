# The Hikayat Abdullah, discourse of dissent

W. Linehan's 1948 article, published in the Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, argues that the ancient Kedah kingdom known as Langkasuka derived its name from *Lanka Asoka*—"The Island of Asoka"—in honour of the great Mauryan Buddhist emperor, and that the Malay etymological derivation offered in the Kedah Annals (from *suka*, "gladness") is a later folk etymology that obscured the original reference. The piece draws on Chinese, Tamil, Javanese, and Arab sources alongside the Kedah Annals to reconstruct the name's transmission and to explain why Arab writers consistently used "Kedah" rather than any variant of Langkasuka.

## Summary

Linehan's central problem is one of onomastic archaeology: how did the name Langkasuka come to be attached to the settlement at the base of Kedah Peak, and what does it actually mean? The Kedah Annals (*Hikayat Marong Mahawangsa*) offer an explanation—when the legendary founder Raja Marong Mahawangsa completed his audience hall, it was named Langkasuka because the construction was accompanied by feasting and rejoicing (*ber-suka-sukaan*). Linehan dismisses this as an etymological fiction, noting that the Annals' own author seemed to sense the derivation was unsatisfactory and laboured to justify it (pp. 120–121).

The alternative proposed is that the name is a compound of *Lanka* (island) and *Asoka*, the Mauryan emperor whose reign (c. 264–228 B.C.) saw the spread of Buddhism across South and Southeast Asia. Linehan marshals a chain of external attestations: the *History of the Liang Dynasty* (502–556 A.D.) records a Buddhist country called *Langgasu* or *Langga* whose people claimed a four-hundred-year history; I-tsing (c. 692 A.D.) mentions *Langkasu*; the Tanjore Inscription of 1030 A.D. uses *Ilangasogam* alongside *Kadaram*; Chau-Ju-kua (c. 1225) writes *Lang-ya-si*; and the *Nagarakrtagama* (1365) mentions both *Langkasuka* and *Kedah* (p. 121). The Tamil form *Ilangasokam*, with its long *a*, is endorsed by Professor K. A. Nilakanta Sastri as transparently supporting the *Lanka* + *Asoka* reading (p. 122).

A significant negative argument is built from the silence of Arab geographers. Ibn Khordadbeh (846), Sulaiman (c. 920), Ibn Muhalhal (c. 941), and Idrisi (1154) all use variants of "Kedah" and never mention Langkasuka. Linehan interprets this as evidence that the name had already fallen out of active use by the time Arab commercial networks reached the peninsula, and that the Annals' author—writing under the influence of Arab missionaries who had converted Kedah to Islam around 1474—simply did not know the original meaning and therefore invented the *suka* derivation (p. 122). The geographical element *Lanka* ("island") is supported by Quaritch Wales's reconstruction that Kedah Peak was an almost-island when the earliest Indian colonists arrived, the connecting neck of land between Bedong and Gurun still being submerged (p. 123).

### Key Findings

- The *History of the Liang Dynasty* (502–556 A.D.) records a Buddhist country *Langgasu/Langga* whose people stated the kingdom had existed for four hundred years, implying a foundation date around the first or second century A.D. (p. 121).
- The Tanjore Inscription of 1030 A.D. uses the Tamil form *Ilangasogam*, which Nilakanta Sastri confirms as a transparent rendering of *Lanka* + *Asoka*, with the initial *i* and *ga* (written *ka*) being standard Tamil transliteration features (p. 122).
- No Arab geographer between the ninth and twelfth centuries (Ibn Khordadbeh, Sulaiman, Ibn Muhalhal, Idrisi) uses any variant of Langkasuka; all employ forms of "Kedah," suggesting the name had already been lost from active regional usage before the Arab commercial presence (p. 122).
- The Pasai Annals record the destruction of Langkasuka around 1370 A.D., providing a terminus for the kingdom's political existence under that name (p. 121).
- Quaritch Wales's geographical analysis indicates that Kedah Peak was "almost an island" at the time of the earliest Indian settlement, with the low-lying land north and south still under the sea, lending plausibility to the *Lanka* ("island") element of the name (p. 123).
- Asoka's known missionary activity—sending monks Sona and Uttara to Suvannabhumi (Thaton, Burma) and his son Mahinda to Ceylon—establishes a plausible vector for Buddhist Indian colonists to reach the Kedah coast, where gold was already being exported (p. 123).

### Conclusion

Linehan concludes that Langkasuka was named by early Indian Buddhist colonists in honour of Emperor Asoka, and that the name's original referent was so thoroughly lost over the centuries that the Malay annalists, writing under Arab-Islamic influence, constructed a folk etymology from *suka* ("gladness") to explain a name they no longer understood. The persistence of the name in a tributary of the Perak River and in the northern Malay fairy-tale *Negeri alang-kah suka* ("Land of all one's wishes") are, in Linehan's view, only distant echoes of the original *Lanka Asoka* (p. 122).

## Context

- The article draws on the Sturrock romanisation of the Kedah Annals (J.R.A.S. Straits Branch, 1916), Low's 1849 translation, and the 1908 Bangkok reprint, alongside Chinese, Tamil, Javanese, and Arab source traditions; it represents a pre-war MBRAS tradition of etymological and onomastic inquiry into early Malay political history.
- Linehan's identification of *Lanka Asoka* as the source of Langkasuka has not become the standard scholarly consensus; subsequent work (notably by O.W. Wolters and others) has treated the name's origins as more complex, but the article remains a useful catalogue of the external attestations and a clear demonstration of the Annals' etymological difficulties.
