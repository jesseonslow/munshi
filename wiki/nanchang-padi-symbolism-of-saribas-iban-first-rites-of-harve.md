# Nanchang padi: symbolism of Saribas Iban first rites of harvest

Clifford Sather's 1977 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the *nanchang padi* rite as performed by a single Iban family at Kerangan Pinggai on the Paku river, Sarawak, during the 1976–77 farming season. Sather argues that this brief harvest ritual functions as a condensed symbolic formation through which fundamental Iban beliefs about rice souls, divine kinship, and agricultural protection are simultaneously expressed, enacted, and transmitted across generations.

## Summary

Sather's central concern is not merely to document a single ritual performance but to demonstrate how the *nanchang padi* rite operates as a node of interrelated meanings within the broader Iban agricultural symbolic order. Drawing on Victor Turner's concept of symbolic "condensation," he shows that the bound clumps of ripened padi—the *padi tanchang*—serve simultaneously as a physical shrine, a representation of the aggregate rice souls (*semengat padi*), a vehicle for their safe return to the longhouse, and a symbolic "maiden" embodying latent fecundity. The act of tying the padi together, performed by the senior woman of the family, is understood as the earthly enactment of an action carried out on a spiritual plane by the agricultural gods Anda Mara and Simpulang Gana, thereby reaffirming the family's sacred kinship with the divine.

The article is grounded in Sather's direct observation of the rite as performed by Nisi anak Changgai, mother of the longhouse headman Renang, who agreed to reenact the ceremony two days after its actual performance so that each stage could be explained and recorded. This methodological choice allows Sather to present the ritual in its full sequence—selection of the padi, insertion of the *tangga samengat*, tying with red cotton and *kikat padi* creeper, installation of the *serepak* protective device, anointing, waving with a cockerel, and the final casting of panicles to the spirits of predation—while also capturing the performer's own interpretive framework. The ethnographic detail is enriched by the specific social and economic context of the Saribas Iban, who by the 1970s had largely shifted from hill rice to pepper, rubber, and illipe-nut cultivation, making the persistence of rice ritual all the more significant as a marker of cultural continuity rather than economic necessity.

Sather uses this case to challenge the tendency in the anthropological literature to treat Iban hill-rice farming as a monolithic "way of life." He demonstrates that while the symbolic order—padi souls, the *serepak*, the *tangga samengat*, left-right symbolism, divine personification—is shared broadly across Saribas Iban communities, its actual deployment is refracted through the economic corporateness of the individual *bilek* family. Each family maintains its own body of prayers, interdictions, and ritual paraphernalia, and may alter or ignore elements as circumstances require. The symbolic system remains intact even as the economic activity it once animated has been largely abandoned.

### Key Findings

- The *nanchang padi* rite was performed on 25 January 1977, exactly 153 days after sowing, and was completed in approximately forty minutes excluding travel time (p. 150, 153).
- The *padi tanchang* is constructed from three clumps of standing grain (*tiga pun padi*) bound with red-dyed cotton thread and *kikat padi* (*Lygodium splendens*), a creeper whose tendrils coil counter-clockwise, reinforcing the rite's association with lefthanded symbolism (pp. 159–160).
- The *serepak*—a porcupine quill crossed with a sharpened sliver of *temeyang* bamboo—serves as a protective device against both natural predation and the curses (*nepang*) of *orang tau tepang* (witchcraft practitioners), whose envious attention is considered most dangerous at harvest time (pp. 160–161).
- Violation of the *pemali* (interdiction on field entry) following *nanchang* carries a fine of one hen, one knife blade (*duku*), and *sigi jabir* (one Malaysian dollar), the smallest unit in the graded scale of Iban adat fines (p. 166).
- At Kerangan Pinggai, only one of six *bilek* families still grew rice; pepper holdings ranged from 200 to over 600 vines per family, and individual families realized as much as M$3,000–4,000 from illipe-nut sales in a favourable fruiting season (pp. 147–148, n. 3–4).
- In a highly exceptional case recorded during the 1976–77 season, a farmer named Bugak anak Duat was compelled to untie and re-perform *nanchang* after nearly treading on a hamadryad snake (*belalang*) directly in his path upon leaving the field—the only such instance anyone at Kerangan Pinggai could recall (p. 164).

### Conclusion

Sather's definitive takeaway is that the Iban agricultural symbolic order is not a fixed system bound to a particular economic practice but a flexible repertoire of beliefs and symbols that individual families draw upon, reinterpret, or set aside according to their own circumstances. The persistence of *nanchang padi* among the Saribas Iban, even after the near-total abandonment of hill rice in favour of cash crops, demonstrates that the symbolic order remains culturally significant precisely because it is not economically compelled—it is called forth by families as a matter of choice, identity, and spiritual obligation rather than subsistence necessity.

## Context

- The study is based on Sather's fieldwork at Kerangan Pinggai (Rumah Ragai) on the Paku river, Second Division, Sarawak, during the 1976–77 farming season, with primary reliance on the detailed knowledge and cooperative reenactment of Nisi anak Changgai and Renang anak Bryon (p. 153, n. 2).
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the ethnographic literature on Iban agriculture by shifting focus from the technical and comparative description of swidden systems (Freeman 1955; Jensen 1965) to the internal logic of ritual symbolism as it is actually deployed by a single family in a context of profound economic change, thereby complicating earlier characterizations of hill rice as "the Iban way of life" (p. 169).
