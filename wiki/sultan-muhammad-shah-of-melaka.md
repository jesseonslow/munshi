# Sultan Muhammad Shah of Melaka

Sultan Muhammad Shah of Melaka, also known as Sri Maharaja, was the third ruler of the Malacca Sultanate, reigning from 1424 to approximately 1444. He succeeded his father, Megat Iskandar Shah, and is attested in the Chinese Veritable Records as *Hsi-li-ma-ha-la-che*, a name Wang Gungwu identifies with the Malay title Sri Maharaja. His reign marked the final period of direct Chinese imperial engagement with Malacca, and his extended visit to the Ming court in 1433–35 stands as the last royal audience of a Malacca king at Peking. [[1, pp. 21–22]](#ref-1)

## Succession and Confirmation at the Ming Court

Sultan Muhammad Shah's accession is recorded in the *Yung-lo Shih-lu* for 20 April 1424, when he brought his consort and chiefs to court at Peking with a tribute of local goods, explicitly because "his father had died and he had newly succeeded to the throne." The audience was granted personally by Emperor Yung-lo, who had already met both Parameswara in 1411 and Megat Iskandar Shah in 1414, making him the only Ming emperor to receive all three successive kings of Malacca. Yung-lo gave the new ruler a farewell banquet on 30 April and departed on his final northern campaign two days later, dying while on campaign three months hence. [[1, pp. 21]](#ref-1)

The succession was not without administrative complexity. Between his father's death and his own arrival at Peking, Sultan Muhammad Shah dispatched a mission to the newly enthroned Emperor Hung-hsi in December 1424, and another to Hung-hsi's successor, Emperor Hsuan-te, in June 1426. These missions maintained the tributary relationship during a period of rapid dynastic change at the Chinese court. [[1, p. 21]](#ref-1)

## The Extended Visit of 1433–35 and the End of Chinese Involvement

In 1433, Sultan Muhammad Shah undertook a second and far more prolonged visit to China, travelling with Cheng Ho's seventh and final expedition. He brought his family, his younger brother Raden Bala, and a retinue of 228 persons. The mission arrived at Nanking on 28 November 1433 and the king had an audience at Peking on 26 May 1434. He remained in China until at least April 1435, when he departed from Canton, meaning he was absent from Malacca for at least two and a half years. He was still in China when Emperor Hsuan-te died in January 1435. [[1, pp. 16]](#ref-1)

Wang Gungwu interprets this extended stay as a consequence of the withdrawal of Chinese naval power from the region. After 1434, no Chinese fleets sailed to Malacca again, and no other Malacca king ever visited China. Sultan Muhammad Shah was the last to make the journey, and the absence of available Chinese shipping to return him home prolonged his stay. Wang Gungwu suggests the ruler must have recognised the change in Chinese policy and understood that Malacca would henceforth have to defend itself against Siam and other threats. [[1, pp. 21–22]](#ref-1)

## Legacy and Documentation

The identification of Sultan Muhammad Shah rests on the convergence of Chinese, Malay, and Portuguese sources. In the *Sejarah Melayu* (1536), he appears as Raja Tengah, Sultan Muhammed Shah, son of Raja Kechil Besar, with a reign of 57 years; in *Bustan-al-Salatin* (1638), he is listed as Radin or Raja Tengah, son of the second ruler. The Portuguese *Suma Oriental* (1512–15) does not name him directly but records the succession of rulers that places him between the second and fourth kings. [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1)

Wang Gungwu's 1968 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* was a direct intervention in the Society's own historiographical record, correcting the earlier identification by R.O. Winstedt, whose 1935 *History of Malaya* had appeared in the same journal. By demonstrating that Emperor Yung-lo personally received three distinct Malacca kings, Wang Gungwu restored Sultan Muhammad Shah to his rightful place as the third ruler and argued that all history books in use in the country should be corrected accordingly. [[1, pp. 11]](#ref-1)
