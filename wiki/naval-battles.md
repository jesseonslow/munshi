# Naval battles

## The Orienting Lede

Naval battles in the waters of the Malay Peninsula and the surrounding straits constitute a distinctive chapter in the maritime history of Southeast Asia, shaped by the convergence of indigenous seafaring traditions, the ambitions of European colonial powers, and the strategic importance of the shipping lanes that linked the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea. The Singapore Strait, the Johore River, and the approaches to Malacca served as recurring theatres of engagement from the sixteenth century through the early nineteenth, when Portuguese, Dutch, and British naval forces confronted local fleets — most notably those of Aceh and its allies — in contests that determined control of trade routes and the security of colonial outposts. These engagements were not merely military events; they reflected the economic stakes of the pepper and spice trade, the diplomatic entanglements between Malay polities, and the evolving technology of naval warfare in tropical waters. The archival record, while fragmentary, reveals a pattern of repeated confrontations in which numerical inferiority was offset by tactical innovation, and in which the loss of documentary evidence — whether through the destruction of manuscripts or the failure of official despatches to reach their destination — has left significant gaps in the historical narrative.

## Scope and Historical Definition

The term "naval battle" in the context of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo encompasses a range of engagements, from large fleet actions involving dozens of vessels to smaller skirmishes between individual craft. The most extensively documented of these is the sequence of three naval engagements fought between the Portuguese and the Achinese off Singapore in the 1570s, the first of which took place on 1 January 1577 during the captaincy of Mathias de Albuquerque as Captain-Major of the Seas of Malacca [[1, pp. 5–6]](#ref-1). MacGregor demonstrated that these battles had been entirely absent from Malayan historiography since 1800, a lacuna attributable to their omission from Diogo do Couto's *Decades*, the standard printed source for Portuguese history in Asia [[1, p. 5]](#ref-1). The extant version of the *Decades* makes no mention of happenings at Malacca between 1575 and 1580, a gap first noted by Tiele in 1881 [[1, p. 5]](#ref-1).

A second, later engagement recovered from the record is a naval action fought in the Macassar Strait on 26 July 1806, in which two British vessels under Admiral Sir Thomas Troubridge's command, based at Penang, engaged a Dutch squadron of four ships [[2, p. 202]](#ref-2). This battle, too, had been absent from standard naval histories such as Parkinson's *War in the Eastern Seas*, and was recovered by Tregonning from an early issue of the *Prince of Wales Island Gazette* [[2, p. 202]](#ref-2). A third reference, to two Dutch-Portuguese sea-fights, is attested in the title of a paper by J. V. Mills published in 1938, though the full text of that article is not available in the present record [[3]](#ref-3).

The geographic scope of these engagements is defined by the narrow waterways of the region: the Singapore Strait, the Johore River, the approaches to Malacca, and the Macassar Strait. These were not open-ocean battles but confined actions in which the proximity of shore, the presence of sandbanks, and the seasonal winds played decisive tactical roles [[1, pp. 8–10]](#ref-1).

## The Battle of 1 January 1577

The first of the three Portuguese-Achinese engagements off Singapore is the most thoroughly reconstructed in the available record. MacGregor drew on three sources: the printed work of Padre Francisco de Sousa, S.J. (*O Oriente conquistado a Jesu Christo pelos Padres da Companhia de Jesus*, Lisbon, 1710), which contains the account of the Jesuit Amador da Costa; a manuscript biography of Mathias de Albuquerque preserved in the Public Library and District Archives at Évora (Codex CXV/1–13); and the *Historia dos servicos com martirio de Luis Monteiro Coutinho*, written by Manuel Godinho de Eredia and dated 1615, held in the National Library at Lisbon [[1, p. 5]](#ref-1).

The strategic context was one of sustained Achinese pressure on Portuguese Malacca. Between 1564 and 1585, Achinese war fleets were very active in Malayan waters, having attacked the capital of Johore in 1564, besieged Malacca in 1568, 1573, and 1575, and by the late 1570s were cruising freely in the approaches to the Singapore Straits, impeding the passage of shipping and able to take refuge in the Johore River [[1, pp. 6–7]](#ref-1). The Portuguese response was the dispatch of Mathias de Albuquerque, a collateral descendant of Afonso de Albuquerque and a man of high social position, who left Lisbon on 2 March 1576 with two vessels, the *Santa Catherina* and the *Sao Jorge* [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). The passage was troubled: the crews suffered badly from sickness, and the *Sao Jorge* foundered near Mozambique, though all hands and stores were reportedly saved [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1).

Albuquerque arrived at Malacca in December 1576, a date supported by meteorological reasoning: vessels sailing from the Mozambique Channel to Malacca in the second half of the year normally set out in September to catch the northerly winds off western India and the westerly winds blowing between Ceylon and the entrance of the Straits of Malacca, reaching their destination in September or October [[1, pp. 9–10]](#ref-1). A longer stay at Mozambique would have made a December arrival in the same year impossible without a mid-1577 departure, which neither source supports [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1).

Soon after reaching Malacca, Albuquerque set out for the Singapore Straits to meet the annual trading vessel from China and escort it to Malacca [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1). His fleet comprised, according to the certificate of Antonio Moniz Barreto, two galleasses, one galleon, three galleys, and six foists — twelve vessels in all [[1, p. 17]](#ref-1). The *Vida* gives the same total but with a slightly different breakdown: one galleon, two galleasses, three galleys, three galliots, and three brigantines, and names the captains of each vessel [1, p. 11, n. 33]. Da Costa's account gives thirteen sail: three high-sided vessels, three galleys, and seven foists [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1).

The Achinese fleet, sighted on the eve of the feast of the Circumcision (31 December 1576), numbered 150 sail according to da Costa and Moniz Barreto's certificate, of which 40 were royal galleys with two rows of oars [[1, pp. 12–13]](#ref-1). The *Vida* gives a lower total of 115 vessels, broken down as 36 royal galleys, 50 galliots, foists, and launches, and 29 light craft [1, p. 12, n. 38]. The Achinese were commanded by Ciri maralela (Sri Maharaja Lela) and contained 10,000 fighting men and much artillery, accompanied by Raiaale (Raja Ali), who was married to a daughter of the Sultan of Acheh [1, p. 12, n. 39]. Eredia adds that the soldiers on board were Turks, Arabs, Deccanis, and Achinese [1, p. 12, n. 39].

The disparity in armament was striking. The Achinese galleys were fitted with spheres and basilisks — muzzle-loading cannon of moderate calibre — which, according to da Costa, fired a shot as large as a man's head, while the Portuguese possessed no more than two spheres [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1). The Achinese had instructions from their king not to fight the Portuguese in the open sea but only along the shore, and the galleys were accordingly stationed in order [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1).

The engagement itself lasted from morning until after midday, a bombardment of six hours' duration, during which the Portuguese fleet could not reach the enemy, who lay to windward [[1, pp. 13–14]](#ref-1). The turning point came when the wind shifted to the Portuguese side. Albuquerque had the trumpets sounded, all sails unfurled, and put his own galley into the midst of the enemy fleet, pressing them until they became confused, each vessel seeking its own salvation [[1, p. 15]](#ref-1). Albuquerque then made chase of the enemy flagship, and when the bows of his galley were near its stern and he was in terms of grappling with it, the wind calmed and the enemy gained safety [[1, p. 15]](#ref-1).

The Achinese left behind three galleys, including the Vice-Admiral's vessel, which carried all the powder and the most noble men of the Achinese. The captain of that galley, seeing that he could not escape, cut his hair and cried *amoucou* — that is, "let us all die" — and his men, forming themselves into one body, charged like lions but were killed to the last [[1, p. 15]](#ref-1). Portuguese casualties were 13 killed according to da Costa, or 10 to 12 killed and many wounded according to Moniz Barreto's certificate [[1, pp. 15]](#ref-1). Achinese losses were 1,600 killed and captured per da Costa, or 300 Moslems dying per the certificate [[1, pp. 15]](#ref-1).

In a follow-up operation, Albuquerque sent dom Henrique Bendarra — a member of the royal family of Tidore who had become Christian and been made *bendahara* of Malacca by the Portuguese — with two galleys and nine *bantins* to deal with four Achinese galleys and one *lanchara* in the Kedah River. Bendarra burned the *lanchara* and one galley and captured the other three, which were brought back to Malacca loaded with much pepper and other merchandise, including twenty-three pieces of artillery, three of which bore the Portuguese arms [[1, p. 18]](#ref-1).

## The Macassar Strait Action of 1806

The naval engagement of 26 July 1806 in the Macassar Strait represents a later phase of maritime conflict in the region, now involving British and Dutch forces in the context of the Napoleonic Wars. The action was fought by H.M.S. *Greyhound* (Captain Elphinstone) and H.M.S. *Harrier* (Captain Troubridge), part of Admiral Sir Thomas Troubridge's fleet based at Penang, against a Dutch squadron of four vessels [[2, p. 203]](#ref-2).

The British employed a raking tactic that Tregonning notes as a "Nelsonian touch": the two British ships subjected the leading Dutch frigate to fire from both sides simultaneously [[2, p. 203]](#ref-2). The result was the capture of the frigate *Pallas* (36 guns), the armed ship *Victoria* (18 guns), and the *Batavia* (10 guns), while the corvette *William* escaped [[2, p. 203]](#ref-2). British losses were minimal: one seaman killed and six wounded [[2, p. 203]](#ref-2). The captured *Pallas* was commissioned as H.M.S. *Macassar* under Captain Troubridge, the admiral's son [[2, p. 203]](#ref-2).

The obscurity of this battle in standard naval histories is attributable to a chain of documentary failure: the despatches left Penang on the *Blenheim*, which foundered off Madagascar, likely consigning the prize-money and official reports to obscurity [[2, p. 204]](#ref-2). Tregonning recovered the account from an early issue of the *Prince of Wales Island Gazette* [[2, p. 202]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The investigation of naval battles in Malayan waters by the Society's journal reveals a pattern of recovery from documentary silence, driven by the loss or inaccessibility of primary sources. MacGregor's 1956 article on the 1577 battle was explicitly motivated by the gap identified by Tiele in 1881: the absence of events at Malacca between 1575 and 1580 in Couto's *Decades* [[1, p. 5]](#ref-1). MacGregor's method was one of critical collation, drawing on three independent witnesses — two in manuscript, one in print — and resolving their contradictions through internal consistency and meteorological reasoning [[1, pp. 8–10]](#ref-1). The key historiographical contribution was the demonstration that the battle had been entirely absent from Malayan historiography since 1800, not because it was unimportant, but because the standard printed source simply did not contain it [[1, p. 5]](#ref-1).

The primary archival collections underpinning this research are the Évora Public Library and District Archives (Codex CXV/1–13, *Vida de Mathias de Albuquerque*; Codex CXVI/1–26, *Descripcam dos cercos de Malacca*); the National Library, Lisbon, Fundo Geral no. 414 (Eredia manuscript); and the Arquivo Nacional da Torre do Tombo, S. Lourenco 4 and Livraria no. 320 [[1, pp. 19–20]](#ref-1). The Eredia manuscript, in particular, had been thought lost without trace until its recovery, and MacGregor's work drew attention to its internal inconsistencies, including three date discrepancies and a probable confusion between the battle of 1 January 1577 and a later combat off Changi Point [[1, pp. 8–9]](#ref-1).

Tregonning's 1959 note on the 1806 Macassar Strait action illustrates a different mode of recovery: the rediscovery of a contemporary newspaper account in a colonial gazette, where the event had been recorded at first hand but subsequently lost to the broader historical record due to the loss of official despatches [[2, pp. 202–204]](#ref-2). The Mills paper of 1938 on two Dutch-Portuguese sea-fights, while attested in the journal's record, is not available in full in the present archive [[3]](#ref-3).

The shifting priorities of the Society's literature are evident in the transition from the sixteenth-century Portuguese-Achinese confrontations, which required the reconstruction of events from scattered manuscript sources in European archives, to the early nineteenth-century British-Dutch engagements, which could be recovered from colonial newspapers and official records. In both cases, the common thread is the fragility of the documentary record and the necessity of archival detective work to restore forgotten events to the historical narrative.
