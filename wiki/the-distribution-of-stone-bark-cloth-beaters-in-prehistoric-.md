# The distribution of stone bark-cloth beaters in prehistoric times

G. de G. Sieveking's 1956 article in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the typology, distribution, and cultural attribution of cross-hatched stone bark cloth beaters from the Malay Peninsula, arguing that this tool type is a diagnostic marker of the Malayan Neolithic cultures and that its wider distribution into Sumatra and North Borneo reflects the reach of those prehistoric cultural spheres.

## Summary

Sieveking begins by establishing the ethnographic context: bark cloth is still manufactured across Malaya, Indo-China, Borneo, and parts of Indonesia, and the instruments used to beat the bark share a common functional feature—a raised, serrated working surface. He then turns to the prehistoric stone variant, a pebble or rock fragment pecked into a roughly cylindrical form with one end broken flat and cross-hatched with V-shaped grooves cut at right angles, producing a chequer-board pattern of truncated pyramidal blocks. The design is simple but functionally specific: the broad working surface and the gaps between raised areas maximise the efficiency of separating bark fibres without severing them entirely. Sieveking distinguishes this Malayan type from the flat, squared, parallel-grooved hammerstones used in modern Central Celebes, which represent a different design tradition with its own prehistoric antecedents in the Philippines and Celebes.

The core of the article is a distributional and chronological analysis. Sieveking compiles thirteen recorded examples from the Malay Peninsula, all from the southern portion (Kelantan, Pahang, Perak, Selangor), and demonstrates that four are found in unambiguous Neolithic associations—including one recovered beside a Neolithic skeleton at Gua Cha, Kelantan, alongside cord-impressed pottery and quadrangular stone axes. A further example from Changkat Menteri, Perak, was found in close contiguity to an Iron Age cist grave, indicating the tool's persistence into the early historic period. Modern specimens of the same design are known from Sipora (Mentawi Islands, Sumatra) and from the Kelabit of North Borneo, though Harrisson considered it certain that none of the Bornean examples were in use after 1914.

Sieveking's broader argument is that the restricted distribution of this type within the Peninsula, combined with its presence in Sumatra and Borneo, points to an extension of Malayan prehistoric cultural influence into western Indonesia during the period when these tools were still in active use. He explicitly links this evidence to Heine-Geldern's theoretical framework for early population movements in South-East Asia, which posits a mainland-to-island direction of influence for the pre-Austronesian cultural sphere characterised by cord-impressed pottery and polished stone tools.

### Key Findings

- Thirteen cross-hatched stone bark cloth beaters are recorded from the Malay Peninsula, all from the southern half (Kelantan, Pahang, Perak, Selangor); four of these bear a broad hafting groove just above the working face (p. 82).
- The tools measure 90–125 mm in length with a working surface diameter of 60–80 mm; the body is roughly cylindrical with an abruptly rounded butt (p. 79).
- Four examples are associated with Neolithic assemblages; one was found beside a Neolithic skeleton at Gua Cha, Kelantan, with cord-impressed pottery and quadrangular stone axes (pp. 82–83).
- A single example from Changkat Menteri, Perak, was found with an iron tool outside an Iron Age cist grave, indicating use into the early historic period (p. 82).
- Modern parallels exist in Sumatra (a wooden specimen from Sipora, Mentawi Islands, in the Raffles Museum collection) and North Borneo (stone examples from the Kelabit in the Sarawak Museum), none in use after 1914 (p. 83).
- The Malayan cross-hatched type is typologically distinct from the flat, squared, parallel-grooved hammerstones of modern Central Celebes and the "Horned Bark Cloth Beater" of the Philippines, which represent separate design traditions (pp. 80–81).

### Conclusion

Sieveking concludes that the cross-hatched stone bark cloth beater is a clearly defined Malayan type of restricted distribution and definite cultural significance, decisively associated with the Malayan Neolithic cultures. Its presence in Sumatra and North Borneo, alongside the broader pattern of cord-impressed pottery and quadrangular polished stone tools, provides material support for Heine-Geldern's model of early mainland-to-island cultural diffusion in South-East Asia.

## Context

- The article draws on museum collections in Singapore (Raffles Museum), Perak (Perak Museum), and Kuala Lumpur (National Museum), as well as excavation records from Gua Cha, Kelantan (1954) and earlier sites documented by Evans, Tweedie, and Linehan.
- Historiographically, the piece sits within the mid-century tradition of Malayan prehistoric archaeology that sought to define cultural "horizons" through diagnostic artifact types, and it explicitly engages with Heine-Geldern's 1932 population-movement theory as a framework for interpreting the distributional evidence.
