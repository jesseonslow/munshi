# Bulls

Bull-fighting in Kelantan, known locally as *pelaga lembu*, is a traditional Malay sport in which two bulls are pitted against each other in a fenced enclosure, distinct from the Spanish spectacle in that the bull remains a free agent throughout the contest. The practice is concentrated in the state of Kelantan on the east coast of the Malay Peninsula, where it has been documented as the principal form of organised animal combat since at least the late nineteenth century, and is acknowledged by the Kelantanese themselves as a borrowed sport introduced from Siam, where it remains extremely popular [[1, p. 74]](#ref-1). The fighting bull is of Zebu stock, a small, compactly built animal standing less than four feet to the shoulder, with a large dewlap and a hump approximately nine inches high upon the withers [[1, p. 74]](#ref-1). Beyond its sporting function, the institution of the bull-ring has served as a site of considerable economic activity, encompassing the importation of animals from Siam, the training and preparation of fighters, and a substantial betting culture that, by the late 1920s, had become a significant feature of Kelantanese social life [[1, pp. 78–81]](#ref-1).

### Scope and Historical Definition

The Kelantanese fighting bull belongs to the same stock as the Zebu or humped ox of India, and the animal is characterised by a smooth coat that shows a fine sheen when in fighting condition [[1, p. 74]](#ref-1). Brown, writing in 1928 from direct observation of the Kota Baharu and Kubang Krian rings, records a considerable variety of colouration, listing at least fourteen distinct types: *jebat* (ordinary black with perhaps a slight show of white under the belly), *kumbang* (jet-black, the deepest possible black with no relieving colour), *laka* (black with a little brownish red under the shoulders and belly), *buah tal* (the commonest colour, black on the hind-quarters and brown or grey on neck and shoulders), *tapong* (black with large splashes of white), *arau* (black with small white spots), *kēlēmbak* (a dirty grey), *lasat* (the lightest of all colours), *kijang* (the reddish colour of the barking-deer), *lipan tanah* (the same as *kijang* but with black on neck and shoulders), *kubing* (the same as *kijang* but with a black tail), *sela* (the darkest of all the reds, really a brown), and *kabul* (a grey body with generally brownish shoulders) [[1, pp. 74–75]](#ref-1).

A.H. Hill, writing in 1952 from post-war fieldwork in Kelantan, provides a somewhat different and expanded taxonomy of colouration, recording the terms *kumbang*, *jebat*, *gedabu*, *sela*, *kubing*, *kijang*, *langsat*, and mixed types [[2, p. 20]](#ref-2). Anker Rentse, in a short note published in 1931, lists thirty-four Kelantanese Malay names for bullocks classified by their coat colour, ranging from simple single-colour animals to complex multi-coloured patterns involving spots, patches, and mixed hues [[3, pp. 143–145]](#ref-3). Rentse notes that his list is an extract of earlier notes written in Kelantanese Jawi by the grandfather of Nik Man, a teacher at Kampong Tengku Temenggong, Kota Baharu, and that some spellings may be uncertain [[3, p. 145]](#ref-3). The variation between these three inventories—Brown's fourteen, Hill's expanded list, and Rentse's thirty-four—suggests that the colour nomenclature was both more extensive and more fluid than any single observer could capture in a single season of observation.

The morphology of the horns is of equal importance in the assessment of a fighting bull. Brown records that the favourite horns are those which are closely set (*pekalan sempit*), long and upright (*panjang bediri*), as such a bull is likely to get inside the guard of an adversary whose horns are wide apart (*pekalan luas*) and curve outwards [[1, p. 75]](#ref-1). Hill, writing twenty-four years later, catalogues a more elaborate set of horn types: *dogol*, *debek*, *chakar*, *molek*, *sodo'*, *kupit*, *kerutut*, and *kerutok* [[2, p. 20]](#ref-2). The development of this technical vocabulary between the two accounts indicates a maturing of the sport's internal taxonomy over the interwar and post-war periods.

### Fighting Styles and the Ritual of the Match

Brown identifies two broad classes of fighter. The first consists of bulls for whom the fight is a horning match pure and simple, subdivided into *penikam* ("stabbers"), who use the upward stroke known as *rembas*, and *penebas*, who employ a downward and sideways stroke analogous to a man cutting undergrowth [[1, pp. 75–76]](#ref-1). The second class comprises bulls for whom the battle is mainly a test of speed, strength, and endurance, subdivided into three types: *pandai* (the hustler who sets the pace and drives the adversary against the fence), *berani* (the slow but sure type who hangs on and parries until the opponent's wind gives out), and *pemulas* (the interlocker who twists the adversary's head while pushing him back) [[1, pp. 76–77]](#ref-1). Hill records a corresponding but differently named set of fighting techniques: *tandok*, *ambil rusok*, *pulas*, *bembing*, *gonggong*, and *tanggang* [[2, p. 20]](#ref-2).

The preparation of a bull for the ring involved a combination of practical and magical elements. Brown records that the favourite specific for adding weight was Guinness' stout, administered daily for ten days, and that the bull's grass had to be of special quality, sometimes fetched from a mile or two away [[1, p. 78]](#ref-1). The bull had to be exercised daily and kept under constant watch to prevent flies and, more seriously, to guard against the practice of doping the adversary's animal, which Brown notes was not obsolete even in the 1920s [[1, p. 79]](#ref-1). In addition to these practical measures, the soothsayer (*nujum*) played a considerable part in the preliminaries, determining from a compass-shaped chart the station at which the bull should take up at the outset of the fight according to the day of the week [[1, p. 79]](#ref-1). Brown observes that the book-makers based their opening prices on the soothsayer's prognostications [[1, p. 79]](#ref-1).

The ritual of the fight itself followed a fixed procedure. The two bulls were led to a mark in the centre of the ring, held by cords passed through their nose-rings, and loosed simultaneously when both noses were over the mark [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1). The trainers squatted inside the ring, about five yards from the bulls, and exhorted their charges by signs and word of mouth [[1, p. 82]](#ref-1). A typical *penikam* fight lasted little over five minutes, whereas a contest between two *berani* bulls could continue for nearly an hour [[1, p. 83]](#ref-1). The fight was not concluded until the victor had taken up his stand at the central mark and the vanquished bull, having been caught and led back, had been given the opportunity to re-engage; if the defeated bull refused to fight in the second or third round, the result stood [[1, pp. 82–83]](#ref-1).

Hill's 1952 account records a particularly dramatic contest between two *molek*-horned bulls, "Bingkai Badang" and "Langsat Mata," in which the former lifted his opponent entirely off the ground [[2, p. 20]](#ref-2). He also documents the sympathetic magic practices once employed to weaken an opponent's bull, including the smearing of the bull's head and neck with a *pakan* made from pounded leaves of *kekabu* and *medang pulut*, and the application to the horns of a *peruang* made from human milk mixed with the milk of a tigress [[2, p. 20]](#ref-2). Brown had recorded similar practices in 1928, noting that the *pemedeh*—preparations smearing the horns to add to the pain of wounds—had been prohibited by the time of his observation, and that only preparations producing no detectable physical effect, however great their magical properties, were now permitted [[1, pp. 79–80]](#ref-1).

### Economic and Administrative Framework

The economics of Kelantan bull-fighting were substantial. Proven fighting bulls imported from Siam, particularly from Petani, could cost anything up to $300 by the time they reached Kota Baharu, while an untried animal should not exceed $80 [[1, p. 78]](#ref-1). Stakes (*tarohan dalam*) between rival owners had historically reached $1,000 a side, but by 1928 a stake of $250 was considered "big money," with matches between untried bulls typically staked at $25 to $100 a side [[1, pp. 78–79]](#ref-1). Ring-side betting (*tarohan luar*) had become a major feature of the sport, and the book-makers (*tok jora*) operated with remarkable informality, occupying no regular pitch, giving no tickets, and recording no bets [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1). Brown records a local commentary on the ways of book-makers that captures the casual and sometimes fraudulent nature of the practice, noting that the *orang darat* (country people) were frequently victimised by scoundrels who borrowed smart suits of silks and a couple of $5 notes, collected bets at slightly better prices than the more reputable members of the fraternity, and then disappeared into the crowd [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1).

The physical infrastructure of the sport was modest. The Kota Baharu bull-ring (*bong*) measured approximately 60 yards square, enclosed by a high outer palisade to shut out the view of those who had not paid admission, while the ring itself was enclosed by a low fence round which the spectators stood [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1). Hill, writing in 1952, describes the Kota Bharu arena as sixty yards square, enclosed by stout bamboo posts with the top horizontal rail approximately eight feet from the ground [[2, p. 20]](#ref-2). The two principal permanent rings were at Kota Baharu and at Kubang Krian, the property of the Raja Muda, about four miles from Kota Baharu; temporary rings were erected at Tumpat, Pasir Puteh, Pasir Mas, and Kuala Krai in honour of the Sultan's birthday celebrations [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1). Admission (*pitis bong*) was generally 10 cents, rising to 20 cents for a special fight [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1).

The administrative status of bull-fighting in Kelantan was subject to change. Hill records that the Sultan of Kelantan had made bull-fighting and all animal contests illegal before the war, primarily to curb gambling, and that the ban was lifted in August 1950 at the suggestion of the Kelantan University Appeal Fund Committee [[2, p. 20]](#ref-2). This places the resumption of the sport in the immediate post-war period, at a time when several traditional entertainments were in flux due to the growing influence of cinema and other modern forms of recreation [[2, p. 20]](#ref-2). Brown, writing in 1928, makes no mention of any prohibition, and his account suggests a sport operating openly and with considerable public participation, though he does note that the practice of doping the adversary's bull, while not obsolete, was becoming less common as the sport grew "a far more gentlemanly affair than it used to be" [[1, p. 79]](#ref-1).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Kelantan bull-fighting spans a period of over thirty-five years, from Brown's 1928 account to Muhammad Taib Osman's 1964 text on the rules of the Kelantan bull-fight, and reveals a shifting emphasis from broad ethnographic description to more specialised linguistic and regulatory documentation. Brown's article, written from the perspective of a Malay States Civil Service officer embedded in the ring-side culture, remains the most comprehensive single account of the sport's social, economic, and ritual dimensions [[1, pp. 74–83]](#ref-1). Its evidential basis is entirely the author's personal observation and informal interviews with local experts, trainers, and book-makers during his posting, and it draws on no formal archival collections [[1]](#ref-1). The piece is notable for its sympathetic tone and its explicit argument that Kelantan bull-fighting is fundamentally distinct from the "cruel" spectacle of Spanish bullfighting because the bull remains a free agent throughout [[1, p. 76]](#ref-1).

Rentse's 1931 note, though brief, represents a different scholarly approach: a systematic linguistic inventory of colour terminology, cross-referenced with R. J. Wilkinson's account of similar water-buffalo colour names from Negri Sembilan and with Brown's own article [[3, p. 145]](#ref-3). The fact that Rentse's list of thirty-four names substantially exceeds Brown's fourteen suggests either that the nomenclature was more extensive than Brown had recorded, or that the two observers were working from different informants and different seasons of the ring. Hill's 1952 survey, while treating bull-fighting as one of four principal forms of Malay recreation, provides the most detailed post-war account and is grounded in personal fieldwork supplemented by comparison with earlier colonial-era publications by Brown, Clifford, and Swettenham [[2, p. 20]](#ref-2). The article was received in August 1950, placing it in the immediate aftermath of the Sultan's lifting of the pre-war ban, and it documents the sport at a moment of institutional transition [[2]](#ref-2).

Muhammad Taib Osman's 1964 text on the rules of the Kelantan bull-fight represents a further shift in scholarly priority, moving from ethnographic description to the documentation of the sport's internal regulatory framework [[4]](#ref-4). The progression from Brown's broad cultural account, through Rentse's linguistic inventory and Hill's post-war survey, to Osman's focus on the rules themselves, traces a trajectory from the sport's social and economic context to its internal technical and regulatory logic—a trajectory that mirrors the maturation of the sport itself as an institution with its own codified practices.
