# Christianity

Christianity in the Malay Peninsula and Borneo encompasses a diverse constellation of Protestant and Catholic missions that, from the early nineteenth century onward, established institutional presences across the Straits Settlements, the interior of Sabah, and the rural districts of Perak. The region's Christian communities are stratified along denominational, linguistic, and ethnic lines, with Roman Catholics constituting the largest single body among Indian Christians in the Peninsula [[1, p. 74]](#ref-1) and with Protestant and Catholic missions alike having shaped the educational, medical, and agricultural infrastructure of indigenous communities in Borneo [[2, p. 53]](#ref-2). The historical record reveals that missionary activity in the region was never reducible to evangelism alone: it introduced printing, vernacular literacy, modern medicine, and agricultural innovation, and it generated a body of translated literature—above all successive Malay Bibles—that shaped the linguistic and cultural landscape of the archipelago [[3, pp. 35–52]](#ref-3).

## Scope and Historical Definition

The earliest Christian presence in the region was Catholic, arriving with the Portuguese in the sixteenth century, though in the era of Portuguese colonial domination of the Indonesian Archipelago and Melaka, little attention appears to have been given to the translation of the scriptures, as Latin was regarded in Catholic countries as the sole official language of Christian theology and worship [[3, p. 35]](#ref-3). The Protestant Reformation in Europe spurred interest in vernacular versions of the Bible, and the extension of Dutch political and economic power into the archipelago, where Malay was the lingua franca of trade, made Malay the first language into which the Bible was translated outside of Europe and the Middle East [[3, p. 35]](#ref-3). The Dutch East India Company, which controlled all facets of Dutch involvement in the East Indies, sponsored pastors for the Dutch population and tolerated some evangelistic efforts, though it did not actively encourage missionary activity [[3, p. 36]](#ref-3).

The British colonial period inaugurated a new phase. The London Missionary Society (LMS) established its first station at Melaka in 1815, with William Milne arriving on 21 May of that year and opening a school within three months [[4, p. 62]](#ref-4). The LMS's central activity—translating and printing Christian texts in Chinese, Malay, and Bugis—effectively introduced a print culture to the region, training local printers and producing newspapers, dictionaries, and vernacular literature that shaped intellectual life independently of their religious purpose [[5, pp. 25–29]](#ref-5). By the end of 1820, the LMS presses in Melaka had produced 140,000 books and periodicals in Chinese and over 20,000 items in Malay; the Chinese translation of the Bible completed by Morrison and Milne was published in Melaka in 1823, the first such translation published in the region and only the second published anywhere [[5, pp. 25–26]](#ref-5). The LMS's total output, including Medhurst's Batavia publications, exceeded eight million pages by 1836, distributed through a network of ships' captains, junk masters, and missionaries who boarded vessels bound for destinations as far as America, St. Helena, Tibet, Japan, and the Moluccas [[4, pp. 96–97]](#ref-4).

In the Catholic sphere, the French missionary Mathurin-Pierre Pécot of the Société des Missions Étrangères de Paris arrived in Penang on 13 November 1821, where the Catholic community numbered roughly 600 [[6, p. 169]](#ref-6). Within months, Pécot had produced a Malay-language missal and catechism, *Baktian Saharian*, with the assistance of the catechist Aloysius Bretaud (de Brito), a Siamese-born layman educated at Pondicherry who knew Malay, Siamese, Latin, and French [[6, pp. 170]](#ref-6). This manuscript was ultimately published in Paris in 1828 as *Kebaktian Sahari Harian dan Sembahiang Iang didjadikan deri Parochius Romanus*, with two companion catechisms for children and adults printed in 1826 and 1827 [[6, p. 171]](#ref-6). Pécot's letters reveal that English had already displaced Portuguese as the commercial *lingua franca* of the region by 1821, and that Protestant missionary schools were drawing the children of upwardly mobile families away from Catholic influence [[6, p. 174]](#ref-6).

In North Borneo, the Catholic mission was founded by Carlos Cuarteron, a former Spanish smuggler and slave-runner who, after a near-fatal typhoon in 1849, converted to Catholicism and became the first Prefect Apostolic of North Borneo and Labuan [[7, pp. 168–169]](#ref-7). Cuarteron was born at Cadiz in 1816, entered the College of the Propaganda Fide in Rome in 1850, was ordained in 1855, and was immediately appointed to the Prefecture [[7, pp. 168–169]](#ref-7). Two Milan Fathers arrived in Labuan in April 1857 but departed for Hong Kong in 1860, leaving Cuarteron to work alone for eighteen years in what Antonissen describes as a "very poor life" of penance and good work in the Borneo jungle [[7, p. 169]](#ref-7). He died in Cadiz in 1878, and the Prefecture passed to the Mill Hill Fathers (St. Joseph's Foreign Missionary Society) in 1881, with Msgr. Jackson as the new Prefect Apostolic [[7, p. 169]](#ref-7).

## Educational, Medical, and Developmental Impact

Missionary activity in the region produced societal impacts that extend well beyond conversion. Sandra Hudd argues that both the LMS and the Infant Jesus Sisters (IJS), a French Catholic teaching order that arrived in Singapore in 1854, can be usefully conceptualized as faith-based development organizations [[5, pp. 21–40]](#ref-5). The IJS established schools for girls, an orphanage, and a women's refuge, operating in ways that map directly onto contemporary development goals: reducing child mortality, educating girls, improving health outcomes, and empowering women [[5, pp. 30–32]](#ref-5). The IJS orphanage in Singapore took in sixteen abandoned children on its first day; numbers grew to 93 by 1861 and 200 by 1892, with most girls leaving with at least a primary education and vocational skills [[5, pp. 30–31]](#ref-5). The convent's women's refuge housed 60 women in 1870 and 200 in 1872, many of whom had been brought from China for the brothels or sold as domestic workers [[5, p. 32]](#ref-5). The Convent school was officially classified as a 'government-aided school' after an 1881 inspection and achieved Grade 1 status under the 1899 grading system; by the late 1920s the curriculum included algebra, geometry, science, shorthand, typing, and book-keeping [[5, p. 35]](#ref-5).

In Sabah, the developmental impact of missions on indigenous communities has been the subject of sustained scholarly attention. Mat Zin bin Mat Kib argues that missionaries succeeded in converting interior indigenous groups—particularly the Rungus, Dusun-Kadazan, and Murut—by combining evangelical work with tangible social services including education, medicine, agricultural training, and land tenure reform, a strategy that proved far more effective than the earlier and less institutionalized spread of Islam among the same populations [[2, p. 53]](#ref-2). Literacy among indigenous Sabahans stood at 6.9 per cent in 1921, 9.0 per cent in 1931, and 11.7 per cent in 1951; the first government school in Labuan was not built until 1936, whereas Catholic and Anglican mission schools had been operating since the 1880s [[2, pp. 53]](#ref-2). At least two former Heads of State (Tun Mustapha Harun, Tun Ahmad Koroh) and five former Chief Ministers (including Joseph Pairin Kitingan, Peter Lo Su Yin, Bernard Dompok, Donald Stephens, and Chong Kah Kiat) were products of Christian mission schools [[2, pp. 58–59]](#ref-2).

The 1981 Sabah statistical bulletin recorded 84,042 Dusun-Kadazan Christians against 16,890 Muslims, and 14,734 Christian Muruts against only 1,510 Muslims, demonstrating the comparative success of Christian evangelization in the interior [[2, p. 62]](#ref-2). No fewer than 30 Christian denominations operated in Sabah, divided into major, medium, and minor categories; the major denominations (Catholic, Anglican, BCCM, SIB, SDA, PCS, True Jesus) were all established between 1850 and 1937 [[2, p. 55]](#ref-2). The Catholic Welfare Society's Bundu Tuhan scheme (1959) and the BCCM's Sapong scheme (1963) introduced cash crops and terracing techniques, linking conversion to material improvement [[2, pp. 10–11]](#ref-2). The BCCM channelled mountain water to Murut villages in Pensiangan district, replacing silted water sources [[2, pp. 10–11]](#ref-2).

In Peninsular Malaysia, the Roman Catholic Church's rural mission strategy, exemplified by the St. Joseph's Mission near Bagan Serai, Perak, contrasted with other denominations that initially concentrated on urban Indian populations [[8, p. 74]](#ref-8). The settlement, established by the French priest Michael Maria Fee in the 1880s as an agricultural mission exclusively for Indian Catholics, comprised by January 1889 at least 101 families with a total population of 430, approximately 148 baptisms and 43 marriages recorded, a school with 170 students, and around 450 acres of land cleared for padi cultivation [[8, p. 74]](#ref-8). By 1900 the Roman Catholic Church had established a presence from Penang in the north to Johore Bahru in the south, with major centres at Bagan Serai, Nibong Tebal, Parit Buntar, Telok Intan, Tapah, and Kajang [[8, p. 74]](#ref-8).

## The Translation of the Bible into Malay

The history of Bible translation into Malay spans nearly four centuries and was shaped by linguistic, institutional, and political forces rather than any single mission strategy [[3, pp. 35–52]](#ref-3). The first period of translation was sponsored by the Dutch East India Company: between 1628 and 1638, Albert Cornelius Ruyl published a translation of the gospels of Mark and Matthew in diglot form with the then current Dutch translation; Jan van Hasel added Luke and John in 1646; Justus Heurn added the Book of Acts and revised the entire translation for publication in 1651; and the first complete New Testament was published by Rev. Daniel Brower in 1662 [[3, pp. 35–36]](#ref-3). The translation of the entire Bible was begun by Dr. Melchior Leidekker in 1691 on the order of the Church in Batavia; when he died in 1701 the work was 90 per cent completed, and was finished by Rev. Peter van der Vorm in that same year, though it was not published until 1733 in Roman script and 1758 in Jawi script [[3, pp. 36–37]](#ref-3). This translation became the standard for nearly two centuries, remaining in use until 1916 in Indonesia and until 1853 in Malaysia [[3, p. 37]](#ref-3).

The British colonial period brought a new generation of missionaries with a strong bias in favour of distributing the Bible widely and the means to print large quantities in Southeast Asia itself [[3, p. 37]](#ref-3). The LMS's efforts to produce a Malay Bible were dominated for forty years by the influence of Munshi Abdullah bin Abdul Kadir, a language teacher whose contribution was rarely acknowledged publicly and was sometimes hidden [[3, p. 38]](#ref-3). Claudius Thomsen, who arrived in 1815 with a specific assignment to work among the Malay people, became both a close friend and student of Abdullah's, and worked from 1818 to 1832 to improve Leidekker's translation [[3, p. 38]](#ref-3). However, Thomsen's efforts were hampered by the LMS's constant shifting of policies and its priority for Chinese literature; only 1,500 copies of his final revision of the Gospels and Acts were printed [[3, p. 39]](#ref-3).

When the LMS abandoned Malaysia for China in the 1840s, Benjamin Keasberry and Munshi Abdullah were left to carry on the work of creating a new version of the Bible in Malay [[3, p. 40]](#ref-3). The result was that in 1852 the New Testament was finally published in Singapore using Roman script, followed by a Jawi New Testament in 1856; these were distributed not only in Peninsular Malaysia but in Sumatra and Borneo as well [[3, p. 41]](#ref-3). Keasberry's death in 1875 brought to an end any extensive work in the Malay language on the Peninsula for twenty years [[3, p. 41]](#ref-3).

The next major translation was undertaken by William Shellabear, a soldier turned Methodist missionary who had been appointed to command a company of Malay soldiers in Singapore in 1886 [[3, p. 42]](#ref-3). Shellabear's New Testament was completed in 1904 and his Old Testament in 1909 (published 1912); a Baba Malay New Testament for the Straits Chinese followed in 1913 [[3, pp. 43–47]](#ref-3). The preparation of this translation became mired in disputes with the Netherlands Bible Society and the Methodist Publishing House over spelling, remuneration, and the need for any new translation whatsoever [[3, pp. 43–46]](#ref-3). The core of the spelling issue was the representation of vowels ordinarily not shown in Jawi, particularly in the first syllable of a word; a final agreement made provision for both spelling systems to be used, with guarantees that there would not be competition in sale [[3, p. 44]](#ref-3).

The attempt to produce a "union version" for both the Dutch and British colonies, initiated in 1929 under Rev. Werner Bode, was ultimately thwarted by Shellabear's opposition, who insisted on what he regarded as the pure Malay of Melaka and Johore [[3, pp. 47–50]](#ref-3). World War II halted work on the Old Testament; Bode himself, a German, had been interred by the Dutch at the beginning of the war, and the ship carrying him to Britain was sunk by the Japanese while he was being transferred to India, with the partial set of Old Testament manuscripts lost [[3, p. 50]](#ref-3). The "Today's Malay Version," employing the dynamic equivalence method, was commissioned in 1969; the New Testament appeared in 1974 (revised 1976) and the complete Bible was published in 1987 [[3, pp. 51–52]](#ref-3).

## Diplomatic and Institutional Dimensions

Christianity in the region also intersected with diplomatic and sovereign politics. Sultan Abu Bakar of Johore's 1885 visit to Italy, in which he secured audiences with both King Umberto I and Pope Leo XIII during a transit stop en route to London, was a calculated diplomatic manoeuvre designed to bolster his claim to sovereign status ahead of his audience with Queen Victoria, where he ultimately obtained the coveted title of Sultan [[9, pp. 43–54]](#ref-9). The Congregation de Propaganda Fide's recommendation for the Papal audience was grounded in Abu Bakar's documented support for the Catholic mission in Johore, including the granting of land for a church in 1882, which was officially opened on 29 May 1883 [[9, pp. 48–49]](#ref-9). The *Osservatore Romano* reported the Papal audience on 25 April 1885, describing Abu Bakar as an "independent Sovereign living in the Malacca Peninsula" and noting that the Pope expressed appreciation for the churches he had built or intended to build [[9, p. 50]](#ref-9). Bishop Gasnier's letter of 18 May 1885 records that the news of the Papal audience was celebrated in Johore with a toast, and that Father Saleilles "was announcing everywhere that the King had gone to see his Great Priest in Rome" [[9, p. 52]](#ref-9).

The internal diversity of the Indian Christian community in Peninsular Malaysia, estimated at approximately 100,000 in 1984, is stratified along linguistic, denominational, and caste lines [[1, p. 71]](#ref-1). Roman Catholics constitute 81,650 individuals (75.4 per cent of Indian Christians) per the 1989 Official Church Directory; the Methodist Tamil Church had 5,981 members in 1980 across 26 centres [[1, pp. 74]](#ref-1). The Syrian Orthodox churches (Mar Thoma and Jacobite) are exclusively Malayali in composition, reflecting the linguistic homogeneity of the Kerala diaspora [[1, pp. 78–80]](#ref-1). Caste among Indian Christians is "highly diluted" compared to their Hindu counterparts, with an "unconscious move towards a state of castelessness" driven by Christian egalitarianism, yet caste associations such as the Nalliar Association and KRISWAM demonstrate that caste persists as a vehicle for socio-economic mutual aid rather than as a system of ritual purity [[1, pp. 82–83]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Christianity in the region reveals a shifting of priorities across more than a century of publication. The earliest contributions, such as Dabin's 1885 journal of a missionary's journey through Laos [[10]](#ref-10) and Pécot's letters from the 1820s [[6]](#ref-6), are first-hand missionary accounts that document the material conditions of travel, the political geography of the interior, and the origins of Catholic vernacular publishing in Malay. These texts reflect the missionary's contemporary framing of local populations as requiring conversion, and of the Siamese administrative apparatus as rudimentary [[10, p. 103]](#ref-10).

The mid-twentieth century saw a turn toward institutional and demographic history. Antonissen's 1966 note on Cuarteron and the Catholic mission in Sabah [[7]](#ref-7), Fee's 1963 study of Kampong Padre [[8]](#ref-8), and O'Sullivan's 1984 comprehensive record of LMS personnel and printing operations [[4]](#ref-4) each drew on mission archives, government records, and denominational publications to reconstruct the institutional development of specific missions. O'Sullivan's work, drawing extensively on the LMS archives at SOAS London, demonstrated that the Society's presses introduced mass communication and vernacular literacy to Peninsular Malaysia, making its missionaries among the most active pioneers of social change in the region irrespective of their conversion aims [[4, p. 97]](#ref-4).

The 1990s and 2000s introduced more theoretically informed approaches. Hunt's 1989 article on Bible translation synthesized institutional records with linguistic analysis to foreground the role of Malay-language consultants—Abdullah, Guru Sleiman, Ehe Mashohor—who are largely absent from the historical record [[3, p. 53]](#ref-3). Daniel's 1992 sociological survey of Indian Christian diversity [[1]](#ref-1) and Mat Zin's 2004 study of Christianization and indigenous development in Sabah [[2]](#ref-2) each applied social scientific frameworks to ecclesiastical and demographic data. Hudd's 2019 article challenged the dominant historiographical framing of missionaries as either willing enablers of empire or culturally insensitive imperialists, arguing instead that they can be usefully reconceptualized within the field of humanitarian development [[5, pp. 21–40]](#ref-5). The Reids' 2020 edition of Pécot's letters [[6]](#ref-6) represents the most recent contribution, providing the first complete English translation of a primary source that documents both the political upheaval of the Siamese conquest of Kedah and the origins of Catholic vernacular publishing in Malay.

A recurring historiographical tension runs through these works: the extent to which missionary activity should be understood as a form of cultural imperialism, a genuine development intervention, or something more complex that resists simple categorization. Hudd's deployment of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals as an analytical lens [[5, pp. 21–40]](#ref-5) and Mat Zin's treatment of missionary activity as a development variable rather than solely a religious phenomenon [[2, p. 53]](#ref-2) represent the most explicit attempts to move beyond the binary of missionaries-as-imperialists versus missionaries-as-benevolent-agents. The archival record, however, consistently reveals that the missionaries' own accounts were shaped by the assumptions of their era, and that the full impact of their work—particularly on the linguistic and cultural landscape—remains an object of ongoing scholarly investigation.
