# Thomas Forrest, an eighteenth century mariner

D.K. Bassett's 1961 article reconstructs the career of Thomas Forrest, a versatile mariner in the East India Company's Marine who served in Southeast Asian waters for over thirty years during the mid-eighteenth century. Drawing on India Office and British Museum archives, Bassett corrects the assumption of Sir John Laughton that Forrest's own publications were the only source of significance, and demonstrates that Forrest's strategic contributions to British policy in the Strait of Malacca—particularly his advocacy for Penang and the Mergui Archipelago—have been historically overshadowed by the better-known efforts of Francis Light.

## Summary

Bassett traces Forrest's career from his probable birth around 1729 and early Royal Navy service through his long association with the Company's Marine at Bencoolen, Balambangan, and the Bengal station. The narrative is organised not as a simple chronology but around the major phases of Forrest's service: his early commands in the Bencoolen establishment (1750s–1760s), his celebrated voyage to New Guinea in the *Tartar Galley* (1774–1776), his wartime service on the Malabar coast during the Anglo-French conflict (1781–1783), and his final strategic proposals for British bases in the Bay of Bengal and Strait of Malacca (1783–1785). Bassett uses company court minutes, Fort Marlborough correspondence, and Forrest's own letters to the governor-general to illuminate gaps in the published record, particularly the circumstances of Forrest's reinstatement at Bencoolen in 1770, his secret "Employs" for the Committee of Secresy in 1780, and the precise sequence of events that led to his recommendations on Penang.

A central argument of the article is that Forrest's 1784 proposals from Kedah—advocating simultaneous occupation of Cheduba, Penang, and St. Matthew's Island in the Mergui Archipelago—represented a coherent strategic vision that anticipated the logic behind Light's acquisition of Penang in 1786. Bassett argues that Forrest's sketch maps and descriptions of Penang and its hinterland, sent to Warren Hastings in September 1784, made a considerable impression on the governor-general and helped foster the governmental enthusiasm that Light later capitalised upon. The article also highlights Forrest's role as an intelligence asset during the Anglo-French war, when his tracking of Suffren's fleet through the Strait of Malacca in late 1782 provided advance warning that saved British merchant shipping in the Bay of Bengal.

Bassett's account closes with Forrest's declining relationship with the court of directors, who refused to settle his financial claims after 1789 and denied him further command. He returned to India in 1793 as a "Free Mariner" and appears to have died between 1801 and 1804, his reputation as a navigator of the eastern seas sustained by his published works but his institutional standing long eroded.

### Key Findings

- Forrest commanded the H.E.I.C. sloop *Neptune* from Madras on 14 August 1757; the vessel capsized and sank with seventeen of her crew, the accident attributed to "the great disproportion of her Mast to a Vessell of so small a Burthen" (p. 107).
- The *Tartar Galley* voyage to New Guinea departed Balambangan on 9 November 1774 and returned in January 1776 to find the settlement destroyed by Suluk pirates; the vessel was subsequently sold at public auction by William Marsden for £9.7.6 (pp. 110–111).
- Forrest received an advance of £800 from the court on 23 May 1777 because he was "in distress for money"; a further application for £500 was rejected in March 1779, and a recommended gratuity of £500 was not finally approved until 9 February 1780 (pp. 111–112).
- During the Anglo-French war, Forrest's intelligence that Suffren was still at Acheh when he put into Kedah in December 1782 reached Vizagapatam on 20 December—the same day Suffren put to sea—saving a number of ships from capture (p. 115).
- Forrest's 15 September 1784 letter from Kedah to Hastings recommended occupation of Cheduba, Penang, and St. Matthew's Island, arguing that "our deferring it may ruin our Posterity" (p. 118).
- The court refused to recognise any further financial obligation towards Forrest in May 1789, and he was permitted to return to India only as a "Free Mariner" in June 1793 (pp. 120–121).

### Conclusion

Bassett's definitive takeaway is that Forrest's strategic contributions to British expansion in Southeast Asia—his intelligence work against Suffren, his advocacy for Penang, and his proposals for a base in the Mergui Archipelago—were as significant as those of Francis Light, yet have been systematically neglected in the historiography. Forrest's failure to secure a lasting institutional position, despite his exceptional knowledge of the eastern seas and his personal relationships with rulers from Aceh to Celebes, reflects the limitations placed on a working seaman without independent means within the Company's bureaucratic structure.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the India Office Library collections in London (Court Minutes, Sumatra Factory Records, Bengal Secret Consultations, Home Miscellaneous Series, Marine Records) and Additional Manuscripts in the British Museum (notably Add. MSS. 29,164, 29,210, and 34,466).
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in its correction of Laughton's DNB entry and its repositioning of Forrest within the narrative of British strategic planning in the Strait of Malacca, arguing that the Penang acquisition was not solely Light's achievement but the culmination of a process in which Forrest played a formative role.
