# Hikayat Ganja Mara

H. Overbeck published this synopsis of the *Hikayat Ganja Mara* in 1928, presenting for the first time in print a previously unrecorded Malay literary text that he had acquired from a private collector in Penang. The article situates the work within the declining Straits Malay book-market and argues for the tale's probable Indian origin, while providing a detailed narrative summary that reveals a complex story of royal intrigue, supernatural intervention, and the triumph of Islam over idolatry.

## Summary

Overbeck's primary purpose is twofold: to document a text that appears in no known manuscript catalogue and to trace its literary genealogy. He notes that the *Hikayat Ganja Mara* is listed in several printed catalogues of Eastern books but has never been the subject of a synopsis or critical study. The colophon at the end of the text states it was copied in 1303 AH (1885/86 CE) by Mohamad Kassim at Penang from a manuscript in the possession of a "white merchant" (*saudagar puteh*), a detail Overbeck finds puzzling but interprets as indicating the source was held by a European collector rather than composed by one. He searches the accessible literature of India, Persia, and Arabia without finding a cognate tale, though he acknowledges the story's probable Indian provenance based on its structural and thematic features.

The narrative itself is a sprawling *hikayat* in the classical Malay tradition, following the displaced prince Keta Buana (later Ganja Mara) from his infancy in Gangga Buana through his exile on Pulau Kubin, his education under the Jin king Nila Suba, and his eventual conquest of the idolatrous kingdom of Rangga Duli. The story interweaves multiple plot threads: the substitution of the true prince by the jealous Siti Selih, the parallel marriage plot involving princess Kemalih of Jangga Dupa, the ten riddles posed by the queen Seri Maharaja Puteri, and a protracted military campaign against the fire-worshipping kingdom of Jangga Lawi. Overbeck's synopsis is remarkably detailed, rendering the full arc of the narrative including the resolution of all subplots, the conversion of the Brahmans, and the final coronation of Ganja Mara as Sultan Fazl Shah Aalam Nobadz Shah of Dul Akbar.

The article also serves as a brief meditation on the state of Malay publishing in the late 1920s. Overbeck observes that the old lithographic press tradition in Singapore and Penang is dying out, replaced by cheap moral tracts, and that the "big stories" of the classical *hikayat* corpus are disappearing from circulation. His acquisition of the *Hikayat Ganja Mara* from a Haji who had abandoned plans to reprint it for want of a competent scribe illustrates this broader cultural loss.

### Key Findings

- The manuscript was copied in 1303 AH (1885/86 CE) on the first day of Shaban by Mohamad Kassim at Penang, from a source held by a "white merchant" (*saudagar puteh*) (p. 79).
- The text is absent from all known Malay manuscript catalogues and has no prior synopsis in the literature; it appears only in several printed catalogues of Eastern books (p. 45).
- The story's protagonist bears four birthmarks: a shining mark on the brow, two on the shoulders, and a fourth from a knife wound inflicted by his cousin Taj Salim, corresponding to the four directions of the wind and signifying universal sovereignty (pp. 46–48).
- The narrative spans at least four kingdoms (Gangga Buana, Jangga Dupa, Rangga Duli, Jangga Lawi) and involves supernatural agents including the Jin king Nila Suba, invisible servants Tamsun and Jamsir, the sword of Alexander the Great, and the battle-horse Dul Akbar of Saidina Ali (pp. 48–77).
- The final battle against Genta Lokam lasts four days and nights; Genta Lokam is killed when Dul Akbar crushes his head with a kick after Ganja Mara cuts the legs of his horse (pp. 76–77).
- The story concludes with the founding of the kingdom of Dul Akbar, named after the dead horse, and the coronation of Ganja Mara as Sultan Fazl Shah Aalam Nobadz Shah, with Janus (Thalib) appointed grandvizier Mangkubumi (pp. 77–79).

### Conclusion

Overbeck presents the *Hikayat Ganja Mara* as a text of genuine literary merit that deserves a new edition, and whose publication in the *Journal* might help trace its ultimate origin in Indian or Persian narrative tradition. The story's explicit moral framing—that it exists "to show the omnipotence of God, so that readers may become righteous and acquire merit"—places it firmly within the didactic conventions of the Malay *hikayat* genre, while its elaborate plot architecture and supernatural elements mark it as a product of the late classical period of Malay literary culture.

## Context

- The manuscript was acquired from a private collector (an "old Haji") in Penang who had intended to commission a new copy for his lithographic press but could not find a competent scribe; Overbeck's purchase thus represents a rescue from potential loss in the context of the collapsing Straits Malay print trade of the 1920s (p. 45).
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in documenting a previously unrecorded text and proposing its Indian provenance, though Overbeck acknowledges the limits of the comparative literature available to him at the time.
