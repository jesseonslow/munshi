# The advent of Muhammadanism in the Malay Peninsula and Archipelago

R. O. Winstedt, a British colonial administrator and Malay scholar serving in the Straits Settlements, published this survey in 1917 tracing the gradual penetration of Islam across the Malay Peninsula and Archipelago from the earliest Arab trading contacts in the 10th century through the consolidation of Muslim polities in the 17th century. The article's overarching thesis is that Islam spread not through a single missionary wave but through a slow, multi-generational process driven by trade, intermarriage, royal conversion, and the political leverage of Muslim courts, with the succession of great trading ports—Kedah, Pasai, Malacca, Acheen—serving as the relay stations for the faith's advance.

## Summary

Winstedt constructs his narrative by interweaving three categories of evidence: literary sources (the *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai*, the *Kedah Annals*, and the *Bustanu's-salatin*), foreign travel accounts (Marco Polo, Ibn Batuta, Albuquerque, Chinese chronicles), and material archaeology (tomb-stones at Pasai, Malacca, and Pahang). The article is not a theological or doctrinal study but a political and commercial history of conversion, treating the adoption of Islam as a consequence of the economic and diplomatic position of Muslim trading communities rather than as a purely spiritual phenomenon.

A central argument is that the conversion of ruling courts was the decisive mechanism for mass adoption. Winstedt demonstrates this pattern repeating at each major port: the marriage of Merah Silu to a Muslim princess at Pasai, the accession of Sultan Muhammad Shah at Malacca, and the conquest of Kedah by Acheen under Sultan Iskandar Muda. The *Sejarah Melayu* anecdotes about the Arab missionary Sadar Jahan—his quarrels with the drunkard Sri Rama and the linguistically challenged Tun Mai—serve Winstedt's point that the missionary's path was "still hard" and that conversion was neither swift nor uncontested. The tomb-stones, particularly those identified by the Dutch archaeologist Magnette as originating from Cambay in India, provide material corroboration that Indian Muslim craftsmen and merchants were active agents in the process, not merely Arab missionaries.

The article's geographic scope extends beyond the Peninsula to Sumatra, Java, Borneo, the Celebes, and the Moluccas, sketching the broader archipelago-wide pattern. In Java, the fall of the Hindu kingdom of Majapahit in 1478 is presented as the pivotal moment that released its dependencies (Palembang, Jambi, Indragiri) to Muslim influence. Winstedt closes by pointing to the genealogies of ruling families across the archipelago as a further source for refining the chronology of conversion.

### Key Findings

- Arab traders reached Kedah by 915 A.D.; Marco Polo found Muslims at Perlak but not yet at Samudra-Pasai in 1292 A.D. (pp. 17–18).
- Sultan Maliku's-Salleh of Pasai, the first Muslim ruler there, died in 1297 A.D.; Muhammadan tomb-stones at Pasai are dated 1407 A.D. and identified by J. P. Magnette as originating from Cambay, India (pp. 18–19).
- Chinese records confirm the Malays of Malacca had become Muslim by 1409 A.D.; Sultan Muhammad Shah, the first Muslim prince of Malacca, reigned 1403–1414 A.D. (p. 19).
- The tomb-stone of Sultan Mansur Shah (d. 1475 A.D.) bears an Arabic inscription dated 880 A.H.; the stone at Pangkalan Kempas records the grave of Shaikh Ahmad Makhtar Ramali, erected A.H. 872 (1467 A.D.) "in the reign of Sultan Shah Mansur" (p. 20).
- Sultan Iskandar Muda of Acheen ruled from 1606 to 1636 A.D. and conquered Indrapura, Deli, Siak, Johor, Kedah, and Perak; during his reign the *Sejarah Melayu* and *Bustanu's-salatin* were composed at Acheen (p. 21).
- The Muhammadan princes of Java overthrew the Hindu kingdom of Majapahit in 1478 A.D., after which the ruler of Demak proclaimed himself Sultan and drove Hinduism to Blambangan and Bali (p. 22).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that the Islamisation of the Malay world was a protracted, commercially mediated process in which the rise and fall of great trading ports determined the tempo and direction of the faith's spread. No single event or missionary effort accounts for the transformation; rather, it was the cumulative effect of royal conversions, the political authority of Muslim courts, and the material presence of Indian and Arab Muslim communities that, over roughly four centuries, converted the archipelago's ruling classes and, through them, their subjects.

## Context

- Winstedt was a senior British colonial officer in the Straits Settlements (later a member of the Malay and Siamese Branch of the Civil Service) and a prolific scholar of Malay literature and history; this article reflects the administrative-intellectual culture of the colonial service, in which officials produced scholarly work on the societies they governed.
- The article draws on the Royal Asiatic Society's own Malay manuscript collection (catalogued by Van der Tuuk) and on Dutch archaeological and linguistic publications, situating it within the collaborative Anglo-Dutch scholarly network of the period.
