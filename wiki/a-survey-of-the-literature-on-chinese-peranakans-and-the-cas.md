# A survey of the literature on Chinese Peranakans and the case for a regional resource centre

Ch'ng Kim See's 2009 survey, published in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, assesses the fragmented state of Peranakan documentation across Malaysian and Singaporean institutions and argues for the establishment of a dedicated regional resource centre in Penang to preserve and consolidate the community's heritage before it is lost.

## Summary

The article situates itself within a broader cultural resurgence of Peranakan identity that has accelerated since the 1990s, manifesting in commercial ventures (Nyonya cuisine, kebaya fashion, antiques), academic publications, performing arts, and the reactivation of community associations. Ch'ng traces the institutional lineage from the Straits Chinese British Association (founded 1920) through its post-independence reconstitutions into the State Chinese Penang Association, The Peranakan Association of Singapore, and the State Chinese Association of Malacca, noting how these bodies have become the primary vehicles for identity preservation in the absence of state-supported infrastructure. The author identifies a critical gap: while Singapore has developed a state-backed Peranakan Museum and a substantial library collection, Malaysia—home to a larger Peranakan population—lacks any equivalent facility, and the materials that do exist are scattered across clan associations, private collectors, and under-resourced public libraries.

The core argument is that Penang is the optimal site for a regional resource centre owing to its strategic location in the northern littoral, its large and established Peranakan community, its UNESCO World Heritage status (conferred 7 July 2008), and the existing physical premises of the SCPA on Perak Road. Ch'ng envisions the centre as a multilingual, multimedia depository that would house core collections, conduct oral history recording, maintain a digital catalogue, and function as a referral and clearing house for the wider region including Medan, Phuket, and Rangoon. The proposal is framed not merely as an archival necessity but as a cultural and intellectual investment that would institutionalize Peranakan knowledge and motivate the community to deposit personal papers before they are discarded.

The survey draws on the author's own prior work in library and information science (notably his 1996 and 2003 concept papers on a regional resource centre for Southeast Asian Chinese studies) and on direct knowledge of institutional holdings in both countries. The article functions as both a documentation audit and a policy brief, bridging the gap between the vibrant popular revival of Peranakan culture and the inadequate scholarly infrastructure that supports it.

### Key Findings

- The National Library of Singapore published an annotated bibliography of 1,534 English-language Peranakan titles in 2007, but the collection is not comprehensive and comprises mostly secondary sources (p. 184).
- Peranakan population estimates remain imprecise: Singapore was estimated at 10,000 (Pakir 1986), Malacca at 5,000 (Tan 1988), and Penang extrapolated to approximately 19,000 based on 3 per cent of its 635,300 Chinese residents in 2006 (p. 189, n. 20).
- The National Library of Malaysia has no programme in collection development for Chinese Overseas or Peranakan studies, and the Penang State Library Corporation functions primarily as a public library rather than a research repository (p. 185).
- The Singapore Peranakan Association expanded its membership to 1,800 following a recruitment exercise, and the state-supported Peranakan Museum (part of the Asian Civilizations Museum complex) has acquired heritage artefacts from the peninsula, provoking "envy and chagrin" among Malaysian Peranakans (p. 182).
- The first Baba Convention was held in Penang in 1987; by 2009 the 22nd was hosted in Singapore, and a dedicated workshop on Chinese Peranakan Networks was co-organized by the SCPA and the University of Malaya in June 2008 (p. 180).
- Private collections in Penang are held across at least a dozen entities—including the SERI, Penang Heritage Trust, Khoo Kongsi, Lim Kongsi, Han Chiang College, and the SCPA—but are described as "fragmented, inconsistent, and narrow in focus," with large gaps arising from turnover in office-bearers (pp. 185–186).

### Conclusion

Ch'ng's definitive takeaway is that the sustained revival of Peranakan cultural interest has outpaced the institutional capacity to document and preserve it, and that without a dedicated, professionally managed resource centre, valuable primary materials—personal papers, grey literature, oral histories, and small-print publications—will continue to be lost. The proposed Penang centre is presented as the first of its kind globally and as an urgent prerequisite for serious Peranakan scholarship.

## Context

- The article draws on the author's direct familiarity with library and archival holdings in both Malaysia and Singapore, as well as his earlier concept papers (1996, 2003) on regional resource centres for Southeast Asian Chinese studies, positioning this piece as an updated and more specific application of that framework.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the emerging field of Peranakan studies by providing the first systematic audit of documentation infrastructure in the region, filling a gap between the popular cultural revival and the scholarly apparatus needed to sustain it.
