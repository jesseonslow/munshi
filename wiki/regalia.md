# Regalia

## Regalia

Regalia in the Malay world refers to the ceremonial insignia, weapons, and symbolic objects that denote sovereign authority, rank, and ritual status among the rulers and chiefs of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo. These objects—ranging from iron rods and swords to staffs and other implements—functioned not merely as decorative attributes of power but as material embodiments of political legitimacy, hereditary right, and the capacity to declare or settle conflict. The study of such regalia has drawn on linguistic, archaeological, and ethnographic evidence to trace the distribution of specific terms and objects across the Malay Archipelago and, in some cases, far beyond it, revealing connections that reach into Polynesia, Melanesia, Micronesia, and even the Americas [[1]](#ref-1).

## Scope and Historical Definition

The term *regalia* in this context encompasses a range of objects whose precise boundaries have been the subject of scholarly attention. Among the rulers of Johore, one such object is the *tokin*, a ceremonial iron rod that forms part of the insignia of the sultanate [[1]](#ref-1). Winstedt's 1931 note identifies the *tokin* specifically as an element of Johore's royal regalia, distinguishing it from equivalent objects in other Malay states. In Perak, for instance, the sword given to commoner chiefs carries a different name: the Sakai word *baur*, meaning 'staff' [[1]](#ref-1). This distinction between the regalia of hereditary rulers and that of commoner chiefs is significant, as it indicates a stratified system in which different grades of authority were marked by different material symbols.

The *tokin* itself is described as a ceremonial iron rod, a designation that places it within the broader category of rod- and staff-like implements found across the Malay world as markers of rank. Winstedt notes that the word *tokin* is unknown elsewhere in Malaya, which limits its attested distribution to Johore within the peninsula [[1]](#ref-1). This geographic restriction is itself a point of interest: it suggests either that the object was unique to Johore's court, or that equivalent objects in other states carried different names that have not yet been identified as cognates.

## Linguistic Distribution and Comparative Evidence

The most substantial evidence for the wider significance of the *tokin* comes from comparative linguistic work. Dr. J. Imbelloni, writing from Vienna in 1928, traced the word *tokin* across Polynesia, Melanesia, Micronesia, and parts of South America, where it consistently denotes a stone weapon, a symbol of power, a hereditary chief, or a ritual object used in declarations of war and peace [[1]](#ref-1). This distribution is remarkable in its breadth, spanning the Pacific and extending into the Americas. In Chile, the form *tokin* carries the meaning 'to rule, command,' a semantic field that aligns closely with the object's function as an insignia of sovereignty in Johore [[1]](#ref-1).

Imbelloni further suggested a possible connection between *tokin* and the Red Indian word 'tomahawk,' as well as the English phrases 'take up the hatchet' and 'bury the hatchet' [[1]](#ref-1). While Winstedt does not endorse or refute this etymological proposal in detail, the association is noteworthy for its implications: if the connection holds, it would link a ceremonial rod in a Malay sultanate to a weapon of war among indigenous peoples of North America, and to idioms of conflict and reconciliation in the English language. The semantic thread running through these connections—power, authority, the capacity to make or end war—remains consistent across all the attested uses.

Winstedt himself is cautious about the ultimate origin of the word in Johore. He observes that it may have an aboriginal origin, though he has not been able to confirm this [[1]](#ref-1). The reference to the Sakai word *baur* in Perak as the equivalent term for a sword given to commoner chiefs introduces the possibility that the regalia of the Malay states drew on indigenous substratum vocabulary, particularly in the case of lower-ranking insignia [[1]](#ref-1). Whether the *tokin* shares this substratum origin or represents a later borrowing remains unresolved in the available evidence.

## The *Tokin* as Evidence for Pre-Columbian Contact

Winstedt's most consequential claim is that the distribution of *tokin* from Asia to South America constitutes part of the evidence for pre-Columbian Oceanic influence on the Americas [[1]](#ref-1). This assertion places the *tokin* within a much larger and more controversial debate about trans-oceanic contacts before the age of European navigation. The argument proceeds as follows: if a word denoting a specific type of ceremonial or martial object is found in geographically distant regions with consistent semantic content, and if the object itself (a rod, staff, or weapon used as a symbol of power) is attested in those regions, then the most parsimonious explanation may be direct or indirect contact between the populations concerned.

Winstedt does not develop this argument at length in his short note; he presents it as a conclusion drawn from Imbelloni's comparative work rather than as a thesis he himself has independently verified [[1]](#ref-1). The note's brevity—occupying a single page of the journal—means that the methodological basis for the claim, the specific forms of *tokin* attested in Polynesian and Melanesian languages, and the nature of the South American evidence are not detailed. What the note does establish is that the *tokin* of Johore is not an isolated curiosity but a data point in a wider pattern that, if the comparative evidence is sound, has implications for the history of human migration and cultural exchange across the Pacific.

## Research and Documentation

The documentation of Malay regalia in the Society's literature has been episodic rather than systematic. The 1931 note by Winstedt represents a focused, single-object study: it takes one item of Johore's royal insignia and subjects it to linguistic and comparative analysis, drawing on external scholarship (Imbelloni, 1928) to extend the discussion beyond the Malay world [[1]](#ref-1). The note is characterised by its concision and its willingness to follow a thread into speculative territory—the pre-Columbian contact hypothesis—without fully developing the argument or addressing counter-evidence.

A later entry, Maxwell's 1997 'Notes and Queries,' appears in the same journal but provides no substantive detail in the available record beyond its bibliographic identification [[2]](#ref-2). The gap between these two records—sixty-six years—reflects the broader pattern in the Society's literature, where regalia have been treated as items of note within larger studies of Malay history, language, or material culture rather than as a sustained field of investigation in their own right. The *tokin* note stands as the most explicit and detailed treatment of a specific regalia object in the available records, and its value lies less in the resolution it offers than in the questions it raises: the aboriginal origin of the term, its relationship to the Sakai *baur*, and its place in a trans-oceanic pattern of linguistic and cultural distribution.
