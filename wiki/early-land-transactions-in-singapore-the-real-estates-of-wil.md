# Early land transactions in Singapore: the real estates of William Farquhar (1774―1839) and John Crawfurd and their families

Leong Foke Meng's 2004 article traces the land holdings and transactions of Singapore's first two Residents—William Farquhar (1774–1839) and John Crawfurd (1783–1868)—and their families, from the founding of the settlement in 1819 through the early twentieth century. Drawing primarily on Singapore Land Registry title deeds, lease records, and contemporary directories, Meng reconstructs how these officials and their descendants accumulated, managed, and disposed of property in a colony where land tenure was still being formalized. The article argues that while Farquhar was a cautious civil servant who ultimately lost his investments to financial crisis, Crawfurd was a far more astute land speculator whose holdings were preserved for his descendants through a carefully structured trust that endured until compulsory acquisition in 1982.

## Summary

The article opens by establishing the institutional context of land administration in early Singapore. Raffles initially treated the island as a military post with no formal land ownership, but by 1822 Farquhar had begun issuing location tickets and registering sales. Raffles' return in October 1822 triggered a dispute with Farquhar over unauthorized land allocations, leading to Farquhar's dismissal and the implementation of the Jackson Plan—the first town plan of Singapore. The reclamation of Commercial Square and the subsequent auction of lots created the conditions for the land transactions that form the article's core subject.

Meng then follows the Farquhar family's property trajectory across three generations. William Farquhar held the Residency (32,680 sq ft, now the Supreme Court site), a large beachfront compound at Kampong Glam (85,800 sq ft), and a plantation hill at Bukit Cawah/Mount Emily that, upon final survey, proved to be over 151 acres rather than the 20 acres he had reported to Raffles. His son Andrew, a merchant and later Coroner, acquired properties at High Street, Commercial Square, and Kampong Glam before dying in 1829. The financial crisis of the 1830s forced William to sell Mount Emily for $1,500 and Nonya Clement's Telok Ayer property for $900. His Eurasian daughter, Elizabeth Caroline George, emerged as the family's most significant property investor, acquiring over 31 acres at Tanglin, 90 acres at Geylang, 51 acres at Siglap, and 180 acres at Toa Payoh through her agent T. O. Crane, who used her capital to build his own agricultural reputation.

Crawfurd's land strategy is presented as markedly different. In June 1826, through his agent A. L. Johnston, he purchased thirteen of the twenty-eight properties at Commercial Square, controlling 90,130 sq ft—over 41.6 per cent of the precinct. He also acquired Tan Che Sang's market-adjacent lot and two properties at Circular Road. After leaving Singapore in 1826, Crawfurd managed his estate from England, subdividing leases and selling selectively. His 1863 will established a trust that preserved his core holdings until 1910, when the trustees reissued a consolidated lease (No. 2760). The property was not finally extinguished until compulsory acquisition in 1982 for the Golden Shoe Car Park development.

### Key Findings

- Raffles issued 574 location tickets before leaving Singapore in June 1823; no location tickets were issued by Farquhar, who departed on 23 December 1823 (pp. 3–4).
- Farquhar's Mount Emily plantation was reported to Raffles as 20 acres but measured over 151 acres upon final survey in March 1828; it was sold to Charles Robert Prinsep in November 1831 for $1,500 (pp. 7–10).
- In three days (3–5 June 1826), Crawfurd purchased 13 of 28 properties at Commercial Square, totalling 90,130 sq ft (41.6% of the precinct's 216,540 sq ft), via his agent A. L. Johnston (p. 16).
- Elizabeth Caroline George acquired freehold agricultural land exceeding 31 acres at Tanglin, 90 acres at Geylang, 51 acres at Siglap, and 180 acres at Toa Payoh from 1847 onward, through her agent T. O. Crane (p. 12).
- Crawfurd's trust-held property (Lease No. 2760, reissued 26 August 1910) was compulsorily acquired by the government in 1982 for urban redevelopment, becoming the Golden Shoe Car Park site (p. 18).
- Andrew Farquhar's Kampong Glam house was sold in December 1830 for $900 to J. H. Moor; his High Street property (Lease No. 297, 33,398 sq ft) was assigned to his father in June 1833 for $100 (pp. 9–10).

### Conclusion

Meng's definitive takeaway is that the two Residents represent contrasting models of colonial land engagement: Farquhar was a rule-bound administrator whose personal investments were eroded by commercial crisis and whose legacy passed to a daughter who became Singapore's first Eurasian woman property entrepreneur, while Crawfurd was a shrewd speculator whose trust structure preserved his Commercial Square holdings for nearly a century, outlasting both the free-trade era and the colonial administration itself.

## Context

- The primary archival source is the Singapore Land Registry (SLR) title deeds, lease plans, and indentures, supplemented by the *Straits Times Directory* (1846–1851), *Singapore Consultations*, and *Singapore Chronicle* (1827–1828).
- The article fills a gap identified by Meng in the opening: while Raffles' founding and the Residents' appointments are well documented, "very little has been published on land administration, in particular sales transactions as practised in Singapore" (p. 22). It contributes to the historiography of early Singapore urban development by linking individual property transactions to the broader institutional evolution of land tenure from location tickets to 999-year leases.
