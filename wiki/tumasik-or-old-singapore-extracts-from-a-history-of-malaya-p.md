# Tumasik or old Singapore {extracts from A History of Malaya, published in the Journal as Part I of Volume 13, 1935

R.O. Winstedt, writing in 1935 (republished in the *JMBRAS* in 1969), reconstructs the history of ancient Singapore (Tumasik) from a confluence of Chinese, Javanese, Portuguese, and Malay sources, arguing that the island was a strategically important entrepôt port that fell to Majapahit in the fourteenth century, and that the legendary account in the *Malay Annals* is a late compilation of myth and anachronism rather than reliable history.

## Summary

Winstedt opens by surveying the persistent problem of identifying ancient Singapore in foreign sources. He examines attempts to equate the island with I-tsing's "Mo-ho-sin" (690 A.D.), the "Hasin" of an eleventh-century Javanese inscription, Ibn Khordadzbeh's "Salahit," and Marco Polo's "Malayur." He argues that "Malayur" — a Tamil compound meaning "hill-town" — was a generic toponym applied to multiple settlements across the region, making any single identification uncertain. The revised Marco Polo text's sailing directions from Bintan, however, fit a monsoon route to Singapore more plausibly than to Jambi, and the Yuan annals' "Ma-li-yu-eul" of 1295, involved in a Siamese dispute, would also suit the island's position.

The historian's "sure land-fall," as Winstedt puts it, comes with fourteenth-century evidence. Wang Ta-Yuan's 1349 account provides the most vivid picture of the port: a place of poor soil and few rice-fields whose economy depended entirely on intercepting Chinese trade, a community living mixed among Chinese settlers, and a polity that had recently repelled a Siamese naval attack. The Javanese *Pararaton* and *Nagarakrtagama* confirm Majapahit's conquest, with Gajah Mada's famous oath to subdue Tumasik among ten target states and a later reference listing it as a subject territory. Ming chronicles add further detail about the political fragmentation of Sri Vijaya in the 1370s and the killing of Chinese imperial envoys by Javanese forces.

Winstedt then subjects the *Malay Annals* to sustained critical scrutiny. The annals' five-king dynasty, he shows, is an "olla-podrida" of borrowed titles and anachronisms: Sang Sapurba is a corruption of a Majapahit princely title, Nila Utama's name echoes a Majapahit queen's style, and the final king's Muslim title "Iskandar" was inserted at the only plausible seam between the Hindu and Islamic periods in the Malacca genealogy. The annals' account of the city's fall — a traitor opening the gates to Javanese invaders — is contrasted with the geographical reality that defenders could easily have dispersed into the surrounding jungle, suggesting the massacre narrative is legendary.

### Key Findings

- The walled port of ancient Singapore, traced by Crawfurd during his residency (1823–26), ran roughly a mile inland and was just over half a mile broad, bounded by the sea, Fort Canning (Hill Tabu), the Singapore River, and an earth wall sixteen feet broad at the base running along the Bras Basah River (p. 5).
- Wang Ta-Yuan (1349) describes a port whose only products were "loot from the Chu'an-Chou traders," with two or three hundred pirate junks attacking returning Chinese vessels near Carimon Island; the town successfully resisted seventy Siamese junks for a month just before 1349, until a Chinese fleet appeared (p. 8).
- Gajah Mada's oath in 1338 (*Pararaton*) to subdue ten countries including Tumasik; the *Nagarakrtagama* (1365) lists Tumasik among Majapahit's subject territories (p. 8).
- A Majapahit-script inscription that once stood at the mouth of the Singapore River was destroyed by the Public Works Department approximately a century before 1935; one undecipherable fragment survives in Raffles' Museum (p. 9).
- Fourteenth-century gold ornaments, including a pair of Majapahit bracelets, were found at the root of a tree on Fort Canning, the Hill Tabu where the Malay kings are reputed to have lived (p. 9).
- The Ming chronicles record that by 1373 three kings of Sri Vijaya existed, and in 1377 the Chinese emperor sent a seal to a new ruler whose envoys were waylaid and killed by Javanese who had already conquered Sri Vijaya (pp. 8–9).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive historical takeaway is that ancient Singapore was a real and significant port kingdom, but its detailed history is irrecoverable due to the destruction of material evidence and the mythological character of the *Malay Annals*. The island's importance lay in its position as an entrepôt dependent on external trade rather than local production, and its fall to Majapahit in the fourteenth century marked the end of its independent political life, leaving only fragments — a broken inscription, a pair of gold bracelets, and the red laterite of Fort Canning — as physical testimony.

## Context

- Winstedt was a senior British colonial official in the Straits Settlements (later Director of the National Museum, Singapore); this extract from *A History of Malaya* (1935) reflects the scholarly conventions of colonial-era historiography, relying heavily on foreign (Chinese, Javanese, Portuguese) sources to bypass what he considers the unreliable Malay tradition.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its systematic critical comparison of the *Malay Annals* against external sources, establishing a framework for separating legend from verifiable history in early Singaporean historiography that remained influential for decades.
