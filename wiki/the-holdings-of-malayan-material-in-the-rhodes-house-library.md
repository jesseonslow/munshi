# The holdings of Malayan material in the Rhodes House Library Oxford

P. W. Tolmie's 1989 article provides a systematic guide to the Malayan manuscript holdings in Rhodes House Library, Oxford, spanning from the mid-nineteenth century through the 1960s. Written as a finding aid for researchers, the article's overarching purpose is to demonstrate the breadth and depth of primary sources available for the history, administration, and social life of Malaya, Sarawak, and North Borneo.

## Summary

Tolmie organises the holdings into four chronological periods—nineteenth century, pre-war twentieth century, the Japanese occupation, and the post-war era through Independence—and several document types, including official papers, memoirs, diaries, letters, interviews, and photographs. The nineteenth-century material is the thinnest, dominated by the Brooke family correspondence dating from 1841, supplemented by a handful of diaries and a single memoir. The pre-war twentieth century is far better represented, with official papers from across the Malayan Civil Service, descriptive articles on mining, housing, and public health, and a notable body of personal correspondence from officers in the Administrative, Customs, and Police branches.

The Japanese occupation period, though the shortest chronologically, is described as "proportionately the most fully covered," with diaries kept by internees and prisoners of war on the thinnest scraps of paper, lectures delivered in Changi camp, and official papers on internment conditions and health. The post-war section is similarly rich, encompassing papers on Malayanisation, the Emergency, land questions, and development schemes in Sarawak, with material extending into the early 1960s. Tolmie also identifies collections from individuals and organisations with indirect but significant connections to Malaya, including the historian Robert Heussler's papers on the M.C.S., the Colonial Office files of Arthur Creech Jones, the Fabian Colonial Bureau, and the Anti-Slavery Society's records on the Mui-Tsai system.

The article functions as a practical inventory rather than a historical argument, but its implicit thesis is that Rhodes House holds a substantial and underexploited corpus of primary material that complements the official archives in Kuala Lumpur and London, particularly for social and personal history.

### Key Findings

- The Brooke family correspondence (MSS. Pac. s. 90) is the dominant nineteenth-century collection, dating from 1841 when Sir James Brooke became the first white Raja of Sarawak, and provides detailed information on early political and administrative life in the region (p. 89).
- The Japanese occupation period is "the shortest but proportionately the most fully covered in manuscript material," including POW diaries by K. J. Ball (1942–45), J. Kellett (1942–45), and E. C. Vardy (1941–45), as well as lectures given in Changi camp by G. A. de C. de Moubray (1943) and Sir Lewis Heath (1942) (p. 92).
- J. E. Kempe's diaries as District Officer in Perak comprise 11 volumes covering 1911–1936 (MSS. Ind. Ocn. s. 94), representing one of the longest continuous personal records of a single M.C.S. officer in the collection (p. 90).
- Arthur Creech Jones' papers (MSS. Brit. Emp. s. 332) include Colonial Office correspondence and memoranda on the Malayan Emergency (1934–58), the Cession of Sarawak, and education policy (1936–39), offering a ministerial perspective on key policy decisions (p. 94).
- The Fabian Colonial Bureau's Malayan papers (MSS. Brit. Emp. s. 365, Boxes 154–160) cover 1941–1962 and include memoranda, correspondence, and Colonial Office press releases on education, industry, the constitution, the Emergency, and the Cession of Sarawak (p. 95).
- Papers by Malayans or Malaysians themselves are "few and far between," with only a 1970 interview transcript of Abdul-Rahman Putra and 1963 speeches by Tun Abdul Razak bin Hussain noted as examples (p. 94).

### Conclusion

Tolmie's definitive takeaway is that Rhodes House Library's Malayan holdings constitute a "useful and valuable source of material" for researchers, particularly for periods and angles—personal experience, social life, internment, and policy formation—that are less well served by official government archives. The article implicitly argues that the collection's strength lies in its diversity of document types and its coverage of the human dimension of colonial administration, from the earliest Brooke-era correspondence to the final years of British involvement in the 1960s.

## Context

- The article is a finding aid for Rhodes House Library's manuscript collections, which are classified under three sections: Indian Ocean (Malaya and Singapore), Pacific Ocean (Sarawak and North Borneo), and British Empire (multi-territorial collections) (p. 95, n. 1).
- Historiographically, the piece serves as a bridge between the library's holdings and the growing field of Malayan social and administrative history in the late 1980s, drawing attention to personal papers and organisational archives that supplement the more widely known official records held in the National Archives of Malaysia and the Colonial Office files at The National Archives, Kew.
