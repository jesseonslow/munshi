# Ulun-no-bokan (Murut) folklore

E. P. Gueritz's 1884 survey of British North Borneo, published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, provides a comprehensive geographical, economic, and administrative portrait of the territory under the British North Borneo Company shortly after it assumed governance in July 1882. Drawing on nearly three years of personal residence and the official reports of Company officers, the paper presents the colony's natural resources, strategic harbour value, and early fiscal performance as a case study in colonial administration.

## Summary

Gueritz's paper was prepared at the suggestion of J. S. O'Halloran, Secretary of the Royal Colonial Institute, and read on his behalf at the Montreal Meeting of the British Association for the Advancement of Science in August 1884. The work functions as both a geographical survey and a promotional document for the Company's colonial enterprise, addressing an audience of British scientific and imperial policymakers. The author systematically catalogues the territory's physical features—its mountain backbone culminating in Kina Balu at 13,698 feet, its navigable rivers, and its strategically positioned harbours—before turning to the economic potential of the land for plantation agriculture, timber extraction, and mineral exploitation.

The central argument is that British North Borneo occupies a position of "more than local importance" due to its location on the maritime route between China and Australia, and that its natural advantages in soil, waterways, and harbour facilities make it a viable colonial enterprise if properly administered. Gueritz emphasizes the strategic value of harbours such as Sandakan, Kudat, and Gaya as potential coaling stations and naval refuges, arguing explicitly that they "should not be in the hands of any foreign and possibly hostile power." The paper also addresses the social question of slavery, noting the Company's 1883 regulations that prohibited importation of slaves, barred seizure of debtors, and declared children born to slave parents after November 1883 to be free.

The article is supplemented by an editorial appendix containing remarks by A. Dent, read before the Royal Colonial Institute in May 1885, which provides updated fiscal figures and clarifies the legal basis of the Company's authority—namely, that all powers were derived from the Sultans of Brunei and Sulu, with the British Government's role limited to incorporation by Royal Charter.

### Key Findings

- The territory encompasses approximately 20,000 square miles with a coastline of about 500 miles, situated between 116°–119° East longitude and 4°–7° North latitude (p. 323).
- The Gomanton caves on the Kinabatangan river yielded an annual value of $25,000 from edible birds' nests, and the bats' guano found within was valued at £8–£15 per ton (p. 329).
- The Moara coal mines at the mouth of the Brunei River, leased by the Sultan of Brunei to Messrs. Cowie Brothers of Labuan, were working five seams of 26, 24, 6, 5, and 4 feet in thickness (p. 329).
- Fiscal revenue for 1884 showed a 60 per cent increase over 1883, while land sales decreased by 39 per cent, yielding a net total increase of 23 per cent; timber exports for 1884 amounted to $10,000 (p. 334).
- The Company's police force numbered 180 men, deemed sufficient to maintain order; the population was roughly estimated at 150,000, with Sandakan alone containing approximately 3,000 Chinese residents (pp. 332, 335).
- Slavery abolition regulations promulgated in November 1883 prohibited slave importation, barred creditor seizure of debtors, and declared all children born to slave parents after that date to be free (p. 333).

### Conclusion

Gueritz concludes that the British North Borneo Company's experiment in colonization, governed through and with the assistance of the native population, should within a reasonable time realize the expectations it has aroused by exploiting the territory's natural advantages. The supplementary remarks by A. Dent reinforce this optimism while candidly acknowledging that progress had been slower than anticipated since the charter was obtained at the end of 1881, and that the labour question remained the colony's most pressing unresolved challenge.

## Context

- The paper was read at the British Association for the Advancement of Science (Montreal, 29 August 1884) and the Royal Colonial Institute, situating it within the broader Victorian discourse on colonial administration and imperial strategy in Southeast Asia.
- The editorial appendix clarifies a common misconception about the Company's legal authority: all powers were derived from the Sultans of Brunei and Sulu, with the British Government's role confined to incorporation by Royal Charter—a distinction of considerable constitutional significance for the colony's governance (p. 335).
