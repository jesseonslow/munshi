# Gold and Indian influences in west Borneo

Tom Harrisson, Curator of the Sarawak Museum, published this comprehensive survey in 1949 to demonstrate that West Borneo—long dismissed as culturally peripheral—was in fact a major early gold-producing region in Southeast Asia whose material record reveals a complex pattern of Indian and Javanese cultural contact, concentrated in the south-west corner and strongest in the Majapahit period of the 14th century. The article synthesises approximately 115 recorded gold objects, numerous stone and ceramic artifacts, and roughly 50 golden legends to argue that Indian influence in West Borneo was largely indirect, late, and locally modified rather than the product of large-scale settlement.

## Summary

Harrisson's central problem is one of evidential poverty: Borneo's archaeological record is so fragmentary that most gold objects have been lost, stolen, or recorded only in the vaguest terms, and no single find-site can be precisely re-located. Against this backdrop, he constructs a geographical and chronological framework by dividing West Borneo into seven districts and mapping the distribution of gold objects, Indian-influenced artifacts, and golden legends against known gold-bearing geology. The result is a striking concentration in the south-west corner (Montrado–Sambas–Bau), with a secondary cluster at Brunei Bay, and a pronounced blank across the central west coast. This distribution, he argues, confirms that the main line of cultural penetration ran overland from the south and south-east coast northward and westward—the same axis along which Chinese gold-workers migrated in the 18th and 19th centuries and along which Dayak populations historically expanded.

The core of the argument rests on the Limbang hoard, which Harrisson identifies as the most informative single find after the Sambas figures. Through detailed comparison with the Franks Collection in the British Museum, the Fort Canning hoard from Singapore, and Javanese parallels identified by Coedes and Sastri, he demonstrates that the Limbang rings—particularly the collared gem ring, the fish ring, and the clam ring—belong to a Javanese craft tradition of the late Hindu (Majapahit) period, roughly the 14th century. The single inscribed ring, bearing Nagari characters read as "Araksara" or "A-Ra-Kta-Ra," provides the only datable epigraphic evidence from West Borneo and anchors the hoard to the 13th–14th century. Harrisson is careful to distinguish between the presence of objects and the presence of their makers, emphasising that small precious items are highly mobile across space and time, and that the finding of a Javanese-style ring at Limbang does not prove Javanese craftsmen were working there.

A second major thread concerns the nature of Indian influence itself. Harrisson argues against the then-prevailing assumption that early empires such as Sri-Vijaya or the Pallavas left a direct imprint on West Borneo. No early inscriptions, no Sailendra-period objects, and no Sumatran genealogical links of the kind found elsewhere in the region support such a claim. Instead, the material and legendary evidence points to a final, fading wave of Indianised culture arriving via Majapahit Java in the mid-14th century, after which Islam gradually absorbed and transformed what remained. The transition was not a violent overthrow but a slow "taking over," as Harrisson puts it, with Hindu elements persisting in Brunei ceremonial, in the veneration of megalithic stones, and in the iconography of Dayak sword-hilts that Heine-Geldern had already linked to 13th–14th century Javanese models.

### Key Findings

- Approximately 115 gold objects (excluding the Sambas hoard) have been traced in West Borneo, of which about 60 were examined in original form, 16 in replica, and 40 are known only by written reference with no surviving specimen (p. 48).
- The Limbang hoard comprises 8 rings, 2 chains, 4 ear or nose ornaments, 2–3 coat fasteners, a gold bar or buckle, gold beads, and a fragment of a Persian coin struck in 960 A.D.—the earliest positively datable object from the area (pp. 52–53, 62).
- The inscribed Limbang ring bears Nagari script dated by Sastri to the 12th–14th century and by Barnett and Coedes to the 14th–15th century; the reading "Araksara" is interpreted as a personal name or office designation (pp. 57–58).
- Crawfurd (1820) reported 32,000 men and 4,000 women working 13 large and 57 small mines at Montrado, with an estimated total Chinese population of 200,000 in the area; Raffles placed the Montrado–Sambas annual yield at 250,000 ounces in 1812 (pp. 34–35).
- The Borneo Company's highest recorded yield at Bau was 68,108 fine ounces in 1907; by 1949, over one million ounces (valued at over $35 million) had been exported from the Bau field, and Lucas estimated over $5,000,000 worth of extractable gold remained in the old tailings alone (p. 44).
- The collared gem ring from Limbang (item 9i) is "very nearly identical" to a ring in the Franks Collection (British Museum nos. 2441–4), listed as "Java" with no date, and the Limbang fish ring closely resembles the goose ring from the Fort Canning hoard, whose bracelets are attributed to "the Majapahit period at its best, i.e. the middle of the 14th Century" (pp. 66–67).

### Conclusion

Harrisson's definitive takeaway is that West Borneo received "the last gasp of the ideologies of the West, the final faint ripples"—Hindu influence just reaching as far as the Philippines and visibly petering out even in North Borneo, Arab and Mohammedan influence failing to directly convert a large majority of the population, and European influence following the same attenuating pattern. The region was not a cultural backwater but a marginal zone where successive waves of external influence arrived diluted and were absorbed into local pagan and Malay frameworks rather than imposing themselves wholesale. The gold, in this reading, was both the economic magnet that drew these contacts and the material medium through which they became visible to the archaeologist.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: Sarawak Museum catalogue and holdings (Kuching), the Franks Collection and Department of Coins and Medals (British Museum), the Raffles Museum (Singapore), and unpublished Brunei manuscripts held in the Sarawak Museum archives.
- Historiographical contribution: This is the first systematic survey of gold and Indian-influenced objects in West Borneo, correcting the prevailing assumption (exemplified by Coomaraswamy's 1947 survey, which confined itself to East Borneo) that the western half of the island was archaeologically insignificant. It establishes the south-west corner as the primary zone of Indianised material culture in the island and provides the baseline distribution data against which all subsequent Borneo archaeology has been measured.
