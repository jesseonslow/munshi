# Slavery and debt bondage

Slavery and debt bondage constituted two legally distinct but socially intertwined systems of unfree labour that persisted across the Malay Peninsula into the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In the courts of Perak, Pahang, and Johor, the institution operated under Malay customary law (*hukum adat*) in a state of perpetual tension with Islamic jurisprudence (*hukum syara*), producing a dual structure in which true slaves (*'abdi*) and debt-bondsmen (*kawan*) occupied different legal positions yet shared the practical experience of compelled service [[1, pp. 247–249]](#ref-1). The system was not a uniform phenomenon: it varied by state, by the degree of aristocratic control, and by the economic pressures of the tin-mining districts, where compounding interest mechanisms could reduce a modest obligation to lifelong servitude within a single year [[1, pp. 251–252]](#ref-1). British protectorate administration after 1874 initiated a gradual erosion of both categories, though formal legal instruments and social practices continued to reflect the older order well into the twentieth century [[2, p. 185]](#ref-2).

## Legal Categories and Customary Foundations

The foundational distinction in Malay law separated the *'abdi*—a true slave acquired through war, capture of non-Muslims, criminal surrender (*hulur*), or birth from a slave mother—from the *kawan*, a debt-bondsman who remained in principle a free man (*mardahika*) but was compelled to serve his creditor in menial labour [[1, pp. 247–249]](#ref-1). Maxwell, writing as Assistant Resident of Perak in 1890, argued that the debt-bondage system was wholly opposed to Islamic law, which the Quran explicitly renders lenient toward debtors, and was instead sustained by Malay custom in its perpetual tension with religious law [[1, p. 248]](#ref-1). The customary value of a free man in Perak was fixed at $25 (100 *bidor*), which theoretically capped any fine and entitled the offender to release upon payment of that sum, regardless of the nominal fine imposed by a Chief [[1, p. 249]](#ref-1).

In Pahang, a third category—*orang hulur*—occupied a distinct position. Linehan, drawing on an explanation from Haji Abu Bakar, Malay Secretary to the Sultan of Pahang, distinguished *orang hulur* from both *abdi* (non-Muslim slaves) and *hamba berhutang* (Malay debt-serfs): *orang hulur* were Malays adjudged insolvent and declared by the Bendahara to have suffered loss of caste, a degradation that only the raja could impose and that required formal sanction by the Bendahara for reinstatement [[2, p. 185]](#ref-2). A sealed certificate dated the eighteenth day of Muharram, year 1295 A.H., in which the Engku Besar of Pahang declared that Hassan of the Kuala had ransomed himself and was released from his degraded condition, illustrates the formal machinery through which such status changes were effected [[2, p. 185]](#ref-2).

## Economic Dynamics and the Mining Districts

The economic engine of the debt-bondage system in Perak was the tin-mining industry, particularly in the Kinta district. Maxwell documented a compounding mechanism in which a debt of one *bahara* of tin (worth approximately $30 locally) that went unpaid for six months was recalculated at the Penang export price—roughly three times the local value—creating a cycle that could enslave a debtor for life [[1, pp. 251–252]](#ref-1). This was not a theoretical possibility but an observed practice: creditors claimed the wives and children of debtors for the full amount of the debt, and daughters were given in marriage by their masters with the dower accruing to the creditor rather than reducing the original obligation [[1, p. 250]](#ref-1). Maxwell characterised this as a perversion of native custom into a tool of aristocratic oppression, far exceeding even the limits that *hukum adat* had originally prescribed [[1, p. 248]](#ref-1).

The Perak Code of Laws (*Undang-undang ka-Raja-an*) prescribed a graduated redemption scale for recaptured runaway slaves ranging from 2 *kupang* (within the town) to half the slave's full value (beyond Sedili and Trengganu), with the captor also entitled to seize small articles of trifling value carried by the fugitive [[1, pp. 269–271]](#ref-1). These provisions, while appearing to regulate the institution, simultaneously reinforced the property character of the slave and the economic incentive for recapture. The code also accorded extraordinary privileges to royal slaves (*hamba Raja*): a freeman who killed a *hamba Raja* was required to surrender himself as a hostage to the Raja, while a slave who committed the same act was to be strangled and beheaded, and if done with the master's knowledge the master was fined one *kati* and five *tahils* of silver [[1, p. 273]](#ref-1). The Johor code, published in 1855, stipulated that a free man who killed a slave of the king was fined seven times seven-fold the slave's value, or alternatively put to death or made a slave to the king for himself and his family [[1, p. 296]](#ref-1).

## Emancipation and Colonial Administration

The arrival of the British Resident in Perak after 1874 and the Perak campaign of 1875–6 had already begun to erode the system, with many slaves and debt-bondsmen quietly assuming free status [[1, p. 249]](#ref-1). Maxwell, writing in 1890, noted that approximately 3,000 slaves and debt-bondsmen remained in Perak, representing about one-sixteenth of the Malay population [[1, p. 249]](#ref-1). He proposed a graduated emancipation scheme—redemption at the customary price of a free man ($25), government-backed labour reimbursement for those unable to pay, and unconditional release after three years for the aged, women, and children—though he acknowledged that the liberation of slaves and debtors in Perak had by the time of publication "long since been effected" [[1, p. 249]](#ref-1).

The persistence of formal instruments into the twentieth century is evidenced by the Pahang reinstatement certificate of 1295 A.H. (1878 A.D.), which Linehan published in 1926 [[2, pp. 184–186]](#ref-2). The seal on the document bears the date 1275 A.H. (1858 A.D.) and styles Ahmad as Bendahara, but Ahmad did not become undisputed ruler of Pahang until 1863–64, and the seal's wording shows Ahmad no longer purporting to derive authority from Johore [[2, p. 186]](#ref-2). This anachronism, while a matter of documentary provenance, underscores the continuity of the administrative vocabulary of degradation and reinstatement well after the practical abolition of the systems it described.

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on slavery and debt bondage in the Malay Peninsula reflects a progression from colonial administrative documentation to narrower philological and archival interest. Maxwell's 1890 article in the *JSBRAS* remains the most comprehensive treatment: a work of legal scholarship grounded in the Perak Code of Laws and the Johor code, prefaced by his 1882 official minute to Parliament, and explicitly framed as a product of protectorate administration [[1, pp. 247–297]](#ref-1). Its primary source material—the *Undang-undang ka-Raja-an*, described as "the laws of Perak, Pahang and Johor"—provided an authentic native-source picture of the slave's legal condition, while the Menangkabau laws (*Undang-undang dua-belas*) yielded nothing of value on the subject [[1, p. 256]](#ref-1).

Subsequent contributions were more limited in scope. Linehan's 1926 note in the *JMBRAS* reproduced a single sealed certificate and offered a brief typological distinction between categories of unfree persons in Pahang, without engaging in the broader economic or legal analysis that Maxwell had undertaken [[2, pp. 184–186]](#ref-2). The shift in emphasis from systemic critique to documentary preservation is characteristic of the Society's later period, in which the living institution had been superseded by its archival traces. The 1977 Centenary Volume, which selected articles from both the *JSBRAS* and *JMBRAS* spanning 1878 to 1976, included material on the subject but did not advance the analytical framework established by Maxwell [[3]](#ref-3). Patrick Sullivan's 1982 study of social relations of dependence in nineteenth-century Perak represents the most recent substantive engagement with the topic in the Society's literature, situating the institution within broader patterns of patronage and obligation in the Malay state [[4]](#ref-4).
