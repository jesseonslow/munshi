# Crimes and criminals

### Scope and Historical Definition

Gang robbery, or *dacoity* as it was termed in colonial Burma and India, was a form of rural crime endemic to the central rice-growing plains and northern frontier areas of Kedah between 1909 and 1929. The gang robbers came from various ethnic groups, but the majority rose from the ranks of Malay peasants and the *Sam-Sam*, a frontier community of mixed Siamese and Malay origin who spoke both Malay and Siamese fluently [[1, pp. 98–99]](#ref-1). The English social historian E.J. Hobsbawm categorised the "social bandit" as a special type of peasant protest, distinguishing such figures from professional robbers by the perception of their local communities, who regarded them as heroes, champions and avengers rather than mere criminals [[1, p. 99]](#ref-1). Cheah Boon Kheng's 1981 study applies this framework to the Kedah outlaws Salleh Tui and Nayan, arguing that folk perceptions of these figures diverged sharply from those of state authorities, who denounced them as "dangerous criminals" [[1, p. 99]](#ref-1).

### Socio-Economic and Administrative Context

The weak rural policing in Kedah was a major contributory factor to the outbreak of crime. In 1909 the state had a 500-strong police force, but its presence diminished sharply in remote areas; large rural districts such as Kubang Pasu, Padang Terap and Kuala Muda were covered by only a few area police posts each manned by six or seven men under a corporal [[1, p. 93]](#ref-1). The abolition of the *kerah* (forced labour or corvee) system in October 1909, replaced by land-tax and land-rent, accelerated peasant indebtedness and created conditions in which many turned to robbery, theft and house-breaking, particularly during the off-season of rice cultivation [[1, pp. 104–106]](#ref-1). Cattle stealing was a crime peculiar to the border areas, facilitated by the porous Kedah–Siam frontier and the complicity of local *penghulus* (district headmen), who were required to register cattle sales and issue documents for a fee [[1, p. 96]](#ref-1). Gang robberies in Kedah peaked at 100 in 1921, compared with 107 for the entire Federated Malay States in 1920 [[1, p. 100]](#ref-1). By 1929 the police force had grown to 764 men and was reported to be "well up to the highest Malayan standards," and crime figures had dropped appreciably by 1936 [[1, p. 104]](#ref-1).

### The Social Bandits: Salleh Tui and Nayan

Salleh Tui, described by Tunku Abdul Rahman as "the greatest of all the bandits known in Kedah," operated in the Padang Terap and Kubang Pasu districts and was believed to have committed about twenty murders. He was shot dead at the Siamese frontier in December 1910 after being betrayed by an informer [[1, pp. 110–111]](#ref-1). Despite his cruelty, his folk legend endures among the frontier *Sam-Sam* communities, who recall his generosity to the villagers of Kampung Tolak, where he spent a considerable part of his takings on feasting [[1, p. 112]](#ref-1).

Nayan, born probably in 1896 in Paya Kelubi, Pendang district, rose to prominence as a bandit chief in the Kota Setar and Kuala Muda districts. He was given the military title *Panglima* (commander) and was associated with the criminal organisation of Sulaiman Kerekau, a rural entrepreneur who operated illegal gambling dens and bull-fights [[1, pp. 113]](#ref-1). The Kedah government offered a reward of $1,000 for his capture—the largest ever offered for a bandit at that time. Nayan was killed in early December 1920 at Jeneri by Panglima Hussain and Che Wang, who shared the reward approved by the State Council on 16 December 1920 [[1, pp. 120]](#ref-1). His legend as "Malaya's Robin Hood" was compiled by the Kedah-born writer Mansor Abdullah in 1960, though Cheah noted that much of Mansor's account could not be verified against oral sources [[1, pp. 113]](#ref-1).

### Research and Documentation

Cheah Boon Kheng's article, published in the *JMBRAS* in 1981, represents a significant intervention in the historiography of rural crime in colonial Malaya. Drawing on police records, Kedah Annual Reports, and extensive oral history collected from rural informants in the Kuala Nerang, Pendang, Yan and Sik districts, the study foregrounds folk perception as a counter-narrative to official colonial records [[1, pp. 98]](#ref-1). The article was presented in part at the Eighth Conference of the International Association of Historians of Asia in Kuala Lumpur in August 1980, and attracted commentary from scholars including Professor Sartono Kartodirjo, Professor Ruth McVey and Professor James C. Scott [[1, p. 130]](#ref-1). Cheah explicitly noted the absence of evidence for messianism or millenarianism in Kedah's social banditry, and challenged a claim by the Kedah historian Tuan Haji Abdullah bin Haji Abbas Nasution that the bandits' activities were connected to an anti-colonial movement led by local *ulemas* (Islamic scholars), stating he had found no evidence to substantiate this [[1, p. 130]](#ref-1). The study also raised unresolved questions about the colonial basis for classifying the *Sam-Sam* as a "criminal tribe," and about the extent to which Nayan was a genuine champion of the poor or merely an instrument of local agrarian elites [[1, p. 128]](#ref-1).
