# Sabah

Sabah, the northernmost state of Malaysia on the island of Borneo, occupies approximately 20,000 square miles of coastline and interior between the fourth and seventh parallels of north latitude [[1, p. 323]](#ref-1). Its demographic, economic, and political history is shaped by the interplay of indigenous highland and coastal communities, Chinese commercial immigration, and the timber and plantation industries that have defined its modern economy [[2, p. 63]](#ref-2), [[3, pp. 137–138]](#ref-3). The territory's name itself is a product of Victorian-era chartered-company promotion rather than an ancient indigenous designation, and its borders were assembled piecemeal from cessions by the Sultans of Brunei and Sulu between 1877 and 1902 [[4, pp. 76–83]](#ref-4), [[5, pp. 176–192]](#ref-5). From the founding of Sandakan in 1878 to the state's entry into the Malaysian federation in 1963, Sabah's development has been characterised by rapid demographic transformation, the consolidation of colonial authority over a fragmented interior, and the emergence of a timber-exporting economy that by 1970 accounted for half the state's gross national product [[3, pp. 137–138]](#ref-3), [[2, p. 63]](#ref-2).

### The Name and the Cessions

The designation "Sabah" has no basis as a pre-colonial territorial name. P.J. Rivers demonstrates that the only attested place-name in the pre-colonial period is Kampong Saba, a water village in Brunei Town situated at the downstream end of the Brunei River, where the term simply denotes position relative to the river's flow [[4, p. 76]](#ref-4). In the *Silsilah Raja-Raja Berunei* (Book of Descent of the Rajas of Brunei), the word appears exclusively as a directional term—*arah ke saba*, meaning "in the direction of downstream," which in Brunei Malay corresponds to a northeasterly bearing along the coast [[4, pp. 76–77]](#ref-4). Scholars such as Hugh Low and Amin Sweeney, working from Jawi manuscripts that lack upper-case letters, mistakenly rendered *saba* as the proper noun "Sabah," thereby creating the illusion of a territorial entity where only a directional reference existed [[4, pp. 77–78]](#ref-4).

The territorial entity was assembled through a series of cessions. The Sultan of Brunei granted the first cession to Baron von Overbeck on 29 December 1877, covering four grants from Gaya Bay to the Sibuco River for an annual payment of $15,000 [[6, pp. 163–164]](#ref-6). The Sultan of Sulu's grant followed on 22 January 1878, conferring "Datu Bandahara and Raja of Sandakan" and describing Overbeck as "supreme and independent ruler"—a translation of *maha raja* as a functional description rather than a hereditary title [[4, pp. 78]](#ref-4). I.D. Black shows that the Company's acquisition of the West Coast rivers was a slow, haggling process shaped by the internal mechanics of Brunei's territorial system, in which the nobility held *tulin* (hereditary) rights to specific rivers and followers that the Sultan could not dispose of unilaterally [[5, pp. 176–192]](#ref-5). The final cessions of 1898 and 1901 completed the Company's territorial acquisition, with the southern territories purchased for a mere $600 per year [[5, pp. 16–17]](#ref-5). The Royal Charter of 1 November 1881 was the first document to yoke "Sabah" and "North Borneo" as synonyms in the phrase "Maharajah of Sabah (North Borneo)"—the bracketed addition being an interpolation not present in the original Brunei commission [[4, pp. 82–83]](#ref-4).

The Philippine claim to Sabah, declared by President Marcos in 1968, rests on a single disputed word—*padjak*—in the 1878 Overbeck-Sulu agreement, which the English version renders as "cede" but which Filipino nationalists insist meant "lease." K.G. Tregonning rejects this reinterpretation on the grounds that the Sultan's own subsequent conduct treated the agreement as a cession, and that no international body or government ever questioned this reading for nearly a century [[6, pp. 161–170]](#ref-6). The Madrid Convention of 1885, the Treaty of Paris of 1898, the Boundary Convention of 1930, and the 1967 Philippine Constitution all fixed the Philippine boundary at nine miles off the Borneo coast, excluding Sabah [[6, pp. 167–168]](#ref-6).

### Founding and Early Administration

The founding of Sandakan in 1878 marks the beginning of sustained European settlement in the territory. William L. Pryer, formerly a bookkeeper for the Shanghai firm of Thorne and Company, was deposited at Kampong German in Sandakan Harbour on 11 February 1878 as "an animated keep off sign" while Overbeck and Dent hastened to London to float a Company [[7, pp. 35–36]](#ref-7). Pryer's main assets were an immense energy, a great interest in the country and its people, and a desire to improve their lot [[7, p. 36]](#ref-7). His diary, begun the day he landed, records the rapid consolidation of authority: by the end of his first month he had won the support of the Bajau tribe in the bay, as well as the Sulus and the *kampong* German natives [[7, p. 36]](#ref-7).

The early years were marked by crises that collectively established the authority of the British North Borneo Company in the bay. The Spanish warship *Eldorado* entered Sandakan Harbour on 4 September 1878, demanding the hoisting of the Spanish flag; Pryer's show of armed native resistance and diplomatic pressure caused the Spaniards to withdraw without returning [[7, pp. 41–42]](#ref-7). Pryer's original village was burnt to the ground on 15 June 1879, with losses including $3,000 worth of rattans belonging to Toongal and $1,000 to Hadji Basoh; he relocated to Booly Sim Sim at the harbour mouth and named the new site "Elopura" [[7, p. 42]](#ref-7). By 1891, Sandakan had grown to a population of 6,350 [[7, p. 48]](#ref-7).

The Mat Salleh revolt of 1894–1905 was less a product of indigenous resistance to colonial rule than a direct consequence of the Company's financial mismanagement under Managing Director William C. Cowie, whose grandiose infrastructure projects and new taxation created the grievances that a charismatic Bajau-Sulu leader exploited [[8, pp. 2–3]](#ref-8). The attack on Gaya Island on 9 July 1897 destroyed the largest west coast depot, and the government and *towkays* lost an estimated $100,000 [[8, p. 5]](#ref-8). The January 1900 siege of Salleh's final fort at Ranau left approximately 300 dead when the inner stronghold caught fire; Salleh himself was killed emerging from a tunnel on 31 January [[8, p. 13]](#ref-8). The ironic legacy of the rebellion was that the annexation of Gaya Bay and the founding of Jesselton (now Kota Kinabalu) were direct consequences, as the Company seized the opportunity to acquire the independent Brunei rivers that had sheltered Salleh's operations [[8, p. 6]](#ref-8).

### Economic and Demographic Transformation

The economic narrative of Sabah centres on two forces: Chinese immigration and the timber industry. Supriya Bhar documents the repeated failures of early Chinese immigration policy—Medhurst's indiscriminate recruitment of unsuccessful Hong Kong shopkeepers, the tobacco boom's collapse, and the rice tax that halted migration in 1895—before the steady flow resumed after 1903 and accelerated dramatically between 1921 and 1941 [[3, pp. 136–137]](#ref-3). The timber story runs from De Lissa's accidental first shipment in 1885 through the British Borneo Timber Company's monopoly (1920–1952) to the post-independence era in which Chinese *towkays* and indigenous contractors competed for licences [[3, pp. 137–138]](#ref-3). By 1970, forest industries accounted for $402 million of Sabah's $805 million state GNP [[3, pp. 137–138]](#ref-3).

The demographic transformation of the 1960–70 period represents a structural turning point. Lee Yong Leng shows that the total population rose from 454,421 (1960) to 651,304 (1970), an increase of 43 per cent at an annual rate of 3.5 per cent, compared with 36 per cent in the preceding decade [[2, p. 63]](#ref-2). The Chinese proportion of the total population fell from 23.0 per cent to 21.3 per cent for the first time since 1911, while the "Other Indigenous" group surged by 82 per cent, the highest growth rate of any category [[2, p. 64]](#ref-2). The construction of major highways, government land settlement schemes, and the influx of foreign labour into east coast timber and plantation industries fundamentally restructured Sabah's demographic geography, shifting the centre of growth from the west coast to the east [[2, p. 63]](#ref-2). Tawau district grew by 221 per cent over the twenty years 1951–71; the "Others" component (mainly Indonesians and Filipinos) rose from 17 per cent to 42 per cent of the district population, while the Chinese share fell from 60 per cent to 36 per cent [[2, p. 71]](#ref-2). No interior district recorded a population decrease in 1970, a marked reversal from the 1951–60 period [[2, p. 73]](#ref-2).

### Natural History and Antiquities

The natural history of Sabah has been documented since the earliest colonial expeditions. R.M. Little's 1887 report on the ascent of Kinabalu Mountain provided the first revised Western measurement of the mountain's height: his aneroid reading at the summit gap was 11,312 feet, giving a total of 11,562 feet for Victoria Peak, over 2,000 feet lower than the previously accepted 13,698 feet [[9, p. 17]](#ref-9). Little observed a "gulf of unfathomable depth" whose rim was visible through a momentary clearing of mist, and proposed a two-eruption model for the mountain's formation [[9, pp. 17–18]](#ref-9). The recovery of Sir H. Low's 1851 bottle and St. John's 1858 tin from the summit area confirmed the dates and circumstances of the two prior ascents and demonstrated the durability of paper and glass in the mountain environment [[9, pp. 20–21]](#ref-9).

R. Hanitsch's 1899 expedition, though it abandoned the summit attempt at 4,200 feet, yielded six new taxa to science, including two new genera: *Oreocalamus hanitschi* (a new genus of snake) and *Glaniopsis hanitschi* (a new genus of homalopteroid fish) [[10, pp. 67–85]](#ref-10). Hanitsch also documented the altitudinal zonation of amphibian species from the coast to 4,200 feet and the economic life of the Dusun communities along the route, including their currency practices and the role of the chief Malagup in managing the coolie party [[10, pp. 63–66]](#ref-10).

In the domain of antiquities, I.H.N. Evans documented a class of ancient shell beads found in the Tempasuk (Kota Belud) district, known to him since his first visits in 1910–1911 [[11, pp. 168–171]](#ref-11). The beads—small, short cylinders of molluscan shell, approximately 5 mm in diameter, highly polished from long use—were confirmed as molluscan through microscopic examination at the Sedgwick Museum of Geology, Cambridge [[11, p. 170]](#ref-11). Evans traces the Dusun names for the beads—*kinokogis* at Kadamaian and *kokogis* elsewhere—to roots meaning "sand" and "clam," and records a creation legend in which the male deity Kinorohingan scattered sand from the sky during a *rundukan* (seance), and the beads fell from the loosened hair of the women conducting the ritual [[11, pp. 170–171]](#ref-11).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Sabah spans nearly a century and reflects shifting priorities from colonial-era natural history and administrative reporting to modern demographic and social history. The earliest contributions, such as Dalrymple's 1884 account of the Tawaran and Putatan rivers and Gueritz's 1884 survey of British North Borneo, were framed as reconnaissance of the territory's commercial and strategic potential, drawing entirely on first-hand field observation and native testimony [[12, pp. 261–272]](#ref-12), [[1, pp. 323–335]](#ref-1). Little's 1887 report and Hanitsch's 1900 expedition account combined administrative instruments of colonial consolidation with scientific collection, reflecting the standard colonial-era model of specimen extraction and metropolitan identification [[9, pp. 1–25]](#ref-9), [[10, pp. 49–88]](#ref-10).

The mid-twentieth century saw a shift toward administrative and diplomatic history. Tregonning's biographical and political studies of Pryer, the Mat Salleh revolt, and the Philippine claim drew heavily on the British North Borneo Company's Papers in the Colonial Office, London, and functioned as both scholarly analysis and diplomatic advocacy [[7, pp. 35–50]](#ref-7), [[8, pp. 20–36]](#ref-8), [[6, pp. 161–170]](#ref-6). Black's 1968 article on the ending of Brunei rule shifted focus from the London and Brunei court to the ground-level experience of the early Residents and the local populations they governed [[5, pp. 176–192]](#ref-5).

The most recent contributions address demographic transformation and philological reappraisal. Lee's 1978 article provides the first systematic district-level comparison of the 1960–70 period, situating the territory's demographic transition within the broader context of Malaysian federation and post-war economic development [[2, pp. 63–76]](#ref-2). Rivers's 2004 article corrects a persistent anachronism in the Sabah literature—the assumption that "Sabah" was a pre-colonial territorial name—and repositions the name's origin within the politics of Victorian chartered-company promotion rather than indigenous toponymy [[4, pp. 67–99]](#ref-4). Bhar's 1980 social history of Sandakan, using newspaper reportage as a primary source for everyday experience, represents a methodological shift that has since become standard in Sabah social history [[3, pp. 120–149]](#ref-3).
