# The Malay Chetty creole language of Malacca: a historical and linguistic perspective

Noriah Mohamed's 2009 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the Malay Chetty Creole Language (MCCL) spoken by the Hindu Peranakan community of Malacca, tracing its origins to the intermarriage of South Indian Hindu merchants with local women during the Malacca Sultanate period. The article argues that MCCL is a creole that developed from bazaar Malay—the lingua franca of the Malay Archipelago's trading ports—and that its phonological and morphological features place it within a broader family of Malay-based creoles spanning from Sri Lanka to the Maluku Islands.

## Summary

The article addresses two interlocking problems: the historical origins of the Malacca Chetty community and the linguistic classification of their mother tongue. On the historical front, Mohamed challenges the "Prisoner Theory," which posits that the Chetties emerged only during British rule from Indian nationalist prisoners sent to Malacca after the 1857 Mutiny. She refutes this by citing Portuguese archival documents from 1509–11 that mention the word "Chetty" seven times and record the Chetty leader Ninachatu's cooperation with the Portuguese during the fall of Malacca, as well as Eredia's description of Kampung Keling as an established Indian quarter within Malacca's administrative district. The Chetties, she demonstrates, had been present in Malacca for over five centuries before British arrival.

On the linguistic front, Mohamed draws on fieldwork with sixty Chetty informants to document the phonological and morphological features that distinguish MCCL from standard Malay and from neighbouring dialects such as Johore and Malacca Malay. She identifies several key processes: the retention of the phoneme *a* at word-final position (whereas the Malacca dialect uses a central vowel), the deletion of *r* and *h* in various positions, the monophthongization of diphthongs *ai* → *e* and *au* → *o*, the elision of the consonant *b* in homorganic nasal clusters within trisyllabic words, and the insertion of *k* or a glottal stop at word-final position. These features, she argues, are not idiosyncratic but are shared with other Malay-based creoles—the Baba Nyonya creole, the Melayu Ambon creole, the Jakarta (Betawi) dialect, and the Malay Sri Lankan creole—pointing to a common source in the bazaar Malay of the pre-colonial trading world.

The article also explores the sociolinguistic relationship between the Chetty and Baba Nyonya communities, whose long history of intermarriage has produced significant mutual influence. Mohamed shows that many of the phonological changes in MCCL (such as the *ai* → *e* shift and the *k*-insertion) are identical to those documented in the Baba Nyonya creole, suggesting that the two languages have been in prolonged contact and may share a common bazaar Malay substrate.

### Key Findings

- Portuguese documents from 1509–11 contain the word "Chetty" seven times and record the Chetty leader Ninachatu receiving rewards from Alfonso d'Albuquerque for assisting the Portuguese during the 1511 conquest of Malacca (p. 58).
- In 1781, the Dutch government granted approximately 15,879 square feet of land to Teivanayagam Chitty, the community leader, for the construction of the Sri Poyatha Venayagar Moorthi Temple, the oldest temple in Malaysia; nine temples now exist around the Gajah Berang settlement (p. 57).
- The Chetty community in Kampung Tujoh, Gajah Berang, comprises approximately 200 members across about 30 families, representing six generations of continuous settlement (p. 55).
- A 1599 Dutch wordlist (*Vocabulaer vande Javaensche ende Malaysche Woorden*) documents the deletion of the phoneme *h* in word-initial, medial, and final positions across 24 Malay words, providing early evidence for a process still active in MCCL (pp. 62–63).
- The monophthongization of *ai* → *e* and *au* → *o* at word-final position in MCCL is identical to the process documented in the Jakarta Malay dialect (Abdul Chaer 1976), the Sarawak Malay dialect (Madzhi 1988), and the Baba Nyonya creole, supporting a common bazaar Malay origin (pp. 63–64).
- Approximately 200 Chetty families now reside in Singapore (Bedok, Tampines, Paya Lebar), having migrated during the British period from earlier concentrations around Rowell Road, Kinta Road, and Selegie Road (p. 57).

### Conclusion

Mohamed concludes that MCCL possesses a distinctive phonological profile that sets it apart from the Johore and Malacca Malay dialects, yet its shared features with the Baba Nyonya, Melayu Ambon, Jakarta, and Sri Lankan Malay creoles strongly indicate a common origin in the bazaar Malay that served as the lingua franca of the Malay Archipelago's trading ports before Western colonial disruption. The creole's continued vitality as a mother tongue, despite the community's small size and the pressures of standard Malay education and media, underscores its significance as a living witness to the pre-colonial multilingual trading culture of the region.

## Context

- The article draws on Portuguese archival documents (Pintado 1993), the 1599 Dutch wordlist from Ternate (Collins and Schmidt 1992), and Eredia's sixteenth-century description of Malacca (Mills 1997), alongside original fieldwork with sixty Chetty informants.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the growing body of work on Malay-based creoles by situating MCCL within a regional typology and by using historical linguistics to challenge the British-era "Prisoner Theory" of Chetty origins.
