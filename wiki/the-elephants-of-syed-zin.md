# The elephants of Syed Zin

J.M. Gullick's 1986 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* recounts the 1897–1900 ordeal of Syed Mohamed Zin, a member of the illustrious Hadramaut-descended Syed family of Chigar Galah in upper Perak, whose two elephants were seized by a Raman acquaintance and held for over three years before their eventual recovery through diplomatic pressure from the British colonial administration. The article uses this singular incident as a lens into the economic, social, and ceremonial role of domesticated elephants in late nineteenth-century Perak and the broader Perak–Raman boundary dispute with Siam.

## Summary

The narrative begins with a practical problem: Syed Zin's mother-in-law, Wan Fatimah, needed to travel in a manner befitting her aristocratic rank, and in the roadless upper Perak of 1897 that meant an elephant. Syed Zin owned two — a cow and her young calf — but the calf's presence made the pair unsuitable for a long journey. He arranged to borrow an elephant from Mat Amin of the neighbouring Siamese state of Raman, offering his own two elephants as security. Mat Amin returned the borrowed animal but then absconded with Syed Zin's pair, taking them into Raman territory.

What follows is a protracted legal and diplomatic struggle. Syed Zin's personal attempts to recover his elephants — first through the local chief at Kampong Betong, then through the Raja of Raman himself, accompanied by his distinguished kinsman Syed Lam — were frustrated by prevarication, jurisdictional confusion, and the interference of Siamese officials who had recently been imposed on the traditional Malay governance of the southern provinces. The case only gained traction when Syed Lam's report reached the British Resident in Perak, Frank Swettenham, who was already deeply frustrated by the unresolved Perak–Raman boundary dispute. Swettenham pressed the matter through the colonial chain of command to London and Bangkok, where it was entangled with the broader boundary negotiations. The elephants were finally handed over at the frontier on 24 May 1900, six days after King Chulalongkorn's royal yacht reached Singgora on his southern tour — a timing that Gullick suggests implies a sharp royal intervention.

Gullick then broadens the scope to examine the elephant economy of late nineteenth-century Perak: the trapping and taming of wild elephants as a royal and aristocratic pursuit, the commercial hiring of elephants to tin mines, the rising market value of domesticated animals, and the gradual displacement of elephants by bullock carts and motor vehicles. The article closes with the ceremonial persistence of elephants in Malay royal protocol well into the twentieth century, and with a final scene in which Swettenham, dining with King Chulalongkorn in Singapore in 1901, cited Syed Zin's case as evidence of the failures of the Siamese residential system in the Malay states.

### Key Findings

- A domesticated elephant was valued at $350–$450 in 1877 but as much as $1,000 by 1902, reflecting doubled market value over the quarter-century driven by tin-mining demand and the export trade to India via Penang (p. 120).
- Syed Zin's compensation claim was calculated at $400 per annum for the use of an elephant; the total settlement comprised $1,000 for 2¼ years of deprivation plus $319.28 for an additional 292 days of delay, the latter figure computed by a Siamese Foreign Ministry accountant (pp. 117, 118).
- The Perak–Raman boundary dispute involved approximately 2,500 square miles of Perak territory absorbed by Raman during Perak's civil wars of the 1850s; the dispute was eventually settled by a compromise agreement dated 29 November 1899 (pp. 115–116, n. 16).
- Mayer's account of a single trapping operation in Trengganu involved a herd of 60 elephants, three men killed and a dozen injured during the drive, and only 16 animals ultimately exported after months of hobbled, starved taming (p. 119).
- Sultan Idris of Perak arrived at the inaugural FMS Federal Council in December 1909 at the head of a procession of nearly fifty elephants, of which about twenty knelt simultaneously before the building (p. 120).
- An elephant could consume up to 30 complete coconut fronds per day and cover roughly 15 miles in a day at 3 miles per hour (pp. 120–121).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that Syed Zin's story, while appearing as a minor anecdote with an unusual happy ending, is in fact a valuable fragment for reconstructing the economic and social history of elephants in Perak. The article's most unique contribution is the documented income figure of $400 per annum for elephant hire — a data point absent from other contemporary sources — which anchors the elephant economy in quantifiable terms. More broadly, Gullick presents Syed Zin as a representative figure of the Perak aristocracy: courteous, persistent, proud of lineage, and conscious of the demands of *ta'patut*, ultimately reunited with his elephants on 24 May 1900.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on Straits Settlements Despatches (SSD) and Colonial Office papers (CO 273 series) held in the Public Record Office, London, supplemented by published accounts of Birch, Swettenham, Low, Mayer, and Clifford.
- Historiographically, the piece sits within Gullick's broader project of recovering the social and economic texture of colonial Perak from administrative records, adding a human-scale narrative to the institutional history of the Perak–Raman boundary dispute.
