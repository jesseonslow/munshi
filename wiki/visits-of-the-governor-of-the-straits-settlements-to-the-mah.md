# Visits of the Governor of the Straits Settlements to the Maharajah of Johor in 1874, 1880, and 1882. Facsimile reprint

This 2022 JMBRAS publication, edited under the "Documents from Malaysian History" series, reprints three primary-source accounts of official visits by the Governor of the Straits Settlements to the Maharajah of Johor between 1874 and 1882. The documents illuminate the diplomatic protocols, economic ambitions, and evolving political relationship between the Crown Colony of Singapore and the independent Malay state of Johor during the critical period when Britain was extending its influence over the peninsular Malay states through the Resident system.

## Summary

The collection opens with an anonymous 1874 account published in the *Siam Repository*, describing Governor Sir Andrew Clarke's first visit to Johor on 10 March 1874. This visit occurred just two months after the Pangkor Engagement of 20 January 1874, which placed a British Resident in Perak and inaugurated the protectorate system that would eventually encompass nine peninsular states. The account is rich in ceremonial detail—the 17-gun salute, the lantern-lit procession, the addresses from European and Chinese residents—and captures the public rhetoric of the occasion. Clarke's response to the European residents' address, reproduced in full, is notable for its explicit commitment to "diffusing the advantages of civilization" and its framing of Johor's development as a model for the wider peninsula. The presence of James Wheeler Woodford Birch in Clarke's entourage, who would be appointed the first British Resident in Perak in November 1874 and assassinated there in November 1875, lends the visit an additional layer of political significance.

The second document is Governor Frederick Weld's official despatch of 23 November 1880 to the Earl of Kimberley, reporting his visit to Johor on the occasion of the opening of the Maharajah's new Istana. By this time, Selangor and Sungei Ujong had joined Perak in accepting British Residents, and the despatch reveals how the Johor relationship was being leveraged as a showcase of successful "civilising" partnership. The addresses presented to Weld—from European planters, the Arab community, Chinese merchants, and the Malayan Gambier and Pepper Society—function as veiled petitions: the planters sought assistance with labour importation, the Arabs requested "good laws," and the Chinese merchants invoked the protective power of the British Government. Weld's replies, particularly to the European planters, confirm that he was actively pursuing Indian immigration schemes for Johor's new agricultural enterprises.

The third document, a brief despatch of 3 October 1882, records an informal follow-up visit in which Weld inspected the Maharajah's tea, Liberian coffee, and cocoa plantations, a brick-making establishment that had already exported to Bombay, and new infrastructure including hospital wards, a gaol, and telephonic communication with Singapore. The despatch also notes the Maharajah's consent to share costs for a new jetty at Kranji, the terminus of the Singapore road opposite the Istana.

### Key Findings

- The 1874 visit occurred on 10 March, just 49 days after the Pangkor Engagement (20 January 1874), and Clarke's entourage included J.W.W. Birch, who was appointed first British Resident in Perak on 4 November 1874 and assassinated on 2 November 1875 (p. 105).
- The 1880 banquet seated 76 persons, including the Maharajah, the Bandahara of Pahang, the Chief Justice, and the Colonial Secretary; the Maharajah's family and those of the Chief Justice and Mr. Shelford M.L.C. were guests at the Istana for the week, with an average of 65 dining each evening (p. 108).
- The European planters' address of 15 November 1880 explicitly requested government assistance with "the importation of the necessary classes of labour," and Weld confirmed in his reply that he had already made representations to the Home Government regarding Indian immigration (pp. 109–110).
- By 1882, the Maharajah's brick-making company at Johor had exported bricks to Bombay, and telephonic communication between Johor and Singapore had been established by joint conference of the two governments (p. 115).
- The Maharajah consented in 1882 to bear a share of the expenses for constructing a new iron jetty at Kranji, the terminus of the Singapore road, which was "just opposite his Istana" (p. 115).
- The 1880 despatch (CO 273/105/19791) and the 1882 despatch (CO 273/116/19620) are both held in the Colonial Office series at The National Archives, Kew.

### Conclusion

The three documents collectively trace the arc of the Singapore–Johor relationship from a ceremonial affirmation of friendship in 1874, through a more transactional phase in 1880 where economic interests (labour, land, law) were openly articulated, to a mature partnership by 1882 characterised by shared infrastructure and joint fiscal responsibility. The Maharajah of Johor emerges not as a passive recipient of British influence but as an active agent who used his relationship with the Governor to secure concrete material benefits for his state while maintaining his formal independence.

## Context

- The primary documents derive from the *Siam Repository* (1874, Vol. 6) and the Colonial Office despatch series (CO 273/105 and CO 273/116) at The National Archives, Kew.
- The publication contributes to the historiography of early British–Malay diplomatic relations by providing the full text of addresses and replies that are otherwise scattered across archival collections, and by situating the Johor relationship within the broader timeline of the Resident system's expansion.
