# Johor

Johor is a Malay state on the southern tip of the Malay Peninsula whose historical significance extends well beyond its present territorial boundaries. From the early modern period through the nineteenth century, the Johor polity functioned as a major commercial and political node in the Straits of Malacca, its influence reaching across the water to Singapore, Riau, and the eastern coast of the peninsula [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2). The state's economic foundations rested on the cultivation and export of gambier and pepper, the extraction of tin from its interior rivers, and its position as a convergence point for maritime, riverine, and overland trade routes [[3]](#ref-3), [[4]](#ref-4), [[2]](#ref-2). Johor's political history is marked by a succession of rulers who navigated the competing pressures of Portuguese, Dutch, British, and Chinese commercial and military power, and whose diplomatic manoeuvres—ranging from the 1718 succession crisis to Sultan Abu Bakar's 1885 European tour—shaped the state's trajectory from an independent sultanate to a British protectorate [[5]](#ref-5), [[6]](#ref-6), [[7]](#ref-7).

## Early History and Toponymic Origins

The earliest references to the territory that would become Johor derive from Chinese, Javanese, Tamil, and Siamese sources spanning the seventh to the fifteenth centuries. G.P. Rouffaer, in a 174-page study published in 1921 and summarised in English by R.O. Winstedt in 1922, attempted to trace a continuous toponymic chain linking the seventh-century *Mo-ho-hsin* recorded by the Chinese pilgrim I-Tsing (690) with *Mahasin*, a Malay land mentioned in a Majapahit inscription of 1034, and ultimately with *Tasik* or *Temasik* of the fourteenth century and *Singapura* of the fifteenth [[1, pp. 257–258]](#ref-1). Rouffaer further proposed that *Wurawari* (Old Javanese for "clear water"), attested in inscriptions of the tenth and eleventh centuries, was linked to *Ganggayu* and ultimately to Johor itself [[1, p. 258]](#ref-1). The Tanjore inscription of Rajendracola I (1030) lists *Kadaram* (Kedah), *Srivijayam* (Palembang), *Malayur* (Jambi), and *Mayirudingam*, which Rouffaer equated with Mahasin or Singapore [[1, p. 258]](#ref-1).

Rouffaer's reconstruction was explicitly polemical, directed against G. Ferrand's 1918 theory that Malacca had existed as a major polity for seven centuries before the Portuguese arrival. Rouffaer argued that the name *Malayu* (Marco Polo's *Malayur*) referred originally to the Malay homeland in Jambi, not to the Straits settlement, and that Malacca existed as a minor polity by the mid-thirteenth century but only became a significant commercial centre around 1400, following immigration of Malays from Singapore or Tumasik [[1, p. 257]](#ref-1). The *Pararaton* records a *dewa-putera* named Tuhan Wuruju of *Pamelekahan* ("Malacca lands") as a captive in Majapahit in 1328, while Siamese laws enacted in 1360 cite "Ujong Tanah, *Malaka*, *Malayu*, Worawari" as tributary to Siam [[1, p. 257]](#ref-1). Winstedt noted that a Singapore monolith fragment in Raffles' Museum bore lettering that Dr. Krom judged to resemble Majapahit characters and to antedate 1360 [[1, p. 258]](#ref-1).

The early seventeenth century saw the Johor polity at the centre of a complex intermodal trading world. Peter Borschberg's 2019 analysis of three maps by the Eurasian cartographer Manuel Godinho de Erédia (1563–1623), found in his *Miscellany Atlas* (c.1616–22), reveals that the region was far more densely connected than conventional narratives have suggested. Erédia's maps depict Singapore not as a forgotten island but as a strategic gatekeeper community situated within the greater Johor River estuary, functioning as an intermodal port where maritime, riverine, and overland routes converged [[2, pp. 12–13]](#ref-2). The maps show a denser network of overland trails along the eastern coast, with only one major route serving the west coast, and reveal north–south trading axes running from Phattalung on the Isthmus of Kra to the lower Johor River region [[2, pp. 4–6]](#ref-2). Perak yielded more than 300 *bahar* of tin annually (approximately 54–60 metric tons), and the Linggi River produced more than 100 *bahar* (approximately 18–20 metric tons) per year, both under the supervision of the *shahbandar* of Rembau, vassal of the king of Johor [[2, pp. 9–11]](#ref-2). When the Selden map's maritime routes are projected alongside Erédia's riverine and overland networks onto a modern base map, the data show that routes converged near the southern tip of the Malay Peninsula between Singapore and Tioman, at Pahang, and around Patani, with the west coast and Portuguese Melaka featuring far fewer instances of route interfacing [[2, pp. 23–24]](#ref-2).

## The Kangchu System and Chinese Plantation Economy

The most extensively documented economic institution of Johor in the nineteenth century was the *kangchu* system, a semi-feudal administrative arrangement governing Chinese pepper and gambier plantation settlements from the 1830s until its legislative abolition in 1917 [[4, pp. 247–263]](#ref-4). The system originated in the *surat sungai* (river document), a land grant issued by the Ruler of Johor to Chinese capitalists who undertook to open gambier and pepper plantations up named rivers. The holder of such a document, the *tuan sungai*, established a village headquarters called a *kangkar* (literally "feet of the river") and was recognised as the head of the settlement [[4, p. 247]](#ref-4). As the arm of government was short in these isolated river communities, the Ruler formally invested the *kangchu* with powers, duties, and emoluments through a letter of authority (*tauliah*), effectively making him a local magistrate, revenue collector, and agricultural supervisor in one [[4, p. 247]](#ref-4).

Carl A. Trocki's 1976 study demonstrated that the *kangchu* system was not a Johor-specific phenomenon of the mid-nineteenth century, as earlier scholarship had assumed, but the product of a long evolution from Bugis-ordered plantations at Riau dating to approximately 1740 [[3, pp. 132–155]](#ref-3). Trocki traced a continuous developmental arc through the catastrophic disruption of 1784, when the Dutch destroyed the Riau entrepôt and the Malay and Bugis rulers fled, leaving the Chinese planters to govern themselves for over three decades [[3, p. 132]](#ref-3). The shift of the gambier marketplace to Singapore after 1819, the removal of the British import tariff on gambier in 1835, and the subsequent land and labour crises in Singapore's interior together drove the migration of planters across the strait into Johor, where the Temenggong formally recognised their settlements through *surat sungai* grants beginning in 1844 [[3, pp. 132–133]](#ref-3). The earliest *surat sungai* in the Johor Archives is dated 9 October 1844, for Sungai Sekudai; the second, dated 22 October 1844, granted Sungai Tebrau to Kapitan Tan Kee Soon [[3, pp. 144–145]](#ref-3). By June 1845, 62 plantations were operating on four rivers with an estimated population of 500 [[3, pp. 144–145]](#ref-3).

The internal economy of the *kangkar* revolved around a relationship between estate owners (often labourers without capital) and mortgagees (*pajak*), who advanced money and supplies in return for a floating charge over the plantation's products. The *kangchu* stood as intermediary, supervising both parties, recording transactions in a register, and adjudicating disputes [[4, p. 251]](#ref-4). His remuneration took the form of five monopoly rights—public gaming, pawnbroking, spirits, pork, and opium—plus a commission of 12 cents per *pikul* on gambier and pepper exports and on rice imports [[4, pp. 251]](#ref-4). The *Kanun Kangchu* (Law of the Kangchus), codified in 1873 under Sultan Abu Bakar, was an 81-clause regulatory code that governed everything from plantation inspection and marriage registration to the weighing of export produce and the maintenance of a Malay policeman at each *kangkar* [[4, pp. 251]](#ref-4). A *kangchu*'s punitive powers were capped at six strokes of the *rotan*, three days in the stocks, and a fine of $10; transgression of the regulations by the *kangchu* himself carried escalating fines of $100, $500, and $1,000 before dismissal [[4, pp. 253]](#ref-4).

The system's expansion was driven by acute economic pressures. Gambier prices at Singapore fluctuated dramatically: $1.30–1.75 per *pikul* in January 1831, rising to $4.00–5.00 in January 1834, then falling to $0.90–1.00 by January 1849 before recovering to $2.85 by 1861 [[3, p. 141]](#ref-3). In 1836, Singapore's gambier trade totalled 39,624 *pikuls* (22,000 local production plus 19,624 imports, mostly from Riau), with exports distributed to Great Britain (9,921 *pikuls*), Celebes (9,961), Calcutta (7,096), and other destinations [[3, p. 139]](#ref-3). The direct catalyst for the system's migration to Johor was the Ngee Heng secret society's decision in 1846 to order approximately 4,000 planters to leave Singapore for Johor in retaliation against government plantation assessments [[3, pp. 143–144]](#ref-3). By 1862, approximately 37 *surat sungai* had been issued covering 32 *kangkar* settlements: 18 rivers in the Johor Straits, 19 on the Johor River, and 2 on the west coast [[3, pp. 150–151]](#ref-3).

The system's decline was both economic and administrative. Pepper and gambier exports had fallen sharply in the years before 1917, and many *kangchu* had grown negligent in discharging their obligations [[4, p. 262]](#ref-4). The *Kangchu Rights (Abolition) Enactment* of 1917 provided that all *kangchu* rights should cease on 31 December 1917, with compensation fixed at five times the annual net profits derived from the rights over the preceding three years [[4, p. 262]](#ref-4). Records showed 66 *kangkar* in existence at abolition, though A.E. Coope, the Member of the Civil Service who documented the transition, could trace only 40 claims; nearly one million dollars was paid in total compensation [[4, p. 262]](#ref-4). The *Kangkar* of Kambau received over $90,000 in compensation—the largest sum for any single river—and had operated its own private currency, issued c. 1908 with the latest issue in 1927 by Waterlows, in denominations of $1 and 10 cents [[4, pp. 262–263]](#ref-4).

## Diplomatic Relations and the Question of Sovereignty

Johor's diplomatic history is characterised by a persistent tension between its formal independence and the growing influence of European powers. The 1718 succession crisis, documented in the only known European eyewitness narrative by Captain Joao Tavares de Vellez Guerreiro, illustrates the state's military capacity and the complex interplay of external powers. The *Jornada de Antonio de Albuquerque Coelho* records that the Raja Muda commanded over 100 river galleys, a total fleet exceeding 1,000 armed boats, more than 1,000 pieces of bronze ordnance ranging from 2-pounders to 24-pounders, over 2,000 guns, and 5,000 armed men in the Court alone [[5, pp. 120–121]](#ref-5). The Portuguese Governor's intervention—blockading the river mouth, seizing and executing the pretender's scouts, and providing tactical counsel for the capture of the Datuk Bandar's fortress—secured a treaty signed on 7 March 1718 [[5, pp. 142–145]](#ref-5). The episode also illuminates the continued prestige Portugal commanded among the Malays nearly eighty years after the fall of Malacca to the Dutch in 1641, as evidenced by the King's personal intervention to protect Portuguese personnel and the granting of a church site at Johor Lama [[5, pp. 142–145]](#ref-5).

By the 1870s and 1880s, the relationship between Johor and Britain had shifted from one of mutual commercial interest to a more structured diplomatic framework. The 1874 visit of Governor Sir Andrew Clarke to Johor, occurring just 49 days after the Pangkor Engagement that inaugurated the British Resident system in Perak, was explicitly framed as a model of "civilising" partnership [[7, p. 105]](#ref-7). Clarke's response to the European residents' address committed to "diffusing the advantages of civilization" and framed Johor's development as a template for the wider peninsula [[7, p. 105]](#ref-7). Governor Frederick Weld's 1880 despatch, reporting his visit on the occasion of the opening of the Maharajah's new Istana, reveals how the Johor relationship was being leveraged as a showcase of successful partnership: the addresses presented to Weld from European planters, the Arab community, Chinese merchants, and the Malayan Gambier and Pepper Society functioned as veiled petitions, with the planters seeking assistance with labour importation and the Chinese merchants invoking the protective power of the British Government [[7, pp. 109–110]](#ref-7). By 1882, the Maharajah's brick-making company had exported bricks to Bombay, and telephonic communication between Johor and Singapore had been established by joint conference of the two governments [[7, p. 115]](#ref-7).

Sultan Abu Bakar's 1885 visit to Italy represents a calculated diplomatic manoeuvre designed to bolster his claim to sovereign status ahead of his audience with Queen Victoria, where he ultimately obtained the coveted title of Sultan [[6, pp. 43–54]](#ref-6). The audiences with both King Umberto I and Pope Leo XIII took place on 24 April 1885; the *Osservatore Romano* reported the Papal audience on 25 April, describing Abu Bakar as an "independent Sovereign living in the Malacca Peninsula" [[6, p. 50]](#ref-6). The Congregation de Propaganda Fide's recommendation for the audience was grounded in Abu Bakar's documented support for the Catholic mission in Johore, including the granting of land for a church officially opened on 29 May 1883 [[6, pp. 48–49]](#ref-6). The news of the Papal audience was celebrated in Johor with a toast, and Father Saleilles "was announcing everywhere that the King had gone to see his Great Priest in Rome" [[6, p. 52]](#ref-6).

## The Island Periphery and Natural History

The island chain off the east coasts of Johore and Pahang formed a peripheral zone of economic and cultural activity that remained largely unknown to mainland residents due to the violence of the north-east monsoon [[8, p. 78]](#ref-8). H.G. Dalton's 1928 account of an eleven-day voyage in May 1918, undertaken for the purpose of collecting shells, coral, and butterflies, provides a rare first-hand survey of these islands. The voyage covered approximately 188 miles, visiting in sequence Pulau Sribuat, Pulau Tiuman, Pulau Pemanggil, Pulau Aur, Pulau Tinggi, and Pulau Babi [[8, pp. 78–96]](#ref-8). The islands were characterised by steep jungle-covered hills rising abruptly from the sea, with rocky northern shores built to withstand the monsoon and sandy western beaches supporting coconut plantations [[8, p. 78]](#ref-8).

The most significant cultural encounter occurred at Pulau Aur, where the party arrived during a festival to propitiate evil spirits—a ceremony that took place only once every eight or nine years. The islanders had constructed a small sago-palm boat decorated with flowers and coconut leaves (*Puchok*), which was to be set adrift laden with gifts to carry the ghosts of the island away [[8, p. 89]](#ref-8). Dalton and his crew were effectively held as guests of the local Customs Officer, prohibited from collecting shells, killing fish, or going to sea until the ceremony was completed [[8, p. 89]](#ref-8). The island had approximately 200 inhabitants and possessed four cannon believed to have been taken from a shipwreck; a Dutch silver coin dated 1792 was sold to Dalton by the *Penghulu*, suggesting the wreck may date to roughly a century prior rather than the 200 years claimed locally [[8, p. 91]](#ref-8).

The economic life of the islands was modest but functional. Edible birds' nests were collected and exported from Pulau Aur, Pulau Tinggi, and Pulau Babi; copra and dried fish formed the main livelihood of island populations, supplemented by exports of *Siput Gewang* and *Siput Lolak* shells, *Kayu Kemuning* (used for kris sheaths), and *Kayu Penaga* [[8, p. 95]](#ref-8). Pulau Tinggi's Gunong Tinggi rises to 2,000 feet and had a trigonometrical beacon erected in 1917; the island contained about 100 regular residents [[8, p. 92]](#ref-8). The failed Endau Development Company saw-mill at Penyabong, with its galvanised-iron factory building and staff bungalows, had been dismantled by 1918, and the dilapidated state of Kuala Endau on the mainland underscored the limited reach of commercial development into the region [[8, p. 79]](#ref-8).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Johor spans nearly a century and reflects shifting priorities in the study of the region. The earliest contributions, such as Rouffaer's 1921 paper summarised by Winstedt in 1922, represent a Dutch colonial-era attempt to reframe Peninsular history through Javanese and South Asian source traditions rather than relying on the *Sejarah Melayu* or Portuguese accounts, and directly challenge the then-dominant French school on the antiquity of Malacca [[1]](#ref-1). The 1935 publication of Hughes's translation of the 1718 Portuguese account filled a significant gap in the historiography of eighteenth-century Johor, for which no contemporary European account previously existed; the translation was checked by R.O. Winstedt, then General Adviser to Johore, whose *History of Johore* (1933) had just been published [[5, p. 113]](#ref-5).

The 1936 article by Coope, written from within the colonial administration, treats the *kangchu* system as a closed chapter superseded by modern land law, and carries the tone of a colonial official recording a now-extinct institution with mild admiration for the Chinese pioneers but clear satisfaction at the system's demise [[4]](#ref-4). Trocki's 1976 study fundamentally revised this interpretation by demonstrating the system's deep roots at Riau from the 1740s and its centrality to the Singapore economy throughout the nineteenth century, correcting the earlier assumption (following Coope, 1936) that it was a Johor-specific institution of limited significance [[3, p. 130]](#ref-3). Trocki was the first to use systematically the *Buku Daftar Surat-surat Keterangan Berkebun* (1844–1903) in the Johor Archives, which had only become available to scholars in the 1960s [1, p. 130, n. 6].

More recent contributions have expanded the scope of inquiry. Borschberg's 2019 analysis of Erédia's maps represents the first systematic treatment of these cartographic documents as economic and resource sources, and the first integration of their route data with the Selden map to produce a composite picture of intermodal trade networks in the Straits region for the early seventeenth century [[2]](#ref-2). Candilio and Bressan's 2000 study of Sultan Abu Bakar's 1885 Italian visit draws on Italian diplomatic, Vatican, and missionary archives to document an episode previously unexamined in English-language scholarship, contributing to the historiography of Italian-Malay relations and the Vatican's diplomatic engagement with non-Christian rulers [[6]](#ref-6). The 2022 reprint of the Governor's visit accounts under the "Documents from Malaysian History" series provides the full text of addresses and replies that are otherwise scattered across archival collections, situating the Johor relationship within the broader timeline of the Resident system's expansion [[7]](#ref-7). Winstedt's *A History of Johore* (1933, reprinted 1979) remains the foundational synthetic work, though the records available here do not permit detailed assessment of its individual chapters [[9]](#ref-9).
