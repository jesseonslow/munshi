# The ‘discovery’ of Penang Island at Tanjong Tokong before 1785: Bapu Alaidin Meera Hussein Lebai and Captain Francis Light

S. Nagata's brief ethnographic note, published in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 2013, describes the *tageh* sing ritual of the Kensiw Orang Asli of Kedah based on fieldwork conducted in the early 1970s near Baling. The article argues that *tageh* constitutes a distinct genre of religious performance, separate from the shamanistic *pano'* seance, and is unique to the Kensiw among Malaysian Orang Asli groups.

## Summary

Nagata situates the *tageh* within Geoffrey Benjamin's framework of regional differentiation in Orang Asli religious practice, where western groups specialise in seasonal-fruit shamanism and eastern groups in tiger shamanism. The Kensiw, however, possess a third category: a communal singing ritual that does not involve shamanistic possession. The *tageh* is performed on the seventh night after a death, during a *kenduri* that marks the end of the mourning period, and must be conducted in total darkness so as not to frighten the *cenoy* (sky spirits) who are believed to join the singing. The purpose of the performance is to invite these spirits and sing for the well-being of all participants.

The ritual is led by a *cao neges* (sing leader) who opens by inhaling smoke from the embers of a specific forest plant — identified botanically as *Dianella ensifolia* (Liliaceae), locally called *heli kasay* — and blowing it in four directions and skyward to address Tapn (Thunder-God) and request protection of the throat for the all-night singing. The smoke functions as a communicative medium to "seize" (*merampas*) *tageh* from heaven, a concept Nagata links to the Temiar notion of fragrance as a vehicle for spiritual transport. The musical accompaniment is a horizontal bamboo tube (5–6 feet long, 5-inch diameter) called *lebeh* or *gendang*, struck with a sandal-shaped beater of woven *palas* palm; no woman plays this instrument.

Nagata draws on his own field notes, Schebesta's monographs on the Negritos of Asia, Benjamin's unpublished doctoral dissertation, and van der Sluys's work on the Jahay to distinguish the *tageh* from the *pano'* ceremony, which the Kensiw themselves treat as a separate practice with its own songs, prohibitions, and shamanic logic.

### Key Findings

- The *tageh* is performed on the seventh night (*hay tujoh*) after a death; additional *kenduri* may occur on the 14th, 16th, 20th, 40th, and 50th days, but the seventh is mandatory (pp. 79–80).
- The ritual must be conducted in total darkness; participants reported that camera flash caused throat pains and that recording was objected to because *cenoy* would hear it (p. 80).
- The embers are made from *Dianella ensifolia* (Family: Liliaceae), identified by a botanist at Universiti Sains Malaysia from a specimen collected in the Kedah forest (p. 81).
- The *lebeh* (bamboo drum) is 5 inches in diameter and 5–6 feet long, held horizontally and struck with a beater of woven *palas* palm (*pano' palas*); no woman plays it (p. 81).
- The *cao neges* is described as *sedikit hala'* ("a little bit shaman") because he can play the drum and sing, but he is not a full tiger shaman (p. 81).
- The decline of *tageh* performance is attributed in part to complaints from surrounding Malay peasants about the noise of singing and bamboo drums (p. 80, n. 29).

### Conclusion

Nagata concludes that while Orang Asli healing and dream songs are widely disseminated and subject to cross-group borrowing, the *tageh* sing ritual appears to be unique to the Kensiw, with no other group exhibiting a comparable performance, and may therefore represent one polar type within the broader genre of Orang Asli ritual music.

## Context

- Primary source base: Nagata's own field notes (1971–2001, University of Toronto), supplemented by Schebesta's 1954 and 1957 monographs, Benjamin's 1967 Cambridge dissertation, and van der Sluys's 1992 Leiden report on the Jahay.
- The article contributes a previously undocumented ritual genre to the ethnographic record of western Malaysian Orang Asli, filling a gap between the well-studied *pano'* shamanism and the Temiar dream-song tradition documented by Roseman.
