# Towns and town planning

## Towns and Town Planning

The physical form of Malaysian towns is the product of layered interventions—colonial administration, immigrant labour, market forces, and post-independence state policy—that together produced a built environment of remarkable diversity. From the tin-boom reconstruction of Kuala Lumpur in the 1880s to the garden-city planning of the Cameron Highlands, from the organic shophouse terraces of Georgetown to the post-war new town of Petaling Jaya, the region's urban landscapes reflect distinct planning logics operating in different periods and under different political regimes. These towns are not merely containers of economic activity but active agents in shaping settlement patterns, ethnic geographies, and environmental relationships across the Malay Peninsula and Borneo.

## Colonial Town Formation and the Building Industry

The emergence of a formal building industry in the western Malay states between 1875 and 1914 was, as J. M. Gullick demonstrates, a direct consequence of colonial intervention rather than an organic development [[1]](#ref-1). Pre-colonial Malay building was a craft embedded in village reciprocity: houses were sized by the number of posts (*tiang*, literally "posts"), joined without nails using mortise-and-wedge joints, and erected through communal mobilisation. Even the ruling class's larger residences were essentially scaled-up versions of the village model, requiring the temporary conscription of labour rather than the employment of specialists [[1, pp. 80–82]](#ref-1). When colonial administrators arrived in the mid-1870s, they had no building industry to draw upon. Swettenham's first residence at Bandar Langat was a leaking stockade; Birch had to import house-builders from Province Wellesley because, in his words, "it is impossible to get Perak Malays to work" on the required scale [[1, pp. 80–82]](#ref-1).

The industry matured through three broad phases. The first, in the late 1870s and early 1880s, saw the establishment of rudimentary Public Works Departments staffed by surveyors and draughtsmen transferred from the Straits Settlements, and the first use of the "transitional Malay house" model—brick pillars supporting a timber upper storey—which required Chinese masons and imported bricks [[1, pp. 85–86]](#ref-1). The second phase, driven by the tin price rise of 1879 and the relocation of the Selangor capital to Kuala Lumpur in 1880, produced a building boom in which the city was rebuilt street by street in brick and tile [[1, pp. 86–87]](#ref-1). The 4 January 1881 fire in Kuala Lumpur, which left 500 people homeless and caused an estimated $100,000 in damage, catalysed the decision to rebuild the town's core in fire-resistant materials [[1, p. 86]](#ref-1). Houses built in Kuala Lumpur numbered 234 in 1884, 218 in 1885, 159 in 1886, and 180 in 1888; brick kilns in Selangor rose from 15 to 33 and lime kilns from 8 to 16 between 1886 and 1888 [[1, pp. 86–87]](#ref-1). The third phase, from the mid-1880s onward, was marked by the introduction of Neo-Saracenic architecture—beginning with the Bangunan Sultan Abdul Samad in 1897—the establishment of a PWD factory powered by a 40-hp steam engine to process timber and metal, and the increasing use of imported iron, steel, and cement in bridges, waterworks, and commercial buildings [[1, pp. 85–91]](#ref-1). The Connaught Bridge (1890), which carried the railway across the Klang River on cast iron cylinders driven approximately 60 feet to bedrock, exemplified the scale of engineering now possible; its metal components were fabricated at Stockton-on-Tees and assembled by Howarth Erskine [[1, p. 90]](#ref-1). By 1913, J. A. Russell paid more than $1.5 million for Yau Tet Shin's 300-house "new town" estate in Ipoh, illustrating the scale of property investment that the building industry had made possible [[1, p. 94]](#ref-1).

## The Shophouse and Legislative Urban Form in Penang

Georgetown's urban fabric offers a contrasting model of town formation, in which the built environment was shaped less by a single administrative act than by the gradual accumulation of fire-prevention and public-health legislation under the Straits Settlements colonial government. Mai Lin Tjoa-Bonatz traces the transformation of the Penang shophouse from a Chinese courtyard-house form to a British-influenced terrace house, arguing that this shift was driven not by top-down planning but by regulatory pressure responding to the physical realities of rapid urbanisation [[2]](#ref-2).

The origins of the shophouse remain contested. Tjoa-Bonatz reviews three principal hypotheses: that the form derives from Chinese building traditions brought by migrants from the Fukien and Kwangtung provinces of South China; that it first appears with European colonisation, traceable to Portuguese or Dutch urban planning principles; and that it pre-dates the European presence as the traditional dwelling of Southeast Asia connected to market-places along the streets [[2, pp. 123–125]](#ref-2). The term "shophouse" itself first appears in Penang's administrative records in 1884, not in the post-war period as commonly assumed [[2, p. 125]](#ref-2). The five-foot-way, a defining feature of the shophouse frontage, originated in the Penang Assessment Regulation VI of 1826, which specified a 5-foot space between house and drain, predating Raffles' 1822 instructions for Singapore [[2, p. 126]](#ref-2).

The early settlement was characterised by great diversity of building styles and materials. After the severe fires of 1808, 1812, 1813, and 1818, fire-prevention measures were proposed, including set-backs in front of attap houses, open fire breaks between rows of houses, and the realignment and straightening of streets [[2, p. 126]](#ref-2). The use of attap was formally forbidden by a regulation passed on 10 July 1817, and thereafter attap-roofed houses were discouraged by a higher assessment rate [[2, p. 126]](#ref-2). By 1862, John Thomson recorded a town substantially transformed to a more fire-proof city with the predominance of two-storey shophouses: "We pass along long lines of low, red-tiled, dirty buildings, tenanted by Chinese and natives of Hindostan. The lines seem interminable" [[2, p. 127]](#ref-2).

The construction industry was dominated by Chinese craftsmen. The 1881 census recorded 1,362 carpenters in Penang, of whom 1,276 were Chinese; 278 bricklayers and masons, of whom 252 were Chinese; and 123 stonemasons, of whom 90 were Chinese [3, p. 129, Table 1]. The Chinese predominance in the building sector explains their impact on house form and building technology, shaping both construction and decorative details [[2, p. 129]](#ref-2).

The critical transformation from courtyard house to terrace house was driven by public-health legislation. The Straits Settlements Ordinance No. XV of 1896 made a first attempt to establish minimum standards of space, determining the minimum open-space provision of a two-storey shophouse at 10 feet, or 15 feet for houses with more storeys [[2, p. 134]](#ref-2). The 1916 Penang Bye-Laws prescribed detailed construction standards: the width of openings was to be no less than one-tenth of the room frontage, room height no less than 10 feet, the minimum width of open space increased to 10 × 6 feet, the maximum built area between courtyards limited to 40 feet, rounded house corners of at least 15 feet radius introduced, and compulsory back lanes mandated [[2, p. 134]](#ref-2). These regulations effectively ended the back-to-back courtyard-house system and produced the modern terraced shophouse.

The demographic pressures that made such legislation necessary were considerable. Georgetown's population surged by 84.6 per cent between 1891 and 1901, driven primarily by Indian migrant labour, reaching 95,296 by 1901 [3, p. 132, Table 2]. In 1881, Chinese residents constituted 74 per cent of the commercial district and up to 90 per cent of the harbour area, while women formed only about one-third of the urban population [[2, p. 132]](#ref-2). By 1901, the western area around Campbell Street had reached 97 per cent occupation density, and the oldest grid was at its carrying limit [[3, p. 60]](#ref-3).

## Hill Stations and New Towns: Planning as State Project

The Cameron Highlands represent a distinct planning tradition in which the town was deliberately designed from the outset according to an imported theoretical model. R. Weebers demonstrates that the township of Tanah Rata, selected in 1931, was planned using garden city principles and modelled on Baguio in the Philippines, whose layout had been designed by Daniel H. Burnham as a compact garden city for 25,000 to 30,000 people [[4, pp. 105–106]](#ref-4). The 1929 preliminary general zoning plan, chaired by Charles Compton Reade following his official visit to Baguio, divided the highlands into Southern Highlands and Cameron Highlands, with the latter subdivided into service, village, administrative, residential, and recreation zones [[4, p. 106]](#ref-4). The 1931 re-zoning plan designated specific areas for agriculture (Area A), armed services (Area D), a park (Area E), viewpoints (Area F), general administration (Area G), recreation and golf (Area H), schools and churches (Area I), a Brinchang Valley Reserve (Area J), and waterworks and catchment areas (Area K) [[4, p. 107]](#ref-4). The town sits at an altitude of 1,432 metres in the Bertam Valley [[4, pp. 105–106]](#ref-4).

The post-independence period introduced a new planning imperative: the creation of new towns to relieve the demographic pressure on existing urban centres. Lee Boon Thong's study of Petaling Jaya, Malaysia's first new town, traces its origins to the acute housing crisis of post-war Kuala Lumpur, where population growth—accelerated by rural-to-urban flight during the Emergency—outstripped housing capacity and produced widespread squatting on state land. By 1953, some 21,851 families occupied 14,089 squatter shacks [[5, pp. 3–9]](#ref-5). The Federal Housing Trust, established in 1951, and the Federal Town Planning Department conceived a new town modelled on British New Towns, selecting a 1,437-hectare site south-west of Kuala Lumpur that was topographically suitable and free of tin deposits [[5, pp. 3–9]](#ref-5). The Petaling Jaya Authority Ordinance was gazetted on 11 November 1954, and the Federal Government allocated an emergency fund of $3.1 million [[5, pp. 5]](#ref-5).

The initial settlement was rudimentary: timber houses on unpaved roads, shared community taps, and a 60-year leasehold with a $200 premium per lot [[5, pp. 3–9]](#ref-5). Yet the town rapidly escaped its low-cost origins. Three converging factors drove the transformation: the completion of the Federal Highway in 1957, which dramatically improved accessibility for car-owning commuters; the success of the industrial zone in Section 51, which attracted manufacturers under the Pioneer Industries Act of 1958; and the entry of private developers, particularly the Malaya Borneo Building Society, which built higher-quality brick bungalows at prices ranging from $4,800 to $25,000 [[5, pp. 10–14]](#ref-5). Population growth was extraordinary: from an estimated 10,590 inhabitants in early 1955 to 32,000 by 1961, 93,400 by 1970 (exceeding the original target of 70,000), 200,000 by 1977, and 486,040 by 2006, when the town area had expanded to 97.2 square kilometres—nearly seven times its original size [[5, pp. 14]](#ref-5). The absence of a comprehensive town plan until 1973 meant development proceeded piecemeal, and the town's poly-nucleated character—Old Town, New Town, and later SS2, Kelana Jaya, Bandar Utama—emerged organically rather than by design [[5, pp. 15–18]](#ref-5).

## Conservation, Sustainability, and the Legacy of Planning

The long-term consequences of these planning decisions have become increasingly visible. In Penang, the repeal of the Rent Control Act in 2000—introduced in 1948 to address housing shortages and having frozen rents for over five decades—triggered immediate rent increases of 50 to 1,000 per cent and eviction notices, with only 2,830 of 60,000 affected tenants registering for alternative housing [[3, p. 66]](#ref-3). Tjoa-Bonatz critiques the proposed conservation guidelines as promoting "facadeism"—allowing infill buildings up to five storeys or unlimited height in some areas—rather than genuine adaptive reuse of the shophouse morphology [[3, p. 66]](#ref-3). Only 15 per cent of Penang's urban area was covered by the conservation zone gazetted in 1987, and a 1991 newspaper headline reported that six historic buildings were destroyed every week [[3, p. 66]](#ref-3).

In the Cameron Highlands, the tension between economic growth and environmental sustainability remains unresolved. Agriculture is the largest land use at 5,705 hectares, followed by forestry; approximately 80 per cent of tourists are Malaysians [[4, p. 108]](#ref-4). As flat, gently sloping land became exhausted, new development moved onto steep slopes, triggering soil erosion, siltation, landslides, and flash floods [[4, p. 109]](#ref-4). The 2016–2030 local plan proposes an aerial tram between Kuala Terla and Habu or an alternative road from Tringkap to Habu crossing the Mentigi Forest Reserve, both of which would damage environmentally sensitive areas including the Habu water catchment and Robinson Falls [[4, p. 109]](#ref-4).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on towns and town planning reveals a shifting analytical focus across the twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. The earliest contributions, such as Gullick's reconstruction of the building industry in colonial Selangor, are fundamentally economic and social histories that use the built environment as evidence for broader processes of labour organisation, material supply, and administrative capacity [[1]](#ref-1). Gullick's dense use of primary sources—Selangor Secretariat Files, Annual Reports, the *Selangor Journal*, personal diaries, and the *Singapore and Straits Directory*—establishes a methodological model for connecting architectural history, labour history, and administrative history in a way that neither the architectural literature nor the economic history of the tin industry had previously achieved [[1]](#ref-1).

Tjoa-Bonatz's two studies of Penang (1998 and 2000) represent a methodological turn toward micro-level socio-spatial analysis. The 1998 article shifts analytical focus from the shophouse as a static typological object to the legislative and economic processes that produced its successive spatial configurations [[2]](#ref-2). The 2000 article extends this by introducing a household survey of 50 shophouse dwellings, providing one of the first micro-level socio-demographic analyses of a historic inner-city neighbourhood in the region [[3]](#ref-3). Together, these studies fill a gap Tjoa-Bonatz identifies as the absence of a comprehensive urban history of Penang [[3, p. 53]](#ref-3), [[2, p. 123]](#ref-2).

The most recent contributions—Weebers on the Cameron Highlands (2017) and Lee Boon Thong on Petaling Jaya (2006)—reflect the maturation of planning history as a distinct subfield. Weebers situates the Cameron Highlands within the comparative literature on Asian hill stations and connects colonial garden city planning theory to its practical application in Malaya, bridging planning history and environmental history [[4]](#ref-4). Lee's study of Petaling Jaya provides the most detailed single-case study of Malaysia's first new town, bridging the gap between colonial-era administrative records and post-independence development narratives, and situating the town within the broader comparative literature on Asian new towns [[5, pp. 1–2]](#ref-5). The archival base for these studies—National Archives of Malaysia annual reports, budget proposals, development guidelines, memoranda, and plans covering 1960 to 2030, alongside District Office files and Colonial Office papers—demonstrates the increasing availability of post-independence planning documentation as a research resource [[4, p. 101]](#ref-4), [[5, pp. 20–21]](#ref-5).

A notable gap in the literature remains the absence of a comprehensive urban history for any single Malaysian city. Tjoa-Bonatz's observation that "neither the history of the architectural heritage nor a comprehensive urban history of Penang has yet been written" [[2, p. 123]](#ref-2) applies with equal force to Kuala Lumpur, Ipoh, and other major urban centres, suggesting that the field's next phase of development will require synthetic works capable of integrating the economic, social, architectural, and environmental dimensions documented in the existing specialist studies.
