# Chinese newspapers in Singapore, 1945–1963: mediators of elite and popular tastes in culture and politics

Thum Ping Tjin (2010) examines the Chinese-language press in Singapore between 1945 and 1963, arguing that these newspapers functioned as the primary mediators of a distinct Chinese public sphere that operated autonomously from, yet interlinked with, the Anglophone colonial political domain. The article contends that the Chinese press was not merely a recorder of events but an active progenitor of Singapore's emerging civil society, shaping political consciousness, cultural identity, and the linguistic frameworks through which decolonization was understood and contested.

## Summary

The article opens by identifying a structural gap in Singapore historiography: the dominant English-language nationalist narrative, while central to the decolonization story, is constitutionally unable to access the norms, values, and idioms of the Chinese-speaking majority. Tjin argues that English-language sources are "by definition unable to access" the vernacular domain, and that the Chinese newspapers constitute the most accessible and richly layered source for recovering this lost perspective (p. 53). The newspapers are treated simultaneously as political texts, cultural artefacts, and institutional actors—owners and journalists who competed to influence public opinion and who themselves participated in the political campaigns of the day.

A central methodological move is the comparative case study of the 1961 PAP split. The English-language press, particularly the *Straits Times*, framed the expulsion of thirteen assemblymen as a decisive anti-communist stand, while the Chinese-language papers responded with what Tjin characterizes as a "collective sigh and shrug," dismissing the communist framing as a "one size fits all" explanation and redirecting attention to the merger question, which commanded far greater coverage and emotional investment in the Chinese press (pp. 10–11). This divergence is not presented as a matter of bias but as evidence of genuinely different political priorities and analytical frameworks operating within the Chinese sphere.

The article's broader argument concerns cultural and linguistic mediation. Tjin traces how Chinese journalists synthesized traditional Confucian concepts with imported Western political vocabulary, creating a hybrid discourse that gave new meaning to familiar terms. The conflation of "citizenship" and "civil rights" under the single Chinese term *gongminquan* is presented as a case where linguistic structure shaped political perception, making the merger debate's legalistic distinctions between nationality and citizenship appear to the Chinese reader as an assault on fundamental civil rights (pp. 20–21). The newspapers thus developed the new language of decolonization—distinguishing *zizhi* (self-government) from *duli* (full independence), redefining *minzhu zhengzhi* (democratic politics) through the ancient principle of *minben* (the people as foundation of the nation)—while simultaneously mediating between elite intellectual currents and popular political engagement.

### Key Findings

- Between 1945 and 1959, 414 Chinese periodicals were established (19 daily, 76 non-daily), compared with only 163 periodicals (27 daily, 43 non-daily) across the entire pre-war period from 1881 to 1941 (p. 54).
- Internal *Straits Times* polling from the late 1950s found that each copy of the *Nanyang Siang Pau* or *Sin Chew Jit Poh* was read by an average of six to seven people, compared with three and a half for the *Straits Times*, yielding an estimated readership of approximately 550,000 per Chinese daily—214% of those officially classified as literate in Chinese (pp. 8–9).
- The 1947 census recorded 48.3% literacy among Chinese men; by 1957 this had risen to 62.6% of Chinese males over 10, with approximately 50% literate in Chinese and 52% of working-age Chinese men able to speak, read, and write the language (pp. 6–7).
- On 22 October 1958, Chief Minister Lim Yew Hock withdrew permits for eight Chinese newspapers and banned the importation of publications from 43 mainland Chinese and 10 Hong Kong publishing firms, citing the growth of Chinese literacy as increasing exposure to "radical and subversive material" (p. 7).
- Government expenditure on education rose from $4,586,000 in 1949 to $107,888,000 in 1963 (p. 7, n. 10).
- The *Weekly Digest of Vernacular Press*, which translated and summarized Chinese, Malay, and Tamil newspaper articles for senior government officials, operated from late 1952 until mid-1961, with approximately 80% of each issue devoted to the Chinese press (pp. 18–19).

### Conclusion

Tjin's definitive historical takeaway is that an independent, vigorously active, but still budding civil society existed in late colonial Singapore, and that the Chinese press occupied the position of its primary public sphere—mediating between society and state, connecting activists and intellectuals, and generating the institutional frameworks (schools, charitable organizations, commercial bodies) that constituted Chinese civil society. By 1963, the PAP government had begun the systematic process of bringing the press under its control, marking the end of the newspapers' most influential phase and the beginning of the disappearance of this autonomous domain (pp. 22–23).

## Context

- The study draws heavily on the Singapore National Archives oral history collections (SNA-OH), with interviews cited from accessions spanning the 1980s and 1990s, alongside the newspapers themselves as primary sources and the *Weekly Digest of Vernacular Press* as a secondary administrative record.
- Historiographically, the article positions itself against the English-language nationalist narrative and the left/right, Chinese/English dichotomy that has dominated Singapore studies, building on Wang Gungwu's (1970) three-category model of the Malayan Chinese while complicating it through the lens of print culture and public sphere theory (Habermas).
