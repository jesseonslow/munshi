# Notes on Malay subjects

C. A. Gibson-Hill, a naturalist attached to the Raffles Museum, published this comprehensive biological survey of North Keeling in 1948, based on two field visits made in January and July 1941. The article provides the first detailed account of the island's flora, fauna, and human history, documenting a coral island that, though uninhabited and virtually uninhabitable, supports substantial colonies of sea-birds and a distinctive invertebrate fauna shaped by its isolation from the main Cocos atoll.

## Summary

Gibson-Hill's central purpose is to fill a gap left by earlier visitors—Dr. H. B. Guppy (1888) and Dr. F. Wood-Jones (1906)—neither of whom attempted a comprehensive description of North Keeling. The article is organised around the island's physical geography and its four principal vegetation zones: the *Pisonia-Cocos* belt, the *Tournefortia* belt, the *Pemphis* clumps, and the open spaces and sea shore. Within each zone, Gibson-Hill describes the resident invertebrates and the sea-birds that breed there, recording nest dimensions, egg measurements, chick development stages, and population estimates. The narrative is informed by direct observation over two visits spaced six months apart, which allowed him to track seasonal changes in breeding activity and to identify definite reproductive cycles in most species.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to the island's human history, which Gibson-Hill treats as inseparable from its natural history. The Clunies-Ross family's exploitation of North Keeling—first for sea-birds, later for timber—fundamentally altered the island's ecology. The most dramatic episode was the wreck of the German cruiser *Emden* in November 1915, after her defeat by the Australian cruiser *Sydney* in the Battle of Cocos. Gibson-Hill reconstructs the sequence of events from local accounts and official records, noting that the islanders salvaged the wreck over three months in 1915–16, and that the remains of German sailors who perished on the island were eventually buried on the shingle ridge. The article also documents the gradual displacement of bird populations from the main atoll to North Keeling as a consequence of sustained hunting pressure on Cocos, a process traceable from Darwin's observations in 1836 through Forbes (1885) and Wood-Jones (1905) to Gibson-Hill's own findings in 1941.

The faunal lists in the appendices record thirteen species of birds breeding on North Keeling, eight species of terrestrial and semi-terrestrial crabs, and four species of Lepidoptera. Gibson-Hill notes that the absence of rats, lizards, centipedes, millipedes, termites, and earthworms distinguishes the island's invertebrate community from that of the main atoll, and he speculates on the implications for the introduced land birds, particularly the Christmas Island Thrush, which has apparently disappeared from North Keeling owing to the lack of suitable fruit-bearing trees.

### Key Findings

- North Keeling measures 1,250 yards wide by 2,250 yards long; its lagoon reaches a maximum depth of less than six feet at low tide, and the island contains only two brackish wells of twelve to fifteen feet depth (pp. 64, 68).
- The Red-footed Booby (*Sula sula rubripes*) is the most abundant species, with an estimated 8,000–10,000 birds of all ages and approximately 4,000 breeding pairs; the two frigate-bird colonies each contained about 1,000–1,200 nests (pp. 77, 80).
- The White Tern (*Gygis alba monte*) numbered between 200 and 300 pairs; the Masked Booby (*Sula dactylatra bedouti*) was estimated at no more than 50 pairs; the White-tailed Tropic-bird was represented by perhaps a dozen pairs (pp. 74, 85, 73).
- The German light-cruiser *Emden* (3,592 tons) was driven onto the reef fringing the south coast of North Keeling on 9 November 1915 after being disabled by the Australian cruiser *Sydney*; she had previously destroyed over £2,000,000 worth of allied shipping and accounted for more than fifteen merchant vessels in the Bay of Bengal (pp. 93–94).
- During the Clunies-Ross working seasons, forty to sixty Malay labourers lived on the island for approximately three months (November to February), communicating with the main atoll by boat and exchanging nuts and timber for supplies and fresh water (p. 68).
- On each of Gibson-Hill's two visits, the twenty Cocos-Keeling islanders who accompanied him took back approximately five hundred *Fregata* and *Sula* carcasses, confirming that the birds remain a much-sought delicacy despite the species' reduced numbers on the main atoll (p. 97).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive historical takeaway is that North Keeling functions as an ecological refuge: the sea-bird species that were driven from the main atoll by decades of human predation have persisted there in substantial numbers precisely because the island's inaccessibility and lack of fresh water have limited sustained human occupation. The island's fauna is thus a living record of the ecological consequences of the Clunies-Ross colony's exploitation of Cocos, and its present condition—relatively undisturbed, with bird colonies steadily increasing—stands in direct contrast to the depleted state of the main atoll.

## Context

- Gibson-Hill's fieldwork was conducted in 1941, while he was stationed at the trans-Indian Ocean cable relay on Pulo Tikus (Direction Island); the first draft was written on that island in August–September 1941 and preserved through the Japanese occupation of Singapore by the MBRAS clerk T. D. Ree (p. 92).
- The article represents the first comprehensive biological survey of North Keeling and forms the companion volume to Gibson-Hill's earlier "Notes on the Cocos-Keeling Islands" (JMBRAS, Vol. 20, pt. 2, 1947), together providing the most detailed natural history of the group available in the mid-twentieth century.
