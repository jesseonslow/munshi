# Developments relevant to Malayan agriculture in the post-rubber crisis era (1920–1921

Khoo Kay Kim (1999) examines the post-rubber crisis era in Malaya (1920–1921) and the agricultural developments that followed, arguing that while the Stevenson Restriction Scheme dominates the historiography, four lesser-known institutional and economic developments—agricultural diversification, the Rubber Research Institute, the Asiatic Planters' Association of Malaya, and the School of Agriculture at Serdang—were equally consequential for the colony's long-term economic trajectory.

## Summary

The article addresses a gap in Malayan economic historiography: the tendency to treat the rubber crisis of 1920–1 and the subsequent Stevenson Restriction Scheme (1922–8) as the defining narrative of the inter-war period, to the exclusion of parallel developments that reshaped the colony's agricultural landscape. Khoo draws extensively on contemporary press reporting (*Malay Mail*, *Malaya Tribune*, *Straits Echo*, *Sunday Tribune*), government despatches, departmental annual reports, and company records to reconstruct four interlocking threads. The first and longest concerns the attempts—both official and private—to diversify commercial agriculture away from rubber monoculture. The second traces the protracted and often contentious establishment of the Rubber Research Institute (RRI), from the Rubber Growers' Association's early research stations through the Butler proposal of 1918 to the institute's operational difficulties and eventual rehabilitation by 1940. The third examines the founding of the Asiatic Planters' Association of Malaya (APAM) in 1925 as a response to the inequities of the Restriction Scheme as applied to Asian-owned estates, and its dissolution in 1931 in favour of the Malayan Estate Owners' Association. The fourth follows the belated creation of the School of Agriculture at Serdang, from the 1913 committee that recommended against it to the laying of the foundation stone in 1930.

A recurring analytical thread is the tension between the British administration's ambivalence toward agricultural development and the private sector's persistent orientation toward short-term rubber profits. Khoo shows that the government's land policies were contradictory: the Rubber Lands Restriction Enactment of 1917 attempted to curb new rubber planting, yet liberal terms offered to revive sugar cultivation went unheeded, and the Agricultural Department itself was criticised for concentrating its resources on rubber long after the crisis had demonstrated the need for broader research. The press, particularly the *Malay Mail* and *Malaya Tribune*, emerges as a more consistent advocate for diversification than either the government or the planting community, though even its proposals (such as the *Malaya Tribune*'s suggestion for a third class of agricultural training for would-be small proprietors) were ignored.

Khoo also foregrounds the ethnic and institutional dimensions of agricultural organisation. The APAM's founding meeting in May 1925 brought together a remarkable cross-section of the Asian planting community—Chinese, Indian, Ceylonese, Eurasian, and one Japanese representative—yet the association's inability to sustain itself reflected both the limited organisational capacity of its membership and the structural dominance of the European-controlled Planters' Association of Malaya. Similarly, the Serdang School's early graduates reveal the uneven demand for agricultural expertise across ethnic lines, with Chinese graduates finding ready employment while Indian graduates struggled to secure positions.

### Key Findings

- In the FMS, rubber occupied 779,170 acres in 1920 and 1,506,544 acres by 1932, while coconut acreage tripled (72,149 to 240,020), coffee quadrupled (2,364 to 8,789), and "other crops" increased twelvefold (6,112 to 70,494 acres), demonstrating that diversification did occur but rubber's absolute dominance was unbroken (pp. 18–19).
- The Rubber Research Institute was established in September 1925 and financed by an export tax of $10 per picul on rubber leaving Malaya, which yielded $386,000 (approximately £45,000) by 1926; the RGA had previously expended about £70,000 on rubber research in Malaya by 1924 (pp. 25–27).
- Oil palm planted area in the FMS grew from 12,631 acres (1926) to 18,321 acres (1927) and 31,700 acres (1929); palm oil exports rose from 726 tons (1926) to 1,831 tons (1929), and Malayan oil commanded a premium over West African oil for margarine and edible oil manufacture (pp. 31–32).
- The APAM was formed on 1 May 1925 with Choo Kia Peng as President and Tan Cheng Lock as Vice-President; it wound up in 1931 and was replaced by the Malayan Estate Owners' Association, which nonetheless remained primarily the voice of Asian planters (pp. 39–43).
- The School of Agriculture at Serdang had its foundation stone laid on 19 March 1930, began teaching in 1931, and cost $250,000 to build; it was the third institution of its type in the British Crown Colonies and Protectorates, and its first batch graduated in April 1934 (pp. 44–46).
- The FMS Government's sugar revival offer included no land premium, no rent for the first three years (then $1 per acre), and a guarantee that export duty would not exceed 2 per cent *ad valorem* from 1927 to 1931; despite these terms, sugar was not even mentioned in government statistics by 1932 (pp. 20–21).

### Conclusion

Khoo's definitive historical judgment is that Malaya's agricultural potential was never fully realised before World War II because of a persistent misalignment between government policy, the Agricultural Department's research priorities, and the private sector's preference for instant rubber profits. He extends this argument into the present, suggesting that had wiser opinions prevailed in the 1920s and 1930s—had there been greater convergence of efforts among the government, the department, and the planting community—Malaysia's agricultural sector would have been considerably more dynamic and the country better positioned to weather the economic crisis of the late 1990s.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on contemporary Malayan press sources, FMS and Straits Settlements government despatches, Department of Agriculture annual reports, and company records (notably the Arghan Company and Oil Palm Plantations Ltd); it does not rely on a single archival repository but synthesises published and semi-published materials.
- Historiographically, the study complements Colin Barlow's *The Natural Rubber Industry* (1978) and Lim Chong Yah's *Economic Development of Modern Malaya* (1967) by shifting focus from the rubber industry itself to the institutional and educational infrastructure that the crisis prompted, and by giving sustained attention to the Asian planting community's organisational responses.
