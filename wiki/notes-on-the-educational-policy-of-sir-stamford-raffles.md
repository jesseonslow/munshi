# Notes on the educational policy of Sir Stamford Raffles

G.G. Hough, a lecturer at Raffles College, Singapore, published this article in the *JMBRAS* in 1933, examining two primary documents by Sir Stamford Raffles—a 1819 minute proposing a Malay College and a 1823 minute founding the Singapore Institution—to assess the scope and ambition of Raffles' educational vision for the Malay Archipelago. Hough's central argument is that Raffles' conception, which combined native-language instruction with Western science and local research, was not merely "advanced for its time" but potentially superior to the English-only educational path that Macaulay's 1835 minute would later impose on the region.

## Summary

Hough situates Raffles' educational thinking within the geopolitical context of early nineteenth-century British-Dutch competition in the Archipelago. Raffles viewed the entire region—Peninsular Malaya, Sumatra, Java, and even Siam—as a single entity requiring a unified educational approach, a scope far wider than any conceivable in Hough's own day. His motivation was explicitly tied to commerce: he argued that European trade had disrupted existing social structures and destroyed the native educational institutions previously maintained by local princes at Bantam, Menangkabau, and elsewhere, and that education must "keep pace with commerce" to prevent the corruption and sensuality that unregulated trade would otherwise produce (pp. 166–167).

The core of Hough's analysis contrasts Raffles' approach with the Macaulay model. Raffles rejected the idea of "English education alone"; instead he proposed improving education in native languages while adding English and Western science for those best able to profit. His model was explicitly top-down—educating Malay chiefs and the Chinese merchant class first, from whom improvement would diffuse downward—and he condemned any scheme that would "reverse the order" and begin from the bottom (p. 168). Crucially, the institution was also conceived as a research centre for collecting and publishing Malayan literature, history, and natural science, making it something far more than a vehicle for Western cultural transmission. Hough notes the Singapore Institution's remarkable tolerance: students of all religions admitted equally, no compulsion to attend Christian worship, and native teachers judged by their own moral standards (p. 169).

Hough acknowledges the scheme's failure due to hostility and mismanagement, but insists it was "an amazingly advanced conception" for 1823, when England itself had no government provision for education until 1847. He characterises Raffles' spirit not as "advanced" in a modern sense but as a survival from the liberal, cultured eighteenth century, before Victorian utilitarianism had begun to dominate educational thinking (p. 170).

### Key Findings

- The 1823 Singapore Institution comprised three departments: a scientific department, a literary and moral department for the Chinese, and a literary and moral department for the Malays and kindred peoples (p. 168).
- The Malay department was to be staffed by a Principal, a professor of Malayan languages, a professor of Siamese, and native masters in Malay, Siamese, Bugis, Javanese, and other Archipelago languages (p. 168).
- The scientific department included a professor of natural philosophy (teaching Newtonian astronomy, mechanics, and chemistry by experiment) and a professor of natural history (zoology, botany, mineralogy, with a mandate to procure new information from surrounding countries for transmission to the home Committee of Co-operation) (p. 169).
- The Anglo-Chinese College at Malacca, under Dr. Morrison, was proposed for amalgamation into the Singapore Institution, but this was never carried out (p. 168, n.9).
- The Singapore Institution was founded in 1823; no government provision for education was made in England until 1847, and state-supported education was not widely established until 1871 (p. 170).
- Students of all religions were to be admitted on equal terms; native students and masters were not compelled to attend Christian worship; native teachers were to be "correct moral men, according to the opinions of their own nation" (p. 169).

### Conclusion

Hough's definitive takeaway is that Raffles left behind "the plan of something far better than anything that has yet been built." While it is commonplace to say Raffles was ahead of his time, Hough argues he was also ahead of the present (1933), because the English-only path chosen after Macaulay foreclosed the possibility of the richer, locally-rooted educational model Raffles envisioned—one that would have made English culture "real and intelligible" to the peoples of the region while preserving their own linguistic and intellectual traditions (p. 170).

## Context

- **Primary sources:** Two minutes by Raffles reproduced in the *Memoir* (1819 minute, Appendix p. 24; 1823 minute, Appendix p. 74), plus the 1835 Resolution of the Governor-General in Council quoted from *Cambridge History of India*, Vol. V (A. Smith, 1922).
- **Historiographical contribution:** A brief but pointed intervention in the debate over the legacy of colonial education in Malaya, arguing that the Macaulay model was not the only possible path and that Raffles' alternative—native-language instruction combined with Western science and local research—remained unrealised and perhaps superior.
