# Becoming Malay: The Case of the Batak Orphans in 1930s Perak

## Abstract

Huta Uruk Petros emigrated from Sumatra to Perak in the Federated Malay States in 1914. He and his growing family built a new life there until his and his wife’s deaths triggered a battle over custody of his children. This essay analyses the children’s decisions to ‘become Malay’ and to convert from Christianity to Islam as they made decisions about their identity and where they belonged. Their story raises questions about the meaning of religion, gender, age, kinship, law, colonial power, and local custom in a small town in British Malaya around 1930. Decisions hinged upon welfare issues and the age of adulthood in British and Muslim law.

## Summary

This article examines the custody dispute that followed the deaths of Huta Uruk Petros and his wife Cornella in late 1929, leaving six Batak Christian children orphaned in Sitiawan, Perak. Lees uses the family's case file to explore how these children navigated identity, belonging, and survival in a frontier colonial society where ethnic and religious categories were fluid but legally consequential. The core argument is that "becoming Malay" for Petros' children was not a simple act of ethnic assimilation but a strategic, hybridized identity choice driven by welfare concerns, the desire to remain in Sitiawan, and the practical advantages that Muslim adult status conferred under both Islamic and colonial law. The children's definitions of Malayness were shaped by their hybrid Christian-Batak-Malay background rather than by essentialist ethnic categories imposed from above.

The article interrogates the legal pluralism of the Federated Malay States, showing how the Pangkor Treaty's grant of religious authority to the Perak Sultan, the jurisdiction of kathi courts, and the broad discretion of British district officers created a space in which the children could assert agency. Lees demonstrates that the children's own voices—preserved in letters and interview transcripts—reveal a more complex and self-aware process of identity formation than colonial administrators recognized. Daulat, in particular, emerges as a cosmopolitan young man who shifted language codes strategically, claimed adult status through Islamic puberty, and defended his siblings' right to remain in Sitiawan while acknowledging his Christian heritage.

The primary sources are the 26-page case file DO Sitiawan 705/1929, which contains three letters (one from Petros, two from Daulat), transcripts of multiple interviews, memoranda, and administrators' notes. Lees also draws on Methodist mission records and the 1931 census to contextualize the family's social and economic position within the rubber-tapping economy of the Sitiawan district.

### Key Findings

- The 1913 Federal Council definition of a Malay required habitual speech of Malay or a Malayan language **and** profession of the Muslim religion; Petros' family passed the language test but not the religious one, making conversion the principal barrier to their "becoming Malay" (p. 7).
- The Raja Muda of Perak, Abdul Aziz, ruled in January 1930 that Daulat and Nora, having reached puberty, were "at liberty to adopt whatever religion they like," while the younger children remained subject to their relatives' claims—a distinction that empowered the two eldest to choose their own residence (p. 14).
- Daulat's letter of April 1930 to the Assistant District Officer asked whether his siblings who had "joined Malay" would be governed by "adat (custom) and sarat (syarat/religious law)," effectively asserting that Islamic law should protect them from being sent to their Christian Batak grandfather in Sumatra (pp. 15–16).
- In July 1931, Romlus testified that Jasaidi had forced him into approximately twelve-hour workdays tapping and weeding, threatened to beat him, and refused him entry to the house while he worked; both Daulat and Romlus used the word "slave" to describe their treatment (p. 17).
- The Mui Tsai Enactment of 1932 required registration of all female domestic servants under 18 whose guardianship had been transferred by gift, inheritance, pledge, or debt settlement, and mandated state-set wage scales and protections against overwork (p. 18).
- A final note added to the case file in late 1932 recorded: "The girls and Verlien live with Jasaidi and his wife and [are] well looked after," confirming the informal settlement that the children themselves had engineered (p. 23).

### Conclusion

Lees's definitive historical takeaway is that Petros' children, rather than colonial administrators, settled the outcome of their custody case. They opted for cultural change and a new homeland, choosing to enter the Ayer Tawar Muslim community while maintaining ties to local Sumatran kin. For Daulat, being Malay was a hybridized, modern identity fostered within the towns of the Perak export economy and by the adoption of colonial knowledge. The colonial state preserved and shaped what we know of the family but exercised little direct power over the resolution; individuals seeking help found a weak state when individual welfare, rather than law and order, was at issue.

## Context

- **Primary archival collection:** DO Sitiawan 705/1929, National Archives of Malaysia, Kuala Lumpur (26 pages including letters, interview transcripts, memoranda, and administrators' notes); supplemented by Methodist Episcopal Church mission records at Drew University and the United Methodist Church General Commission on Archives and History, Madison, N.J.
- **Historiographical contribution:** The article extends the literature on Malay identity (Milner, Andaya, Shamsul) by grounding the concept of "becoming Malay" in the subjective experience of a culturally hybrid immigrant family, and by demonstrating how legal pluralism in the FMS created spaces for individual agency in identity formation that colonial administrators neither recognized nor controlled.
