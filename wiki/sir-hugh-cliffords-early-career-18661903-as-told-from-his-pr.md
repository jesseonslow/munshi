A.J. Stockwell's 1976 article reconstructs Sir Hugh Clifford's formative colonial career (1866–1903) from the private papers preserved by Clifford's family, arguing that these documents illuminate not merely the administrative chronology of British penetration into the Malay States and North Borneo but also the intellectual and moral tensions that shaped Clifford's subsequent imperial philosophy. The historical setting is the critical period when the Residential System was being extended across the peninsula and chartered company governance was being tested in Borneo.

## Summary

Stockwell's narrative moves through three phases: Clifford's aristocratic upbringing and entry into the Straits service at seventeen; his postings in Perak, Pahang, Selangor, and North Borneo during the 1880s and 1890s; and his two-year convalescence in England (1901–1903) during which he established himself as a literary figure. The central historical problem the article addresses is the friction between metropolitan commercial interests and local administrative judgment in colonial governance, a tension crystallised in Clifford's brief and acrimonious tenure as Governor of British North Borneo. Stockwell draws heavily on three transcribed manuscript letters Clifford wrote to Governor Weld during his 1887 mission to Pahang, which reveal the on-the-ground complexities of mining disputes, succession rivalries, and the limited leverage of a junior officer operating without formal authority. These primary documents allow Stockwell to show how Clifford's early experiences—particularly his observation of Malay misgovernment and his frustration at the inability to reform it—fed directly into his later writings on the "superiority of the white races" and his advocacy for local initiative over centralised control.

The article also uses the papers to trace Clifford's literary development from his Malay-language work (proverbs, Sakai dialect notes, the Penal Code translation) through his short fiction to his contributions to the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* and the unfinished *Dictionary of the Malay Language*. Stockwell argues that Clifford's fiction—especially the stories of Maurice Curzon, Sally, and Saleh—functions as a psychological exploration of the collision between Eastern and Western identities, a theme that the private correspondence confirms was not merely literary posturing but stemmed from lived experience. The Swettenham letters in the papers, full of "hastily scribbled gossip and vitriolic comment," provide a valuable counterpoint to official dispatches and reveal the informal networks through which colonial policy was actually negotiated, particularly during the 1902–1903 dispute over British advisers to Kelantan and Trengganu.

### Key Findings

- Clifford's 1887 mission to Pahang: he reached Pekan on 18 March and secured the Bendahara's letter requesting a treaty on 10 April; the treaty itself was not signed until late September 1887, largely through Johore's mediation (pp. 92–93).
- The *Dictionary of the Malay Language* (Clifford and Swettenham) was published only through the letter "G" despite the manuscript being completed to "O"; printing costs reached approximately $1,700–$1,750 against sales of about $1,075, and the work was abandoned partly because the compilers could only work "by driblets" amid official duties (pp. 103–104).
- Clifford's resignation as Governor of British North Borneo was tendered on 5 July 1900 after he personally walked the entire Pagalan Valley, Penotal Gorge, and Padas river to verify that the railway's projected traffic was unsupported by actual population and produce figures; the Court of Directors accepted his resignation in late September 1900 (pp. 100–101, 110–112).
- During his 1901–1903 convalescence, Clifford was treated with a regimen of no food after 5 p.m., mustard poultices, laudanum, and progressively reduced pills, on a diet of arrowroot, chicken broth, and barley water (p. 102).
- Clifford lent a collection of 56 stone implements (celts and adzes) and portions of 11 others from the Pahang River to the Museum of Archaeology, Cambridge, in 1903 (p. 104, n. 51).
- The Anglo-Siamese dispute over Kelantan and Trengganu: a draft treaty was forced on Siam in early October 1902 and accepted by the Raja of Kelantan, but the Sultan of Trengganu refused; the Foreign Office overruled both Swettenham and the Colonial Office in 1903, ensuring the appointment of W.A. Graham (a Siamese Government officer) as Kelantan's first Adviser (pp. 106–108).

### Conclusion

Stockwell's definitive takeaway is that Clifford's early Malayan service—marked by personal risk, physical privation, and the constant negotiation between imperial idealism and administrative constraint—produced both the man and the writer. The private papers demonstrate that Clifford's later imperial philosophy, with its insistence on local authority, its distrust of metropolitan micromanagement, and its ambivalent regard for indigenous societies, was not an abstract theoretical position but the direct product of specific encounters in Pahang and North Borneo. The tragedy Stockwell underscores is that Clifford's health, broken by tropical service and possibly by poisoning, forced him into a literary interlude that, while productive, ultimately diverted him from the Malayan career he most desired and toward the wider imperial postings where his principles would be tested against even more formidable commercial and political interests.

## Context

- The primary archival collection is the Clifford Papers, held privately by H.C. Holmes (Clifford's son-in-law), comprising some twenty uncatalogued volumes of correspondence, newspaper cuttings, and photographs; Clifford's diaries were reportedly destroyed during World War II, though manuscript diaries for 1888 and 1893 are held at the Arkib Negara, Malaysia (p. 89, n. 4).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its use of private correspondence to supplement and occasionally contradict the official record, particularly regarding the Pahang mission of 1887 and the North Borneo railway dispute, and in situating Clifford's literary output within the administrative experiences that generated it.
