# Jelebu

H.A. O'Brien, a British colonial administrator, published this account in 1884 documenting the constitutional history and political structure of Jelebu, a small Malay state in the Perak–Negeri Sembilan region, tracing its origins from Johor suzerainty to its distinctive dual governance of a Menangkabau Yam Tuan and a hereditary Penghulu. The article serves as both a historical narrative and a contemporary administrative survey of a state in a state of severe depopulation and economic decline.

## Summary

O'Brien reconstructs the political genesis of Jelebu from oral information gathered from local headmen. The four states of Jelebu, Sungei Ujong, Rembau, and Johol were originally governed by Penghulus under Johor's suzerainty. After the Raja of Johor executed the son of the Penghulu of Rembau in a fit of personal rage over a refused marriage proposal, the four Penghulus withdrew from Johor's court and elected a Menangkabau royal as their collective ruler, who took up residence at Sri Menanti. The name "Jelebu" itself was not yet in use at this stage; it was later derived from a man drowned in the Triang River. The Yam Tuan of Sri Menanti subsequently sent a son to Jelebu, who was observed for a period before being elected Yam Tuan of Jelebu with the specific duty of protector of the inhabitants, though without direct jurisdiction over the land.

The constitutional machinery O'Brien describes is a complex system of checks. The Penghulu (Syed Ali bin Zin at the time of writing) is the practical ruler, but his authority is circumscribed by five Lembagas and four Waris, each holding a vote in every act of state. The Penghulu is elected from the Waris body under a fixed rotational succession rule, and no Lembaga may rise to the office of Penghulu. The Yam Tuan, by 1884, had been reduced to a cipher: he had arrogated powers of interference in internal government, was ordered to leave Jelebu in 1880, and signed a bond in January 1884 undertaking not to interfere again. O'Brien notes that the entire process of legislation—originating from the Lembagas or Waris, passing through the other body, receiving the Penghulu's nominal assent, and finally the Yam Tuan's ratification—was designed to protect the common people through its very complexity.

The article closes with a stark assessment of Jelebu's material condition in 1884: O'Brien describes passing through "mile after mile of deserted kampongs" with padi land lying waste and fruit trees still in bearing. The only sign of economic activity was a small tin mine at Jelundong, worked for seventeen years by a Sungei Ujong Chinaman, where the cost of rice was nearly triple that of Sungei Ujong due to the absence of roads.

### Key Findings

- The original investiture chop, bearing the inscription of Sultan Ma'ad Shah, conferred "supreme authority to be the Sultan of Jelebu for ever" upon the Mandelika Mantri, and this formula was used by successive Penghulus (p. 337).
- The Yam Tuan's direct jurisdiction was limited to the district from Bandar Berangan to Sungei Melentang (Batu Gominting), a portion of what later became Klawang, and he could be deposed by his officers for violating custom (p. 338).
- Klawang was ceded to Sungei Ujong as payment in lieu of a monetary fine when a son of the Datu Penghulu of Jelebu violated a daughter of the Penghulu of Klambu and could not afford the settlement (p. 339).
- The five Lembagas held the titles Datu Mantri, Datu Ngiang, Datu Chinchang, Datu Sendara, and Datu Lela Angsa; the four Waris were Raja Balang, Maharaja Indah, Raja Penghulu, and Datu Umbei. The Penghulu was elected from the Waris under a rotational rule, with the Datu Umbei and Raja Penghulu themselves excluded from candidacy (pp. 340–341).
- At the Jelundong tin mine in 1884, one dollar purchased only 2¾ gantangs of rice, compared with 7 gantangs in Sungei Ujong and 10 gantangs at Kuala Triang, illustrating the severe transport costs of the region (p. 342).
- A postscript dated September 1895 records that the bridle-track from Pantei to the mines had been completed, a Collector (Mr. E. P. Gueritz) was appointed in June 1895, the Pahang boundary was fixed at Sungei Dua on the Triang, and government buildings (Custom House, Court, Police Station) were erected at Kuala Klawang (p. 343).

### Conclusion

O'Brien's definitive takeaway is that Jelebu's elaborate constitutional machinery, while seemingly cumbersome, contained inherent safeguards for the common people, and that the state's principal problem in the 1880s was not political but demographic and infrastructural: the absence of population and roads had rendered a once-prosperous territory a wasteland, and the solution lay in the construction of communications and the subsequent return of settlers.

## Context

- O'Brien was a British colonial official (likely the British Resident or an officer of the Straits Settlements administration) conducting an administrative survey of Jelebu's constitution and condition; the article is framed as a practical briefing for the colonial government rather than a scholarly study.
- The 1895 postscript, appended nearly a decade after the original publication, indicates O'Brien's continued involvement in the state's administration and provides a rare contemporaneous record of the early British-colonial infrastructure project in Jelebu.
