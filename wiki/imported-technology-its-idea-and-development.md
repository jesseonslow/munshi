# Imported technology; its idea and development

Goh Chor Boon (1998) examines why colonial Singapore, despite its privileged position as Britain's premier entrepot in Southeast Asia, failed to develop an indigenous manufacturing or technological base during the period of British rule. Drawing on the historiography of technology transfer in the colonial world, the article argues that Singapore's trading culture, the enclavist behaviour of European technical elites, and a deliberately limited educational policy collectively inhibited the diffusion of Western industrial technology to the local population.

## Summary

The article interrogates a central paradox of Singapore's colonial economic history: the island possessed the capital, trading networks, and physical infrastructure to support industrialisation, yet no serious effort was made—by either the colonial government or the indigenous entrepreneurial class—to build a manufacturing sector based on modern technology. Goh situates this problem within the broader literature on technology transfer in the age of new imperialism, particularly the work of Daniel Headrick, who argued that colonial investments favoured physical over human capital and that technology transfer was "more geographic rather than cultural." Singapore, unlike British India, was never conceived as an extension of Britain's industrial production machinery; it was a trading node and naval station, and its economic role reinforced a set of cultural and institutional barriers to industrial development.

The core of the argument rests on three interlocking factors. First, the trading culture that dominated Singapore's economy—sustained by the interlocking partnership between British managing agency houses and Chinese compradors—rewarded short-term speculative commerce over long-term industrial investment. Chinese merchants, operating under a *laissez-faire* regime that protected property and contract, rationally channelled capital into entrepot trade and small-scale commerce rather than into manufacturing ventures requiring heavy capital outlay and technical expertise. Second, the colonial educational system was deliberately underfunded and structurally divisive, producing a small English-educated elite while leaving the vast majority of the population without access to technical or vocational training. Third, European engineers, technicians, and managers occupied all senior technical and managerial positions within an enclavist social structure that minimised interaction with the local population, thereby preventing any meaningful transfer of skills or knowledge.

Primary sources—including the *Singapore and Malayan Directory* (1922, 1940), the memoirs of civil engineer G.J. O'Grady, and statements by colonial administrators such as Swettenham, Weld, and Winstedt—inform the narrative by revealing the attitudes and institutional practices that sustained this enclavist arrangement. The article also draws on secondary scholarship by Wang Gungwu, Chan Wai Kwan, and the Stanworths to contextualise Chinese business culture and British corporate practices.

### Key Findings

- By 1937, only 12 per cent of school-age children in Singapore attended English-language schools; in 1931, education consumed just 5.69 per cent of Straits Settlements total revenue, a proportion the colonial administration explicitly noted was lower than in other important colonies (pp. 48–49).
- Right up to the 1960s, the manufacturing share of Singapore's labour force ranged from 10 to 15 per cent, and its contribution to domestic income stagnated at 5 to 7 per cent (p. 47).
- As late as 1940, all engineers in the Gas, Water, and Electricity Departments of the Singapore Municipality were recruited from Britain; at the Singapore Harbour Board, Europeans held positions from Chief Engineer down to machine shop foreman, while Chinese occupied clerical and cashier roles (pp. 51–52).
- The Port Dickson water supply project (Negeri Sembilan) involved the largest order yet placed in Great Britain for weldless steel mains for service in Malaya, with all material carried from England in British steamers; the Rapid Gravity Water Filter at Province Wellesley was similarly built with British-manufactured mechanical equipment supplied through the Crown Agents for the Colonies (p. 52).
- Tan Kah Kee, the most prominent indigenous industrialist of the period, invested in European rubber-manufacturing technology between 1924 and 1932 and took out numerous patent rights from the government, yet he remained a singular exception rather than evidence of a broader industrialising trend (p. 47).
- United Engineers Limited, formed in 1912 from the merger of Riley, Hargreaves and Company and Howarth, Erskine and Company, represented some seventy British manufacturers of metal products and machinery and served as a major channel for the transfer of machinery into the tin-smelting and rubber-producing industries (p. 42).

### Conclusion

Goh's definitive takeaway is that the popular culture developed in colonial Singapore was fundamentally one that supported trading and brokerage services and kept science and technology at a distance. The colonial state, the European technical elite, and the Chinese merchant class each had rational incentives to maintain the status quo: the British preserved their political supremacy and social exclusivity, the Europeans monopolised technical vocations within their own enclaves, and the Chinese pursued the quickest path to wealth through commerce. This historical legacy—the absence of an indigenous technological culture and a shortage of high-tech entrepreneurs—remained a structural challenge that the independent Singapore government had to actively overcome in its post-1965 drive toward industrialisation and technological excellence.

## Context

- The article is drawn from the author's 1996 PhD thesis at the University of New South Wales, *The Role and State and Society in the Development of Science and Technology in Singapore: A Historical and Sociocultural Perspective*, and represents a contribution to the historiography of technology transfer in colonial Southeast Asia.
- Primary archival sources include the *Singapore and Malayan Directory* (1922, 1940), the unpublished memoirs of G.J. O'Grady (Mss. Ind. Ocn.), and published statements by colonial administrators; the study engages substantively with the secondary literature of Headrick, Adas, Wang Gungwu, and Chan Wai Kwan.
