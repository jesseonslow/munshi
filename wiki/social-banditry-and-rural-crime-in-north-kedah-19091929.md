# Social banditry and rural crime in North Kedah, 1909–1929

Cheah Boon Kheng's 1981 article examines gang robbery and rural crime in North Kedah between 1909 and 1929, arguing that the notorious outlaws Salleh Tui and Nayan were "social bandits" in E.J. Hobsbawm's sense—peasant criminals whom their local communities perceived as heroes and avengers rather than mere criminals. Drawing on police records, Kedah Annual Reports, and extensive oral history collected from rural informants in the Kuala Nerang, Pendang, Yan, and Sik districts, Cheah situates the bandits within a context of weak rural policing, the economic dislocation caused by the abolition of corvee and imposition of land-tax, and the porous Kedah–Siam frontier that enabled criminal gangs to operate with relative impunity.

## Summary

The article first establishes the structural conditions that made rural crime endemic in North Kedah. After the 1909 transfer of suzerainty from Siam to Britain, the colonial administration inherited a state with a 500-strong police force concentrated in towns, while large rural districts like Kubang Pasu and Padang Terap were covered by only a handful of outposts manned by six or seven men under a corporal. Village headmen (penghulus) were frequently complicit with criminal gangs, and in some cases were themselves gang leaders; the state's cattle-registration system, which gave penghulus a 20 per cent commission, actively facilitated cattle theft. The Kedah–Siam border, jungled and inaccessible, allowed raiding parties to cross over and reappear in new locations before any pursuit could be organised. Cheah demonstrates that official crime statistics—cited by the British Adviser Maxwell as evidence of a "satisfactory" state of crime—were deeply misleading, as Kedah's gang-robbery figures for 1920 (66) and 1921 (100) exceeded or rivaled those of the entire Federated Malay States for the same period (pp. 93–103).

The core of the article is a comparative study of two bandits. Salleh Tui, a Sam-Sam from Setul (then Siam), operated in the Padang Terap and Kubang Pasu border areas and was killed in December 1910 after being betrayed while incapacitated by venereal disease. Nayan, a poor padi planter's son from Paya Kelubi in Pendang district, rose to command a gang under the rural entrepreneur Sulaiman Kerekau and operated in the Kota Setar and Kuala Muda districts until his death in December 1920. Cheah contrasts their folk reputations with official accounts: Salleh Tui was feared for his cruelty and reported twenty murders, yet his legend endures among the frontier Sam-Sams as a figure of romantic defiance; Nayan was venerated by poor peasants as a "good bandit" who robbed only the rich and distributed spoils to the poor, though landlord-class informants described him as a heartless kidnapper and killer. Cheah argues that both men's status as social bandits rests on the peasant perception of their invulnerability, their charismatic leadership, and their perceived challenge to the local power structure (pp. 109–127).

Cheah also interrogates the literary source most readers would associate with Nayan—Mansor Abdullah's 1960 novel *Panglima Nayan*—and identifies significant discrepancies between the novelist's romanticised account (a triangular love affair, a dramatic kris duel) and the police statements recorded by ACP Speers in December 1920, which describe a far more prosaic betrayal involving opium, a pre-arranged ambush, and a parang attack on Nayan's legs (pp. 113–127).

### Key Findings

- In 1909 Kedah had a 500-strong police force (260 Malays, 134 Indians, 6 Chinese, 5 Siamese); by 1929 this had grown to 764 men, almost entirely Kedah-born Malays, and the force was reported to be "well up to the highest Malayan standards" (pp. 93, 104).
- Gang robberies in Kedah peaked at 100 in 1921, compared with 107 for the entire Federated Malay States in 1920; Kedah's housebreaking figures for 1913–14 (154 and 150) exceeded the FMS total of 142 for 1920 (pp. 100–101, Table 1).
- The Kedah government offered a reward of $1,000 for Nayan's capture (the largest ever offered for a bandit at that time); the State Council approved the sum on 16 December 1920, to be shared: $500 to Panglima Hussain, $350 to Che Wang, $100 to Che Mat, and $50 to Corporal Sahit from Sik (pp. 120, 126–127).
- The abolition of corvee in October 1909 was replaced by land-tax and land-rent at rates of 50 cents per relong for coastal bendang and 30 cents per relong for inland bendang; Maxwell noted that many peasants would spend five dollars and travel two days to obtain a one-year exemption from a one-dollar tax (pp. 105–106).
- In 1910, 16,741 heads of cattle were licensed in Kedah (against 11,125 in 1909), and 15,808 sales were registered (against 6,599 the previous year); the Veterinary Department's revenue for 1910 was $15,729 (p. 106, n. 44).
- A joint memorandum from the Assistant Commissioners of Police of North and South Kedah, dated 3 August 1920, cited a "reign of terror" in eleven mukims and requested wide powers to shoot armed gangs on sight; the Acting Adviser Halifax and the State Legal Adviser were sceptical but advised that secret service votes in the State budget could be used at the ACPs' discretion (p. 124).

### Conclusion

Cheah concludes that the epidemic emergence of social bandits in North Kedah can only be fully understood through the lens of local peasant perception rather than official records. The folk legends of Salleh Tui and Nayan represent a "little tradition" of the lower classes that diverges sharply from the ruling class's documentation of the past. He leaves several questions deliberately open for future research: the colonial basis for classifying the Sam-Sams as a "criminal tribe," the extent to which Nayan was a genuine champion of the poor versus an instrument of local agrarian elites like Sulaiman Kerekau, and whether Nayan's death resulted from his break with Kerekau's protective patronage network. He explicitly rejects claims of anti-colonial motivation or messianism in the bandits' activities, and notes that the reasons why these social bandits appeared at the specific times they did still elude a satisfactory explanation (pp. 128–129).

## Context

- Primary archival sources include Kedah State Secretariat files (K/SUK series) held at the Arkib Negara Malaysia, Kedah Annual Reports, Police Annual Reports, and Maxwell's Annual Reports for Kedah (1909–1911); oral history was collected from informants in the Kuala Nerang, Pendang, Yan, and Sik districts in 1979–1980.
- The article contributes to the historiography of social banditry by applying Hobsbawm's framework to a Southeast Asian colonial context and by foregrounding oral tradition as a counter-narrative to official colonial records; it was presented in part at the Eighth Conference of the International Association of Historians of Asia (IAHA), Kuala Lumpur, August 1980.
