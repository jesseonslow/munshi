# The ‘Waterfall’ Botanic Garden on Pulau Pinang and the foundation of the Penang Botanical Gardens 1884–1910

D.S. Jones's 1997 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* reconstructs the founding and formative development of the Penang Botanic Gardens from 1884 to 1910, centring on the tenure of Charles Curtis as Assistant Superintendent of Forests for the Straits Settlements. Jones argues that Curtis transformed a malarial waste ground in the Waterfall River valley into a significant botanical and recreational asset through a design philosophy rooted in landscape aesthetics rather than systematic botanical arrangement, and that the Gardens' scientific contributions have been historically overshadowed by their administrative subordination to Singapore.

## Summary

Jones draws almost exclusively on Curtis's and Fox's annual reports to the Straits Settlements Forest Department (1885–1910), supplemented by Ridley's and Cantley's published accounts, to trace the physical and institutional evolution of the Gardens. The narrative is organised around three interlocking threads: the engineering and landscape works that shaped the valley, the botanical collecting and propagation programme that built the Herbarium, and the administrative and health challenges that constrained the project. Curtis arrived in July 1884 with prior experience at James Veitch & Son's Chelsea nursery and field collecting across the Indian Ocean region, and immediately set about extending the site, constructing road circuits, erecting plant sheds, and establishing the nursery infrastructure that would define the Gardens' character (pp. 75–78).

A recurring tension in the article is between Curtis's aesthetic vision and the practical demands of tropical infrastructure. The construction of the Municipal Reservoir in 1891 at the top of the garden, and the subsequent 1902 proposal for a larger reservoir that would have submerged the upper grounds, represent what Jones calls a "daunting two-part spectre" that paralysed planning and likely hastened Curtis's health decline (pp. 78–79). Similarly, the perishability of timber in the humid valley climate forced repeated reconstruction of plant sheds, leading Curtis to advocate for iron-framed structures after observing their use in Calcutta in 1888 (pp. 81–82). The article also highlights the chronic problem of labour: coolies were predominantly South Indian immigrants who frequently left for higher wages in Perak, and Curtis lamented the "lack of intelligent labour" as a barrier to high-class cultivation (p. 88).

The botanical dimension is presented as the Gardens' most under-recognised legacy. Curtis built a Herbarium of over 3,000 specimens by 1890, forwarded duplicates to Kew, and lent the entire collection to Dr George King for *Materials for a Malayan Flora*. He published a catalogue of 1,971 flowering plant and fern species native to Penang in 1894, and the Gardens functioned as a distribution hub, supplying thousands of plants annually for roadside planting across Penang, Province Wellesley, and Perak (pp. 84–86). Jones notes that the planting design was deliberately non-systematic, prioritising the exploitation of natural landscape features over taxonomic ordering—a point corroborated by the later curator F.S. Banfield (p. 87).

### Key Findings

- The site at 579 m elevation received an average annual rainfall of 381 cm in the 1890s; the 1890 rains caused two landslips in the steepest part of the grounds, and malarial fever was described as "of a more virulent type in the Gardens than elsewhere in Penang" (pp. 76–77).
- By the end of 1885, 11,500 trees had been supplied from Curtis's nurseries for roadside planting; between 1886 and 1890, plant distributions ranged from 1,123 to 6,213 specimens per year (pp. 84–86).
- The 1894 catalogue published by Curtis in the *JSBRAS* recorded 1,971 species across 793 genera and 129 natural orders native to Pulau Pinang (p. 86).
- The 1887 budget allocated $4,300 for the Waterfall Gardens, $750 for the plains nursery and Bungalow Garden, and $3,000 for general Forest Department functions; Curtis's own salary was $1,500 in 1888, rising to $1,800 by 1890 (p. 88).
- The swimming pool, opened 1 January 1891 at a construction cost of $77.25 for the dam plus $43.83 for the dressing shed, returned a profit of $180.05 in its first year, covering the caretaker's salary and providing nursery water (p. 83).
- The Gardens were transferred from the Forest Department to the Land Office on 1 January 1895 as part of a retrenchment programme; Curtis retired on 7 December 1903 and was succeeded by Walter Fox (pp. 75, 90).

### Conclusion

Jones's definitive takeaway is that Charles Curtis's legacy—the physical Gardens, the Herbarium, and the roadside planting programme—has been systematically under-recognised, both because of the administrative hierarchy that placed Penang beneath Singapore and because the Gardens' design philosophy (landscape-first, not taxonomy-first) made its scientific output less visible to later institutional memory. The article positions the Penang Botanic Gardens as a significant historical and botanical resource that has yet to receive the international standing it deserves (pp. 91–92).

## Context

- The article is grounded almost entirely in the Straits Settlements Forest Department annual reports (1885–1910) held in colonial administrative archives, supplemented by published obituaries and the 1894 *JSBRAS* catalogue.
- Historiographically, the piece fills a gap in the institutional history of Penang's botanical infrastructure, shifting attention away from the better-documented Singapore Botanic Gardens and restoring agency to a colonial mid-level officer whose work had been absorbed into the broader Ridley narrative.
