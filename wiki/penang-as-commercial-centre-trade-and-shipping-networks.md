# Penang as commercial centre: trade and shipping networks

Loh Wei Leng's 2009 article, published in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 82, Part 2), argues that Penang functioned as the premier commercial centre of the northern littoral of Southeast Asia throughout the colonial, pre-independent era, sustained by dense intra-regional trade and shipping networks rather than merely serving as a feeder port to Singapore. Drawing on port-city theory, Straits Settlements trade statistics, and case studies of Peranakan Chinese merchant families, the article substantiates Penang's role as an entrepot whose hinterland stretched from the Tenasserim coast to northern Sumatra.

## Summary

Loh situates Penang within the broader historiography of Southeast Asian port cities, tracing antecedents from pre-colonial Kedah polities through the Sriwijaya maritime empire to the East India Company's 1786 acquisition. The central analytical move is to challenge the conventional narrative—advanced by Cowan (1950) and Wong Lin Ken (1961)—that Singapore's 1819 foundation diminished Penang's commercial significance. Citing Wong Yee Tuan's doctoral research on the 'Big Five Hokkien Families,' Loh demonstrates that Penang's trade with Aceh, the Pedir Coast, Moulmein, and Pegu remained vigorous well into the 1840s, and that shipping figures for 1843–44 actually showed Penang outperforming Singapore in both native and square-rigged vessel arrivals and departures (pp. 28–29).

The article then shifts to the mechanisms through which Peranakan Chinese merchants sustained these networks. Using Braudel's concept of trade circuits—where commercial networks depend on communications, cooperation, and connections between agents at different points—Loh examines two case studies. The first is Thio Thiau Siat (Cheong Fatt Tse), whose network spanned Penang, the Malay States, and Sumatra through opium syndicates, the Li Wang Company, and the Deli Bank, linking Hakka, Hokkien, and Peranakan Chinese actors across the region (pp. 32–33). The second is the Khaw family, whose shipping firm Koe Guan grew from a few vessels in the 1870s to 16 steamers by 1904, operating regular services to Burma, Siam, Sumatra, Singapore, and China, and which responded to European competition by founding the Eastern Shipping Company in collaboration with other Penang shipowners (pp. 33–34).

Epigraphic and demographic evidence reinforces the argument for deep regional connectivity. A 1788 list of 405 Chinese inhabitants in Penang shows substantial numbers already resident from Kedah, Malacca, Pattani, and Songkla, indicating that the port attracted Chinese who were already embedded in regional networks rather than arriving fresh from China (p. 31). Temple inscriptions from the 1790s and 1800s name donors from Melaka and Songkhla, further evidencing pre-existing commercial and social ties between Penang's Chinese community and those of the wider northern littoral (p. 31).

### Key Findings

- Straits Settlements Blue Book data (1841–1914) show Southeast Asian trade consistently accounted for the largest share of Penang's imports, peaking at 72.70% in 1908, and for exports in most years, confirming the port's primary orientation toward its regional hinterland rather than the West (p. 30).
- In 1843–44, Penang recorded 744 native vessel arrivals and 994 departures, compared to Singapore's approximately 200–300 fewer in the same categories, demonstrating Penang's continued pre-eminence as a regional port a quarter-century after Singapore's founding (p. 28).
- The 1788 list of 405 Chinese inhabitants in Penang includes 47 heads of households from Kedah, 21 from Songkhla, 7 from Pattani, and 6 from Malacca, evidencing that the early Chinese community was drawn from existing regional networks rather than directly from China (p. 31).
- Koe Guan, the Khaw family shipping firm, expanded from a handful of vessels in the 1870s to 16 steamers by 1904 with regular services to Burma, Siam, Sumatra, Singapore, and China, becoming the largest shipping company in Penang (p. 34).
- The 1907–09 Penang opium farm syndicate comprised 17 members whose operations spanned Kedah, Perak, the East Coast of Sumatra, and Singapore, illustrating the geographic breadth of revenue-farming business alliances (p. 33).
- The 1933–34 Straits Settlements Trade Commission formally described Penang as "the natural centre of an area including the East and North Coasts of Sumatra, the West coast as far south as Padang, the Northern Malay States and Southern Siam" (p. 31).

### Conclusion

Loh concludes that Penang's claim to be the primary port city of the northern littoral during the colonial, pre-independent period is well substantiated: its entrepot function—collecting produce from and distributing goods to the northern Straits region—was sustained by Peranakan Chinese merchant networks whose commercial, kinship, and institutional ties spanned the subregion, and whose shipping and revenue-farming operations gave concrete expression to Penang's role as a commercial centre rather than a peripheral feeder port.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on Straits Settlements Blue Books, the 1933–34 Trade Commission Report, Chinese epigraphic materials (Franke and Chen 1985), and Godley's studies of revenue-farming networks, with secondary reliance on Wong Yee Tuan's (2007) doctoral thesis and Cushman's (1991) monograph on the Khaw family.
- Historiographically, the piece contributes to the revisionist strand that challenges the "decline of Penang after 1819" thesis, aligning with Lombard's call for an integrated Southeast Asian economic history and with recent work on autonomous intra-Asian trade circuits (Sugihara 1996; Wade 2009).
