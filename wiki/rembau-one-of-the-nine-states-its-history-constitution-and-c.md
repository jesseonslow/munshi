# Rembau, one of the nine states: its history, constitution and customs

The Annual Report of the Straits Branch, Royal Asiatic Society for 1920, compiled by Honorary Secretary J. C. Moulton and Treasurer V. Knight, provides an administrative and financial overview of the Society's activities during a period of post-war adjustment. Published in 1921, it documents membership changes, journal output, financial positions, and strategic decisions regarding the Society's library and publication programme in the immediate aftermath of the First World War.

## Summary

The report opens with a record of membership fluctuations, noting the death of five members during 1920 — including Sir Evelyn Ellis, the prominent Singapore lawyer, and W. H. Mackray, F.M.S., whose joint work with C. W. C. Parr on the history of Rembau (Journal No. 56, pp. 1–157) is singled out as a "valuable" contribution. The Council also reports the removal of 29 members by resignation, of whom 19 were struck under Rule 6 for non-payment of subscriptions, a housekeeping measure that had been neglected for several years. The resulting active membership stood at 329.

The bulk of the report addresses the Society's publication record and financial health. Two journal numbers (Nos. 81 and 82) were issued in 1920, totalling 226 pages — a figure the Council candidly acknowledges falls short of the 306-page annual average maintained during the Society's first forty years. The Council notes a growing imbalance between technical and non-technical content, urging members to contribute more travel, historical, and natural-history material. Financially, the Society's balance grew from $7,142.89 to $8,309.27, with subscriptions rising to $1,670 (partly due to $435 in arrears collected for 1915–1919) and journal sales at $765. The Council flags a looming cost crisis: Dr. E. D. Merrill's 600-page flora of Borneo, in the press for two years, would absorb a large share of the balance, and rising paper and printing costs meant 1921 journal prices would be approximately 120 per cent above pre-war levels.

A notable administrative decision was the redistribution of portions of the Society's library to institutional partners — botanical journals to the Botanic Gardens, geological journals to the F.M.S. Geological Department, and museum journals to the Raffles Museum — a move prompted by the recognition that many members resided outside Singapore and could not make use of the collection in situ.

### Key Findings

- Active membership stood at 329 after the removal of 29 members (19 under Rule 6 for non-payment), with 83 per cent residing in Malaya but only 3–4 per cent contributing papers annually (pp. viii–ix).
- Journal output for 1920 was 226 pages across two issues, compared to a 306-page annual average over the Society's first forty years (p. viii).
- Total financial balance carried forward was $8,309.27, comprising $2,500 in Victory Loan, $2,200 in S.S. War Loan, $2,000 in fixed deposit, and $1,609.27 in current accounts (pp. ix–x).
- Subscriptions received totalled $1,670, including $435 in arrears for 1915–1919 and $250 from five life-membership compounds (p. ix).
- The cost of printing the two 1920 journals was $1,153; the Council projected that 1921 journal prices would be approximately 120 per cent above pre-war costs due to rising paper and printing expenses (p. x).
- Dr. E. D. Merrill's flora of Borneo, in the press for two years, was expected to run to approximately 600 pages and would absorb a large proportion of the Society's balance (p. x).

### Conclusion

The 1920 Annual Report presents a Society in modest but stable condition, managing the transition from wartime austerity to post-war normalcy. The Council's tone is one of measured concern: membership is adequate, finances are sound, but the intellectual output of the membership base is thin relative to the Society's potential, and the rising cost of publication threatens to outstrip available funds unless the Merrill flora is completed without further delay.

## Context

- This is an official administrative document of the Straits Branch, Royal Asiatic Society, written in the standard colonial-era institutional register. The Council's framing of membership as "fictitiously large" and its appeal to members to "tap" new sources of supply reflect the self-referential governance culture of British colonial learned societies in the Straits Settlements.
- The report's mention of W. H. Mackray's Rembau article (Journal No. 56, pp. 1–157) is the sole reference to that work within this document; the full text of the Rembau study is not contained here.
