# Pepper

## Scope and Historical Definition

Black pepper (*Piper nigrum*) was one of the principal export crops of southern Malaya throughout the nineteenth century, cultivated in close association with gambier on plantations in Singapore and Johore. The two crops were not interplanted but allocated to separate parts of the same holding, and their management was interlocked: the almost continuous collecting of gambier foliage could be integrated with the highly seasonal cycle of pepper production, ensuring year-round employment for the labour force required to tend the vines [[1, pp. 79–80]](#ref-1). As a general rule, pepper occupied one acre for every ten acres of gambier [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1). The vines took approximately three years to reach maturity, after which they produced two crops annually, in December–January and in July–August [1, p. 80, n. 11]. Pepper vines passed their peak of production in their fifteenth or twentieth years [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1).

The economic significance of pepper in this region was substantial. By 1848, Singapore's plantations produced 30,923 *pikuls* of pepper, and together with gambier the two crops occupied over three-quarters of the total estimated cultivated area on the island and accounted for nearly three-fifths of the total value of agricultural produce [[1, pp. 78–79]](#ref-1). In Johore, pepper exports to Singapore rose from 56,203 *pikuls* in 1880 to 117,024 *pikuls* in 1889 [[1, pp. 96–97]](#ref-1). The price of black pepper in Singapore rose from approximately $12 per *pikul* in 1880 to over $20 in 1889 [[1, p. 96]](#ref-1).

## The Kangchu System and Riverine Colonisation

The cultivation of pepper in Johore was organised under the *Kangchu System*, a framework of riverine settlement and landholding that Jackson described as "an entirely unique and perhaps efficacious system of land-tenure and co-operative cultivation" [[1, pp. 82–83]](#ref-1). Chinese agricultural settlers moved into Johore along the main rivers and their tributaries, establishing small villages or "river depots" known as *Kangkars* (literally "foot of the river" in the Teochew dialect), from which cultivation extended only from the river bank to the nearest watershed [[1, p. 83]](#ref-1). Each *Kangkar* was governed by a semi-feudal Chinese headman, the *Kangchu* ("lord of the river"), who held the land under a *surat sungei* ("river document") granted by the Malay ruler [[1, p. 83]](#ref-1). The earliest recorded *surat sungei* in the Johore Archives is dated 9 October 1844, for Sungai Sekudai; the second, dated 22 October 1844, granted Sungai Tebrau to Kapitan Tan Kee Soon [[2, pp. 144–145]](#ref-2).

Trocki traced the origins of this system back to approximately 1740, when the Bugis *Yang Dipertuan Muda* Daeng Celak established gambier plantations worked by Chinese labourers at Riau [[2, p. 132]](#ref-2). After the Dutch destroyed the Riau entrepôt in 1784, the Chinese planters governed themselves for over three decades, achieving a degree of economic and military autonomy that prefigured the *Kangchu System* [[2, p. 132]](#ref-2). The shift of the gambier marketplace to Singapore after 1819, the removal of the British import tariff on gambier in 1835, and the exhaustion of cultivable land in Singapore's interior together drove the migration of planters across the strait into Johore [[2, p. 132]](#ref-2). By 1862, approximately 37 *surat sungai* had been issued covering 32 *Kangkar* settlements: 18 rivers in the Johore Straits, 19 on the Johore River, and 2 on the west coast [[2, pp. 150–151]](#ref-2).

Within each *Kangkar*, the actual cultivated patches were called *bangsals* (a term derived from Malay for "shed or lean-to shelter"), each containing a gambier cauldron and furnaces for boiling purposes [[1, pp. 88–89]](#ref-1). *Bangsals* probably averaged between 10 and 50 acres, producing annually some 100–200 *pikuls* of gambier and 50–150 *pikuls* of pepper each [[1, p. 89]](#ref-1). Each *bangsal* employed about nine or ten men [[1, p. 89]](#ref-1). The *Kangchu* did not by custom own all the land within his *Kangkar*; anyone who planted with his permission was treated as having a freehold, though the land itself was of limited value given the shifting cultivation cycle [[1, p. 89]](#ref-1).

## Economic Dynamics and the Credit System

The financing of pepper plantations was controlled by merchants in Singapore town. Under the "Credit Ticket System," the passages of intending Chinese immigrants were arranged and paid by agencies who passed the cost to the employer in Malaya; the new arrival, or *sinkheh*, was obliged to pay off the expenses by labour, usually working for a year at reduced wages [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1). After completing this obligation, many immigrants opened small plantations of their own, financed by Chinese shopkeepers and merchants in Singapore who claimed a proportion of future crops until the debt was discharged [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1). By 1839, it was estimated that two out of every three plantations in Singapore were subject to such encumbrances, with advances running at high rates of interest and often made in clothes and provisions at above-market rates [[1, pp. 80–81]](#ref-1).

In 1864, Chinese merchants in Singapore claimed to have advanced over one million dollars to develop the Johore plantations, although most of this money had been borrowed originally from European merchants [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1). At that date there were 100 gambier shops and over 200 provision stores in Singapore that relied almost entirely on the Johore trade [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1). The *Kangchu* held monopolies of opium, gambling, pawn-brokering, and the sale of liquor and pork within his *Kangkar*, and also controlled the transportation of all exports [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1). Tan Tek Soon noted that the traders interested in a river and the *Kangchu* would furnish the necessary capital between them to build a gambier *tongkang* of sufficient capacity to carry off the produce in fortnightly trips, and that owing to vested interests, no competition was ever permitted [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1).

The Gambier and Pepper Society (or *Kong-kek*), established in Singapore in 1867, served for the mutual protection of financiers and planters and for the protection of the trade between Johore and Singapore [1, p. 91, n. 73]. The society regulated prices at which produce was sold, typically about 30 per cent below actual market value [[1, p. 91]](#ref-1).

Gambier prices at Singapore fluctuated dramatically: $1.30–1.75 per *pikul* in January 1831, rising to $4.00–5.00 in January 1834, then falling to $0.90–1.00 by January 1849 before recovering to $2.85 by 1861 [[2, p. 141]](#ref-2). This volatility, combined with the exhaustion of cultivable land in Singapore, was a direct catalyst for the expansion into Johore. In 1846, a letter in the *Singapore Free Press* from a secret society headman styled "Keeper of the Quinquangular Seal" announced that the Ngee Heng (Tan Tae Hoe) had commanded approximately 4,000 pepper and gambier planters to leave Singapore for Johore in retaliation against government plantation assessments [[2, pp. 143–144]](#ref-2).

## Decline and Abolition

The "shifting" method of cultivation that underpinned pepper production proved environmentally unsustainable. Gambier was an extremely exhaustive crop, and young pepper vines would not thrive on old worn-out pepper land [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1). A gambier plantation became exhausted in 15 years from its commencement, and the fuel supply for boiling was expected to last only about 25 years [[1, pp. 80–81]](#ref-1). By 1836, complaints were already being made about the jungle being cut down for firewood and plantations being deserted to run to *lalang* grass [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1). In Singapore, only three-quarters of existing plantations were cultivated in 1850, and it was felt that cultivation was "retrograding as the older plantations have all become exhausted" [[1, p. 81]](#ref-1).

In Singapore, the total acreage under gambier and pepper fell from approximately 11,000 acres in 1890 to 600 acres in 1912 and 75 acres in 1913, as Chinese planters shifted to pineapple growing and rubber cultivation [[1, pp. 94–95]](#ref-1). In Johore, exports of gambier fell by over 40 per cent between 1890 and 1910, and between 1913 and 1917 the value of exports of both gambier and pepper declined by nearly 60 per cent [[1, p. 97]](#ref-1). The *Kangchu System* was finally abolished by the *Kangchu Rights (Abolition) Enactment* of 1917, under which all remaining *Kangchus* were compensated financially for the loss of their former rights [[1, p. 85]](#ref-1). By this time rubber had come to claim the main interest of Chinese cultivators in Johore [[1, p. 85]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on pepper cultivation is dominated by two major studies that together provide a comprehensive account of the crop's economic history in southern Malaya. Jackson's 1965 article in the *JMBRAS* was the first systematic treatment of Chinese gambier and pepper planting in Singapore and Johore, drawing on Straits Settlements Blue Books, Annual Reports, the *Singapore Free Press*, the *Singapore and Straits Directory* (1887), maps of Johore from 1887 and 1904, and Chinese-language sources including Tan Tek Soon's 1902 article and Siah U Chin's 1848 contribution to the *Journal of the Indian Archipelago and Eastern Asia* [[1, pp. 77–105]](#ref-1). Jackson's central contribution was the clarification of the *Kangchu System*'s internal structure and spatial logic, distinguishing between *kangkar*, *bangsal*, and the loosely used term *plantation* [[1, pp. 88–91]](#ref-1).

Trocki's 1976 article extended the chronological framework significantly, tracing the system's origins to the 1740s at Riau and demonstrating that it was not a Johore-specific phenomenon of the mid-nineteenth century but the product of a long evolution [[2, pp. 132–155]](#ref-2). Trocki was the first to use systematically the *Buku Daftar Surat-surat Keterangan Berkebun* (1844–1903) in the Johore Archives, which had only become available to scholars in the 1960s [2, p. 130, n. 6]. His work corrected the earlier assumption, following Coope (1936), that the *Kangchu System* was of limited significance, by demonstrating its centrality to the Singapore economy throughout the nineteenth century [[2, p. 132]](#ref-2).

Together, these two studies establish that pepper cultivation in the region was not merely an agricultural activity but the foundation of a complex socio-economic and political institution that shaped the development of Johore as a state and the economic geography of the Singapore–Johore corridor for over a century.
