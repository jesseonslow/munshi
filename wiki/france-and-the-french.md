# France and the French

The French presence in the Malay world, though modest in scale compared to British, Dutch, or even German competitors, spanned a remarkable range of activities—from naval warfare and commercial exploration to mining, plantation enterprise, and Catholic missionary work—across the eighteenth, nineteenth, and twentieth centuries. The records preserved in the Society's journals reveal a pattern of intermittent but persistent French engagement with the Malay Peninsula and its surrounding waters, characterised by ambitious individual initiatives that rarely achieved the structural depth of their Anglo-Dutch counterparts. From the merchantman *St. Jean Baptiste* at Trengganu in 1769 to the rubber plantations of Socfin in the 1960s, French actors moved through the region as traders, soldiers, scientists, missionaries, and industrialists, leaving behind a body of primary-source material that has only recently been made accessible to English-language scholarship [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2).

## Scope and Historical Definition

The French encounter with the Malay world can be divided into several distinct phases, each defined by different institutional frameworks and objectives. The earliest documented contact in the archival record is the four-day stop at Trengganu in July 1769 by the *St. Jean Baptiste*, a 650-ton merchantman built in France for trade in Eastern waters after the collapse of the French India Company's monopoly [[1, p. 145]](#ref-1). This vessel, commanded by Jean-François de Surville, was part of a broader programme of post-monopoly French commercial exploration in the Pacific and Southeast Asia, and her subsequent voyage took her to the Solomons and New Zealand [[1, p. 145]](#ref-1). The visit was brief and commercially modest: the French sold four caskets of opium for 800 piastres and purchased provisions totalling 533 piastres and 150 Flemish knives [[1, p. 151]](#ref-1). Yet the four officers' journals, preserved in the Marine section of the Archives Nationales in Paris, provide a detailed snapshot of the Trengganu sultanate under Sultan Mansur Shah, including its political intelligence, currency system, and judicial practices [[1, pp. 145]](#ref-1).

The Napoleonic era brought a different kind of French presence: military. In January 1804, Admiral Durand Linois's squadron, with the 80-gun ship of the line *Marengo* as flagship, anchored off Pulau Aur at the entrance to the Singapore Strait in an attempt to intercept the East India Company's China fleet [[3, pp. 68–69]](#ref-3). The resulting engagement of 14–15 February, in which the *Marengo* alone fired 380 shots, ended without decisive result when Linois was deceived by the East Indiamen's warship disguises and broke off the action [[3, pp. 72–74]](#ref-3). The journal of Charles Duclos-Legris, the *Marengo*'s coxswain, who recorded the campaign in a manuscript volume now held in the National Maritime Museum, Greenwich, provides a rare French naval perspective on the strait's geography, its inhabitants, and the commercial traffic that passed through it [[3, pp. 68]](#ref-3).

By the mid-nineteenth century, French engagement had shifted decisively toward commercial and scientific channels. J.S.D. Rawlins's comprehensive survey identifies three broad phases: a negligible early presence in Singapore (barely a single trading house among twenty-five European firms, a community of sixty-four persons by 1880); a second phase triggered by the nationalist resurgence following the Franco-Prussian War, in which the Société de Géographie Commerciale dispatched explorers to Perak; and a third phase from the early twentieth century onward, dominated by the rise of Socfin as the largest private planting company in Malaya [[2, pp. 50–94]](#ref-2).

## Commercial and Industrial Enterprise

The most consequential French industrial venture in the Malay Peninsula was the Société des Etains de Kinta (S.E.K.), which grew out of the surveying work of John Errington de la Croix in the Kinta Valley of Perak in 1880–81 [[2, pp. 74–76]](#ref-2). Rawlins argues that this company was the single most important European mining venture in Perak before the turn of the century, pioneering the use of hydro-electric power in 1906 and working the remarkable Lahat "pipe" to a depth of 312 feet, which the F.M.S. Geologist in 1904 declared "nowhere in the world" to be matched [[2, pp. 74–76]](#ref-2). By 1939, French tin mines' authorised capital stood at an estimated 21,000,000 francs (approximately £210,000), constituting virtually the entire non-British, non-local capital in the open-cast section of the industry and roughly one-third of all foreign capital in that section [[2, p. 79]](#ref-2).

In the plantation sector, Socfin (Société Financière des Caoutchoucs) emerged from the humble origins of Fauconnier, Posth, and Audouin's 500-acre concession at Rantau Panjang in 1905, through the transformative leadership of R.M.E. Michaux during the 1920s and 1930s, to its position as the largest private planting company in Malaya [[2, pp. 64]](#ref-2). By 31 December 1963, Socfin's Malayan holdings totalled 65,108 acres, comprising 18,436 acres of rubber and 6,180 acres of oil palm under the Belgian-owned Selangor Plantations, and 16,846 acres of rubber and 23,616 acres of oil palm under the French companies [[2, p. 64]](#ref-2).

Despite these industrial achievements, French trade with Malaya remained marginal in the broader context. French exports to Malaya, which hovered around $1 million at the end of the nineteenth century, peaked at $13.3 million in 1926 (0.6 per cent of Malayan total imports) and fell to $1.4 million in 1933; by 1963, despite a post-1953 recovery, France's share of Malayan imports stood at approximately 1.3 per cent, well below Germany (9.3 per cent) and Japan (27.4 per cent) in manufactured goods [[2, pp. 80–87]](#ref-2). One notable exception was brandy: France maintained 85–90 per cent of the Malayan brandy market by value throughout the entire period under review, with exports valued at $11.5 million (315,200 gallons) in 1963, representing 22.4 per cent of all French exports to the country [[2, pp. 87–88]](#ref-2).

Rawlins attributes French underachievement not solely to British diplomatic opposition—which effectively strangled the Kra Canal scheme of De Lesseps and Deloncle in the 1880s and was consolidated by the 1896 convention and the 1904 Entente Cordiale—but to a deeper structural reluctance to invest capital in Southeast Asia, a reluctance that persisted even in Indo-China where foreign competition was legally restricted [[2, pp. 50–94]](#ref-2). French capital gravitated toward North Africa, and the psychological weight of the loss of Vietnam further dampened commercial confidence in the region [[2]](#ref-2).

## Scientific, Missionary, and Naval Dimensions

The French contribution to the scientific study of the Malay world was substantial, particularly in the field of mineralogy and geology. Xavier Brau de Saint-Pol Lias, a French naturalist and explorer, published "Tin and Its Native Land" in *Popular Science Monthly* in June 1890, drawing on his direct observation of Chinese-operated tin mines in Perak and Pahang during the late 1880s [[4, pp. 193–99]](#ref-4). Lias argued that the Malay Peninsula was not merely the most ancient but also the most productive tin-producing region in the world, and that it may have been the original cradle of tin metallurgy from which knowledge diffused to Europe over millennia [[4]](#ref-4). He cited M. de La Croix's 1888 French government publication, which gave the peninsula's production from official documents as over 24,000 tons, "more than half that of the entire globe" [2, p. 199, n. 1]. His etymological argument, following the French geologist Germain Bapst, posited that the Malay word *tima* (and its compounds *tima poute* for tin and *tima itam* for lead) was the ancestor of the European *tin*, *zinn*, and *tenn*, suggesting that the word and the metallurgical knowledge travelled from Malacca to Europe by slow migration [[4]](#ref-4).

The missionary dimension is documented in the correspondence of Father Mathurin-Pierre Pécot (1792–1823), a French missionary of the Société des Missions Étrangères de Paris who traversed the Malay Peninsula during the Siamese conquest of Kedah in 1821 and the early British colonial consolidation of Penang and Singapore [[5, pp. 167–92]](#ref-5). Pécot's Malay-language missal and catechism, *Baktian Saharian*, was the first Catholic prayer book produced in Malay for the Penang community, and was ultimately published in Paris in 1828 as *Kebaktian Sahari Harian dan Sembahiang Iang didjadikan deri Parochius Romanus* [[5, pp. 170–171]](#ref-5). His trans-Peninsula overland journey in April–May 1822, a seven-day elephant trek through primary forest from Kedah to Ligor (Nakhon Si Thammarat), constitutes a rare first-hand European account of the Peninsula's interior during a period of intense political upheaval [[5, pp. 178–181]](#ref-5). Pécot died of malaria at Penang on 21 July 1823, aged 31, by which time the Penang Catholic community had grown from roughly 600 to approximately 1,000 [[5, pp. 170–171]](#ref-5).

The naval dimension is captured in two further records. Bougainville's 1824 visit to Malacca, recorded in his official journal of the circumnavigation aboard the frigate *La Thetis* and corvette *L'Esperance*, provides a French naval perspective on the strategic logic of the Anglo-Dutch exchange, which he judged "equally advantageous for both nations": the British secured unimpeded command of the Strait for their China trade, while the Dutch obtained Belitung Island to protect their Bangka tin mines [[6, pp. 131–137]](#ref-6). His observation that Malacca's trade was "absolutely non-existent" by August 1824, with only three merchant ships at anchor, all Dutch, captures the settlement's commercial decline following the establishment of Singapore [[6, p. 134]](#ref-6).

Brau de Saint-Pol Lias's 1883 account of the Aceh region of Sumatra, presented to the Société de Géographie during the height of the Dutch military campaign against the Sultanate of Aceh, offers a vivid first-hand ethnographic and military narrative of the west coast, the interior Gayo highlands, and the Dutch colonial administration at Kota Radjah [[7, pp. 205–29]](#ref-7). The paper closes with a passage in which Lias argues that colonisation "is the most vigorous source wherein a nation's strengths can be renewed" and that France cannot afford to remain disinterested in the "sharing between the civilised nations" of the globe [[7, p. 229]](#ref-7).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on French presence in the Malay world reveals a clear evolution in both scope and methodology. The earliest contributions, such as Parkinson's 1952 translation of Duclos-Legris's journal and Dunmore's 1973 article on the *St. Jean Baptiste*, were primarily exercises in primary-source recovery: making accessible unpublished French archival material held in Paris and Greenwich to an English-language readership [[1]](#ref-1), [[3]](#ref-3). These pieces were motivated by the recognition that the better-known English and Dutch records of the region were supplemented, and in some cases contradicted, by French accounts that had never been published [[1, p. 145]](#ref-1).

Rawlins's 1966 survey marked a significant shift toward synthetic economic history, providing the first comprehensive treatment of French commercial activity in the Peninsula, a subject previously addressed only in passing [[2]](#ref-2). His work drew on company records, trade statistics, geological survey reports, and direct interviews with senior company personnel conducted in 1964, and it filled a gap in the economic historiography that had been identified in Allen and Donnithorne's *Western Enterprise in Indonesia and Malaya* (1957) and Wong Lin Ken's *The Malayan Tin Industry to 1914* (1965) [[2]](#ref-2).

The 2020 volume in *JMBRAS* 93(2) represents the most recent phase of this scholarly tradition, bringing together four distinct French-source contributions—Pécot's missionary letters, Lias's tin article, Lias's Aceh account, and Bougainville's Malacca journal—each with substantial editorial framing and translation [[4]](#ref-4), [[6]](#ref-6), [[7]](#ref-7), [[5]](#ref-5). The Reids' edition of Pécot's correspondence, in particular, situates the missionary's work within the broader history of the vernacularisation of Christianity in the region, complementing their earlier publications on the subject [[5]](#ref-5). The editor's note to Lias's Aceh paper explicitly situates it alongside two other French visitors to the region in the early 1880s—de la Croix, engaged in tin mining in Perak, and Jacques de Morgan, a mining engineer seeking a concession—thereby constructing a coherent picture of French scientific and commercial activity in the region during that decade [[7, p. 205]](#ref-7).

A recurring tension in the literature is between the ambition of individual French actors and the structural limitations that constrained their impact. Rawlins is careful to note that British opposition was neither the sole nor even the primary explanation for French underachievement [[2]](#ref-2). The speculative diffusionism of Lias's tin-origin thesis, while now regarded as untenable, reflects the broader intellectual climate of late-nineteenth-century French science and serves as a reminder that the archival record must be read in its own historical context [2, p. 199, n. 2]. The Society's ongoing programme of publishing primary source materials relevant to the history of the Malay world continues to draw on French archival holdings to supplement and complicate the better-known Anglo-Dutch narrative of the region [[1]](#ref-1), [[5]](#ref-5).
