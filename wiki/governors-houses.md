# Governors’ houses

J.M. Gullick's 1998 article traces the origins, construction, and subsequent fates of official residences for British governors and high commissioners in Penang, Kuala Lumpur, and Kuala Kangsar, arguing that these buildings serve as material evidence for understanding the shifting administrative hierarchies, economic fortunes, and social world of the British colonial professional class in Malaya from the early nineteenth century through the 1930s.

## Summary

Gullick opens by establishing the social origins of colonial governors—men drawn from the Victorian and Edwardian professional class rather than the hereditary aristocracy, who occupied a large mansion on a hill as one of the principal gratifications of office. The article then addresses a particular administrative anomaly: in the Malay States, the governor's official base was Singapore, and as "High Commissioner" he was effectively a visitor who did not require a permanent local residence. This arrangement, however, proved unsatisfactory to those involved, and the resulting pattern of construction, contestation, and eventual abandonment of official houses in the states forms the article's central narrative.

The Penang section is the longest and most detailed. Gullick reconstructs the history of Suffolk House, built c. 1808–1810 by W.E. Phillips on a former Francis Light pepper estate, and its steady decline after Penang lost its capital status to Singapore in 1826. The house was progressively stripped of its architectural pretensions over the following decades and ultimately sold off. The companion narrative of "Bel Retiro" on Penang Hill—originally a modest wood-and-atap bungalow that served as a hill station retreat—tracks the development of the hill as a sanatorium and recreation ground, and the building's partial reconstruction in stone in the 1880s–1890s.

The Kuala Lumpur and Kuala Kangsar sections are shorter but politically charged. Carcosa, the grand mansion built for Swettenham as first Resident-General of the FMS (1896–1898), became a site of jurisdictional contest when Governor Anderson in 1904 attempted to make it the exclusive preserve of the High Commissioner, triggering the construction of a separate, smaller residence (King's House) for the Chief Secretary. The Kuala Kangsar residence, erected in 1906–1907 on the site of the celebrated Che Mida's house, reflected the political centrality of Sultan Idris of Perak as informal spokesman for the FMS rulers; by 1930, under the pressure of the economic slump, it had been converted to a residential school and was later demolished.

### Key Findings

- Suffolk House was built c. 1808–1810 by W.E. Phillips on a former Francis Light pepper estate; Governor-General Lord Minto confirmed in 1811 that Phillips "built himself" the house, countering later local claims that Light had constructed it (pp. 124–125).
- The Penang Residency was completed in 1890 at an actual cost of $50,000 against an approved estimate of $43,000, with a further $1,800 spent on grounds improvement in 1890–92 (p. 127).
- Bel Retiro's north wing was rebuilt in stone between 1885 and 1887 at a cost of $14,000, and the south wing was rebuilt in 1889 at an additional $12,000—a substantial outlay by the standards of the time (p. 132).
- Carcosa was sited on twenty-four acres excised from the Lake Gardens, with four acres developed as gardens and the remainder as open park land; it was opened in August 1898 with a lavish fancy dress ball (pp. 132–133).
- King's House (the High Commissioner's residence in Kuala Lumpur) took three years to complete (1910–1913) because the original contractors went bankrupt during the rubber price decline (p. 135).
- The Kuala Kangsar High Commissioner's residence, erected 1906–1907 on the site of Che Mida's house, was converted to a residential school by 1930 and subsequently demolished (p. 137).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that governors' houses are not merely architectural curiosities but material records of administrative power, economic cycles, and the social ambitions of the colonial professional class. Their construction, progressive neglect, and eventual demolition or repurposing track the rise and fall of British institutional presence in Malaya with a precision that official dispatches alone cannot provide.

## Context

- The article draws on a wide range of primary sources including governors' memoirs (Anson, Cavenagh, Swettenham), Colonial Office papers, Straits Settlements Legislative Council records, Perak Annual Reports, and contemporary travel accounts and artists' watercolours.
- Historiographically, the piece contributes to the emerging field of colonial administrative culture and architectural history in Malaya, complementing S.R. Aiken's *Imperial Belvederes* (1994) on hill stations and H.S. Barlow's *Swettenham* (1995) on the political dimensions of official residence.
