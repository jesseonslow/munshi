# Notes on the formation of words in Malay and cognate languages

Nicholas Tarling's 1966 article traces the repeated and ultimately futile British attempts to establish diplomatic and commercial relations with Vietnam between 1822 and 1858, arguing that the Vietnamese court's consistent policy of avoiding European entanglement—reinforced by the violent incursions of the French and Americans—prevented Britain from securing any treaty or meaningful political relationship, leaving the field open for France to establish its colonial foothold in 1858.

## Summary

Tarling extends the narrative begun by Alastair Lamb's 1961 documentary study of the East India Company's earlier missions to Vietnam, covering the period from the Crawfurd mission of 1821–22 through the French seizure of Tourane and Saigon in 1858. The central problem he explores is why Britain, the predominant European power in Asia, failed to establish even a basic commercial treaty with Vietnam, while Siam—facing similar structural pressures—succeeded in doing so. The answer, Tarling argues, lies in the Vietnamese court's deliberate policy of non-involvement with Europeans, a stance that was not merely commercial protectionism but a broader political strategy of preserving independence by refusing to be drawn into the rivalries of European powers.

The article follows a sequence of British missions—Crawfurd (1821–22), the Phlegethon (1845), Davis (1847), Brooke (1850), and Bowring's Wade envoy (1855)—each of which was rebuffed or received with minimal courtesy. Tarling demonstrates that the Vietnamese consistently insisted on dealing with the British Crown rather than the Governor-General of India, refused royal audiences, and used procedural delays and geographical obstacles (the Hue river bar, monsoon rains) to prevent any envoy from reaching the capital. The violent actions of the American frigate Constitution at Tourane in 1845 and the French destruction of Vietnamese junks in 1847 confirmed the court's worst fears about European intentions and hardened its refusal to engage. By the time Bowring dispatched Wade in 1855, the Vietnamese had already brought the royal trade to Singapore to an end, and the court's isolationist posture was effectively unbreakable by diplomatic means alone.

The article's broader argument is that Britain's failure was not due to lack of effort or commercial interest, but to a fundamental misreading of Vietnamese political culture. British officials repeatedly assumed that the example of China's submission to British power after the Opium War would encourage Vietnam to follow suit, or that the contrast between peaceful British approaches and violent French ones would work in Britain's favour. Neither assumption proved correct. The Vietnamese drew the opposite lesson from China's defeat: that European contact brought only danger.

### Key Findings

- In 1830–31, some 35 junks and topes from Cochin-China and Cambodia called at Singapore, aggregating approximately 2,225 tons; by 1835–36, fourteen topes came from central Vietnam alone (pp. 5–6).
- The Davis mission of October 1847 arrived at Tourane on the 9th and departed on the 27th without securing a royal audience or exchanging presents, despite carrying full powers from Queen Victoria (pp. 16–18).
- The French attack at Tourane in April 1847 resulted in the destruction of Vietnamese junks and the killing of seventeen men, prompting an edict excluding Europeans from Vietnam and placing a price on missionary heads (pp. 15, 41).
- Thomas Wade's mission of September 1855 reached the Hue river on the 7th but was confined to a public hall under armed guard for six days before being extracted by the Rattler on the 13th, without delivering Bowring's letter to the court (pp. 26–28).
- The joint Franco-Spanish expedition reached Tourane at the end of August 1858, seized the citadel, and subsequently took Saigon, establishing the nucleus of the French colony of Cochin-China (p. 31).
- Tu-duc brought the royal Vietnamese trade to Singapore to an end following the 1847 Tourane affair, after which only small, defenceless topes continued the voyage at their own risk (pp. 15, 25).

### Conclusion

Tarling's definitive takeaway is that Vietnam's isolation was a rational political strategy, not a commercial aberration, and that Britain's repeated diplomatic failures created a vacuum that France filled through force. The article closes with King Mongkut's 1865 observation that the Vietnamese "were stubborn and determined to hold on to their old policy" and "did not know the real strength of the maritime powers"—a judgment Tarling endorses as the most apt summary of the entire period.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on Foreign Office records (F.O.17 series), India Office Library collections (Board's Collections, Despatches to Bengal), and the Bowring papers at the John Rylands Library, Manchester, supplemented by contemporary press reports from the Singapore Free Press and the Asiatic Journal.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in extending Lamb's documentary account of the 1778–1822 missions into the period of Crown (rather than Company) diplomacy, and in providing a comparative framework with Siam that explains the divergent trajectories of the two kingdoms' relations with Britain.
