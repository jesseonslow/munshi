# Sandakan: gun running village to timber centre, 1870–1979

Supriya Bhar's 1980 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* charts the social history of Sandakan, Sabah, across its first century (1879–1979), tracing its transformation from a gun-running village on the Sulu coast to the timber-exporting centre of the state. Drawing primarily on the *British North Borneo Herald*, William Pryer's diary, and oral history, Bhar argues that Sandakan's identity was shaped by the interplay of Chinese commercial immigration, indigenous Sulu-Bajau subsistence economies, and the timber industry that ultimately defined the town's economic purpose.

## Summary

Bhar structures the narrative around four interlocking themes: the founding and physical development of the town, its population and economic base, its social life, and the educational and cultural changes that accompanied modernization. The founding period is presented as a series of crises—pirate blockades, Spanish gunboat threats, the rivalry between Cowie and Pryer, and the conflict with Pangeran Sama over the Gomantong birds' nest caves—that collectively established the authority of the British North Borneo Company in the bay. The physical town is traced from Pryer's first felled tree at Buli Sim Sim in June 1879 through the great fire of 1886, the reclamation of swamp land, and the near-total destruction of 1945, which explains why the town appears "new" despite its century of history.

The economic narrative centres on two forces: Chinese immigration and the timber industry. Bhar documents the repeated failures of early Chinese immigration policy—Medhurst's indiscriminate recruitment of unsuccessful Hong Kong shopkeepers, the tobacco boom's collapse, and the rice tax that halted migration in 1895—before the steady flow resumed after 1903 and accelerated dramatically between 1921 and 1941. The timber story runs from De Lissa's accidental first shipment in 1885 through the British Borneo Timber Company's monopoly (1920–1952) to the post-independence era in which Chinese towkays and indigenous contractors competed for licences. The social history section, the article's richest passage, reconstructs festival life, dress, food, and the small-community dynamics of European society from newspaper reports and memoirs, while also documenting the gradual breakdown of rigid communal boundaries after the Japanese Occupation.

Bhar's method is explicitly limited by source availability. The *British North Borneo Herald* (1884–1941) and Pryer's diary provide the backbone, but these sources are "naturally pre-occupied with European life," with Chinese and indigenous ways of life appearing only as "sidelights." Bhar fills gaps with oral history, particularly for the Bajau, Sulu, and Sungai communities, and acknowledges that the Chinese community is underrepresented due to the inaccessibility of Chinese-language sources. The result is a social history that is uneven in its coverage but valuable for the texture of everyday life it recovers from fragmentary evidence.

### Key Findings

- Population of Elopura (early Sandakan) stood at 698 people in 57 houses by 28 December 1879, exceeded 1,200 by June 1881, and reached 3,771 by May 1884 (pp. 126–127).
- The great fire of 4 April 1886 destroyed property valued at $53,000 and triggered a building mania that produced 56 two-storey plank houses and 87 attap buildings within a year, with rents of $10–$20 per month for houses costing approximately $500 to build (pp. 130–131).
- The British Borneo Timber Company, formed in 1920 from Harrisons and Crosfield and the Chartered Company, held a virtual monopoly on timber logging until its lapse in mid-1952; by 1970, forest industries accounted for $402 million of Sabah's $805 million state GNP (pp. 137–138).
- Chinese immigration policy produced 800 free passes by 1924 and 1,054 by 1927; by 1938 the government began controlling the flow of unsolicited migrants (p. 136).
- The town was effectively destroyed between October 1944 and June 1945 by Allied bombing, shelling, and Japanese arson; the Governor General in 1946 declared that "no town in the world wears the wounds of war more terribly and more honourably than does Sandakan" (pp. 133–134).
- By 1940, Sabah had 59 Chinese schools with an attendance of 4,779, operating independently of government support and subject only to annual inspection (p. 146).

### Conclusion

Bhar's definitive takeaway is that Sandakan's century was a story of continuous, layered transformation—fishing village to timber port, attap huts to concrete shophouses, Sulu cultural models to Malay and then Malaysian ones—driven by the timber economy and mediated by the educational and media systems that followed independence. The town's apparent "newness" is an artefact of wartime destruction, not a lack of historical depth, and its present character is the product of a hundred years of demographic, economic, and cultural change that remains ongoing.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: Sabah Museum Archives (Pryer's *Sandakan Diary*, 1879–1880; *Chronological Notes*, 1406–1911), the *British North Borneo Herald* (1884–1941), and the *North Borneo News and Fortnightly Record* (1948–1953), supplemented by oral history interviews conducted in 1979.
- Historiographically, the article sits within the tradition of K.G. Tregonning's administrative histories of Sabah but shifts the focus from political and economic structures to social and cultural life, using newspaper reportage as a primary source for everyday experience—a method that has since become standard in Sabah social history.
