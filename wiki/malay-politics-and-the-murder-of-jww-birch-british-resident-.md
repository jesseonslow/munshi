# Malay politics and the murder of J.W.W. Birch, British Resident in Perak in 1875. The humiliation and revenge of the Maharaja Lela

Cheah Boon Kheng's 1998 article re-examines the murder of British Resident J.W.W. Birch in Perak (2 November 1875) by centring the analysis on the Maharaja Lela, the feudal chief of Pasir Salak who carried out the killing. Drawing on previously underutilised colonial correspondence, trial records, and Sultan Abdullah's exile letters, Cheah argues that the event was not a unified anti-colonial uprising but the product of irreconcilable factional rivalry between the two Perak Sultans, in which the Maharaja Lela operated as a Machiavellian actor who ultimately betrayed Sultan Abdullah to protect his own position under Sultan Ismail.

## Summary

The article challenges the dominant Malaysian textbook narrative that presents the Perak uprising as an act of collective Malay resistance against colonialism. Cheah demonstrates that the chiefs were deeply divided between the pro-Sultan Abdullah (Lower Perak) and pro-Sultan Ismail (Upper Perak) factions, and that the Maharaja Lela—commonly misidentified as a supporter of Sultan Abdullah—was in fact aligned with the rival faction. Governor Jervois's correspondence confirms that the Maharaja Lela had "changed sides" after being "inadvertently omitted" from the group of chiefs who signed the Treaty of Pangkor in January 1874, and that he felt "annoyed and slighted" (p. 80). His strategic position at Pasir Salak, midway along the Perak River between the two Sultans' territories, made him the natural choice to execute the assassination, and he ensured that written orders bearing the chops of both Sultan Abdullah and Sultan Ismail were directed to him personally (p. 91).

The immediate trigger for the murder was Sultan Abdullah's capitulation to Birch on 1 October 1875, when he signed proclamations introducing new taxes and accepting direct British rule under threat of being replaced by Raja Yusof. This act shattered the secret pact made at Durian Sebatang in July 1875, in which the chiefs had agreed to kill Birch if poison and sorcery failed. The Maharaja Lela, upon learning of the Sultan's surrender, openly defied him in the presence of other chiefs, declaring he would "never acknowledge the authority of Mr Birch or the white men" and that he had received letters from Sultan Ismail, the Mantri, and the Panglima Kinta instructing him not to obey the British (p. 97). Sultan Abdullah, under pressure, then signed a secret authorisation ordering the Maharaja Lela to kill Birch, witnessed by the Sultan's clerk Mat Rouse (p. 97).

After the murder, the Maharaja Lela's true loyalties became apparent. He sent Birch's dragon boat to Sultan Ismail at Blanja, who accepted it as a token of allegiance, and he later fabricated a document purporting to be Sultan Abdullah's written order to Seputum to kill Birch, offering a $1,000 reward—a document that Governor Jervois correctly identified as a forgery designed to exculpate the Maharaja Lela and implicate the Sultan (pp. 102–103). The planned follow-up attack on the British Residency at Bandar Bahru collapsed due to the absence of a unifying command authority and the failure of Dato Sagor to join as promised (p. 100).

### Key Findings

- The Maharaja Lela was not pro-Sultan Abdullah as Malaysian textbooks claim; he belonged to Sultan Ismail's faction and was motivated by personal pique at being excluded from the Pangkor Treaty signing and by Birch's public humiliation of him at Blanja in January 1875, when Birch called him "a cheat" after he reneged on his promise to sign (pp. 80, 89–90).
- The Maharaja Lela fabricated a document bearing Sultan Abdullah's chop ordering Seputum to kill Birch with a $1,000 reward; Governor Jervois concluded it was "written on a blank paper previously chopped and signed by the Sultan, and made use of by Lela to exculpate himself" (p. 102).
- Sultan Abdullah's capitulation to Birch on 1 October 1875—signing proclamations on threat of being deposed—was the proximate cause that converted a dormant conspiracy into an immediate assassination, as it violated the secret pact at Durian Sebatang and confirmed the Upper Perak chiefs' view that Abdullah "could not be trusted" (pp. 95–96).
- Perak's hybrid feudal system (amalgamating Melakan-Johorean and Acehnese models) meant territorial chiefs wielded more practical power than the Sultans; the Maharaja Lela could *kerah* (compel) approximately 100 men within one hour and exacted feudal services including forced labour and unpaid field work from his villagers (pp. 83–86).
- The planned attack on Bandar Bahru failed because of internal disunity: Dato Sagor did not appear as promised, Wan Hoosein suffered an accident en route, and the attacking party lacked arms, supplies, and a commanding authority (p. 100).
- The Maharaja Lela was executed on 20 January 1877, along with Pandak Indut and Dato Sagor; four of his other accomplices received penal servitude for life (p. 103).

### Conclusion

Cheah's definitive takeaway is that the murder of Birch was neither a spontaneous act of popular resistance nor a simple case of colonial provocation, but the culmination of a complex web of personal ambition, feudal entitlement, and factional rivalry in which the Maharaja Lela played the role of a calculating political operator who used the murder to consolidate his position under Sultan Ismail while simultaneously attempting to destroy Sultan Abdullah politically. The "Malay unity" narrative, Cheah argues, obscures the very disunity that caused the uprising to collapse and that made the British able to divide and conquer the Perak chiefs within eighteen months of the killing.

## Context

- The article draws heavily on Colonial Office records (C.O. 273/90, C.O. 273/97) and the published *Further Correspondence relating to the Affairs of Certain Native States in the Malay Peninsula* (C.1503, C.1505, C.1512, C.1709), several of which had not been consulted by earlier historians such as Winstedt and Wilkinson.
- Cheah identifies a significant historiographical gap: Governor Jervois omitted Sultan Abdullah's written reply to the ten charges and the Government's rebuttal memorandum from the published *Enquiry as to Complicity of Chiefs in the Perak Outrages* (1876), creating a lacuna that caused subsequent historians to overlook the Sultan's own account of the chiefs' disunity (p. 77, n. 12).
