# The Bangunan Sultan Abdul Samad

J.M. Gullick's 1992 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* reconstructs the design and construction history of the Bangunan Sultan Abdul Samad, Kuala Lumpur's most celebrated colonial-era government building, completed in 1897. The article's central thesis is that the building's design credit, long attributed to Government Architect A. C. Norman by virtue of his name on the foundation stone, properly belongs to the younger architect Regent Bidwell, who worked under State Engineer C. E. Spooner's direction.

## Summary

Gullick traces the institutional conditions that made the Bangunan possible. The Selangor State Government's move to Kuala Lumpur in 1880 and the subsequent growth of revenues created demand for more substantial administrative premises, but the Public Works Department under H. F. Bellamy and A. C. Norman lacked the capacity to deliver a major project. When Resident W. E. Maxwell arrived in 1889, he imported C. E. Spooner from Ceylon to reorganise the PWD, establishing a brick-and-tile factory at Brickfields and creating the new post of State Engineer. Spooner's reorganisation was the essential precondition for the building programme that followed.

The core of the article is an investigation into the question of architectural attribution. Spooner's own speech at the 1897 PWD dinner, reproduced at length, provides the primary evidence: he describes how Norman produced a ground plan and Bidwell an elevation in a "Classic Renaissance" style, which Spooner rejected in favour of a "Mahometan style" (the neo-Saracenic idiom developed by British architects in India). Spooner states explicitly that Bidwell "set about the details" and was "most happy in rendering my ideas on paper." Yet the foundation stone, laid on 6 October 1894, names only Norman as Architect. Gullick resolves this discrepancy by showing that Norman, as the senior Government Architect (an ARIBA of ten years' standing), would have insisted on his name appearing on official documents, while Bidwell—a 24-year-old with only a few months' service—had no institutional leverage to claim credit.

Gullick corroborates Spooner's account with later documentary evidence. When Norman applied for promotion in 1899 and again in 1901, Resident H. C. Belfield explicitly stated that Norman's list of buildings was "misleading" because he "had little or nothing to do with their design or construction in a number of instances." Norman was compulsorily retired in 1903 at the age of 45, with Governor Swettenham describing him as "a man of weak character and wanting in energy." Bidwell, by contrast, went on to become a partner of Swan and Maclaren in Singapore, where his work has been described as "inspired" by modern architectural historians.

### Key Findings

- The Bangunan was completed in 1897 at a final cost of $152,824 against an original estimate of $152,000, absorbing 4 million bricks, 2,500 barrels of cement, 18,000 pikuls of lime, 50 tons of steel and iron, and approximately 30,000 cubic feet of timber (p. 30, n. 9).
- The foundation stone, dated 6 October 1894, names "A. C. Norman — Architect" and "C. E. Spooner — State Engineer," with no mention of Bidwell (p. 33, n. 14).
- Spooner's 1897 speech at the PWD dinner explicitly credits Bidwell with preparing the detailed drawings under Spooner's design direction, while acknowledging Norman's ground plan and "critical eye" (pp. 30–31).
- Resident H. C. Belfield's 1901 assessment that Norman's building list was "misleading" because he "had little or nothing to do with their design or construction in a number of instances" constitutes the most direct official repudiation of Norman's claim (p. 34, n. 26).
- The central clock tower stood 135 feet in height; public concern that the daily noon signal gun from Bluff Road would damage the structure led to the discontinuation of the 5 p.m. gun (pp. 31, 37–38).
- Norman was compulsorily retired in 1903 at age 45; Governor Swettenham's contemporaneous comment described him as "a man of weak character and wanting in energy" (p. 34, n. 27).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that the Bangunan Sultan Abdul Samad was the product of Spooner's design vision, executed in detail by Bidwell, with Norman's name on the foundation stone reflecting his administrative position rather than his actual contribution to the design. The article recasts the building's history as a case study in how institutional hierarchy and seniority can obscure genuine creative authorship in colonial public works.

## Context

- The article draws on the *Selangor Journal* (vols. 3–5), the *Annual Reports of the PWD Selangor* (1896–1897), and Colonial Office correspondence (CO 272/272, CO 273/250, CO 273/294) held in the Public Record Office, London.
- Historiographically, the article corrects a long-standing misattribution in the architectural history of colonial Kuala Lumpur and contributes to the broader literature on British "neo-Saracenic" architecture in South and Southeast Asia, drawing on P. Davies's *Splendours of the Raj* for stylistic context.
