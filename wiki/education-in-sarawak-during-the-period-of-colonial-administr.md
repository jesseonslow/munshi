# Education in Sarawak during the period of colonial administration 1846–1961

Ooi Keat Gin's 1990 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the education policies and programmes of the British Colonial Government in Sarawak from the Cession of 1946 to the formation of Malaysia in 1963. The central argument is that the Colonial Government pursued two interlocking objectives—narrowing the vast educational disparity between the native peoples and the Chinese, and creating a unified national education system to foster common citizenship and loyalty to Sarawak—and that while significant progress was made, the goals of educational parity and full integration remained unachieved by the end of the colonial period.

## Summary

The article traces the Colonial Government's educational strategy from its inception in 1946 through to the eve of Malaysian federation. The starting point was a deeply unequal plural school system inherited from the Brooke period: the Chinese community had developed an extensive vernacular school network on its own initiative, while the Malay and non-Malay indigenous peoples had been largely neglected, with the latter left to the limited efforts of Christian missionary societies. The Colonial Government's response was a multi-pronged programme combining expanded primary schooling in the rural outstations, teacher training at the new Batu Lintang Centre, a revised grant-in-aid system, and the Conversion Plan to replace the medium of instruction in secondary schools from Chinese to English. Ooi frames these measures within the broader constitutional project of preparing Sarawak for self-government, arguing that educational integration was a political necessity as much as a social one.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to the responses of the various communities to these policies. The native peoples were generally supportive, though the article documents persistent problems of irregular attendance, parental indifference (particularly among some Iban communities), and the structural difficulties of providing schooling in sparsely populated longhouse settlements. The Chinese response was more complex and contested: the English-educated segment broadly supported the Conversion Plan, while the Chinese-educated management committees of vernacular Middle Schools mounted vigorous opposition, which Ooi attributes in substantial part to Communist infiltration through the Clandestine Communist Organization (CCO). The Cession controversy of 1946–47 also featured prominently as a disruptive event, triggering mass resignations by Malay teachers and the closure of a third of Government Malay schools.

Ooi draws heavily on primary administrative sources—Sarawak Annual Reports, Education Department Triennial Surveys, Council Negri debates and addresses, Colonial Office correspondence, and the Woodhead Report—to reconstruct both the policy rationale and the practical difficulties of implementation. The article also incorporates a captured CCO document outlining the strategy for opposing the Conversion Plan, which provides rare direct evidence of Communist operational methods in the educational sphere.

### Key Findings

- The 1937 enrolment figures reveal the depth of pre-colonial disparity: 11,279 Chinese students versus 2,079 Malay, 345 Sea Dayak, and 297 Land Dayak students; the 1947 census recorded illiteracy rates of 98% among Ibans and other indigenous groups compared to 66% among the Chinese (pp. 40–41).
- Native student enrolment grew from 15,121 in 1954 to an estimated 57,700 in 1961, while total recurrent expenditure on education rose from $1,042,137 to $10,907,492 over the same period, reflecting the impact of the Grant Code (Regulations) of 1956 which made the Government responsible for all approved recurrent expenditure of aided schools (pp. 43–44).
- The Cession controversy caused 87 of 152 Malay teachers to resign and forced the closure of 20 of 62 Government Malay Schools; all 56 student teachers at the Sarawak Malay Teachers Training College also withdrew in protest (p. 54).
- Of sixteen Chinese Middle Schools, eleven accepted the Conversion Plan to English-medium instruction; the remaining five ceased to receive government aid from 1 April 1962 when they opted to retain *Kuo-yu* as their medium (p. 59).
- By 1962, natives comprised only 18.33% of total secondary school enrolment against 80.36% for the Chinese, and approximately 70% of non-Malay indigenous children of school age had yet to receive any formal education (pp. 60–61).
- The Schools Broadcasting service, commenced in 1959 with assistance from the Asia Foundation and Commonwealth governments, reached over 60,000 children in 770 schools by the end of 1960 (p. 46).

### Conclusion

Ooi's definitive assessment is that while the Colonial Government achieved measurable progress in expanding educational access—particularly for native children in the rural interior—and made substantial strides toward a national education system, the fundamental objective of educational parity between the native races and the Chinese remained unfulfilled on the eve of Malaysian federation. The article concludes that the difficulties of Dayak education would require not only greater financial outlay but a "mental revolution" in indigenous attitudes toward formal schooling, and that the transition to Malaysian federation introduced a new complication by replacing English with Malay as the medium of instruction and agent of unity.

## Context

- The study draws on primary materials held at the Sarawak State Archives (Kuching), Rhodes House (Oxford), and the Public Record Office (London), including Education Department Triennial Surveys, Council Negri debate transcripts, Colonial Office correspondence, and a captured CCO document on the anti-Conversion Plan campaign.
- Historiographically, the article fills a gap in the literature on colonial-era Sarawak education by providing a comprehensive administrative account that integrates the perspectives of all major racial communities and the missionary societies, and by incorporating intelligence-derived evidence of Communist subversion in the educational field that had not previously been systematically presented in the scholarly literature.
