# Kapitans and Unofficials: Ethnic Intermediaries in the Straits Settlements, 1786–1942

## Abstract

Prior to 1867, the Chinese communities in the Straits Settlements of Penang, Malacca, and Singapore were administered through the *Kapitan* system, where an influential individual was appointed by the colonial government to represent their ethnic community. Following the establishment of the Straits Settlements Legislative Council in 1867, the colonial government’s administration of Chinese communities shifted away from *Kapitan* to ‘unofficial’ members of the Legislative Council (as opposed to members who were government officials). ‘Unofficial’ legislative councillors linked the Chinese communities of the Straits Settlements with the colonial administration as the Kapitan once did, albeit in a diminished role. This article traces the evolution of ethnic administration in the Straits Settlements and the role of the *Kapitan* and ‘unofficials’ in the expansion of British authority over Asian subjects. It argues that the replacement of the *Kapitan* by the unofficial was part of a broader shift from indirect to direct rule, and illustrates the role played by individuals in these positions in challenging British authority.

## Summary

This article traces the evolution of ethnic administration in the Straits Settlements of Penang, Malacca, and Singapore from 1786 to 1942, arguing that the *Kapitan* system and the position of 'unofficial' member of the Legislative Council should be understood as a continuous thread of colonial intermediary governance rather than discrete, unrelated institutions. Keo contends that both offices represent a specific archetype of colonial intermediary—prominent individuals from ethnic communities who wielded enormous influence over their compatriots—and that the transition from one to the other reflects the broader shift from indirect to direct rule as the British colonial state expanded its institutional reach over the polyglot port-cities. The article also situates the Straits Settlements' comparatively simple *Kapitan* system in comparative context with the more formalised and hierarchical *Kapitan Cina* structures of the Dutch East Indies, and connects the discourse of Straits Chinese unofficials to broader debates about imperial citizenship, the civilising mission, and the tensions between liberal imperial ideology and racialised colonial hierarchy.

The article addresses a significant gap in the historiography: the *Kapitan* system in the Straits Settlements has been largely elusive to scholars due to the fragmentary nature of British record-keeping and the dearth of sources produced by the individuals who held the office. Keo draws on the English East India Company factory records, the proceedings of the Straits Settlements Legislative Council, and contemporary vernacular and English-language press to reconstruct the roles and agency of both *Kapitan* and unofficial councillors. He challenges the conventional narrative that portrays these figures as compromised rubber-stamps for colonial policy, demonstrating instead that they exercised genuine political agency—alternately supporting and challenging the British administration to advance their communities' interests and, in later periods, in pursuit of broader pluralistic and liberal ideals.

### Key Findings

- The *Kapitan* system was formally ended by the implementation of the Second Charter of Justice in 1826, which extended the Court of Recorders and Court of Judicature to Malacca and Singapore, yet the system persisted informally within the Chinese community into the 1870s, with community-elected *Kapitan* continuing to record births, deaths, marriages, and arrivals (p. 55).
- The first non-European 'unofficial' appointed to the Straits Settlements Legislative Council was Hoo Ah Kay (Whampoa) in 1870; before that, all unofficial seats were held exclusively by European merchants and planters (p. 59).
- Hoo Ah Kay's vote in favour of the *Preservation of Peace Ordinance* (1868) and the *Chinese Immigrants' Ordinance* (1873)—both repressive measures—was motivated not by deference to the colonial government but by his desire to protect fellow Straits Chinese merchants from the power of secret societies controlled by *sinkheh* (new arrivals) who monopolised the coolie trade (pp. 61–62).
- The *Societies Ordinance* (1889), targeted at Chinese secret societies, was passed by eight votes to seven through disciplined bloc voting by officials against the unofficials, despite opposition from both European and Chinese unofficials on liberal grounds (p. 63).
- Lim Cheng Ean and Tan Cheng Lock, serving as unofficials during the interwar period, campaigned to abolish the colour bar in the Malayan Civil Service (imposed by Governor John Anderson in 1904) and to expand 'Asiatic' representation; a 1931 petition from the Straits Chinese British Associations of Penang, Malacca, and Singapore was sent to the Colonial Office requesting more seats for Asiatics in the Legislative Council (pp. 64–65).
- The *Kapitan* in the Straits Settlements was a single individual who could deputise community members, in stark contrast to the Dutch East Indies where the Chinese community was governed through the *Kong Koan* hierarchy of *wijkmeesters*, *Luitenant der Chinezen*, *Kapitein der Chinezen*, and (in Batavia, Semarang, and Surabaya) *Majoor der Chinezen* (p. 55).

### Conclusion

Keo's definitive historical takeaway is that the *Kapitan* and the unofficial legislative councillor were not separate phenomena but successive expressions of the same underlying logic of ethnic administration through colonial intermediaries. The shift from one to the other tracked the expansion of the British state's institutional apparatus, but the individuals who occupied these positions consistently demonstrated political agency—using their proximity to the machinery of state to shape policy in service of their communities and, in later periods, in pursuit of broader pluralistic and liberal ideals. This challenges the dominant narrative of these figures as mere instruments of colonial control and repositions them as active participants in the contestation of racialised imperial hierarchy.

## Context

- **Archival collections:** The National Archives (TNA), CO275 series (Proceedings of the Legislative Council of the Straits Settlements, 1867–1941), CO273 series (petitions to the Governor); English East India Company factory records; contemporary press including *The Straits Times*, *Singapore Free Press and Mercantile Advertiser*, *Pinang Gazette and Straits Chronicle*, and *Malaya Tribune*.
- **Historiographical contribution:** Bridges the gap between studies of the *Kapitan* system (predominantly focused on the Dutch East Indies) and studies of the Straits Settlements Legislative Council (largely treated as a legal institution), and extends Goh's (2010) work on Straits Chinese unofficials by arguing for a more active, agency-laden reading of their legislative participation within the framework of imperial citizenship.
