# Negeri Sembilan

### Scope and Historical Definition

Negeri Sembilan, or the Nine States, is a confederacy of Malay polities in the western interior of the Malay Peninsula, situated between the Straits of Malacca and the Titiwangsa range, whose distinctive constitution—a hybrid of patriarchal Malacca-derived territorial chieftainship and matrilineal Minangkabau tribal law—represents one of the most remarkable survivals of Sumatran political culture in the Malay world [[1, pp. 35–41]](#ref-1). The confederacy was unknown to the Portuguese in 1613, to the author of the *Malay Annals* in 1612, and to the Dutch in their *Dagh-Register* printed down to 1682; the original confederacy must have been created after the coming of Raja Melewar, founder of the present royal house, in 1773 [[1, p. 41]](#ref-1). The constituent states that formed the federation extant to the present day were Sungai Ujong, Rembau, Jelebu, Johol, Ulu Muar, Inas, Gunong Pasir, Terachi and Jempul [[1, p. 41]](#ref-1). The name "Nine States" was adopted by the Yam-tuan Besar to ensure that his territory might not appear inferior to Rembau, which had long been described as possessing nine districts [[1, pp. 57]](#ref-1).

The earliest datable evidence of Minangkabau presence in the hinterland is an inscribed stone at Pengkalan Kempas in Sungai Ujong territory, bearing both Arabic and Sumatran Malay inscriptions dated to 1467 A.D., the reign of Sultan Mansur Shah of Malacca, which marks the grave of a Minangkabau immigrant [[1, p. 44]](#ref-1). By the sixteenth century, Portuguese sources confirm a large immigration of Sumatran agriculturists: in 1586 the Minangkabaus behind Malacca fielded 2,000 fighting-men against a Portuguese expedition of 700, and by 1613 Godinho d'Eredia recorded that the district of Naning was "occupied by Monancabos engaged in the trade of *betre*" (betel) [[1, pp. 44–45]](#ref-1). The economic foundation of the early settlements was tin-mining: d'Eredia recorded that the yearly output of tin from Klang exceeded one hundred *bares*, and prehistoric bronze axe-heads and clay crucibles discovered at Kenaboi in Jelebu attest to tin and gold extraction in the neolithic and bronze ages [[1, p. 43]](#ref-1).

### Custom and Constitution

The political system of Negeri Sembilan is structured around a graded hierarchy of officers ascending from the head of a household through the elder of the sub-tribe (*buapa*), the tribal chief (*lembaga*), the territorial chief (*undang*), and finally the Yam-tuan, supreme arbiter in constitutional and judicial questions between the territories [[1, p. 87]](#ref-1). The foundational principle is matrilineal: relationship is reckoned purely through the mother's line, and a special word (*sanak*) designates a blood relation on the distaff side [[1, p. 77]](#ref-1). On marriage, a man passes from his mother's tribe to become a lodger in his wife's house, subject to her tribal chief in all matters affecting her and her family [[1, pp. 78–79]](#ref-1). The sub-tribe (*perut*) is the smallest exogamous unit, and marriage within a prohibited sub-tribe or tribe was punishable by outlawry and confiscation of property [[1, p. 78]](#ref-1).

The territorial chiefs, or *Undang*, represent the patriarchal Malacca inheritance within the matrilineal system. Dato' Klana Putra, Undang of Sungai Ujong, represents a chieftainship held by the Bendahara family of Malacca as far back as the fourteenth century, and the Undangs of Rembau and Johol claim descent from Bendahara Sekudai, a Johor descendant of those same Malacca viziers [[1, p. 90]](#ref-1). The *waris*—the privileged families that provide the territorial chiefs—are descendants of the earliest Malacca chiefs, delegates or relatives of the great patriarchal Bendaharas, and their land was exempt from attachment by a Minangkabau tribe [[1, p. 83]](#ref-1). The Biduanda tribe, a name unknown in Sumatra, incorporated early "Javanese" (a term then including Sumatrans) and Proto-Malay converts to Islam whom the old Malay empire of Malacca had recognised in the hinterland [[1, p. 43]](#ref-1).

The Yam-tuan, or Yang di-pertuan Besar, was elected by the four territorial chiefs and owned no territory, levied no taxes, and lived on offerings of money, rice and coconuts made at his accession and at circumcision and marriage feasts [[1, pp. 92–93]](#ref-1). His jurisdiction was that of a supreme arbiter: as the Jelebu customary sayings record, "the headman's sword is the Raja's—he can behead without reporting it to any suzerain" [[1, p. 92]](#ref-1). The 1898 treaty established the office as elective, held by a royal prince, with the four great territorial chiefs of Sungai Ujong, Jelebu, Johol and Rembau as electors, and explicitly barred the office from interfering in local customs or Muhammadan law [[1, p. 76]](#ref-1).

Martin Lister, the first British Resident of the combined states, provided the earliest systematic British-language account of the constitutional structure in 1887, identifying five foundational maxims that structured authority from the individual upward: *Orang Semenda kapada Tempat Semenda*, *Anak Buah kapada Ibu Bapa*, *Ibu Bapa kapada Lembaga*, *Lembaga kapada Undang*, and *Undang kapada Ka'adilan* [[2, pp. 35–53]](#ref-2). Lister documented the fine jurisdiction escalating by tier: the *lembaga* could fine $7.20, the *undang* $14, and the Yam-tuan $24.80 [[2, pp. 46–50]](#ref-2). The *mas manah* tribute payable by all subjects on the death of a Yam-tuan consisted of one *gantang* of rice, two coconuts, one fowl, and six cents, collected through the chain of *Ibu Bapa* to *Lembaga* to *Undang* to the Istana [[2, p. 53]](#ref-2).

The criminal law divided offences by jurisdiction: elders of the sub-tribes tried cases of minor assault; the tribal chief handled grievous hurt and crimes more serious than pilfering; and the territorial chief could sentence a murderer to be creesed or outlawed [[1, p. 85]](#ref-1). The penalty for homicide was the substitution of a person from the murderer's tribe to the tribe of the dead man (*bunoh beri balas*), a principle that persisted even for accidental homicide [[1, p. 85]](#ref-1), [[3, p. 31]](#ref-3). The customary law text from Sungai Ujong, edited by Winstedt and de Josselin de Jong in 1954, codifies these principles in 113 sections, establishing that "every man is a king in his own place" while subordinating all to the ultimate authority of the Ruler [[4, p. 5]](#ref-4).

### History

The political history of Negeri Sembilan from the fourteenth century through British intervention in the 1870s and 1890s is reconstructed in Winstedt's 1934 study, which demonstrates that the confederacy's unique constitution was the product of successive cultural absorptions rather than a single founding act [[1, pp. 35–111]](#ref-1). The Dutch captured Malacca from the Portuguese in 1641 and immediately sought to impose their authority on the Minangkabau hinterland. The 1641 treaty with Naning imposed a tithe of rice, fruit, betel and pepper, a tenth of money from land sales, and the surrender of all weapons; by 1765 the Dutch had commuted the rice tithe to a nominal tribute of 400 *gantang* [[1, pp. 48–49]](#ref-1). The 1759 treaty between Governor David Bulen and the heads of the nine districts of Rembau is the first authentic mention of the "nine countries" (*negeri*) of Rembau and fixed the tin monopoly price at 38 Spanish reals per *bahar* of 300 *kati* [[1, p. 57]](#ref-1).

The Bugis, who had been called to assist Johor against the Minangkabau pretender Raja Kechil in the early eighteenth century, became a powerful factor in the hinterland's politics. Daing Kemboja, the famous Bugis chief, ruled Linggi and maintained alliances with Rembau; in 1784 Rembau joined in Raja Haji's attack on Malacca, which led to the death of that Bugis warrior and the Dutch expulsion of the Bugis from Riau [[1, pp. 58–60]](#ref-1). It was in this period of Bugis decline that the Minangkabau prince Raja Melewar, who claimed descent from the royal house of Minangkabau, was summoned to the throne by the territorial chiefs and established his capital at Sri Menanti in Ulu Muar [[1, pp. 59–62]](#ref-1).

British intervention began with the occupation of Malacca in 1795. The Naning War of 1831–32, precipitated by Governor Fullerton's erroneous assumption that Naning was part of Malacca territory, cost the British £100,000; post-war revenue for Naning was $762 against expenditure of $463 in 1833, rising to $1,240 revenue and $490 expenditure by 1835 [[1, pp. 65–66]](#ref-1). The 1874 engagement signed by the Klana Putra, Sayid 'Abdu'r-Rahman al-Kadri, and the Dato' Muda of Linggi was a penal bond of $50,000 guaranteeing free passage on the Linggi River and ceding the station at Sempang to British control [[1, pp. 69–71]](#ref-1). The war with Yam-tuan Antah in 1875, in which Antah mobilised a force estimated at 800 to 4,000 men against British-backed authority in Sungai Ujong, fragmented the confederate structure; Antah was restored in 1877 but only as "Yam-tuan of Sri Menanti," a truncated title covering merely the four small districts adjacent to his capital [[5, pp. 1–23]](#ref-5). His twelve-year-old son, Tunku Besar Mohamed, eventually achieved through personal tact and the guidance of Resident Martin Lister the re-unification of Negeri Sembilan under the paramountcy of the Yam-tuan by 1898 [[5, pp. 22–23]](#ref-5).

Governor Weld's despatch of December 1885, reporting on his tour of the independent states in the vicinity of Malacca, presents the transition from armed conflict to settled British administrative control through road-building, police stations, and revenue collection [[6, pp. 127–130]](#ref-6). The territories under British influence were described as "about three times the extent of Malacca" in aggregate area, and Weld requested a loan of $15,000 to fund the development of road infrastructure, the Police Station at Kwala Pilah, and administrative costs, projecting that the states would be self-supporting within three years [[6, pp. 128–130]](#ref-6).

### Economic Dynamics

The Malay peasant economy of Negeri Sembilan in the 1890s, reconstructed by Gullick from Annual Reports, State Council minutes, and Colonial Office despatches, was one of broad self-sufficiency with a narrow range of wealth, in which padi cultivation formed the centrepiece but was increasingly supplemented by cash crops and livestock sales to a rapidly growing Chinese mining population [[7, pp. 38–55]](#ref-7). By 1901 the State's population had risen to 96,028 (56,935 Malays, 32,931 Chinese), the Chinese population having more than doubled from 15,391 in 1891, driven by tin-mining expansion [[7, pp. 38–39]](#ref-7). The buffalo population exceeded 11,000 head valued at $500,000 before the rinderpest epidemic of 1901; in 1888 the export trade was so brisk that chiefs advised against removing the export duty for fear of depleting the ploughing stock [[7, p. 49]](#ref-7).

The transformation of Malay land tenure was a significant feature of this period. The Sungei Ujong Land Regulations of 1887, the State's first Land Code, divided land into Estates, Native Land, and Mining Land; the Registration of Titles Enactment of 1898 extended this to the whole State, and by 1904 a total of 30,567 titles covering 67,382 acres of smallholdings had been registered [[7, p. 42]](#ref-7). Land revenue rose from approximately 30 cents per acre to 80 cents per acre annually as a result of the shift from a flat rate to a 5 per cent assessment on padi crop value [[7, p. 43]](#ref-7). Over 84,000 acres had been alienated for tapioca cultivation by 1888, but the industry was already being wound down due to soil exhaustion; nine steam-powered tapioca factories operated in the State in 1887 [[7, p. 51]](#ref-7). The Arang Arang–Seremban railway, completed in 1891, immediately displaced Malay bullock cart operators on the Linggi road [[7, pp. 52–53]](#ref-7).

In Jelebu, the material condition in 1884 was one of severe depopulation and economic decline: O'Brien described passing through "mile after mile of deserted *kampongs*" with padi land lying waste and fruit trees still in bearing, the only sign of economic activity being a small tin mine at Jelundong where the cost of rice was nearly triple that of Sungai Ujong due to the absence of roads [[8, pp. 337–343]](#ref-8). The December 1896 flood in the lower Triang Valley, documented by Collector Keyser, revealed the vulnerability of riverine communities: at Jerang, water marks in the Police Station stood at four feet, and the island at Pasir Panggil, a permanent geographical feature, was entirely destroyed [[9, pp. 155–158]](#ref-9).

### Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on Negeri Sembilan spans from the 1880s to the present, reflecting shifting priorities from administrative description to anthropological analysis to historiographical correction. Martin Lister's 1887 article, published with the explicit permission of Governor Sir Cecil C. Smith, represents one of the earliest systematic British-language accounts of the *adat perpatih* constitutional system and its distinction from the *adat temenggung* prevailing on the coast [[2, pp. 35–53]](#ref-2). H.A. O'Brien's 1884 account of Jelebu served as a practical briefing for the colonial government, documenting the state's distinctive dual governance of a Menangkabau Yam-tuan and a hereditary Penghulu [[8, pp. 337–343]](#ref-8).

The 1918 publication of Caldecott's Jelebu customary songs and sayings, with Winstedt's preface and notes, marked a methodological advance: Winstedt argued that the people's own sayings and songs constituted the most reliable evidence for the principles of *adat Minangkabau*, and that the comparative method—drawing on Willinck's 1909 monograph and the Dutch *Adat-rechtbundel* series—had corrected textual errors in earlier British publications [[3, pp. 3–6]](#ref-3). Winstedt's own 1934 study remains the foundational work, drawing on the *Dagh-Register*, Malacca Consultations, Straits Settlements Records, the *Sejarah Melayu*, d'Eredia's *Description of Malacca*, and Dutch treaty collections, alongside extensive fieldwork in Rembau, Jelebu, and Johol [[1, pp. 35–111]](#ref-1). Its historiographical contribution lies in demonstrating that the "Nine States" was a late and partly artificial construction, and in providing the first systematic account of Minangkabau matrilineal law as practised rather than merely theorised in the Malay Peninsula.

Subsequent scholarship has addressed specific gaps and corrected earlier assumptions. Gullick's 1951 reconstruction of the 1890s economy filled a historiographical gap concerning how the predominantly Malay population made its living before the rubber and tin booms [[7, pp. 38–55]](#ref-7). His 1954 article on the war with Yam-tuan Antah argued that the conflict was not a simple civil war but a broader aristocratic reaction to British intervention that had disrupted the internal political equilibrium of the federation [[5, pp. 1–23]](#ref-5). Diane Lewis's 1960 study of Inas, based on original fieldwork funded by a Ford Foundation Fellowship, challenged the prevailing scholarly consensus that had located ancient Jelai in Pahang, demonstrating through systematic collection of oral tradition that Inas was the ancient state of Jelai and that its political marginalization resulted primarily from the Penghulu's refusal to cooperate with the British at the close of the nineteenth century [[10, pp. 65–94]](#ref-10).

The 1954 edition and translation of the Sungai Ujong customary law text by Winstedt and de Josselin de Jong provided one of the few surviving codifications of Minangkabau-influenced *adat* law from the western Malayan peninsula, revealing the intricate interweaving of Islamic canon law, ancestral precedent, and village consensus [[4, pp. 1–71]](#ref-4). Annabel Teh Gallop's 2013 article on the seals of Negeri Sembilan fundamentally challenged the chronological framework built by Wilkinson and subsequent scholars, demonstrating that the names cited on the seals—most notably "Sultan Abdul Jalil Muazzam Syah"—are symbolic Minangkabau regnal titles representing the institution of Sumatran kingship, not references to a specific Johor sultan [[11, pp. 1–32]](#ref-11). This reinterpretation showed that Wilkinson's 1911 identification of the seal name with a Johor prince who died in 1761 was a "red herring" driven by peninsula-centric assumptions, and that Winstedt's subsequent dismissal of the 1826 credentials brought from Sumatra by Raja Labu as "ludicrous" was a direct consequence of the same error [[11, pp. 23–24]](#ref-11).
