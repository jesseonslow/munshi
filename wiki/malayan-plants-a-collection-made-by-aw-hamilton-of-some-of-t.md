# Malayan plants: a collection made by A.W. Hamilton of some of the commoner plants and littoral trees found on Singapore Island. Identifications and descriptions. R.E. Holttum

A.W. Hamilton and R.E. Holttum (1930) produced a practical identification guide to approximately 115 common plants and littoral trees of Singapore Island, organized under local Malay and Chinese vernacular names. Drawing on Hamilton's field collection and Holttum's taxonomic authority at the Singapore Botanic Gardens, the work functions as a bridge between indigenous botanical knowledge and Western scientific nomenclature, providing brief diagnostic descriptions for each entry.

## Summary

The article presents a taxonomic key to the commoner flora of Singapore Island, arranged alphabetically by local name. Each entry supplies the vernacular name (Malay or Chinese), the scientific binomial, the family, a concise morphological description covering habit, leaf, flower, and fruit characters, habitat notes, and—where relevant—observations on whether the species is native, cultivated, or introduced. The scope spans mangrove swamps, seashores, open waste ground, lowland jungle, and cultivated plots, encompassing roughly 45 plant families plus a wood-destroying fungus and a seaweed (pp. 319–329).

The ecological range is broad but deliberately restricted to "commoner" species, making the work a field companion rather than a comprehensive flora. Mangrove species (nine in total) and littoral trees form a substantial proportion, reflecting the title's emphasis on "littoral trees." The remaining entries cover ubiquitous weeds of waste ground, cultivated aromatics and vegetables, and a notable scatter of introduced species from Africa, the Americas, China, and India. Several entries reference Ridley's earlier botanical surveys of the Peninsula as an authority, and a few include taxonomic judgements—such as treating *Avicennia lanata* Ridley as merely a hairy-leaved form of *A. officinalis*, or noting that *Melastoma polyanthus* is restricted to the southern Peninsula while *M. malabathricum* replaces it to the north (pp. 327–328).

The work's value lies in its accessibility: by keying on local names rather than scientific ones, it allowed non-specialists—traders, farmers, colonial administrators—to identify plants they already knew by their everyday labels. It captures Singapore's flora at a moment before the island's rapid urban transformation would eliminate much of the waste-ground and secondary vegetation documented here.

### Key Findings

- Approximately 115 taxa are described across roughly 45 angiosperm families, plus one basidiomycete (*Polystictus sanguineus*) and one phaeophyte (*Sargassum* sp.) (pp. 319–329).
- Nine mangrove species are identified: *Excoecaria agallocha*, *Scyphiphora hydrophyllacea*, *Bruguiera caryophylloides*, *Carapa obovata*, *Sonneratia griffithii*, *Lumnitzera coccinea*, *Ceriops candolleana*, *Bruguiera gymnorrhiza*, and *Avicennia officinalis* (pp. 320–329).
- At least eleven non-mangrove littoral and seashore species are included, from *Heritiera littoralis* and *Acanthus ebracteatus* to the sea-grass *Enhalus koenigii* and the seaweed *Sargassum* (pp. 320–328).
- Explicitly introduced species include *Passiflora foetida* (South America), *Mimosa pudica* (America), *Cassia hirsuta* (America), *Opuntia monacantha* (South Africa), *Euphorbia tirucalli* (Africa), *Bryophyllum calycinum* (Africa), *Artemisia vulgaris* (Northern Hemisphere), *Illicium verum* (China), *Triphasia trifoliata* (China), *Melia azadirachta* (India), and *Sesbania grandiflora* (Mascarenes to N. Australia) (pp. 320–329).
- Holttum records a taxonomic correction: *Avicennia lanata* Ridley is reduced to a form of *A. officinalis*, differing only in having very hairy leaves beneath (p. 329).
- A separate short note by T. Kitching on p. 318 describes the Kuala Brang "Keri" (a lead-inlaid sickle handle of rengas wood) from Ulu Trengganu, unrelated to the botanical content but published on the same page.

### Conclusion

The article's definitive contribution is the preservation of a vernacular-to-scientific name correspondence for Singapore's common flora at a point when much of the documented habitat—waste ground, secondary jungle, open seashore—still existed in recognizable form. It is a snapshot of a transitional moment: local botanical knowledge recorded systematically just before urbanization would render much of it obsolete.

## Context

- R.E. Holttum (1902–1979) was a leading figure in Singapore and Malayan botany, later becoming a professor at the University of Malaya; this early collaboration with Hamilton reflects the standard colonial-era practice of pairing a local collector with a metropolitan or station botanist for identification.
- The article draws implicitly on the herbarium and field work of the Singapore Botanic Gardens and on Ridley's earlier floras of the Peninsula; it makes no claim to original taxonomic discovery but serves as a practical compilation.
