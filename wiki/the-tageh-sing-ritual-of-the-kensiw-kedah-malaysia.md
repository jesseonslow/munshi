# The Tageh sing ritual of the Kensiw, Kedah, Malaysia

S. Nagata's 2013 note in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* documents the *tageh* sing ritual of the Kensiw (Orang Asli) of Kedah, based on fieldwork conducted in the early 1970s near Baling. The article argues that *tageh* represents a distinct genre of religious performance—neither shamanistic seance nor seasonal-fruit ceremony—that is unique to the Kensiw and may constitute a polar type within the broader spectrum of Orang Asli ritual singing.

## Summary

The article situates *tageh* within Geoffrey Benjamin's typology of Orang Asli religious practice, which distinguishes between seasonal-fruit shamanism (west) and tiger shamanism (east). Nagata demonstrates that the Kensiw maintain a clear distinction between *penlogn pano'* (the shamanistic ceremony involving a conical structure and tiger-spirit possession) and *penlogn tageh* (a communal singing ritual). The *tageh* is performed at night on the seventh day after a death, during a *kenduri* feast that marks the end of the mourning period. The ritual is conducted in total darkness to avoid frightening *cenoy* (sky spirits) who join the singing, and the purpose of the performance is to invite these spirits and sing for the well-being of all participants (pp. 78–79).

Nagata draws on his own field notes from the early 1970s, supplemented by later recordings from 1991, to describe the ritual's structure. The *cao neges* (sing leader) opens by inhaling smoke from embers of *heli kasa* and blowing it in four directions to address Tapn (Thunder-God) and request protection for the throat, which may suffer from prolonged singing. The leader may also perform brief healing treatments—inhaling breath from a patient's chest and exhaling it outward—before the communal singing begins, accompanied by a large horizontal bamboo drum struck with a woven *palas* palm beater. The smoke from the embers serves as a communicative medium between humans and *tageh* (the spiritual force invoked), a function Nagata compares to the Temiar use of fragrance in incantation (pp. 79–81).

The article also addresses the decline of *tageh* performance, noting that complaints from surrounding Malay peasants about the noise of singing and bamboo drums have contributed to its suppression in resettlement communities (p. 80, n. 29).

### Key Findings

- The *tageh* ritual is performed at night on the seventh day (*hay tujoh*) after a death, during a *kenduri* feast marking the end of mourning; additional feasts may occur on the 16th, 20th, 40th, and 50th days (pp. 79–80, n. 25).
- The ritual must be conducted in total darkness so as not to frighten *cenoy* (sky spirits) who join the singing; the purpose of *penlogn* is to invite *cenoy* and sing for the well-being of all participants (p. 79).
- The musical instrument is a 5-inch-diameter bamboo tube, 5–6 feet long, called *lebeh* or *gendang*, held horizontally and struck with a sandal-shaped beater made of woven *palas* palm (*pano' palas*); no woman plays this instrument (p. 81).
- The embers used in the opening ritual are made from *heli kasa*, identified by a botanist at Universiti Sains Malaysia as *Dianella ensifolia* (Family: Liliaceae) (p. 80).
- The *cao neges* (sing leader) is described as *sedikit hala'* ('a little bit shaman') because he can play the drum and sing, but is not a full shaman (p. 80).
- One reason for the decline of *tageh* performance is complaints from Malay peasants living around the resettlement community about the 'noise' (*bising*) of singing and bamboo drums (p. 80, n. 29).

### Conclusion

Nagata concludes that while Orang Asli healing and dream songs are widely disseminated across ethnolinguistic groups with local innovation and cross-group adoption, the *tageh* sing ritual appears unique to the Kensiw in that no other group performs a similar ritual, and it may therefore represent one polar type within this genre of performance (p. 81).

## Context

- Primary sources: Nagata's own field notes (1971–2001, University of Toronto), supplemented by 1991 audio recordings; the article also draws on earlier ethnographic literature by Benjamin, Schebesta, Evans, van der Sluys, and Roseman.
- Historiographical contribution: The article fills a gap in the literature on western Orang Asli religious practice by documenting a ritual genre (*tageh*) that is distinct from the better-known shamanistic ceremonies, and by identifying the unique musical instrument and botanical materials involved.
