# Stamford Raffles

## The Orienting Lede

Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles (1781–1826) was a British colonial administrator, naturalist, and scholar who served as Lieutenant-Governor of Java (1811–1816) and founding Governor of Singapore (1819). His career spanned the Indian Archipelago at a moment of decisive geopolitical transition, and his legacy is defined by the establishment of Singapore as a free port, his systematic study of Malay legal and linguistic institutions, and his foundational contributions to zoology and botany. Raffles remains one of the most contested figures in Southeast Asian historiography: celebrated by the British colonial establishment and successive Singaporean governments as a visionary founder, yet subjected to sustained revisionist critique from the 1950s onward that interrogates the limits of his administrative competence, the parochialism of his racial assumptions, and the degree to which his achievements were attributable to subordinates rather than to himself.

## The British Occupation of Java and Archipelago Policy

Raffles arrived in the East with a vision of an "Empire of Colonies" that would give Britain leverage to insist on monopoly rights in the Eastern Seas, and he divided the Archipelago into nine zones, examining Dutch claims in each to identify where British "intimate connections, by treaty" with native chiefs could be established as a counterweight to any future Dutch restoration [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1). This forward policy was consistently rejected by both the East India Company's Directors and the Supreme Government in Bengal, whose commercial priorities lay in the China trade rather than in the Eastern Islands [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1). By the time Raffles arrived, the Directors had already concluded that the Moluccas could no longer be used by the Dutch to enforce a world spice monopoly, and the lucrative China trade—worth £1,400,000 annually in European investment by 1793—had rendered the island commerce comparatively insignificant [[1, p. 85]](#ref-1). The conquest of Java itself was a decision imposed on the Company by the Home Government against the Directors' determined wishes, and from the outset the Company regarded the island as a burden rather than an asset [[1, p. 85]](#ref-1).

The core of Raffles's Archipelago policy was implemented through a series of specific ventures, each undertaken in defiance of, or in advance of, explicit instructions from Bengal and London forbidding further engagement with native states [[1, p. 86]](#ref-1). These included the military expedition against the Sambas pirates in 1813, Garnham's diplomatic mission to the Borneo coast, Hunt's commercial and political expedition to Sooloo and Mindanao, the punitive expedition to Bali, and the establishment of the Banjermassin settlement [[1, p. 86]](#ref-1). Raffles supplied Alexander Hare's Banjermassin settlement with more than 2,000 Javanese "criminals" over five years as part of a vigorous colonisation scheme [[1, p. 24]](#ref-1). His February 1814 Minute, which Bastin identifies as the most important despatch of its kind Raffles ever sent home, laid out the logic with the greatest clarity: the independent native states brought within British influence must be "entirely divested of any relation with Java" so that they would not be swept up in any restoration of Dutch power [[1, p. 87]](#ref-1).

The entire edifice collapsed not because of any single dramatic failure but because the authorities who controlled resources simply did not share Raffles's premises [[1, p. 88]](#ref-1). The Board of Control declared his engagements with petty states "impolitic, and injudicious" and ordered the Banjermassin treaty annulled [[1, p. 26]](#ref-1). When the Treaty of 1814 returned Java and its dependencies to the Dutch, the last British settlement at Banjermassin was formally evacuated in November 1815, and Raffles's life's work in the Archipelago was effectively undone [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1).

During the Java period, Raffles's intimate circle of five young East India Company officers who served as his aides-de-camp at Government House, Buitenzorg, constituted what Bastin terms the crucible in which both his greatest loyalties and his most bitter betrayals were forged [[2, p. 1]](#ref-2). Travers, the oldest and most intimate of the group, first met Raffles in Penang in 1806, establishing a twenty-year friendship [[2, p. 3]](#ref-2). Garnham, the most professionally accomplished, held successive residencies at Semarang, Besuki, and Yogyakarta before accompanying Raffles to England in 1816 [[2, p. 7]](#ref-2). By contrast, Methven and Watson both fell spectacularly from grace: Methven's "abominable love of Money" (Raffles's own words) led to his expulsion from Singapore and his death in 1823, while Watson's quarrel at Fort Marlborough culminated in his being sent to Calcutta under arrest in 1822 [[2, pp. 9–12]](#ref-2).

Raffles's membership of Dutch masonic lodges during the interregnum proved beneficial to his personal initiatives and professional career [[3, p. 1]](#ref-3). Jordaan and Carey demonstrate that the correct window for his initiation is September–October 1811, immediately following the British conquest and his appointment as Lieutenant-Governor, rather than the long-standing Dutch consensus of 1813 [[3, pp. 5–6]](#ref-3). The simultaneous initiation of Raffles and his Dutch Council colleague Muntinghe, performed in the presence of Lord Minto and Colonel Gillespie, is presented as the foundational act of a deliberately constructed Anglo-Dutch administrative partnership [[3, p. 3]](#ref-3). By early 1814, 20 of the 61 members of Lodge La Vertueuse in Batavia were British, including Raffles, demonstrating the significant Anglo-Dutch composition of the lodge during the interregnum [[3, p. 13]](#ref-3). Professor B. Schrieke, in his 1929 address to the Royal Batavian Society of Arts and Sciences, argued that Raffles was the first to articulate the principle that Java should be governed for its own benefit rather than for the commercial advantage of the mother country, and that the system of landrent was introduced "within three times twenty-four hours" of Raffles's arrival, with the Dutch advisor Muntinghe as the key instrument [[4, pp. 302–303]](#ref-4).

## The Founding of Singapore and Administrative Governance

Raffles's letter of 10 June 1819 to Colonel Addenbrooke, written from Singapore four months after the settlement's founding, offers the most extensive contemporary justification for its establishment [[5, p. 175]](#ref-5). The letter's central argument is geopolitical: following the cession of Java and the Moluccas to the Dutch, they had attempted to monopolise all Archipelago trade through Batavia, excluding English merchants and threatening the China trade route [[5, p. 177]](#ref-5). Singapore was selected as the station commanding the southern entrance to the Strait of Malacca, and Raffles describes its condition four months after establishment: a population exceeding five thousand, a harbour "filled with Shipping from all quarters," troops healthy, and provisions abundant [[5, p. 178]](#ref-5). The strategic vision is explicit: Singapore is conceived not as a territorial acquisition but as "a great commercial Emporium, and a *fulcrum* whence we may extend our influence politically," with the aspiration that "what Malta is in the West, that may Singapore become in the East" [[5, p. 178]](#ref-5).

The practical administration of the settlement, however, was beset by internal conflict. Raffles's correspondence with Nathaniel Wallich reveals the bitter opposition coalition of Farquhar, Quieros, Bernard, Palmer and others [[6, pp. 17–18]](#ref-6). Quieros falsely claimed his warehouse could not be rebuilt for less than 13,000 dollars when his original estimate had been 5,000 [[6, p. 18]](#ref-6). The January 1823 land auction on the south bank of the Singapore River raised not less than 50,000 dollars in the course of an hour, each lot having been put up at one dollar [[6, p. 16]](#ref-6). Raffles allocated land on Government Hill in Singapore for a Botanical Garden in November 1822, placing it under the charge of Dr. William Montgomerie, and granted the Garden a monthly sum of 60 dollars plus a one-time donation of 1,000 dollars [[7, p. 17]](#ref-7), [[6, pp. 12–13]](#ref-6).

The administrative record of early Singapore was shaped by the patronage system that elevated Raffles's own circle while marginalising those outside it [[8, p. 25]](#ref-8). Wright's re-examination of the career of Francis James Bernard demonstrates that Raffles's characterisation of Bernard as a "low character" was unsubstantiated and motivated by Raffles's desire to consolidate control over the settlement's administration [[8, p. 26]](#ref-8). Contemporaries who actually worked with Bernard—Farquhar, Crawfurd, Presgrave, Prince, and even James Brooke—consistently praised his competence and diligence [[8, p. 27]](#ref-8). Tommy Koh, the doyen of the Singapore diplomatic circuit, observed that Raffles "only spent 38 days in Singapore, between 1819 and his return [to Britain] in October 1822," and that "it was Farquhar who did all the heavy lifting in Singapore" [[9, p. 112]](#ref-9).

## Natural History and Scientific Patronage

Raffles's enthusiasm for natural history predated his famous association with Dr. Thomas Horsfield in Java [[7, pp. 1–2]](#ref-7). During his service in Melaka between 1810 and 1811, he employed four men specifically to search for insects, shells, and wild animals [[7, p. 1]](#ref-7). His Malay scribe Abdullah bin Abdul Kadir Munshi provides accounts of Raffles's employment of collectors in Melaka and Penang [[7, pp. 1–2]](#ref-7). During his tenure in Java, Raffles shifted from personal collecting to patronage, securing Horsfield's vast collections for the East India Company Museum and facilitating correspondence with Sir Joseph Banks in London [[7, pp. 6–8]](#ref-7). Upon moving to west Sumatra, he intensified his personal involvement, employing Dr. Joseph Arnold and later William Jack, whose botanical expertise complemented Raffles's zoological ambitions [[7, pp. 10–12]](#ref-7).

The giant flower *Rafflesia arnoldii* was discovered by Dr. Joseph Arnold in May 1818, though Robert Brown assigned the generic name to honour Raffles [[7, pp. 11–12]](#ref-7). In his 1819 letter to Addenbrooke, Raffles describes the flower as measuring "a full yard across, weighs fifteen pounds, and contains in the Nectary no less than eight pints, each petal being 11 inches in breadth and there being five of them" [[5, pp. 180–181]](#ref-5). A pivotal moment occurred during the voyage home aboard the ship *Fame*, where a fire on 2 February 1824 destroyed between 2,000 and 3,000 drawings, all collections, descriptions, papers, and every document and memoir Raffles possessed, including those of the late Dr. Jack [[7, pp. 18–19]](#ref-7), [[6, p. 34]](#ref-6). Despite these losses, Raffles successfully repatriated preserved specimens and utilised his accumulated knowledge to establish the Zoological Society of London in 1826, serving as its first President alongside Sir Humphry Davy [[7, p. 21]](#ref-7).

Raffles's posthumous publication, "The Maritime Code of the Malays," presents a sweeping survey of Malay legal institutions, the political history of the Straits states, and the origins of the Malay nation as a distinct people [[10, p. 1]](#ref-10). His overarching thesis is that the Malays constitute a comparatively recent formation—shaped by Arabian contact and Islamic adoption—whose maritime law, particularly the *Nacodah*'s (captain's) power of life and death, represents a purely indigenous legal innovation with no parallel in Arab or European sea codes [[10, p. 20]](#ref-10). He frames his project as a systematic collection and translation of the *Undang undang* (Institutes) of the principal Malay states, arguing that a digested compilation would serve both practical diplomatic purposes and the intellectual goal of illustrating "the unjustly degraded character of so extensive a portion of the human race" [[10, p. 3]](#ref-10).

## Legacy and Documentation

The historiography of Raffles has undergone a fundamental transformation since the mid-twentieth century. It was the earlier substantive writings and lectures of Raffles specialist John Bastin from the late 1950s that spearheaded the critique of Raffles and the associated hagiography, and elevated the role played by his associate William Farquhar [[9, p. 111]](#ref-9). By the early 1970s, Malay sociologist Syed Hussein Alatas had daringly painted Raffles as a colonial schemer, concluding that "had Raffles not been around another man would have done the job" and that "to rank him as a humanitarian reformer is to abuse the use of the term" [[9, p. 111]](#ref-9). A much younger Malay scholar, Syed Muhd Khairudin Aljunied, is equally negative about Raffles's jaundiced view of the Malays and their religiosity, arguing that he fostered parochialist tendencies in British rule [[9, p. 112]](#ref-9).

The politics of Raffles's commemoration reached a critical juncture with the launch of Singapore's Bicentennial in 2018. The Bicentennial Office made the strategic decision to embrace upfront the *longue duree* of a 700-year timeline to Singapore's history, thus avoiding many issues relating to the role played by Raffles [[9, p. 107]](#ref-9). Archaeological excavations at Fort Canning since 1984, led by John Miksic, established evidence of a thriving settlement circa 1299–1399 as the kingdom of Temasek/Singapura [[9, p. 107]](#ref-9). However, the "black hole" between the sacking of Singapura (c. 1399) and Raffles's landing (1819) remains a period of contested interpretation: Peter Borschberg's analysis of Portuguese and Dutch sources found evidence of a substantial settlement inconclusive, with the majority of references pointing to a maritime gateway rather than a continuous community [[9, pp. 108–109]](#ref-9).

The "sleepy fishing village" metaphor, first evoked by John Crawfurd and embedded as popular public imagery through the vivid prose of Mary Turnbull, lent credence to the argument that Raffles "founded" Singapore [[9, p. 109]](#ref-9). Lee Kuan Yew, in his 1969 speech commemorating Singapore's 150th anniversary, referenced the "fishing village" metaphor twice and praised Raffles highly, noting that "there was no organized human society in Singapore, unless a *fishing village* can be called a society" [[9, p. 110]](#ref-9). The PAP government not only retained the bronze Raffles statue in the heart of Singapore's civic district but constructed yet another larger, white-washed version for a site nearer the Singapore River and Parliament House [[9, p. 111]](#ref-9).

The Society's own archival record reflects the long-running programme of publishing Raffles's collected manuscripts and translations. His "The Maritime Code of the Malays" appeared in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1879, more than fifty years after his death [[10, p. 1]](#ref-10). His letter of 10 June 1819 was published in the same journal in 1878, presented to the Raffles Museum by W. W. Willans on 3 December 1878 [[5, p. 175]](#ref-5). Bastin's 1981 edition of twenty-four letters from Raffles to Nathaniel Wallich (1819–1824) provides the most intimate surviving record of Raffles's final years, drawn from two archival collections now held at the Calcutta Botanic Garden and the Koninklijke Bibliotheek, The Hague [[6, p. 1]](#ref-6). The 1929 presentation of a bronze bust of Raffles to the Royal Batavian Society of Arts and Sciences in Batavia fulfilled a promise he had made to that Society in 1816, an interval of 114 years caused by the restoration of Java to Dutch rule, after which Raffles was politically unpopular [[4, p. 290]](#ref-4). Schrieke proposed that the two Societies undertake a joint publication of the unpublished documents of the British occupation period, including the complete set of the *Java Government Gazette* held by the Batavian Society, as a "monumentum aere perennius" for Raffles [[4, p. 305]](#ref-4).
