# Maya (image) in indigenous Riau world-view: a forgotten concept of Malayan animist thought and practice

## Abstract

The early literature on Malay animism and magic includes a passing reference to a concept called *maya*. This reference is hardly noticeable in the literature, and when Kirk Endicott wrote his *Malay Magic*, he omitted the word altogether. In this article the author uses ethnographic material from the Malayan-speaking Orang Sakai of Riau to examine the concept of *maya* (image) as it relates to a ‘lifeless soul’ inherent in material objects, giving physical objects vitality of form, appearance and use.

## Summary

The article addresses a lacuna in the early literature on Malay animism. The term *maya* (rendered *mayo* in Sakai pronunciation) appears only once in Annandale and Robinson's 1903 account of Patani Malay beliefs, in brackets, and was subsequently dropped by Endicott in his 1970 analysis. Porath argues this single passing reference conceals a concept of central importance to Malayan animic ontology. Drawing on 18 months of ethnographic fieldwork with the Orang Sakai of Riau (Sumatra), he restores the term to the literature and demonstrates its structural role in the indigenous world-view.

The core argument is that *maya* is not merely a synonym for *semangat* but a distinct aspect of the pan-soul-substance (*Semanget*) that gives inanimate physical objects their form, appearance, and use without conferring life-full consciousness. This distinguishes the Sakai ontological framework from Endicott's pan-psychic concentration model, which treated all objects as having degrees of *semangat*. Porath shows that *maya* operates across multiple domains: in dreams (where one sees the *maya* of objects and people), in shamanic ritual (where palm-leaf model offerings are made for their *maya* and later transferred to spirit-tutelaries), and in the practice of *ilmu batin* (inner-power-knowledge), where photographs and body-substances function as *maya* through which a practitioner can target another person's embodied *semangat*.

The article also repositions the Sakai shamanic experience against the Malay spirit-medium model. Where Malay *jin* can possess the body and speak through it, Sakai spirits (*'antu*) cannot materialize in the physical body; they interact only through embodied *semangat*, and their presence is registered as *maya* (images) seen through the inner eye (*mato batin*). This distinction has implications for how we understand the broader Malayan animic tradition and the role of Indic-derived terminology within it.

### Key Findings

- The term *maya* appears only once in the early literature, in Annandale and Robinson (1903: 81, 100), where Patani fishermen are described as believing every boat has a *mayor prahu* (boat-soul) and every house a *semangat rumah* that comes into existence when parts are assembled. Endicott (1970: 51) omitted the term entirely, substituting *semangat*.
- Porath's fieldwork was conducted over 18 months (April 1996–April 1997; November 1998–February 1999; plus a two-month visit in mid-1995) with Orang Sakai communities in mainland Riau, Indonesia, during which he lived with Sakai shamans and participated in numerous healing sessions (p. 9, n. 26).
- In the Sakai ontological framework, *Semanget* (capital S) manifests in three aspects: spirit (*'antu*), embodied *semanget*, and *maya* (image). Physical objects possess *maya* (refined *Semanget* substance) but not life-full *semanget*; without *maya*, physical matter (*bahan kasa'*) withers and disintegrates (*melayu*) (pp. 10–13).
- During the *kelonkap* (all-night celebratory shamanic ritual), palm-leaf model offerings (boats, yachts, cars, palaces) are made for their *maya*. The models shimmer yellow at the start of the ritual and begin to wilt as the *maya* departs; after three days the shaman enters trance to hand the *maya* to his spirit-familiar, after which the physical model is discarded (pp. 17–18).
- The 'healing the house' (*bu'ubet 'uma*) scenario in *kelonkap* involves the shaman slamming a staff into the four cross-beams and miming hammering iron nails (*paku bulano*) to strengthen the *maya* of the house, which symbolically represents the community and the cosmic order (pp. 18–19).
- Photographs are understood as capturing a splinter of a person's embodied *semanget* materialized as *maya* on paper; medicine-women use photographs of people they have never met to locate and approach that person's *semanget* in dreams, a practice that explains why Sakai individuals resist having their photographs taken (p. 16).

### Conclusion

Porath's definitive takeaway is that *maya* is a concept of central importance to Malayan animic ontology that was lost through the analytical choices of earlier scholars. Its restoration reveals that the Malayan pan-soul-substance world-view is not simply a matter of degrees of *semangat* concentration (as Endicott proposed) but involves a structurally distinct category—image—that mediates between the physical and non-physical dimensions. The Sakai shamanic experience, in which spirits are seen as *maya* through the inner eye rather than possessing the body, represents a more fully developed articulation of this concept than is found in Muslim Malay village practice, and the Indic-derived term *maya* is indispensable to understanding the logic of Malayan ritual and belief (pp. 19–21).

## Context

- Primary ethnographic data: 18 months of participant-observation fieldwork with Orang Sakai communities in Riau, Indonesia (1995–1999), including residence with Sakai shamans and participation in recorded healing sessions (p. 9, n. 26).
- Historiographical contribution: The article intervenes in the historiography of Malay animism by recovering a term present in the primary colonial-era sources (Annandale and Robinson 1903) but subsequently dropped by the most influential modern synthesis (Endicott 1970), and by demonstrating through contemporary ethnography that the concept remains structurally operative in indigenous Malayan-speaking communities.
