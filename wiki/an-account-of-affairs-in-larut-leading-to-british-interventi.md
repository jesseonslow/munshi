# An Account of Affairs in Larut Leading to British Intervention. Facsimile reprint

Leonard Wray, Jun. (1852–1942), a Perak civil servant and later Curator of the Perak Museum, compiled this account in 1894 from first-hand reports and official documents covering the period immediately preceding British intervention in Larut in 1874. The text, originally published in *The Tin Mines and the Mining Industries of Perak and Other Papers*, presents Captain Speedy's narrative of the Larut mining district's development from its 1848 discovery through the devastating faction wars of 1872–1873, supplemented by a full Chinese miners' petition of May 1873 that provides a ground-level account of the violence.

## Summary

The article reconstructs the sequence of events that led from the initial tin discovery at Larut to the Pangkor settlement of January 1874. Speedy's account frames the history as one of progressive disorder: the 1848 opening of the first mine by Che Long Jaffar with twenty Chinese labourers, the 1862 expulsion of the Fui Chin faction, and the catastrophic faction war of 1872–1873 between the Sih Quan and Goh Quan groups. The Mantri Ngah Ibrahim's vacillation between factions is identified as the central administrative failure that allowed the conflict to escalate beyond control.

The embedded petition of 3 May 1873, submitted by headmen of the See Yip Long kongsee to the Governor of the Straits Settlements, provides a detailed and graphic account of the violence from the miners' perspective. It documents specific massacres, the Mantri's active participation in attacks, the use of Malay Datus to pursue fleeing miners along the Krian route, and the Mantri's attempts to import armed reinforcements from China. The petitioners' central demand is the removal of the Mantri from Larut, which they identify as the root cause of the fighting.

The narrative concludes with the practical aftermath of the Pangkor agreement: the disarming of both factions, the drawing of a boundary line dividing the mining country between Sih Quan and Goh Quan, and the rapid demographic recovery of the district. Speedy's account of the founding of the towns of Thaipeng and Kamunting in 1874 serves as a coda demonstrating the immediate economic benefits of British administrative intervention.

### Key Findings

- The 1872–1873 faction war reduced Larut's population to approximately 4,000 fighting men, with no traders remaining; within eleven months of the Pangkor settlement, the population recovered to 33,000 (26,000 Chinese) (p. 201).
- Penang's import returns for 1872 and 1873 showed a decrease of nearly $1,000,000 due to the cessation of tin imports from Larut (p. 198).
- The 1862 Fui Chin expulsion resulted in a compensation sum of $17,500 extracted from the Chin Seah faction at the demand of the Raja (p. 197).
- Captain Speedy's 1873 agreement with the Mantri provided for a salary of $500 per month plus one-tenth of all district revenues for ten years; he recruited 100 men in India and returned to Larut in late September 1873 (p. 198).
- The See Yip Long petition of 3 May 1873 records that approximately 2,000 of their 8,000 men were killed in a single Mantri-orchestrated attack, and a further 3,000 were killed or perished during their flight to Penang, with women sold into slavery (pp. 199–200).
- By the close of 1874, the planned town of Thaipeng (Goh Quan division) held 5,000 inhabitants and Kamunting (Sih Quan division) held 4,000 inhabitants and 300 shops (p. 201).

### Conclusion

Wray's compilation presents the Larut crisis as a case study in the failure of indigenous administrative capacity to manage the social pressures of rapid mining expansion, and in the effectiveness of British intervention in restoring order and economic productivity. The juxtaposition of Speedy's official narrative with the Chinese miners' petition offers a rare dual perspective on the same events, though the overall framing remains firmly within the colonial administrative logic that justified the Pangkor settlement as a necessary and beneficial act of governance.

## Context

- Wray served in the Perak civil service from 1881 and was Curator of the Perak Museum; his 1894 publication was produced under the auspices of the Perak Government Printing Office, reflecting the colonial administrative interest in documenting the district's economic history.
- The text draws on Captain Speedy's unpublished account and the original Chinese petition, both of which survive in the Perak state archives; the 2021 facsimile reprint in JMBRAS makes these documents accessible to a wider scholarly audience.
