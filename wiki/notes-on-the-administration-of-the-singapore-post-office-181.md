# Notes on the administration of the Singapore Post Office, 1819–67

C.A. Gibson-Hill's 1958 article traces the administrative evolution of the Singapore Post Office from the settlement's founding in 1819 to the formal separation of postal and maritime functions in 1858. The central argument is that the coupling of the Post Master and Master Attendant roles—initially a practical arrangement for a small military outpost—became progressively dysfunctional as commercial traffic and mail volume expanded, and that the eventual decoupling was the product of sustained local pressure rather than imperial initiative.

## Summary

The article reconstructs the institutional history of Singapore's postal service through the lens of its administrative placement within the Master Attendant's office. Gibson-Hill demonstrates that from 1819 to 1823 the mails were handled by military officers (Cantonment Adjutants) acting under the Resident, a arrangement that reflected the settlement's origins as a temporary military station. The appointment of Charles Ryan as the first civilian Post Master in February 1823, and his rapid dismissal by May 1824, led to the formal coupling of the two posts under Captain Flint, a union that persisted—with two brief interruptions—until December 1858 (pp. 145–146).

The narrative's turning point is the arrival of the P&O steamer service in September 1845, which exposed the inadequacy of the existing establishment in dramatic fashion: over 4,750 covers arrived for despatch, and the mail was loaded unsorted and half an hour late (p. 11). Governor Butterworth's subsequent proposals for an expanded staff, better accommodation, and a younger officer were met with bureaucratic inertia from the Supreme Government at Fort William, which took ten to fourteen months to approve measures that Butterworth had already implemented locally (p. 14). The article uses this episode to illustrate a broader pattern: the postal department's chronic under-resourcing relative to its revenue-generating capacity, and the difficulty of securing imperial approval for local administrative reform.

Gibson-Hill draws on East India Company records (particularly the EIC. Rec. series held in the India Office), contemporary newspapers (the *Straits Times* and *Singapore Free Press*), station orders, and the Resident's diary to build a detailed institutional narrative. The piece functions as a companion to his longer study of the Master Attendants, isolating the postal dimension of an office that had accumulated responsibilities far beyond its original maritime remit.

### Key Findings

- Postal revenue grew from Rs 1,812 in 1833 to Rs 8,516 in 1841 and Rs 18,740 in 1844, while the total staff salary remained at Rs 1,680 per year—less than a tenth of the 1844 takings (pp. 7, 8).
- In 1844, 744 square-rigged vessels entered Singapore harbour, averaging just over two per day including Sundays and public holidays (p. 8).
- The P&O steamer's first west-bound arrival on 9 September 1845 brought over 4,750 covers; the mail was loaded unsorted and half an hour late, prompting a public outcry in the *Straits Times* (p. 11).
- The formal separation of the Post Office from the Master Attendant's department was approved by the Secretary to the Government of India on 29 October 1858 and announced in Singapore on 4 December 1858 (p. 17).
- By 1854 the English mail was delivered in under 34 days from closure in London, compared with roughly 100 days for the Thames-to-Batavia run during Raffles's Java occupation (p. 14).
- William Cuppage served in the postal department for nearly 42 years, retiring in 1871; from 1869 his appointment was designated acting Postmaster-General at a salary of $300 per month (p. 19).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive takeaway is that the Singapore Post Office's administrative history is a case study in the lag between institutional capacity and commercial growth in a colonial free port. The coupling of postal and maritime functions was a rational early solution that became a structural impediment, and its resolution required not a single decisive reform but a protracted process of local advocacy, improvised action by governors, and reluctant imperial approval spanning over three decades.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: East India Company Records (India Office), particularly series L, M, N, Q, R, S, Z, and AA; supplemented by the *Straits Times*, *Singapore Free Press*, and *Singapore Chronicle*.
- Historiographical contribution: This article complements Gibson-Hill's separate study of the Master Attendants (JMBRAS 33, 1960) and provides the postal-specific dimension of Singapore's early administrative history, a topic previously treated only in passing in general settlement histories such as Melville's *One Hundred Years of Singapore* (1921).
