# History of the Malay Regiment 1933–1942

Dol Ramli's 1965 article traces the formation, expansion, and wartime destruction of the Malay Regiment from its inception at Port Dickson in March 1933 to the fall of Singapore in February 1942. The overarching thesis is that the Regiment fulfilled a decades-long political aspiration of the Malay rulers and their subjects to share responsibility for the defence of their homeland, and that despite being a young, partially-trained force at the outbreak of hostilities, it acquitted itself with a distinction that became foundational to the Regiment's subsequent tradition.

## Summary

Ramli situates the Malay Regiment within a long history of Malay military culture and colonial defence policy. He argues that the delay between the first formal proposal in the Federal Council in 1913 and actual formation in 1933 was driven by a confluence of factors: lingering colonial suspicion of Malay martial capacity rooted in the recent memory of the 1870s–1880s state wars, the absence of any tradition of regular Malay armies since the Malacca Empire, the high cost of the Burma Rifles garrison maintained under the 1895 Treaty, and the economic pressures of the Depression which made a locally recruited force financially attractive. The British Government's growing concern over the European political climate in the early 1930s provided the final impetus, and the Colonial and War Offices approved the scheme in principle in September 1932.

The article then follows the Regiment's development through its experimental phase, its expansion to full battalion strength, and its further growth to two battalions in the immediate pre-war period. Ramli draws heavily on Federal Council proceedings, regimental orders, annual reports, and—crucially—interviews with veterans of the inaugural squad and senior officers, which lend the narrative an insider's perspective on training standards, morale, and the cultural accommodations made to preserve the Regiment's Malay character. The wartime sections reconstruct the campaigns on both the east and west coasts and the Battle of Singapore with considerable detail, relying on regimental war diaries, after-action reports, and the recollections of participants such as Lieutenant MacKenzie and the officers of "C" Company.

A recurring thread is the tension between the Regiment's political purpose—as a symbol of Malay participation in national defence—and the practical military realities of fielding an untested force against a battle-hardened enemy. Ramli does not shy from acknowledging the Regiment's limitations (incomplete training, insufficient officer establishment, lack of reserves) but consistently frames these within the broader context of the garrison's overall unpreparedness.

### Key Findings

- The first 25 recruits of "The Experimental Company" began training at Port Dickson on March 1, 1933, selected from over a thousand applicants; the annual cost was estimated at $100,000, compared with the Burma Rifles' average cost of $860,478.95 per year (1927–1931) (pp. 199–205, 208).
- By October 1938 the Regiment had nearly reached its full peace-time complement of 17 British officers, 6 Malay officers, 11 British warrant-officers, and 759 other ranks, organised into three rifle companies, one Support Company, and a Headquarters Wing (p. 214).
- The 2nd Battalion was officially formed on December 1, 1941, with a total strength of 453 men under Lt.-Colonel F. W. Young; at the time of the Battle of Singapore the Regiment numbered approximately 1,400 including raw recruits (pp. 218–219, 230).
- During the Malayan Campaign the Regiment suffered a total of 159 killed (six British officers, seven Malay officers, 146 other ranks), concentrated mainly on February 12–14, 1942 (p. 244).
- Within a fortnight of the surrender, five Malay officer-internees were summarily executed for refusing to serve under the Japanese or accept civilian release; a party of 98 Malay personnel was machine-gunned near the Gap (p. 244).
- The first four Malay regular officers—Second-Lieutenants Ismail Tahar, Raja Lope Nor Rashid, Ariffin Haji Sulaiman, and Ibrahim Sidek, all from Perak—were commissioned on November 4, 1936, having risen from the ranks of the original 25-man squad (p. 215).

### Conclusion

Ramli's definitive historical takeaway, echoed in his closing quotation from Lt.-General Percival, is that the Malay Regiment's "stubborn defence of the Pasir Panjang Ridge at the height of the Battle of Singapore" established "a great tradition in the Regiment and an inspiration for future generations" (p. 245). The article thus functions as both a regimental history and a founding narrative for the post-war Regiment, which was revived on September 5, 1945, and grew to seven battalions by 1955.

## Context

- Primary sources include the *Proceedings, Legislative Council, Federated Malay States* (1913–1938), *Malay Regiment Regimental Orders* (1936), *F.M.S. Annual Reports*, and direct interviews with veterans conducted by the author in 1954–1955.
- As one of the earliest regimental histories written by a Malay officer for the JMBRAS, the article represents a significant historiographical contribution in that it combines official colonial administrative records with Malay military oral testimony, offering a perspective that predates the more extensive post-war regimental histories.
