# The well-prepared death of Lee Kuan Yew

## Abstract

The Prime Minister’s Office of Singapore declared a week of national mourning after the country’s first Prime Minister, Lee Kuan Yew, passed away on 23 March 2015. Approximately one-fifth of the country’s population visited at least one mourning site during the week. The state used this time and space to establish and crystallize a desired memory of Lee. A national narrative of mourning was constructed by providing both physical and emotional venues such as community tribute sites, the parliament site where the body lay in state and online platforms such as the Facebook page ‘Remembering LKY’. Through interruption, repetition, and formality, this structure enacted, guided, and amplified the expression of specific emotions from the participants. An over-arching atmosphere of mourning was created in that week, allowing the whole nation to participate in the guided remembering of Lee.

## Summary

Li Nanlan examines the week of national mourning declared by the Singapore government following the death of its first Prime Minister, Lee Kuan Yew, on 23 March 2015. Drawing on ritual theory—particularly Stanley Tambiah's performative approach, Emile Durkheim's concept of collective effervescence, and the work of Sally Falk Moore and Barbara Myerhoff on secular ritual—she argues that the state deliberately designed the mourning apparatus to manage, guide, and amplify public emotions. Three structural features—interruption of daily routines, repetition of symbols, and formality of procedure—operated across physical spaces (community tribute sites, the Parliament lying-in-state, the state funeral procession) and digital platforms (the Facebook page 'Remembering LKY') to create an overarching atmosphere in which the entire nation could participate in a guided remembering of Lee (pp. 108–112).

The article's central analytical move is to identify gratitude as the dominant and most distinctive emotion elicited by the rituals, distinguishing it from generic grief. The author draws on Tong Chee Kiong's analysis of Chinese death rituals as reciprocal gift exchange to frame the relationship: Singaporeans expressed personal indebtedness to Lee's vision while simultaneously reaffirming their own identity as inheritors of the nation he founded. Wang Gungwu's argument that Lee's vision was intrinsically tied to Singapore's particular model of national identity—adapted to a resurgent Asia rather than the European nation-state tradition—further grounds this reading (pp. 111–112).

Nanlan is careful to resist a purely constructivist interpretation. While the state laid the structural ground for the fermentation and amplification of emotion, she notes that the massive participation was largely an autonomous decision by citizens, not the product of overt coercion. The mourning week thus functioned as an extended, geographically distributed form of collective effervescence in which the collective spoke through individuals (p. 112).

### Key Findings

- Approximately 454,700 people attended the lying-in-state at Parliament House, and 1.2 million visited condolence centres across the island during the 23–29 March mourning week; the latter figure represents over 30 per cent of Singapore's resident population (5,535,000) and 35 per cent of its citizens (~3.375 million) (p. 108).
- Eighteen community tribute sites were established in public housing areas, open spaces, sports courts, and shopping mall entrances; their placement deliberately blocked and altered pedestrian routes to make participation nearly unavoidable for nearby residents (pp. 108–109).
- The state funeral procession on 29 March was led by Headquarters Singapore Artillery, with Lee's casket on a gun carriage escorted by 48 military personnel on a ceremonial Unimog; the repeated formal military salutes were voluntarily echoed by male civilians, eliciting the shared National Service experience (p. 110).
- Condolence boards at sites such as the Istana featured grids of 54 rectangular slots holding uniformly designed message cards (faint grey orchid pattern), processed by military staff in white shirts and black trousers; the formalized queuing and display procedure transformed private mourning into a collective act (pp. 110–111).
- A photograph of Lee's quotation about giving up his life for a successful Singapore, posted on the PAP's Facebook page after being cited by Prime Minister Lee Hsien Loong, accumulated 77,373 likes and 28,109 shares by January 2016—the highest engagement among all Lee quotation images posted by the party (p. 111).
- Radio and television played downbeat tribute music, newspapers darkened their mastheads, digital billboards were blanked out, and television stations ran death-announcement tickers; many employers gave employees time off to attend the lying-in-state (p. 109).

### Conclusion

Nanlan concludes that the mourning week was a well-prepared civic ritual through which the Singapore state crystallized a specific national memory of Lee Kuan Yew as the indispensable founder to whom every citizen owed a personal debt. The deliberate architecture of interruption, repetition, and formality did not manufacture emotion from nothing but provided the conditions for its collective expression and amplification, binding individual grief and gratitude into a shared national identity that reinforced both Lee's legacy and the coherence of the Singaporean polity.

## Context

- The article is grounded in the author's own ethnographic observation of the mourning period, including visits to condolence sites at the Istana and Clementi conducted with her professor and classmates at Yale-NUS College, as well as observation of the state funeral procession on Clementi Road (pp. 110–111).
- Historiographically, the piece extends Lily Kong and Brenda Yeoh's (1997) analysis of Singapore's National Day Parade as a site of national identity production to the domain of state-organized mourning, and integrates Chinese death-ritual scholarship (Tong Chee Kiong) with Western ritual theory to offer a cross-cultural framework for understanding Singapore's civic memory-making.
