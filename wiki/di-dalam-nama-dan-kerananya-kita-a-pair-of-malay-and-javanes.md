# “Di dalam nama dan kerananya kita”: A pair of Malay and Javanese letters by Sir Thomas Stamford Raffles

Teoh Boon Seong and Lim Beng Soon (1999) document the Malay lexical items embedded in Baba Hokkien of Penang, a creole-like variety spoken by the Straits Chinese (Peranakan) community. Their central argument is that Baba Hokkien of Penang is essentially a Hokkien dialect with a significant Malay lexical superstratum, fundamentally distinct from the Malay-based Baba Malay of Malacca and Singapore.

## Summary

The article addresses a gap in Peranakan linguistics, where most prior work—Shellabear (1913), Vaughan (1879), Clammer (1980), and Khoo Joo Ee (1996)—has focused on the Malacca and Singapore varieties of Baba Malay because of their superficial similarity to standard Malay. The authors contend that Baba Hokkien of Penang, while syntactically Hokkien, contains a substantial layer of Malay lexical items that have been phonologically remoulded, semantically narrowed or broadened, and fossilized into formulaic expressions. Their empirical data derives from two months of observation and transcription of naturally occurring speech within a single Peranakan family in Penang (May–June 1998), supplemented by taped conversations of spontaneous speech (p. 126).

The analytical framework adapts Tan Chee Beng's patterns of diversity—loan words, phonological differences, lexical differences, and semantic differences—to the specific context of a Chinese dialect with a Malay superstratum. The authors demonstrate that Malay borrowings undergo systematic phonological adaptation conforming to both Hokkien tonal structure and the north-west Malay dialect of Penang and Kedah, including high vowel lowering in final closed syllables, *l*-deletion, and diphthongization (pp. 129–130). They further identify semantic narrowing (e.g., *balai* restricted to 'police station'), semantic broadening (e.g., *senduk* covering both spoon and ladle), and semantic expansion into meanings unknown in standard Malay (e.g., *buta* meaning 'all for nothing') (pp. 130–131).

The authors propose two historical scenarios for the development of Baba Hokkien of Penang: either the Peranakan community was less influenced by Malays due to Penang's smaller indigenous population at the time of British acquisition in 1786, or Baba Malay gradually absorbed Hokkien elements as the community assimilated into mainstream Chinese culture after the Peranakan heyday (pp. 132–133).

### Key Findings

- The Malay word *hanya* is contracted to *nya* in Baba Hokkien of Penang, functioning as 'only'—a morpheme-level assimilation less evident than the particle-level borrowings *lah* and *pun* (p. 125).
- Phonological adaptation follows the north-west Malay dialect rules of Penang and Kedah: *bisul* > [bisoi], *sanggul* > [sanggoi], via high vowel lowering, *l*-deletion, and diphthongization (pp. 129–130).
- Semantic narrowing is pervasive across all word classes: *balai* is restricted to 'police station' (no *balai bomba* or *balai rakyat*), *amok* functions only as a verb, and *haram* means 'bearing a grudge' rather than 'forbidden' (p. 130).
- The appendix catalogues Malay borrowings across seven semantic categories—Trinkets, Animals, Things, Methods of Cooking/Food, Body Terms, Actions (Verbal and Physical), and Interjections—totaling well over 200 entries (pp. 134–137).
- Clammer (1980) estimated that by 1975 approximately 42 per cent of Malacca and Singapore Babas could speak Chinese, with the figure higher in Penang, indicating the relative strength of Hokkien in the Penang Peranakan community (p. 132).
- Malay usage above the level of individual lexicon is fossilized into formulaic expressions such as *kepala angin* ('temperamental'), *tiga suku* ('crazy'), and *ketam batu* ('a stingy person'), with positive descriptors being far rarer than negative ones (p. 131).

### Conclusion

The authors conclude that Baba Hokkien of Penang represents a distinct linguistic outcome from the Baba Malay of Malacca and Singapore, resulting from either the smaller indigenous Malay population in Penang at the time of British acquisition or the gradual assimilation of the Peranakan community into mainstream Chinese culture. The Malay elements retained in Baba Hokkien of Penang are largely fossilized, restricted in semantic range, and concentrated among older speakers (above 50), indicating an ongoing process of language shift away from Malay and toward Hokkien and English.

## Context

- The study draws on fieldwork data collected in 1998 from a single Peranakan family in Penang, supplemented by secondary literature on Baba Malay (Shellabear 1913, Tan Chee Beng 1979, Clammer 1980, Pakir 1986).
- The article contributes to Peranakan linguistics by extending the analytical framework from Malay-based Baba Malay to a Hokkien-based variety, and by documenting the phonological interface between Malay borrowings and the north-west Malay dialect of Penang and Kedah.
