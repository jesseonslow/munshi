# Archaeological discoveries in Peninsular Malaysia (1987–1990

Adi Haji Taha, writing in 1991 for the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, surveys archaeological discoveries made across Peninsular Malaysia between 1987 and 1990, documenting a period of renewed institutional momentum as local universities—particularly Universiti Sains Malaysia and Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia—resumed and expanded fieldwork that had been largely dormant since the colonial era. The article's overarching significance lies in recording how this new generation of research fundamentally revised the peninsula's prehistoric chronology and material culture record.

## Summary

The article functions as a state-by-state catalogue of new finds, chance discoveries, and excavation results, but its deeper importance is institutional. The late 1980s saw the establishment of the *Projek Arkeologi Malaysia* at USM under Siti Zuraina Majid and parallel programmes at UKM under Nik Hassan Shuhaimi, both of which re-engaged with colonial-era sites (Kota Tampan, Kuala Selinsing, Gua Tok Bidan) while opening entirely new ones. Taha, a Museums Department officer, positions himself as a synthesiser and occasional site visitor, explicitly deferring to the primary researchers for detailed interpretation and urging readers to consult their published reports.

The most consequential findings concern deep-time chronology. At Kota Tampan, Zuraina's re-excavation produced a lithic assemblage she interpreted as a Palaeolithic workshop, with volcanic ash layers attributed to the Toba eruption providing a terminus ante quem of approximately 30,000–35,000 years ago—a date that disputes Sieveking's earlier, more conservative estimate. At Gua Harimau, a burial context yielded a bronze celt and its stone mould with a radiocarbon date of circa 5,000 BP, which Taha presents as evidence that the peninsula was not a "backwater in civilisation" but an early bronze-using region. At Gua Gunung Runtuh, a 30-year-old male *Homo sapiens* skeleton dated to 10,000–11,000 years ago was identified as the earliest human remains yet found in the peninsula.

The article also documents more recent-period material culture with considerable detail: the Bujang Valley Site 50, a 12th–13th century Saivite temple of *vimana-mandapa* type; the Kuala Kelumpang shell-mound settlement (200 BC to 10th century AD) with its glass bead production evidence and unusual gemstone assemblage; and the Sungai Pemandi riverine deposit in Johor yielding 17th–18th century Malay domestic artefacts, Qing porcelain, and tin coins of Sultan Abdul Jalil I. Together these findings illustrate the breadth of the period's output, from deep Palaeolithic to early modern historical archaeology.

### Key Findings

- **Kota Tampan**: Palaeolithic workshop dated to approximately 30,000–35,000 years ago; volcanic ash in excavated trenches identified as matching the Toba eruption; disputes Sieveking's (1958) earlier dating (p. 81).
- **Gua Harimau**: Bronze celt and associated stone mould recovered from a burial context; C14 date of circa 5,000 BP; seven human remains in total, one with rich burial goods (p. 81).
- **Gua Gunung Runtuh**: 30-year-old male *Homo sapiens* skeleton dated to 10,000–11,000 years ago, identified as the earliest human remains found in the peninsula; food remains include monkey, tapir, mousedeer, and deer (p. 83).
- **Site 50, Bujang Valley**: Laterite temple of *vimana-mandapa* type (vimana 6.25 m × 6.25 m; mandapa 6.75 m × 6.20 m), dated 12th–13th century AD; Saivite indicators include a *linga* fragment and *kala* head; associated finds include Song/Yuan stoneware and green glass (p. 79).
- **Kuala Kelumpang**: C14 dates on shells range from 200 BC to 10th century AD; 11 human remains with evidence of teeth filing; gemstones identified as beryl, sodalite, moldavite, plasma, jasper, aventurine, quartz cat's eye, and analcine; raw glass and clustered production-failure beads indicate on-site bead working (pp. 87–88).
- **Sungai Pemandi, Kota Tinggi**: Tin coins of Sultan Abdul Jalil I bearing Arabic script "Al-Adil" and "Kali Malik Al-Adil" (1 cm diameter, ~1 g); wooden domestic artefacts including trays, *kukur*, and spinning implements; blue-and-white Qing and Qingbai porcelain (pp. 92–93).

### Conclusion

Taha's definitive historical takeaway is that the 1987–1990 period marked a genuine inflection in Malaysian archaeology. University-led research produced evidence that pushed human occupation of the peninsula back to at least 30,000 years, established an indigenous bronze tradition of considerable antiquity, and simultaneously enriched the historical record of the early modern period through riverine and settlement deposits—collectively demonstrating that the peninsula's archaeological narrative was far richer and more complex than colonial-era scholarship had allowed.

## Context

- The article draws on excavation reports published in *Jurnal Arkeologi Malaysia*, *Purba*, and conference proceedings by Zuraina Majid, Nik Hassan Shuhaimi, and their teams, as well as Taha's own site visits and Museums Department registration records.
- Historiographically, the piece serves as a transitional document recording the shift from colonial-era, museum-driven archaeology (Evans, Sieveking, Quaritch Wales) to a university-based research model that would dominate Malaysian archaeology from the 1990s onward.
