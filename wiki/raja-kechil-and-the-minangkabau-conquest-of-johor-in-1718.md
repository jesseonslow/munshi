# Raja Kechil and the Minangkabau conquest of Johor in 1718

Leonard Y. Andaya's 1972 article in the *JMBRAS* examines the 1718 Minangkabau conquest of Johor, then the most powerful kingdom in the Malay world, arguing that the enigmatic figure of Raja Kechil was most likely an ingenious Minangkabau leader with genuine connections to the Pagar Ruyong court who exploited the deep legitimacy crisis within the Bendahara dynasty to orchestrate the kingdom's collapse. By cross-referencing divergent Malay court chronicles with contemporary Dutch and Portuguese records, Andaya demonstrates that the conquest was less a military victory than a product of internal Johorese disintegration, and that the elaborate origin story preserved in the *Siak Chronicles* was a later mythologization of a more prosaic political maneuver.

## Summary

The article addresses a persistent puzzle in early Malay historiography: the widely divergent portrayals of Raja Kechil and the key actors in the 1718 conquest across Malay sources, set against the relatively consistent but differently focused accounts in European records. Andaya's method is to place each source in its own context of composition and audience. The Malay chronicles—the *Siak Chronicles*, the *Tuhfat al-Nafis*, and the *Sejarah Raja Riau*—all accept the story that Raja Kechil was the son of the murdered Sultan Mahmud Shah (r. 1685–1699) raised at Pagar Ruyong, but they disagree sharply on who betrayed Johor: the *Siak Chronicles* blame the Bendahara, while the *Tuhfat* blames the Laksamana. The European sources, by contrast, omit or treat as peripheral the origin story but provide detailed eyewitness and interview accounts of the actual events. Andaya shows that the Dutch records, which include translated interviews with the refugee Shahbandar and Bendahara of Johor conducted immediately after the conquest, largely verify the *Siak Chronicles* version while revealing nuances that the Malay authors either suppressed or embellished.

The core of Andaya's argument concerns the internal dynamics that made Johor vulnerable. The Bendahara dynasty, which seized the throne through the regicide of Sultan Mahmud Shah in 1699, had never fully consolidated its legitimacy. A bitter power struggle between the Raja Muda Tun Mahmud and the Bendahara Tun Abdullah, compounded by the protracted war with the Buginese from 1715 onward, had fractured the court and alienated key groups including the Orang Laut and the Minangkabau settlers. The court at Pagar Ruyong, particularly the Putri Jamilan (the hereditary Queen Mother who exercised considerable moral and political authority in the Minangkabau world), provided Raja Kechil with the symbolic instruments—a *chap*, the sword Sapurjaba, and formal installation—that legitimized his summons of Minangkabau forces across the east coast of Sumatra. Raja Kechil's claim to be the son of Sultan Mahmud Shah functioned as a psychological weapon that resonated precisely because of the unresolved trauma of the 1699 regicide; it gave disaffected Johorese Orang Kaya a culturally acceptable rationale for their *derhaka* (disloyalty) and for removing the Bendahara dynasty from power.

Andaya further argues that the *Siak Chronicles* transformed what was originally a political claim into a full biographical myth, complete with miraculous birth, supernatural tests, and a formal coronation ceremony at Pagar Ruyong. Tellingly, later Siak rulers did not claim direct descent from the Malaccan dynasty through Raja Kechil but instead linked their prestige to the Minangkabau rulers of Pagar Ruyong, suggesting they recognized his true origins in the Central Sumatran highlands. The conquest thus marked not merely a dynastic change but the emergence of the Minangkabaus as a recognized political force in the Straits of Malacca, operating with the same kind of strategic weight that the Buginese already possessed.

### Key Findings

- The letter of 4 December 1717 from the "raja of the Minangkabaus" at Pagar Ruyong to the Dutch Governor of Malacca requested gunpowder and lead for the "brother's son" sent to avenge the death of Sultan Mahmud Shah, and was suspected of forgery because it contradicted earlier letters from Pagar Ruyong that affirmed unity between Johor and Pagar Ruyong (pp. 51–53).
- The Bendahara's defensive fortress at the narrow point of the Johor river was armed with fourteen cannons of twelve, sixteen, and twenty-four pounders, supported by four well-armed sloops and twenty-four galleys, with 4,000 armed men from the court alone; nevertheless the position was outflanked and abandoned (p. 58).
- The Raja Muda Tun Mahmud fled Johor on 4 March 1718 carrying so much gold that it required thirty men to transport it onto two sloops, after which he and his followers were forced to abandon the vessels and flee inland (p. 58).
- In November 1716, approximately 100 Minangkabau men, women, and children arrived at Jambi reporting that the Sultan of Johor had robbed them of everything; about 300 then departed Johor on seven *balok* bound for Indragiri, Palembang, and Jambi, indicating active persecution of Minangkabau settlers within Johor prior to the invasion (p. 71).
- In his first formal letter to the Dutch on 22 January 1719, Raja Kechil styled himself "Baginda Raja Kechil who rules over Pulau Gontong and acts as Shahbandar for Baginda Sri Sultan of Pagar Ruyong," notably omitting any claim to be the son of Sultan Mahmud Shah (p. 72).
- The *Siak Chronicles* record that at Raja Kechil's coronation the Yamtuan Sakti declared: "nothing else is needed, for the Minangkabau heirloom Si-Buyong [Raja Kechil] is in himself sufficient," and the four chief ministers of Minangkabau (the *Besar Empat Balai*) came to pay homage (pp. 63–64).

### Conclusion

Andaya's definitive historical takeaway is that Raja Kechil was most probably an ingenious Minangkabau leader with genuine, if not royal, connections to the Pagar Ruyong court, who accurately assessed the depths of dissension within the Bendahara dynasty and deployed the claim of being Sultan Mahmud's son as a calculated psychological weapon. The *Siak Chronicles* subsequently elaborated this claim into a full state myth to provide a prestigious genealogical link with the Malaccan line, yet the later Siak rulers' own decision to anchor their legitimacy in Pagar Ruyong rather than in Malacca implicitly acknowledged the truth of Raja Kechil's Minangkabau origins. The conquest of 1718 thus stands as the moment when the Minangkabaus, previously a peripheral cultural presence in the Malay world, became a recognized and feared political force capable of toppling the region's most powerful kingdom.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: Dutch East India Company (VOC) records in the General State Archives (*Algemene Rijksarchief*), The Hague (KA series), particularly the Malacca, Jambi, and Palembang registers for 1717–1721; the Portuguese eyewitness account of Captain Joao Tavares (published by T.D. Hughes, *JMBRAS* XIII, 1935); the *Siak Chronicles* (Cod. Or. 7304, Leiden University Library); and the *Tuhfat al-Nafis* (Raja Ali al-Haji, 1965 ed.).
- Historiographical contribution: This article established a source-critical method for early Malay historiography by systematically cross-referencing Malay court chronicles with contemporary European diplomatic and military records, resolving specific contradictions in the narrative of the 1718 conquest and demonstrating how political context shaped the composition of each source tradition.
