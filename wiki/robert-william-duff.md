# Robert William Duff

Robert William Duff (1860s–1932) was a British colonial administrator turned entrepreneur whose commercial enterprise, the Duff Development Company, dominated the economic landscape of Kelantan from 1900 to 1932. Arriving in British Malaya in 1892, Duff served in the Federated Malay States (F.M.S.) Service before invaliding out in 1900 to establish a vast land concession in the Siamese tributary state of Kelantan. His career has been variously characterised as that of an agent of British imperialism, a shrewd free-trader, or an economic baron who exploited the ambiguities of imperial policy for personal gain [[1, pp. 1–2]](#ref-1). The Duff Company's operations—spanning mining, rubber cultivation, timber extraction, and the opium and liquor trades—shaped Kelantan's economic development for three decades, while its entanglement with British and Siamese diplomatic manoeuvring made it a focal point in the transfer of the Siamese Malay States to British protection in 1909 [[1, pp. 9–10]](#ref-1).

## Career and Formation of the Duff Development Company

Very little is known of Duff's life before 1890, and much of what is recorded concerns his activities in British Malaya from 1892 to 1932 [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). In the F.M.S. Service he held administrative positions in Pahang, Perak and Selangor, serving in the Department of Public Works in Perak and as District Officer of Rawang in Selangor [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). In 1892 he was appointed acting Superintendent of Police in Pahang, and having impressed the British Resident Hugh Clifford, he was appointed Head of Pahang's Armed Police Force from 1894 to 1897 [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). It was in this capacity that he accompanied Clifford on the 1895 expedition into Kelantan, during which local Malays related tales of the state's mineral wealth that evidently captivated Duff [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). In 1896 he applied for the post of British Consul in Brunei but was passed over, and in February 1900 he was appointed District Officer in Ulu Selangor [[1, pp. 3–4]](#ref-1). He was eventually invalided out of the F.M.S. Service and returned to England on 20 April 1900 to establish the Duff Development Company [[1, p. 4]](#ref-1).

The company's origins are traced by Rupert Emerson to the Clifford expedition of 1895 [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1). Shaharil Talib Robert, drawing on Colonial Office and Foreign Office correspondence, reconstructs the Syndicate's internal architecture in greater detail. Duff assembled a consortium of "large commercial houses of high repute connected with the Far East" with an initial registered capital of £10,000 [[2, p. 81]](#ref-2). The original six members included James and Shakespeare (metal brokers), Paterson and Simons (East India merchants), Price, Boustead and Company, Pittar, Levrson and Company (diamond merchants), J.R. Cutbertson, and Wilberforce Bryant of Bryant and May Limited [[2, p. 81]](#ref-2). By 1902 the Syndicate had grown to include T.E. Vickers of Vickers, Maxim and Company (capital £4,250,000), E. Pratt Barlow of John Dickson and Company (£650,000), Charles Ingram of the *Illustrated London News* (£1,060,000), C. Sugden of the Borneo Company (£300,000), A.P. Adams, and Loke Yew [[2, p. 81]](#ref-2).

The Partnership Deed of 10 October 1900 granted Duff exclusive rights over a vast tract of Kelantan's interior for forty years, with an initial payment of $20,000, a 4 per cent royalty on mineral receipts, a 5 per cent export duty, and 200 shares (later increased to 2,000) allocated to the Raja [[2, pp. 17]](#ref-2). By 1905 an agreement was reached and ratified by the Siamese government, granting Duff 3,000 square miles (776,996 hectares) of mining and agricultural land concessions; the Raja in turn received £2,000 and 200 shares in the Duff Development Company [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1).

## Diplomatic Context and the Anglo-Siamese Treaty of 1909

Duff's commercial ambitions were inextricable from the diplomatic contest between Britain and Siam over the Malay States. Kelantan, like Kedah, Perlis and Trengganu, existed in a tributary relationship with Siam, and together these states were perceived as one of the weakest points in Siamese provincial administration [[1, pp. 6–7]](#ref-1). Between 1874 and 1892, the Ministry of the Interior in Bangkok, under Prince Damrong, implemented the *thesaphiban* (superintendent) system of provincial administration with the studied purpose of maintaining strong control over the Siamese Malay States and strengthening the Kingdom's territorial integrity against continued pressure from France and Great Britain [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). Even so, Siamese influence never became very strong, partly due to the lack of capable local administrators and partly due to the lack of finance [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1).

Duff was well versed in this diplomatic landscape. He was aware of Article 12 of the 1826 Treaty of Bangkok, which opened the doors to English traders in "Calantan," and was cognisant of Britain's secret convention of 1897 with Siam, concluded for the strategic purpose of keeping out France and Germany [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). This convention also placed Siamese control over its Malay States in British hands [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1). The Siamese authorities viewed Duff's economic scheme as part of Britain's expansionist policy, and the stage was set for a conflict between Siamese political rule and British economic development [[1, p. 7]](#ref-1).

The critical sticking point in the 1901–1902 negotiations was the Siamese demand for confirmation of all transfers and sub-leases. The Syndicate's directors declared that "English financial men would refuse to consider a business proposition which was based on a title dependent on Siam for confirmation" [[2, p. 13]](#ref-2). The Syndicate threatened to dispose of its rights to a foreign power, specifically Germany, if the British Government would not support its position—a bluff that the Colonial Office took seriously, noting that "the very danger, against which we have been secretly planning, will have come into being" [[2, p. 19]](#ref-2). Duff himself threatened to float his company in Paris or St. Petersburg rather than in London when the Foreign Office offered only weak support, a tactic that embarrassed the Foreign Office and accelerated British diplomatic engagement with Siam [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1). The concession was formally ratified at Bangkok on 10 July 1902 on the basis of a "Simple Ratification": Siam agreed to ratify unconditionally, with an honourable understanding that the Foreign Office would be consulted before any transfer or sub-lease [[2, p. 27]](#ref-2).

The signing of the Anglo-Siamese Treaty of 1909, described as "the most important British transaction in Southeast Asia in the decade before the First World War," exposed the ambitions of both the Colonial Office and Robert Duff [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1). The Colonial Office wanted British control extended over all the Malay Peninsula and was relieved to obtain control over the Siamese Malay States, comparing them to "diseased limbs that must be amputated if the body was to be saved" [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1). For Siam, the loss of these states was "a blessing in disguise," for they would no longer have to worry about a financial loss [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1). Duff, for one, did not lament the exchange of extraterritorial rights in return for the Siamese Malay States, especially Kelantan; the Duff Company had experienced financial loss and hoped that after the 1909 transfer these difficulties would be resolved [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1).

## Economic Activities and the Conflict with Colonial Administration

By 1900 Kelantan no longer had large mineral deposits compared to Perak and Selangor on the West Coast [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1). Although the state had the reputation of being an "El Dorado," where Chinese miners from Pulai in Ulu Kelantan made themselves wealthy in the 1850s, little gold and tin deposits were left by 1900, and the Duff Company's endeavour to mine these two minerals has been described as the "Invincible Error" [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1). Some gold was mined on the Galas, Neggiri and Lebir rivers. In 1904 the Kelantan Gold Dredging Company, a subsidiary of the Duff Company, was formed, but the mining returns by 1906 were poor and by 1910 the picture was disappointing [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1). Prospecting for tin was equally disappointing, and the company eventually abandoned the mining of gold and tin because of unsatisfactory revenue and high transportation costs [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1).

With unsatisfactory revenue from the mining industry, the company turned in 1906 to leasing land for coconut and rubber cultivation in Ulu Kelantan [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1). By 1908 three areas in the Duff Concession were leased to other rubber companies, and by 1910 Kelantan had a total of 23 rubber estates covering 64,290 acres (26,017 hectares), the largest of which—Taku, Kluat and Kenneth, totalling 1,254 acres (507 hectares)—belonged to the Duff Company [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1). Three years later the company had 13,000 acres (5,260 hectares) of rubber trees under cultivation, and by 1928 it had almost doubled the acreage to 24,880 acres (10,068 hectares) [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1). The company also pursued an ambitious scheme to cultivate coffee, coconuts, rice, sugar and tapioca, and gave serious consideration to the extraction of resin [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1).

The timber industry and the trade in opium and alcohol proved more lucrative than mining. The company established a sawmill in 1907 and exported timber to Singapore [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1). Loke Yew, who was "probably the wealthiest and most influential Chinese in the Straits Settlements at that time," was interested in the extraction and export of timber, rattans, and other jungle produce from Kelantan, and was also involved in the opium, tobacco and liquor trades [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1). He purchased the franchise for these three products and bought shares in the Duff Company [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1). The Kelantan government, though critical of the Duff Company's running of gambling and opium farms, preferred these "un-Islamic" activities to be conducted by an established company rather than have to combat smuggling [[1, p. 12]](#ref-1).

To enhance trade in the agricultural and mining industries, the Duff Company was involved in improving rail and river communications within its concessions, operating river steamers between Tumpat and Kota Bharu and between Kota Bharu and Batu Mengkabang, and owning barges, motorboats and one tug [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1). Plans were made to build a railway line, but this project clashed with the Kelantan government's scheme to improve rail and road communications. The government abandoned its plans to build a railway line because it considered such a line unnecessary to "the welfare of the State," a decision made under the Graham administration [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1). Duff viewed the failure of the government to build the railway line from his concessions to Kota Bharu as a breach of contract, and it was the railroad issue which became the main point of contention in the arbitration proceedings from 1916 to 1920 [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1).

The conflict between Duff and the colonial administration centred on the figure of W.A. Graham, the Siamese-appointed adviser to Kelantan from 1903 to 1909. Graham's initial objectives were to restore law and order, build up Kelantan's administrative structures, strengthen the position of the Sultan, improve the state's financial position, and alleviate the land tenure problem [[1, p. 10]](#ref-1). By 1904, within one year of Graham's administration, the *Straits Times* admitted that administrative improvements had taken place [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1). However, Graham was unable to implement a broad state land policy because of opposition from the State Council and the Duff Company [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1). By 1907 Graham was "completely at logger heads" with Duff: the adviser demanded more revenue from the Duff concessions and a limitation of their privileges, and treated Duff more harshly than if there had been no English adviser [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1). Graham was so much a partisan of Kelantan's interests that Siam thoroughly supported the British adviser, whose advent it had arduously resisted [[1, p. 11]](#ref-1).

After the 1909 treaty, the Kelantan government reminded Duff that he was just another entrepreneur in the state. The following year the government tried to get the Duff Company to surrender most of its concessions but failed to do so; both parties did agree to jointly lease 56,400 acres (22,824 hectares) of land [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1). The government eventually succeeded in 1912 by getting the Duff Company to sign a Deed of Cancellation, thus nullifying the 1905 Agreement [[1, p. 13]](#ref-1). Duff viewed the Deed as "an unfortunate [one], full of ambiguities, and it invites trouble" [[1, p. 14]](#ref-1). He agreed to surrender the lease over 50,000 acres (20,234 hectares) of agricultural land for a cash payment of £300,000 and a loan of £3 million to be paid to the company in instalments [[1, p. 14]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The historiography of Robert Duff and the Duff Development Company has evolved through several distinct phases, each reflecting shifting interpretive frameworks. The earliest substantial treatment is Shaharil Talib Robert's 1972 article, "The Duff Syndicate in Kelantan, 1900–1902," which drew extensively on Colonial Office and Foreign Office correspondence to reconstruct the Syndicate's formation and its negotiations with the Siamese government [[2]](#ref-2). Robert argued that the Syndicate functioned as a dual instrument—simultaneously advancing British imperial influence in the Northern Malay States and pursuing the commercial interests of a consortium of major London and Singapore houses—and that its negotiations exposed the fundamental contradictions in British policy toward Siam's suzerainty over Kelantan [[2]](#ref-2). The article contributed to the understanding of the 1899–1902 transition in British-Siamese relations by demonstrating how a private commercial enterprise interacted with, and was shaped by, the competing imperatives of the Colonial Office and Foreign Office during the period between the 1897 Secret Convention and the 1902 treaty [[2]](#ref-2).

Earlier, in 1970, Hunter A. Crawford published "The Birth of the Duff Development Company of Kelantan 1900–1912" in *Malaysia in History*, providing a chronological account of the company's early years [[1, p. 4]](#ref-1). Ira Klein's 1969 article, "Britain, Siam and the Malay Peninsula, 1906–1909," in *The Historical Journal*, introduced the analytical distinction between "High" and "Liberal" Imperialism that would prove essential to subsequent interpretations of Duff's role [[1, p. 1]](#ref-1). Klein's work, together with John Gallagher and Ronald Robinson's 1953 essay "The Imperialism of Free Trade" and Oliver MacDonagh's 1962 counter-argument, "The Anti-Imperialism of Free Trade," provided the theoretical framework within which Duff's career could be assessed [[1, p. 1]](#ref-1).

Ernest Levos's 1997 article, "Robert W. Duff: a British seigneur in Kelantan, 1892–1932," represents the most comprehensive treatment to date, drawing heavily on Colonial Office records (C.O. 273/275, C.O. 717/8, C.O. 717/10) and the Clifford Papers, supplemented by contemporary press coverage in the *Straits Times* and earlier publications in the Society's journal [[1]](#ref-1). Levos's central argument is that Duff was not an active agent of British imperialism but an entrepreneur in the liberal free-trade tradition who took advantage of imperialistic policies to enhance his economic positions [[1, pp. 1]](#ref-1). He contends that Duff's view of imperialism was similar to that of his mentor Hugh Clifford, who subscribed to the Liberal school that favoured loosening imperialistic ties and operating on established commitments and treaties, as opposed to the High Imperialism of Frank Swettenham and W.A. Graham, which advocated the use of "tactics of extreme intimidation to achieve expansion" and the appointment of British Residents who would exert economic control [[1, pp. 4–5]](#ref-1).

The debate over Duff's character has been shaped by the available evidence. K.G. Tregonning viewed him as a shrewd and adventurous businessman who, with the power he hoped to exercise over the land concessions he received from the Raja of Kelantan in 1900, could have become another White Rajah, akin to the Brookes who ruled Sarawak [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1). Shaharil Talib Robert portrayed the company as troublesome and retarding the economic progress of Kelantan, but also as a modernising agent [[1, p. 3]](#ref-1). The 1917 speech by the chairman of the Duff Development Company at its general meeting, giving both Duff and the company credit for having added Kelantan to the British Empire, has been one of the principal pieces of evidence used to portray him as an imperialist agent [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1). Rupert Emerson, however, noted that "Duff's claim of having brought Kelantan into the Empire can scarcely be taken too seriously since it seems reasonable to assume that that step would have been taken sooner or later at all events" [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1).

The final arbitration proceedings, which concluded in 1925 with an award of £378,000 and costs to the Duff Company for the Kelantan government's breach of contract over the railway, and the subsequent financial arrangements—including the write-off of a £300,000 F.M.S. loan and a further £4,125,000 interest-free loan to Kelantan—proved costly for the state [[1, p. 15]](#ref-1). The High Commissioner of the F.M.S., Sir Lawrence Guillemard, received the brunt of the criticism for his "short-sightedness, especially over the Deed of Cancellation of 1912, and for financially involving the other Malayan Governments" [[1, p. 15]](#ref-1). By 1925 the Duff Company had received a new Deed, but the final agreement was not signed until 1930 [[1, p. 15]](#ref-1). The company fell victim to the Great Depression, going into liquidation in 1932, but it reorganised the same year and operated under the same name as in 1905 [[1, p. 15]](#ref-1).
