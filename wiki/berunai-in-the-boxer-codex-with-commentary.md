# Berunai in the Boxer Codex: with commentary

Sir William E. Maxwell, with romanization by R. O. Winstedt, published this verbatim transcription of an oral Malay romance in 1910 (JMBRAS No. 55), presenting a Perak village storyteller's adaptation of the Ramayana as performed in the late nineteenth century. The work documents the living oral tradition of Malay *penglipur lara* (storytellers) and demonstrates how the great Hindu epics were absorbed, transformed, and localized in the Malay cultural sphere.

## Summary

The text records the performance of Mir Hassan, a native of Kampar in south-eastern Perak, who was brought to Singapore in 1886 at Maxwell's request to have his legendary lore committed to writing. Maxwell, then Assistant Resident in Perak, had witnessed Mir Hassan's storytelling talents and sought to preserve them before the diffusion of printed books eroded the oral tradition. The narrative was taken down verbatim by native writers, with Maxwell obtaining explanations of obscure passages from the storyteller himself. The resulting text is a full Malay-language romance with interspersed metrical passages in a rhythm Maxwell notes is "not unlike specimens of Dayak blank-verse" (p. 2).

The story follows the Ramayana's broad outline but is thoroughly Malay in its cultural texture. Seri Rama (Rama) of Tanjong Bunga, childless after three years of marriage, consults his elder brother Raja Laksamana, who performs a divination ritual identical to that of a contemporary Malay *pawang* (p. 4). The couple's sea voyage, the transformation into monkeys at a green lake, the birth of a monkey son (Kera Kechil Imam Tergangga, a corruption of Hanuman), his banishment, his adventures with the sun and the Princess Renek Jintan, the abduction of the Queen by Maharaja Duwana (Ravana), and the eventual battle and reconciliation all follow the epic's skeleton. Yet the narrative is saturated with Islamic references—Koran readings, Muslim greetings exchanged with the sun, the presence of *Lebais*, *Hajis*, *Imams*, and *Khatibs*—and with specifically Malay material culture: the *keris* with its named damask patterns, the *tengkolok* (turban) with its love-compelling secrets, the *langkah* (ritual gait) for safe travel, and the *sirih* (betel) ceremony (pp. 6–7, 33–34).

Maxwell's preface frames the text as evidence of the "profound influence which the Ramayana and Mahabharata have had in the Farther East" (p. 2), noting that no village stage in Siam, Malaya, or Java is unconnected to these sources. He highlights the value of an oral version over a written one compiled by a scribe who "may have knowingly borrowed from foreign sources" (p. 2), arguing that the village singer's version preserves points of interest absent from literary adaptations.

### Key Findings

- The text was recorded verbatim from Mir Hassan of Kampar, Perak, in Singapore in June 1886, with Maxwell present to obtain explanations of obscure passages (p. 2).
- The narrator's version contains a significant corruption: Hanuman is rendered as "Shah Numan," with Maxwell noting the Perak storyteller "has blundered over the first syllable and has supplied the word 'Shah' as one having a specific meaning" (p. 12).
- The divination scene performed by Raja Laksamana—using a candle a cubit long, parched rice, yellow rice, sacred water, and eight cubits of white cloth, with the practitioner remaining in abstracted contemplation from sunset to daybreak—matches exactly the procedure of a contemporary Malay *pawang* (pp. 4–5).
- The monkey son's appetite is quantified: he consumes forty-four baskets of tender shoots in a single night, prompting Shah Numan to expel him from his kingdom (p. 54).
- In the final battle at Tanjong Bunga, Maharaja Duwana's forces are reduced from seven thousand men to seven hundred, and a single well-directed shot from a wall-piece kills Raja Laksamana, who is subsequently revived by a remedy fetched from Mount Inggil-beringgil (p. 21).
- The transformation scene: the monkey-skin, when burned, produces smoke that turns into white cloth and ashes that are found to be gold (p. 23).

### Conclusion

Maxwell presents this text as a specimen of genuine Malay legendary romance in its oral form, arguing that the village singer's version, with its anachronisms, local corruptions, and Islamic inflections, offers a more authentic window into the cultural reception of the Ramayana than any literary compilation. The definitive takeaway is that the Ramayana's transmission in the Malay world was not a matter of textual importation but of living performance, in which the epic was continuously reworked to reflect local religious, social, and aesthetic values.

## Context

- The text was recorded in 1886 and published in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, No. 55 (1909–1910); Maxwell's preface and Winstedt's romanization constitute the scholarly apparatus.
- The work contributes to the historiography of Malay oral literature by preserving a complete performance text with the narrator's own dialectal and metrical features intact, rather than a normalized literary version.
