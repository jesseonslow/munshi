# The power behind the throne: the role of queens and court ladies in Malay history

R. O. Winstedt's 1917 article traces the chronological diffusion of Islam across the Malay Peninsula and Archipelago from the earliest Arab trading contacts at Kedah in the tenth century through the establishment of Muslim polities in Java, Borneo, the Celebes, and the Moluccas by the seventeenth century. Writing as a senior colonial administrator and scholar, Winstedt synthesizes Malay chronicles, Chinese records, Portuguese accounts, and archaeological evidence to construct a unified narrative of religious conversion driven primarily by trade, dynastic marriage, and missionary activity.

## Summary

Winstedt's central argument is that Islam spread through the archipelago not as a single wave of conquest or mass conversion but as a gradual, multi-vector process in which commercial contact, royal intermarriage, and the arrival of Arab and Indian missionaries operated in concert over several centuries. He traces a clear succession of trading centres—Kedah, then Pasai, then Malacca, then Acheen—each of which served as a node from which Muslim influence radiated to neighbouring polities. The article is structured geographically and chronologically, moving from the peninsula to Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and the eastern islands.

The primary sources underpinning the narrative are the *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai*, the *Kedah Annals* (*Hikayat Marong Mahawangsa*), Chinese diplomatic records, Marco Polo's travel account, and Albuquerque's *Commentaries*. Winstedt supplements these textual sources with archaeological evidence, particularly the inscribed tomb-stones at Pasai, Malacca, and Pangkalan Kempas, which he uses to anchor dates and to demonstrate the custom of importing ornamental stone from India. He also draws on Malay folk-tales and literary works such as the *Hikayat Awang Sulong* to illustrate the cultural penetration of foreign goods and ideas into Malay society.

A notable feature of the article is its attention to the human dimension of conversion. Winstedt reproduces two extended anecdotes from the *Sejarah Melayu*—the confrontation between the drunk courtier Sri Rama and the Arab missionary Sadar Jahan, and the pronunciation dispute between Tun Mai and the same teacher—to illustrate the friction and humour that accompanied the introduction of a foreign religious tradition. These passages serve to humanize the process and to show that conversion was neither instantaneous nor unresisted.

### Key Findings

- Arab traders reached Kedah by 915 A.D., and by 1292 A.D. Marco Polo found established Muslim communities at Perlak, while the rulers of Samudra and Pasai were still non-Muslim (pp. 2–3).
- Sultan Muhammad Shah, the first Muslim ruler of Malacca, ascended the throne in 1403 A.D.; Chinese records confirm that the Malays of Malacca had become Muslim by 1409 A.D. (p. 3).
- The tomb-stone of Sultan Mansur Shah (d. 1475 A.D.), bearing an Arabic inscription dated 880 A.H., is carved in a style Winstedt attributes to Indian rather than local craftsmanship, and now lies in two fragments outside the Malacca Residency (p. 4).
- The tomb-stone of Shaikh Ahmad Makhtar Ramali at Pangkalan Kempas is dated A.H. 872 (1467 A.D.) and records its erection "in the reign of Sultan Shah Mansur"; the ornate "Sword" element bears "Allah" in high relief, indicating it was a genuine Muslim relic rather than an adapted Hindu stone (p. 4).
- The fall of the Hindu kingdom of Majapahit in 1478 A.D. released its dependencies—Palembang, Jambi, and Indragiri—allowing Islam to gain ground in those regions (p. 5).
- The *Kedah Annals* record that the king of Acheen and Shaikh Nuru'd-din sent copies of the *Babu'l-nikah* and *Siratu'l-mustakim* to the raja of Kedah on the occasion of his country's conversion to Islam, a date given as 1474 A.D. in the Achinese account (p. 5).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that the Islamization of the Malay world was a protracted, geographically layered process in which the succession of great trading ports provided the infrastructure for religious diffusion, and in which dynastic conversion at the court level—often precipitated by marriage to a Muslim princess or by the influence of a resident missionary—set in motion the broader adoption of the faith across a polity. The article closes by pointing to the genealogies of ruling families in Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and Mindanao as the key to further refining the chronology of this process.

## Context

- Winstedt was a senior British colonial administrator in the Straits Settlements and a prolific contributor to the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*; this article reflects the scholarly interests of the colonial administrative class in the early twentieth century.
- The article draws on the manuscript collections of the Royal Asiatic Society and on Dutch archaeological publications (notably Magnette's 1912 study of Pasai and Grisek tomb-stones), situating it within the collaborative Anglo-Dutch scholarly tradition of the period.
