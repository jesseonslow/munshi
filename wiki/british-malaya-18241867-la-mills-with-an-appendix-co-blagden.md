# British Malaya, 1824–1867. L.A. Mills. With an appendix C.O. Blagden

Geoff Wade's 2005 article, published in *JMBRAS* Vol. 78, Part 1 (pp. 37–58), offers a revisionist reassessment of the early fifteenth-century maritime expeditions commanded by the Ming eunuch Zheng He. Wade argues that these missions, conventionally celebrated in Chinese popular memory as peaceful "voyages of friendship," were in fact aggressive instruments of the Yong-le emperor's (r. 1403–24) three-pronged southern expansion—alongside the land-based invasions of Yunnan and Đại Việt—and should be understood as a form of maritime proto-colonialism aimed at establishing a *pax Ming* across the Indian Ocean world.

## Summary

Wade situates the Zheng He voyages within the broader strategic context of Yong-le's imperial project, which pursued simultaneous expansion into the Tai polities of Yunnan, the conquest and occupation of Đại Việt (1406–1428), and the dispatch of eunuch-led armadas to the Western Ocean. The article's central argument is that the maritime missions shared the same logic as the land-based campaigns: the use of military force or its threat to compel submission, extract tribute, and secure economic advantage. The orthodox Chinese narrative—embodied in statements by PRC officials, academics, and the International Zheng He Association—is systematically challenged by evidence of military violence, the capture and deportation of foreign rulers, and the establishment of garrison depots at strategic nodes.

The article draws heavily on the *Ming shi-lu* (reign annals) and the *Wu-bei-zhi* (military treatise) to document specific acts of coercion: the attack on Old Port in Sumatra (1407), the killing of 170 Chinese troops in Java and the subsequent demand for 60,000 *liang* of gold in compensation, the threat against Ava-Burma (1409), the invasion of Sri Lanka and capture of its king (1411), and the capture of Su-gan-la of Samudera (1415). Wade also incorporates later European sources (Joseph of Cranganore, De Barros) and Chinese literary texts (Luo Mao-deng's 1597 novel) to corroborate the violent character of these encounters. The establishment of military-cum-treasury depots at Melaka and Samudera is presented as evidence of a deliberate strategy to control the Straits of Malacca and the broader East–West maritime trade network.

In the final section, Wade engages with the historiography of colonialism, drawing on definitions by Armitage, Osterhammel, Emerson, and others to justify the label "maritime proto-colonialism" for the Zheng He voyages. He distinguishes this from the full territorial colonialism applied to Đại Việt and the Tai polities of Yunnan, arguing that the maritime missions controlled nodes and networks rather than territory per se. The comparison with the later Portuguese maritime empire—described by Pearson as a "protection racket"—is used to underscore the structural parallels.

### Key Findings

- The Yong-le emperor's southern expansion comprised three simultaneous prongs: the colonization of Yunnan's Tai polities, the invasion of Đại Việt (800,000 troops mobilized per the *Ming shi-lu*, though Whitmore estimates 215,000 as more likely), and the eunuch-led maritime missions (pp. 39–45).
- Shipbuilding for the maritime missions was massive: 137 ocean-going ships ordered from Fujian in 1403, approximately 400 additional ships from various military units the same year, 1,180 ships ordered from Zhejiang in 1405, and 48 "treasure-ships" (*bao-chuan*) assigned to the Ministry of Works by 1408 (p. 45).
- Individual missions comprised between 50 and 250 ships with personnel figures of 27,000–30,000; in one documented case, 26,800 of 27,400 aboard were rank-and-file military men, sailors, and clerks (p. 45).
- Military garrison depots (*guan-chang*) were established at Melaka and at the northern end of the Straits of Malacca near Samudera, visible on *Wu-bei-zhi* charts (p. 47).
- The attack on Old Port in 1407 resulted in 5,000 persons killed, 10 ships burnt, and 7 captured; the polity was subsequently recognized as a "pacification superintendency" with a likely Ming-appointed ruler, and references to it end in 1430 (p. 47).
- A 1445 reference in the *Ming shi-lu* to the capture of three Javanese traders en route to Siam suggests that Ming policing of regional maritime trade continued for at least half a century after the formal end of the Zheng He voyages (p. 52).

### Conclusion

Wade's definitive takeaway is that the Zheng He voyages were not benign diplomatic or commercial enterprises but a form of maritime proto-colonialism in which a dominant power used military coercion to control strategic port-nodes and trade networks, extracting political submission and economic tribute. The failure of this proto-colonialism to develop into the full territorial colonialism later pursued by European powers is attributed to the death of the Yong-le emperor, the enormous expense of the missions, and sustained opposition from civil ministers. Wade closes by arguing that the study of colonialism in Southeast Asia must extend its temporal boundaries backward to include the actions of successive Chinese polities, not only those of European powers from the sixteenth century onward.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the *Ming shi-lu* (Academia Sinica photolithographic edition, 1963–1968), the *Wu-bei-zhi*, Ma Huan's *Ying-yai sheng-lan* (Mills translation, 1970), and a range of secondary scholarship on colonialism theory (Armitage, Osterhammel, Emerson, Ferro, Doyle).
- Historiographically, the article intervenes in the debate over whether Chinese state expansion in the early Ming can legitimately be termed "colonial," and it extends the temporal scope of Southeast Asian colonial history beyond the European period.
