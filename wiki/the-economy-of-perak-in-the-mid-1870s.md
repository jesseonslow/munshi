# The economy of Perak in the mid-1870s

John Gullick's 2010 article reconstructs the economic life of Perak during the turbulent mid-1870s—a brief window between the Larut fighting of 1872 and the consolidation of colonial rule under Hugh Low—arguing that this period represents the final phase of an autonomous, decentralized economy before British administrative control fundamentally restructured the state.

## Summary

The article addresses a historiographical gap: the mid-1870s in Perak have been overshadowed by the dramatic political events of the Larut wars and the "Perak War" of 1875–6, yet this was a period when ordinary economic activity continued across the state. Gullick draws on the reports and journals of British administrators—Birch, Speedy, Swettenham, McNair, Maxwell, and Low—who were exploring a territory they did not yet control, to piece together a picture of how tin mining, agriculture, trade, and local governance functioned in a state without a recognized capital or unified authority. He is candid that these are "colonial records history" but insists they provide factual information about the local economy that is otherwise unavailable (p. 27).

The core argument is that Perak's economy was fundamentally decentralized: an aggregate of local economies linked by the Perak River and coastal trade routes to Penang, rather than an integrated system. Tin mining, increasingly dominated by Penang Chinese financiers who imported their own labour, was the engine driving the economy, but the value of exports was dispersed through a complex web of royalties, taxes, and local trade. The political fragmentation—rival Sultans, semi-independent chiefs, and the absence of effective central authority—meant that economic activity was shaped as much by local power dynamics as by market forces. The multiplicity of arbitrary imposts on river traffic, for instance, could bring trade to a halt and deny essential supplies to local populations (p. 33).

Gullick then surveys the districts of Perak in turn, from the Siamese-controlled headwaters at Intan through the Perak River valley to the coastal settlements, illustrating how political instability, the absence of unified authority, and the particular character of each locality shaped economic life. The article also examines the material culture of the period—wooden construction, elephant transport, mining techniques—and the social dynamics between Malay and Chinese communities in mixed mining ventures, where conflict of interest was a recurring source of instability (p. 32).

### Key Findings

- In 1876, Larut produced 30,576 piculs of tin out of a state total of 37,924 (80 per cent); the labour force had recovered from 4,000 in early 1874 to support 120 working mines by year's end (p. 27, n. 1).
- Rice imports in 1876 were valued at $180,621, more than a fifth of total Perak imports, indicating the state's net food deficit (p. 34).
- By 1879, annual "political allowances" to the Perak aristocracy totalled $54,456, estimated to equal the traditional revenues of the entire Perak River valley in pre-colonial times (p. 33).
- The tax rate on tin at Larut in 1874 was 27 per cent, compared with only 7 per cent in Lower Perak (p. 32).
- Taiping's population fell from 26,000 in 1874 to 9,000 in 1877 following the tin price slump (p. 43).
- Elephant freight rates: $2 to carry 100 gantang of rice between the Perak River and Gopeng; $2½ for a load of four piculs of tin (p. 40).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive takeaway is that the mid-1870s represented the last moment of Perak's autonomous economic order. The imposition of British control—through the Pangkor Engagement and the centralized collection of taxes—paradoxically unified the Perak economy by creating a single fiscal authority and improving communications, but at the cost of the decentralized, locally-driven system that had characterized the state before colonial intervention (p. 43).

## Context

- Primary sources: The article draws heavily on the published journals and reports of British administrators (Birch 1976; Swettenham 1975; Low 1954; Maxwell 1882; McNair 1978; Speedy 1875), supplemented by secondary works on Perak's tin industry (Wong 1965), political history (Khoo 1972; Parkinson 1960), and economic history (Lim 1977; Hill 1977).
- Historiographical contribution: The article fills a gap in the economic history of Perak by focusing on the transitional period between the Larut wars and the consolidation of colonial rule, a phase that has been neglected in favour of either the pre-colonial period or the post-1877 colonial administration.
