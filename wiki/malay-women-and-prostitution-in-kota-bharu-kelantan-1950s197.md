# Malay women and prostitution in Kota Bharu, Kelantan, 1950s–1970s

Haryati Hasan, in this 2005 article for the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, examines the social history of Malay women's involvement in prostitution in Kota Bharu, Kelantan, during the 1950s–1970s. Her central thesis is that the persistence of prostitution in this conservative northern Malay community is rooted not in moral failure but in structural socio-economic conditions—divorce, poverty, and lack of education—that are themselves products of Malay community values and attitudes towards women (p. 97).

## Summary

Hasan situates her study within a long history of prostitution in the Malay world, tracing its origins from pre-colonial slave-based systems through the colonial commercialization that created rural poverty and indebtedness in the Unfederated States. She draws on recently declassified police reports, Social Welfare Department case files, and records of the Council of Religious Affairs in Kelantan, supplemented by oral interviews conducted among former and practising prostitutes in Kota Bharu between 2001 and 2003. This combination of official documentation and first-hand testimony allows her to reconstruct the lived experiences of individual women while also mapping the broader institutional landscape of the trade (pp. 97–98).

The article's core analytical move is to shift the explanatory framework away from moral or religious condemnation toward socio-economic determinism. Hasan examines the backgrounds of individual prostitutes—their age, place of origin, educational attainment, and marriage history—to demonstrate that prostitution functioned as a rational economic choice for women who had been denied education, married young, divorced without financial support, and left with no viable employment alternatives. She shows how the gendered double standard in Malay society meant that men who patronised prostitutes faced little consequence, while women were criminalised, condemned by religious authorities, and ostracised by their families (pp. 97–98, 104–105).

A significant portion of the analysis is devoted to the structural conditions that made prostitution a recurring feature of Kelantanese life. The exceptionally high divorce rate in the state, the seasonal and long-term migration of men to other states for agricultural and industrial work (leaving wives without adequate financial support), the limited educational opportunities available to rural girls, and the absence of suitable formal employment for uneducated women collectively created a pipeline into the trade. Hasan also documents the transnational dimensions of prostitution in Kota Bharu, including the presence of Thai women brought in from the southern Thai border towns and Chinese women from Penang and Singapore, as well as the role of trafficking syndicates that exploited vulnerable women (pp. 102–104, 119–120).

### Key Findings

- A confidential police report dated 4 December 1968 identified 83 premises in Kota Bharu as brothels, comprising 3 hotels, 2 boathouses, 11 temporary dwellings, 9 permanent homes, 7 trafficking centres, and 51 makeshift structures (p. 100).
- A police report of 2 October 1965 estimated approximately 113 prostitutes in Kota Bharu town, of whom about 70 had their own homes where clients visited them (p. 101).
- Muslim divorce rates in Kelantan averaged more than 50 per cent from 1948 to 1970, with the rate peaking at 93.1 per cent in 1948 (11,625 divorces out of 12,488 marriages) and remaining above 50 per cent through 1970 (pp. 110–111).
- Between 1957 and 1970, approximately 55,700 Kelantanese migrated to other states for work; in 1967 alone, 25,000 migrated to Kedah for paddy harvesting, and between 10,000 and 15,000 had migrated to southern Thailand as rubber tappers (p. 120).
- The 1956 State Select Committee on Moral Discipline Report found that many women trapped in prostitution were between 15 and 25 years old, with a recorded minimum age of 15 (p. 103).
- The Badan Pencegah Maksiat of Kelantan arrested 339 prostitutes in 1972, and a further 113 were arrested between January and May 1973 (p. 102, n. 13).

### Conclusion

Hasan concludes that divorce and economic necessity were the major factors for the prevalence of prostitution in Kelantan, and that the practice's ability to survive, adapt, and disguise itself over time reflects the Malay community's own values and attitudes towards women rather than any external moral corruption. Legislation, religious counselling, and police enforcement have all failed to check the trade because they address symptoms rather than the structural conditions—gender inequality in education, the economic vulnerability of divorced women, and the absence of alternative livelihoods—that produce it (pp. 97, 120).

## Context

- The study draws primarily on police records from the Kelantan Contingent and Kuala Krai District (file series PR 35/41 and SR 35/41), Social Welfare Department case files from Machang and Ulu Kelantan, and the 1956 report of the State Select Committee on Moral Discipline held by the Kelantan Attorney-General's Office.
- Historiographically, the article represents one of the first systematic social history studies of prostitution in Kelantan, applying gender history methodology to a topic previously unexamined in the Malaysian context and contributing to the broader field of Malaysian social history and gender studies (p. 97).
