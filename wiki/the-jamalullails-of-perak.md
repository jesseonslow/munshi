# The Jamalullails of Perak

Syed Jaafar Aznan, a retired Malaysian civil servant and PhD holder from Brunel University, published this article in 2020 in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 93, No. 318). Set against the history of the Perak sultanate from its founding in 1528 to the present, the article presents the Jamalullail family's own revised account of their origins, genealogy, and political role, correcting significant errors in the colonial-era scholarship of R. O. Winstedt.

## Summary

The Jamalullails of Perak trace their descent from Syed Husain Al Faradz, a Hadrami religious scholar of the Alawiyin clan who settled in Perak in the early sixteenth century and was appointed Menteri in the court of Sultan Muzaffar Shah I (1528–49). Aznan's central intervention is genealogical: he argues, drawing on the *Shams al-Zahirah* and family oral tradition, that Winstedt's 1918 account misidentified both the number of generations (23 rather than 25) and the specific lineage path (through Abdur-rahman Sakaf rather than Ahmad Ba Hasan) connecting Syed Husain Al Faradz to Muhammad Rasulullah (pp. 121–122). The corrected genealogy places Syed Husain Al Faradz as a great-grandson of Muhammad Jamalullail (d. 1441, Tarim), the eponymous founder of the Jamalullail clan worldwide.

Aznan also challenges Winstedt's dating of the family's arrival. Winstedt had estimated that Syed Husain Al Faradz "flourished in the first half of the XVIIth century," but Aznan marshals multiple lines of evidence—the *Hikayat Misa Melayu* (1784), the appointment of a sixth-generation descendant as Bendahara in 1750, and the oral tradition that Syed Husain Al Faradz was already present when Raja Muzaffar became sultan in 1528—to place the arrival firmly in the early sixteenth century (pp. 125–126). This earlier dating, Aznan argues, also explains the family's rapid assimilation into Malay society and their acceptance as Menteri despite their foreign origin.

The article then traces the family's political trajectory across five centuries. The Jamalullails held three of the four major court positions—Bendahara, Orang Kaya Besar, and Orang Kaya Menteri—continuously from the founding of the sultanate until 1862, when the Menteri position lapsed after the death of Syed Usman bin Syed Ajmadin (p. 128). The "Ninety-Nine Laws of Perak," a significant legal contribution to the state, were in fact the personal laws of the Jamalullail family, applied through their advisory role as Menteri (p. 130, n. 31).

### Key Findings

- Syed Husain Al Faradz was a 25th (not 23rd) descendant of Muhammad Rasulullah, and his lineage ran through Ahmad Ba Hasan bin Abdullah bin Muhammad Jamalullail, not through Abdur-rahman Sakaf as Winstedt recorded (pp. 121–122).
- The family's arrival in Perak is dated to the early sixteenth century (c. 1528), not the first half of the seventeenth century as Winstedt estimated, based on the *Hikayat Misa Melayu*, generational arithmetic, and oral tradition (pp. 125–126).
- The Jamalullails held three of the four highest court positions in Perak continuously from 1528 until 1862, a span of over three centuries (p. 128).
- The "Ninety-Nine Laws of Perak" were essentially the personal laws of the Jamalullail family, applied through their role as Menteri advising the sultan on legal matters (p. 130, n. 31).
- British colonial researchers lacked access to Arabic genealogical sources such as the *Shams al-Zahirah* (c. 1911) and relied on unverified local information, making their genealogical accounts subject to critical revision (pp. 120–121).
- The family has reached its 17th generation and is probably the largest 'Syed' family in Malaysia today, with descendants concentrated around Teja, Chegar Galah, Parit, Kuala Kangsar, Sungai Siput, and Chenderiang (pp. 127, 129).

### Conclusion

Aznan's definitive takeaway is that the Perak Jamalullails were among the earliest Alawiyin to settle in the Malay Peninsula and have been continuous participants in Perak's political and legal life since the sultanate's founding. Their contribution—spanning state administration, Islamic scholarship, and the codification of the Ninety-Nine Laws—was foundational to the development of Perak as a sovereign entity, and their history, as told by the family itself, corrects and deepens the colonial-era record in ways that matter for understanding the interplay of Hadrami diaspora, Malay statecraft, and Islamic law in early modern Southeast Asia.

## Context

- The article engages directly with Winstedt's 1918 article "The Hadramaut Saiyids of Perak and Siak" and Wilkinson's 1908 work, both of which remain standard references in MBRAS collections on Perak's court history.
- The *Shams al-Zahirah* by Abd-al-Rahman al-Mashhur (c. 1911), the principal Arabic genealogical source for the *ahl al-bait*, was not available in Southeast Asia until well after the colonial period, explaining the gaps in earlier British scholarship.
