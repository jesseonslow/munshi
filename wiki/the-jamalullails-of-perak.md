# The Jamalullails of Perak

## Abstract

For well over a century, close personal ties underlay relations between the rulers of Kedah and Siam. In the latter part of the nineteenth century, Kedah emerged as Siam’s most favoured Malay tributary, and the state’s rulers pursued parallel agendas of socio-economic and administrative modernization. That special tie ended abruptly in 1909 when Siam signed a treaty ceding the Kedah to Great Britain, but Tunku Badlishah, a key figure in the Kedah administration, was a naturalized citizen of Siam and had close personal ties with King Vajiravudh. After the latter’s death in 1925, the relationship faded away, but Badlishah’s Thai background continued to influence his career in various ways even after he became Sultan in 1943. His reign coincided with Malaya’s push for independence, and he became a key figure in pressing for a continuing leadership role for Malaya’s sultans and a retention of traditional authority.

## Summary

This article traces the life of Tunku Badlishah (r. 1943–58), the Sultan of Kedah whose unique biography embodies the long history of personalized relations between the Kedah ruling house and the Siamese court. Suwannathat-Pian situates Badlishah within a broader narrative of Kedah-Siam ties that began when Tunku Pangeran (later Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin Halim Shah) submitted himself to King Rama I in 1802 to secure the Kedah throne, making Kedah a Siamese fief rather than a conventional tributary. The author argues that this origin created a qualitatively different relationship from that of other Malay tributary states, one built on personal bonds, intermarriage, and mutual obligation that persisted for over a century. Badlishah represents the final and most extreme expression of this tradition: a Kedah prince raised as an adopted son of King Vajiravudh, educated at King's College and Oxford, naturalized as a Siamese citizen, and serving in the Thai civil service before returning to Kedah.

The article then shifts to Badlishah's political career in post-war Malaya, where his Thai upbringing and personal temperament made him a fierce defender of the Malay rulers' traditional authority against both British colonial overreach and the rising political power of UMNO. Suwannathat-Pian draws on extensive correspondence between the Kedah court and the Siamese Ministry of the Interior, as well as British Colonial Office files, to reconstruct the negotiations over Badlishah's return from Bangkok, his father's formal request for the prince's services, and Vajiravudh's reluctant but gracious consent. The author also draws on Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra's memoirs and UMNO political intelligence reports to illuminate the bitter rivalry between Badlishah and his half-brother, which was compounded by the influence of the State Secretary Shariff Osman.

The study ultimately positions Badlishah as a transitional figure whose extraordinary personal circumstances—foreign education, multilingualism, and dual cultural identity—equipped him to navigate the shift from traditional to constitutional rulership, even as his rigid defence of royal prerogative placed him at odds with the new political order that his own brother would lead to independence.

### Key Findings

- Tunku Badlishah was born on 17 March 1894, the twelfth child of Sultan Abdul Hamid Halim Shah, and was sent to Bangkok around age 7–8 (c. 1901/2) through the Thai connection of his stepmother Che Puan Manjalara, becoming an adopted son of Crown Prince Vajiravudh (pp. 124–125).
- He entered Wadham College, Oxford in 1919 to study law and history, making him the first Malay ruler to attend Oxford; upon returning to Siam in 1921 he joined the Siamese Privy Purse Department and rose to the rank of Phra Si Surintharawiwongsa within three years (pp. 125–126).
- The 1905 Loan Agreement required Kedah to borrow $2,600,000 from Bangkok with a Financial Adviser attached, and the 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty ceded Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan, and Trengganu to Great Britain in exchange for the cancellation of the 1855 Anglo-Siamese Treaty and favourable loan terms for a Siamese southern railway (pp. 123, 128).
- Badlishah was appointed State Treasurer of Kedah in 1925, became Raja Muda in 1935, Regent in 1937, and succeeded his father as Sultan in May 1943; the Japanese deposed him in October 1943 and ceded Kedah to Thailand, but his reign is officially recognized as continuous from 1943 to July 1958 (p. 129).
- During the Malayan Union crisis of 1945–46, Badlishah signed the MacMichael Treaty under threat of deposition but immediately telegraphed the Colonial Office that his signature was given under duress, and in February 1946 formally complained to London that he had been placed under a promise of secrecy and unable to inform his people (pp. 130–131).
- In 1948, Kedah UMNO affiliates (Saberkas and Persatuan Melayu Muda) formally requested that Badlishah be replaced by Tunku Yaakob, arguing his appointment was invalid because it was made by the Japanese and recognized only under the abrogated Malayan Union Order-in-Council (p. 132).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian's definitive takeaway is that Tunku Badlishah stands as the last significant exemplar of the personalized Bangkok-Kedah relationship, a bond that predated the modern era and was rooted in the 1802 succession crisis. His extraordinary personal trajectory—foreign upbringing, naturalization, and dual cultural identity—shaped his bold and outspoken leadership style, enabling him to serve as the most powerful voice for the Malay rulers' traditional authority during the critical post-war years of 1945–51, even as the political landscape shifted irrevocably toward the constitutional order his own half-brother would establish.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Thai National Archives (Documents Reign 6, B.3.1/4, the Tunku Chah file), British Colonial Office records (CO 273, CO 535, CO 717, CO 537), and the Rhodes House Library's Political Intelligence Journal at Oxford, supplemented by Tunku Abdul Rahman Putra's published memoirs.
- The article extends Suwannathat-Pian's long-standing historiographical project on Kedah-Siam relations (building on her 1988 monograph and 1999 JMBRAS article) by shifting focus from the institutional tributary framework to the micro-history of a single individual whose life encapsulates the personal dimension of that relationship.
