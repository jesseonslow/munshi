# The career of Francis James Bernard: nepotism and patronage in early Singapore

## Abstract

Francis James Bernard (1796–1843) was a founding pioneer in the British settlement at Singapore, becoming the first Master Attendant, engaging in maritime surveying and serving as inaugural head of the police department. He also established the *Singapore Chronicle* newspaper. His character has not fared well in history, largely due to adverse comments made by Sir Stamford Raffles, who treated Bernard unfairly, and a negative appraisal by historian Carl Gibson-Hill. Based on an examination of East India Company records as well as the comments of other of Bernard’s contemporaries, this article offers a more positive assessment of Bernard’s achievements and character, re-assessing a figure whose career path was shaped by the nepotism and patronage prevalent in Singapore’s early years.

## Summary

This article re-examines the career of Francis James Bernard (1796–1843), a minor colonial official in early Singapore whose reputation was damaged by Sir Stamford Raffles' personal animosity and subsequently cemented by Carl Gibson-Hill's 1960 assessment. Wright argues that Bernard's negative legacy is an artifact of patronage politics rather than any genuine moral failing. Drawing on East India Company correspondence and the private papers of Farquhar, Raffles, and Crawfurd, she reconstructs how Bernard's appointments, salary disputes, and eventual dismissal were driven by the nepotistic networks that characterised Singapore's founding years. The article also traces Bernard's post-Singapore life in the Indonesian archipelago, where he continued to press compensation claims until his death in 1843.

The core argument is that Raffles' characterisation of Bernard as a "low character" was unsubstantiated and motivated by Raffles' desire to consolidate control over the settlement's administration. Wright demonstrates that contemporaries who actually worked with Bernard—Farquhar, Crawfurd, Presgrave, Prince, and even James Brooke—consistently praised his competence and diligence. The article thus functions as a corrective to the hagiographic narrative of Raffles' Singapore, showing how the patronage system that elevated Raffles' own circle also produced collateral damage in the form of unfairly treated subordinates whose contributions were subsequently obscured.

### Key Findings

- Bernard was appointed Acting Master Attendant and Marine Storekeeper at a provisional salary of $300 per month, which was halved to $150 in 1820 when Governor-General Hastings ordered cost-cutbacks to the settlement (p. 28).
- As Police Assistant, Bernard's salary was raised from $100 to $150 in March 1822, then to $180 when confirmed as Head Assistant, with an additional $50 per month as Interpreter in Native languages to the Resident's court and magistrates (pp. 30–33).
- The Supreme Government ultimately awarded Bernard $3,757 in combined house compensation and salary arrears, based on Crawfurd's recommendation that his effective salary had averaged less than $200 per month against a benchmark of $300 for comparable posts (pp. 39–40).
- Bernard's first annual police report (July 1821) recorded 49 crimes since the department's establishment, with only two or three petty thefts after an auxiliary night watch was introduced in the European town (p. 29).
- The Town Improvement Committee, on which Bernard served from February 1823, oversaw approximately $48,460 worth of transactions—mainly between $5 and $20 each—during its operations until Crawfurd's departure in November 1825 (p. 41).
- Bernard died in Batavia on 19 December 1843, having spent his final years in Wajo (South Sulawesi) and Surabaya, where he had become involved in Bugis language, culture, and local politics (p. 42).

### Conclusion

Wright concludes that Bernard's negative reputation rests entirely on Raffles' unsubstantiated accusations and Gibson-Hill's uncritical acceptance of them. The testimony of Bernard's actual colleagues and superiors uniformly contradicts Raffles' portrayal, and the Supreme Government's financial award vindicated his claims. Wright's final point is that Bernard Street's disappearance in the 1990s should not be matched by the continued erasure of Bernard's contribution to Singapore's founding.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: India Office Records (IOR), Straits Settlements Records (SSR) at the National Archives of Singapore, and the *Prince of Wales Island Gazette*.
- Historiographical contribution: Challenges the assessments of Gibson-Hill (1960) and Kathirithamby-Wells (1969), and more broadly interrogates how Raffles' patronage network shaped the administrative record of early Singapore, elevating his own nominees while marginalising those outside his circle.
