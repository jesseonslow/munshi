# The election and installation of Tungku Muhammad, C.M.G. bin Tungku Antah, as the Yang Di Pertuan Besar, Negri Sembilan

E.W. Birch, the British Resident to Negri Sembilan, published this account in 1906 in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, documenting the 1898 formal installation of Tungku Muhammad as Yang di-Pertuan Besar and the restoration of the state's ancient constitutional framework after a generation of inter-state estrangement. The article serves as both a procedural record of the ceremony and a statement of administrative philosophy regarding the governance of Malay polities under British protection.

## Summary

The article addresses a specific constitutional crisis in Negri Sembilan: since approximately 1868, three of the four Undang (Lawgivers)—Sungei Ujong, Jelebu, and Rembau—had refused to acknowledge allegiance to the Yam Tuan of Sri Menanti, and no formal installation of a Yang di-Pertuan had taken place since the accession of Tungku Antah. When Birch assumed his duties as British Resident in early 1897, he found that the machinery of the Negri Sembilan government was effectively inoperable, with the four Lawgivers claiming that the wording of the August 1895 Confederation agreement recognised their absolute independence from the Yam Tuan. Birch's central argument is that the key to resolving the impasse lay in recognising *adat* as the "mainspring" of Malay political and social life, while simultaneously demonstrating that the Malays were reasonable enough to accept the re-establishment of their own ancient constitution once the political logic was made clear to them (pp. 9–10).

The narrative is structured around two primary documents: the written agreement of 29 April 1898 between the Yam Tuan and the four Lawgivers, which Birch drafted in Malay and which formally bound the Lawgivers to elect and install the Yam Tuan while guaranteeing them freedom from interference in internal affairs; and the detailed procedural account of the installation ceremony of 7 May 1898, including the summoning of the Lawgivers through the hereditary heralds, the arrangement of reception halls, the reading of the proclamation by the Bentara Kanan, and the sequence of obeisances performed by each Chief (pp. 11–21). Birch presents the ceremony as a carefully managed diplomatic operation in which the British Resident's role was to remove obstacles and ensure punctuality, while the substance of the act—the election and installation—was performed entirely by Malay actors according to ancient custom.

The article also provides a brief genealogical and constitutional history of the Nine States, tracing the original Sakai colonisation, the four Lawgivers, the 1773 installation of Raja Melewar as the first Yang di-Pertuan Besar at Sri Menanti, and the subsequent succession of states that filled the gaps left by the defection of five of the original nine (pp. 9–10).

### Key Findings

- The written agreement between the Yang di-Pertuan and the four Lawgivers was executed on 29 April 1898 (8 Zil-haijah 1315), in seven copies, each bearing the signature and chop of the parties and the State seal (pp. 11–12).
- The installation ceremony took place on Saturday, 7 May 1898, lasting two and a half hours and concluding at 3 p.m.; the concourse numbered between three and four thousand persons (pp. 21–22).
- British Protection over the component states had been established at different times: Sungei Ujong (April 1874), Rembau (March 1884), Jelebu (September 1886), and Sri Menanti (June 1887), with the full Confederation arranged by the agreement of August 1895 (pp. 8–9).
- The four Lawgivers who signed the agreement were: Dato' Klana Petra and Dato' Bandar (Sungei Ujong), Dato' Mendika Mentri Akhir Zaman Sutan (Jelebu), Dato' Johan Pahlawan Lela Perkasa Setiawan (Johol, represented by Dato' Baginda Tan Amas due to illness), and Dato' Sedia Raja (Rembau) (pp. 11, 13).
- The first Yang di-Pertuan Besar, Raja Melewar, was installed by the four Lawgivers in 1773 or thereabout, and took the title Yam Tuan Besar Sri Menanti (p. 9).
- The restored constitution comprised three tiers: the Yang di-Pertuan, the Four Undang (Sungei Ujong, Jelebu, Johol, Rembau), and the Four Serambi Undang (Ulu Muar, Jempol, Terachi, Gunong Pasir), the latter representing the former in the Yam Tuan's absence (p. 22).

### Conclusion

Birch's definitive takeaway is that the ceremony of 7 May 1898 successfully restored the ancient constitution of Negri Sembilan and healed a generation of inter-state jealousy, producing a complete and functioning governmental structure. He frames the achievement as one of diplomatic patience and respect for Malay custom, arguing that the British Resident's proper role was to facilitate rather than direct, and that the Malays' own attachment to *adat* made them willing participants in the restoration once the political logic was presented in terms they recognised as legitimate.

## Context

- Birch wrote as the sitting British Resident to Negri Sembilan (from 1897), and the article is explicitly a first-person account of his own administrative achievement, presented as a model of successful colonial mediation.
- The framing is that of a contemporary official dispatch elevated to scholarly publication: Birch positions the restoration as evidence that British protection had not undermined Malay constitutional traditions but had, in fact, enabled their repair.
