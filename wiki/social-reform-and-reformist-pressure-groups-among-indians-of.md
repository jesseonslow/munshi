# Social reform and reformist pressure groups among Indians of Malaya and Singapore 1930–1955

S. Arasaratnam, writing in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1967, examines the development of social reform movements among the Indian community in Malaya and Singapore between 1930 and 1955. His central thesis is that, in stark contrast to India where an English-educated elite directed both political and social reform, the Malayan reformist impulse was driven by the Tamil-educated intelligentsia and small business entrepreneurs who operated in a structural void created by the absence of any connected leadership class within the plantation economy. The article traces how these groups addressed caste discrimination, temperance, religious ritual, and marriage law, and how their efforts were shaped by the community's internal divisions over the proper role of the state.

## Summary

Arasaratnam opens by explaining why social reform was slow to take root among Malayan Indians. The plantation social structure contained no built-in leadership class: the small sub-managerial middle class was caste apart from the Tamil labouring masses, lacked intellectual equipment, and was ethnically distinct (largely Malayalee or Ceylonese). There was no educational pathway from the proletariat to an elite, and the English-educated urban middle class that gradually grew had no social connection to plantation workers. When reformist movements appeared in the late 1920s and 1930s, they were therefore led not by the English-educated class but by Tamil-educated individuals and small family business holders, particularly in Singapore. This produced movements that were ill-organised, lacked access to mass media, and achieved only limited success, but it represents a significant structural difference from the reform trajectories in India and Ceylon.

The article then surveys the principal reform domains. Caste discrimination, particularly the treatment of depressed castes in housing, employment, and temple access, was the earliest and most persistent concern. The temperance movement took on a fundamentally different character before and after the war: pre-war it was a middle-class imposition on labourers, but post-war it became a worker-led direct action campaign that merged with the trade union movement and gave working-class youths their first experience of autonomous collective action. The most complex terrain was socio-religious reform, where the reformers sought to ban practices such as *kavadi*-bearing with self-mortification, blood sacrifice, and fire-walking. Here a fundamental ideological split emerged between radicals who invoked state legislative power and a conservative middle-class faction that insisted on voluntary, evolutionary change through temple management committees. A parallel Dravidianisation current, importing the anti-Brahminical and Tamil-language revivalism of the Madras Dravidian movement, worked to displace Sanskritic ritual forms in worship and marriage, with the practical effect of preventing Brahminical dominance from becoming entrenched in Malayan Hinduism.

The marriage law question proved the most intractable. Despite a series of legislative attempts from 1924 onwards, compulsory registration and the monogamisation of Hindu marriage were never achieved in the period under review, because the government refused to legislate without unanimous community consent and the major representative associations remained silent. Arasaratnam closes by drawing attention to a persistent dichotomy: the formal political associations (the Central Indian Association of Malaya and later the Malayan Indian Congress) were preoccupied with political power, while the vital social movements of adjustment to modern conditions were happening at an entirely different level, conducted by the Tamil-educated intelligentsia with whatever modest support they could muster.

### Key Findings

- The Mahamariamman Temple at Penang was thrown open to all Hindus in 1935, the first of the major Agamic temples in Malaya to admit depressed castes (p. 55).
- The police fired at Bedong in February 1947 during a temperance picketing campaign; this incident partly prompted the Federal Legislative Council to establish an Estates Toddy Shops Committee in March 1947 (pp. 57–58).
- The Tamil Reform Association, formed in Singapore in 1931, ran the weekly *Munnetram* from 1929 and maintained continuous activity for approximately 35 years, sustained by the financial patronage of small business entrepreneurs (pp. 65–66).
- In April 1950 the management committee of the Thandayuthapani Temple, Tank Road, Singapore voted to ban fire-walking and self-mortification within its premises, but rescinded the decision for the 1951 festival season after being flooded with requests from prospective *kavadi*-bearers (pp. 61–62).
- The Civil Marriages Ordinance No. 9 of 1940 (Straits Settlements) provided for the first time a purely civil marriage between Hindus, making such marriages monogamous and entitling the wife to full legal privileges (p. 64).
- In 1955 the Nehru Government of India passed legislation making all forms of Hindu marriage monogamous; no equivalent reform was effected in Malaya or Singapore during the period studied, though Singapore's Women's Charter of 1961 later achieved this (p. 65).

### Conclusion

Arasaratnam's definitive historical takeaway is that the Indian community in Malaya exhibited a profound split between its political and social spheres. The professed national leadership, embodied in the C.I.A.M. and the M.I.C., was exclusively oriented toward political power and influence, while the Indian masses showed little interest in political questions. The actual work of social reform—addressing caste, alcohol, religious ritual, and marriage—was carried out at a separate level by the Tamil-educated intelligentsia, a small and often under-resourced group that nonetheless produced the community's most significant internal readjustments to modern conditions.

## Context

- The article draws on the minutes of the Tamil Reform Association Executive Committee, Annual Reports of the Labour Department, Federal Council and Advisory Council proceedings, and contemporary press coverage in the *Straits Times*, *Tamil Nesan*, *Indian Daily Mail*, and *Murasu* (pp. 55, 59, 60, 62, 65).
- Historiographically, the study was among the first to apply M. N. Srinivas's concepts of Sanskritisation and Dravidianisation to the Malayan Indian context, and it challenged the prevailing assumption that the English-educated middle class was the natural leader of all Indian reform, redirecting scholarly attention to the Tamil-educated and business classes as the actual agents of social change (pp. 54–55, 58, 61).
