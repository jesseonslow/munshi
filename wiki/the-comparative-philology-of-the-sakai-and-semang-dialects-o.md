# The comparative philology of the Sakai and Semang dialects of the Malay Peninsula – a review

C. O. Blagden's 1903 review, published in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 39), assesses Professor Schmidt's monograph on the Sakai and Semang dialects of the Malay Peninsula — the first systematic comparative philological treatment of these languages. Blagden endorses Schmidt's methodology and dialectal classification while mounting a sustained critique of his central thesis that all aboriginal dialects of the Peninsula are branches of the Mon-Annam stock, arguing instead for a more complex layered origin involving multiple waves of linguistic influence.

## Summary

Blagden begins by acknowledging the scope of Schmidt's compilation, which collates virtually all published materials on the aboriginal dialects — from Newbold and Roberts through De Morgan, Vaughan Stevens, Klaproth, Tomlin, Low, Borie, and Maclay — and subjects them to comparative analysis for the first time. He praises the resulting comparative vocabulary and the "admirable" first attempt at a comparative grammar, particularly the section on prefixes and infixes, which reveals structural parallels between the Mon-Annam and Malayan families of speech. However, Blagden identifies a critical methodological flaw: Schmidt's deliberate omission of words of Malayan origin artificially inflates the percentage of Mon-Annam vocabulary (approximately 240 out of 1250 words, or 20 per cent), because it excludes archaic Malayan terms such as *to'ot* "knee," *asu* "dog," *awe* "rattan," *siah* "salt," *manuk* "fowl," *kebus* "dead," and *hirum* "black," which point to a Malayan dialect distinct from Malay itself and of very ancient date (pp. 50–51).

The most consequential objection concerns the numerals. Blagden demonstrates that the Mon-Annam numeral series is preserved in its purest and most extended form (beyond four) not in the "pure" Sakai dialects but in the mixed Besisi group of outlying southern dialects, while the pure Sakai numerals diverge markedly from the Mon-Annam type. This distribution, he argues, is inexplicable on the hypothesis of native Mon-Annam origin and instead suggests that the numerals were imposed from without by a Mon-Annam race of higher civilization (pp. 54–55). He further notes that the so-called Mon-Annam family itself may be a mixed formation, and that comparisons with Cham are unreliable because Cham is partly a Malayan language (p. 52).

Blagden's own positive contribution is the identification of an alien element in the Semang dialects — a body of words neither Mon-Annam nor Malayan — which he regards as the probable remnant of the original Negrito speech. He provides a set of seven Semang words (*keto'* "day," *kawau* "bird," *mako* "egg," *ekob* "snake," *ek* "dog," *yus/nyus* "tooth," *chas* "hand") that have no cognates in Sakai or Mon-Annam, and argues that it is in these words, not in the shared vocabulary, that the true affinities of the Semang must be sought (pp. 58–59). He also proposes a refined four-group classification — pure Semang, "low country" Semang, Tembe' (northern) Sakai, and Senoi (central-southern) Sakai — which he considers substantially justified by the existing materials and which corresponds closely to the racial distinction between Negritos and Sakais (pp. 56–57).

### Key Findings

- Schmidt's comparative vocabulary comprises approximately 1,250 words, of which about 240 (20 per cent) are identified as Mon-Annam; Blagden argues this figure is overstated due to the arbitrary exclusion of archaic Malayan terms (pp. 50–51).
- The Mon-Annam numeral series (extending to seven) is preserved in its purest form in the mixed Besisi group of southern dialects, not in the pure Sakai dialects, which Blagden takes as evidence of external imposition rather than native origin (pp. 54–55).
- An alien lexical element, neither Mon-Annam nor Malayan, exists in the Semang dialects and likely represents the remnant of the original Negrito language; seven specific words are cited as examples with no known analogues elsewhere (pp. 58–59).
- The dialects fall into four groups: (i) pure Semang (Northern Kedah to southern Kelantan), (ii) "low country" Semang, (iii) Tembe' (northern) Sakai, and (iv) Senoi (central) Sakai with southern dialects including Besisi; the main division corresponds to the Negrito–Sakai racial boundary (pp. 56–57).
- The prefix and infix system of the aboriginal dialects bears a strong structural resemblance to that of the Mon-Annam family, but also to the Malayan family (particularly the Philippine languages), making it difficult to assign the dialects to either family on morphological grounds alone (pp. 53, 59–60).
- Blagden proposes a model of two waves of Mon-Annam influence and two of Malay in the Peninsula, with the Sakai dialects representing a more distantly related Mon-Annam form and the Semang having substantially abandoned their own language in favour of an imposed Mon-Annam speech (pp. 61–62).

### Conclusion

Blagden's definitive historical takeaway is that the aboriginal dialects of the Malay Peninsula cannot be reduced to a single Mon-Annam stock, as Schmidt maintains. The distribution of the numerals, the presence of archaic non-Malay Malayan vocabulary, and the alien Semang element together indicate a layered linguistic history involving at least two waves of Mon-Annam influence and an independent Malayan substrate, with the Semang retaining a substratum of their own original speech. He calls for the urgent collection of genuine spoken sentences — the most critical *desideratum* — and urges the colonial governments to support a systematic linguistic survey of the Peninsula before these dialects become extinct (pp. 62–63).

## Context

- The review was written during the early Residential System period, when European access to the Native States had recently opened the central Peninsula to linguistic enquiry; Blagden notes that the purer forms of the Sakai dialects, situated in the centre of the Peninsula, had remained unknown until this time (p. 49).
- Blagden explicitly calls on the Governments of the Straits Settlements and the Native States to follow the example of the Indian Government's Linguistic Survey of India, framing the work as a matter of scientific urgency in the face of imminent dialectal extinction (p. 63).
