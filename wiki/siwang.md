# Siwang

## Scope and Historical Definition

The Siwang, more commonly known in the modern literature as the Chewong, are a small foraging and shifting-cultivation people of the mountainous rain forest of western Pahang, on the Malay Peninsula. By the mid-1950s their population numbered approximately 140 individuals, distributed across the Teris and Dong areas, and they were in the process of being forcibly relocated from their ancestral territory during the Malayan Emergency [[1, pp. 52]](#ref-1). The group's name has been a point of scholarly contention: the earlier literature, following C. S. Ogilvie's three articles of 1940, 1948, and 1949, used the form "Che Wong," while the informants themselves preferred "Siwang" [[1, p. 50]](#ref-1). The Chewong are one of several Orang Asli groups of the region, and their immediate neighbours include the Bateg Negritos, the Jah Chong, and the Bi Nyeb [[1]](#ref-1).

The earliest systematic records of the group derive from Ogilvie, a British official who produced word lists and descriptions of food, dress, and material objects, recording 1,021 Chewong words and phrases in English and mapping six main settlements [[2, p. 105]](#ref-2). These contributions, while valuable as a factual base, provided "practically no information on kinship, political authority, or other features of the social organisation" [[1, p. 50]](#ref-1). It was not until Rodney Needham's two-day interview with two Siwang informants at Kuala Tahan in May 1955 that the first substantive data on the group's social structure became available [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1).

## Social Organisation and Political Authority

Needham's 1956 ethnographic notes, drawn from questioning two informants—Begng, aged approximately 40, and Patong, aged 28, both employed by the Game Department at Kuala Tahan—established that the Siwang recognised a three-tiered political hierarchy of hereditary offices [[1, pp. 57–59]](#ref-1). The supreme authority, *to menteri*, held ultimate jurisdiction; the *to pelima* functioned as the effective administrator, conducting marriages and divorces and settling disputes; and the *to jinang* served as assistant to the *to pelima*. All three positions were succeeded patrilineally by males only. At the time of Needham's fieldwork, the *to menteri* position was vacant following the death of Maroi in 1944; his son Begng was entitled to the office but had not yet been recognised as commanding authority [[1, pp. 57–59]](#ref-1).

The kinship system was ambilineal in principle but exhibited a strong patrilineal bias in degree-reckoning [[1]](#ref-1). Marriage was endogamous, and the recorded data reveal a pattern of close-kin union: of 18 Siwang–Siwang marriages, one was with an aunt, 13 with a cousin, and 4 with a niece. Only 3 of 19 total marriages (including one with a Bi Ja man) were matrilocal [[1, pp. 62–64]](#ref-1). Union with a second cousin was permitted only on the strict condition that no divorce would follow [[1, p. 64]](#ref-1). The traditional bridewealth comprised 3 spears, 5 rings, plates, and a cooking pot, though in practice items and quantities varied; one recorded case, that of Wen's marriage to Main, involved no spears at all [[1, p. 64]](#ref-1).

The legal system was fine-based, with all penalties expressed in spears according to a peculiar arithmetic convention in which a fine of three was pronounced "63" [[1, pp. 65–66]](#ref-1). Theft and seduction were each fined 3 spears (the latter with the addition of 1 plate); adultery for a single night carried the same penalty, while a continued liaison attracted 6 spears and 1 plate; rape was fined 4 spears and 1 plate. Homicide after extreme provocation was fined 100 spears and 1 plate, but a murderer who could not plead provocation might be killed, the sentence being carried out by a close kinsman of the victim empowered by the *to menteri* [[1, pp. 65–66]](#ref-1).

## Economy, Cosmology, and Language

The Siwang economy combined shifting cultivation with intensive forest foraging [[1]](#ref-1). Crops included maize, cassava, sweet potatoes, plantains, papayas, red peppers, sugar cane, and tobacco. Forest clearings were cultivated for two years according to Ogilvie's account, or three to four years according to Begng, then abandoned [[1, p. 56]](#ref-1). Land was cleared cooperatively by the whole settlement; there were no individual land rights, though crops belonged to the planter. Outsiders—Bi Ja and Bi Nyeb—could plant only with special permission, while Malays had no right at all [[1, p. 56]](#ref-1).

The religious system, as documented by Needham, centred on a single God (Ta'ala), a class of protective ancestral spirits, and the shamanic office of *putau*, associated with a distinctive tree-platform burial rite [[1]](#ref-1). Signe Howell's later monograph, *Society and Cosmos: Chewong of Peninsular Malaysia* (Oxford University Press, 1984), extended this picture considerably, documenting a cosmology in which the *maneden* spirit is described as having "only one arm and one leg — if a right arm then a left leg and vice versa," a unilateral figure assignable to a worldwide class of such representations [[2, p. 108]](#ref-2). Incest (*tanko*) was punished by thunderbolts striking "the hip or knee joints" of offenders, while the *yinlugn* (the spiritual aspect of a dead person) was characterised by having "all its joints loose" and moving at night—the reverse of the living world [[2, pp. 108]](#ref-2). At a ritual, the Chewong officiant blew incense smoke in the four cardinal directions and uttered the Arabic-Malay formula "Salaam alaikom, alaikom assalaam, minta maaf" [[2, p. 109]](#ref-2).

A striking feature of the Chewong language, noted by both Needham and Howell, is the high proportion of words identical to or directly derived from Malay, including terms for central cosmological concepts such as *bajo* (cloak), *naga* (mythical snake), *pantang* (rules), and *tankal* (spell or talisman) [[2, p. 109]](#ref-2). This heavy Malay impress implies long-sustained contact inconsistent with the Chewong narrative of recent displacement from the lowlands, a cultural-historical problem that Needham flagged but did not resolve [[2, pp. 109]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The Siwang literature has evolved through three distinct phases, each marked by a shift in analytical priority. The first phase, represented by Ogilvie's three articles (1940, 1948, 1949) and Collings's 1949 appendix, treated the group as a linguistic and material-culture curiosity, accumulating vocabulary and describing artefacts without engaging the social structure [[1, p. 50]](#ref-1), [[2, p. 105]](#ref-2). The second phase, inaugurated by Needham's 1956 article, shifted the Siwang to a case study in the comparative anthropology of small-scale social organisation, providing illustration for the study of societies "composed of small, isolated, and relatively self-sufficient local groups" [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1). Needham was explicit that his data, gathered in a few hours of questioning, could not match the reliability of intensive fieldwork, but he argued that a trained anthropologist conducting a rapid survey could still ascertain "certain structural essentials" [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1).

The third phase, represented by Howell's 1984 monograph and Needham's 1984 commentary, moved the discussion into the domain of cognitive and cosmological analysis. Howell's achievement, as Needham assessed it, lay not in the substantive features she documented—the Chewong lack the lineages, politics, and rituals prominent in many ethnographies—but in her capacity to identify and comprehend what was actually present, deploying concepts of opposition, inversion, and metamorphosis to render Chewong collective representations intelligible [[2, pp. 106–107]](#ref-2). Howell proposed that Chewong thought emphasises juxtaposition over hierarchical subordination, and advanced a mode she termed "nomenclature"—the assignment of a common name to class members who share "nothing more in common than their being named as such" [[2, p. 109]](#ref-2). Needham connected this to Cassirer's notion of "appellation" and to Lévy-Bruhl's observation that mythical thought "does no more than juxtapose, it does not classify" [[2, p. 109]](#ref-2).

Needham's most pointed theoretical intervention targeted the "principle of separation" that Howell elevated as a structuring concept in Chewong thought. Drawing on his own earlier work on blood sacrifice and the mockery of animals among the Penan, Ngaju, and Semang, he argued that separation is not an explanation of social order but a precondition of it: without conceptual separation there could be no objects among which relational order obtains, making the principle a tautology rather than an analytical tool [[2, pp. 110–111]](#ref-2). This critique positioned Howell's work within a specific Oxford-based comparative tradition descending from Kirk Endicott's *An Analysis of Malay Magic* and R. H. Barnes's *Kedang*, and ultimately from the broader *Année Sociologique* lineage of Durkheim, Mauss, and van Gennep [[2, p. 106]](#ref-2). The debate over the analytical utility of the "principle of separation" in the study of Orang Asli and broader Southeast Asian cosmologies thus remains an open question in the literature [[2, p. 111]](#ref-2).
