# Shaer Dandan Setia

H. Overbeck's 1932 synopsis of the *Shaer Dandan Setia* provides the first European-language account of one of the longest Malay *shaer* epics, a poem of approximately 5,400 quatrains whose latest lithographed edition appeared in Singapore in 1925/26. Overbeck's central contribution is to demonstrate that the poem is a Malay adaptation of the Javanese Panji legend, transposed into a local milieu and enriched with an abundance of combat, romantic intrigue, and *pantun* exchanges that explain its enduring popularity with Malay readers.

## Summary

Overbeck's article is primarily a detailed plot synopsis of the *Shaer Dandan Setia*, following the 1925/26 two-volume edition. The narrative spans multiple kingdoms—Dendam Birahi, Karang Bersulam, Alam Biduri, Telaga Ratna, and Badja Angsana—and follows the hero Dandan Setia through a sequence of trials: his initial cruelty toward the hideously ugly princess Intan Terpileh (Gemala Ajaib), her magical transformation into a beauty after a hawk tears off her caul, his subsequent obsessive love, a crocodile swallowing him for three months, and a protracted rivalry with the celestial being Dewa Shah Peri and the mortal prince Shah Beranda over the princess's hand. The story culminates in a fourfold wedding and the coronation of multiple sultans. Overbeck frames this narrative as a Malay reworking of the Panji cycle, noting that the author was clearly acquainted with Javanese literary traditions, as evidenced by comparisons between the heroes and Inu Kertapati (Raden Inu Kuripan) and a princess with Galoh Chendera Kirana.

The article also serves as a brief textual and bibliographical study. Overbeck documents the poem's publication history, its performance life (a four-part stage adaptation by the Union Star Opera in Singapore in 1926), and the anachronisms embedded in the text—European and Dutch loanwords such as "pistol," "tropong" (telescope), "opas" (policeman), "stolob" (glass-lantern), "stocking," and "slipper"—which complicate any attempt to date the original composition. He notes that the Malay epilogue states the poem was "finished" in 1300 A.H. (c. 1883) in Singapore, but he cannot determine whether this refers to the composition of the original or merely a later edition, nor can he trace a prose version or manuscript in any accessible catalogue.

### Key Findings

- The *Shaer Dandan Setia* comprises approximately 5,400 quatrains (21,600 lines), making it one of the longest Malay *shaer* in existence (p. 141).
- The latest complete edition was lithographed in Singapore in 1343/44 A.H. (1925/26 A.D.) in two volumes; the poem's Malay epilogue states it was "finished" on 22 Moharram A.H. 1300 (c. 1883) in Singapore (pp. 141, 158).
- A four-part stage adaptation, "Great Malay Historical Play 'Dandan Setia'," was performed in Singapore by the Union Star Opera in 1926 (p. 141).
- The text contains anachronistic European and Dutch loanwords including "pistol," "tropong" (telescope), "pokul sembilan," "milyun" (million), "opas" (Dutch *opas*, policeman), "stolob" (Dutch *stolp*, glass-lantern), "stocking," and "slipper" (pp. 141–142).
- Javanese lexical items appear in the Malay text, including *edan asmara*, *anon*, *lanang*, and *nonton*, confirming the author's familiarity with Javanese literary language (p. 141).
- No prose version (*hikayat*) or manuscript of the *shaer* could be located in any accessible manuscript catalogue at the time of writing (p. 141).

### Conclusion

Overbeck's definitive takeaway is that the *Shaer Dandan Setia* is a Malay adaptation of the Javanese Panji legend, successfully transplanted into a local setting where it retained its narrative charm and found a wide popular audience. The poem's survival through multiple printed editions and its adaptation for the stage attest to its cultural vitality, even as its anachronisms and the absence of any known manuscript or prose source leave its precise origins and compositional history unresolved.

## Context

- Overbeck's synopsis relies exclusively on the lithographed 1925/26 edition; no manuscript or prose *hikayat* was available to him, limiting the study to a single textual witness.
- The article represents an early attempt to bring a major Malay popular epic to the attention of European scholars of Malay literature, filling a gap in the then-existing bibliography of Malay *shaer*.
