# The economics of the handloom industry of the east coast of Malaya

C. W. S. K. composed this obituary in 1899 to commemorate Sir William E. Maxwell, K.C.M.G., a Straits Settlements administrator and scholar whose death in the Gold Coast represented, in the author's words, the most severe loss the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society had sustained since its foundation in 1877. The piece traces Maxwell's dual legacy as a colonial administrator—particularly in land legislation and district governance—and as a foundational figure in the scholarly study of Malay language, folklore, and history.

## Summary

The obituary reconstructs Maxwell's career from his early service in the Supreme Court (1855–1869), through his appointment as Assistant Government Agent in Province Wellesley (1874), his service in the Perak War (1875), and his successive posts as Assistant Resident of Perak, Commissioner of Land Titles, Resident Councillor of Penang, British Resident of Selangor, Colonial Secretary of the Straits Settlements, and finally Governor of the Gold Coast, where he died. The author emphasizes that Maxwell's administrative innovations—particularly the system of District Administration and his land legislation—were carried out "in spite of determined opposition" and have since been vindicated by their successful reproduction in the Federated Malay States (p. 2).

The second half of the piece is devoted to Maxwell's scholarly output, which the author presents as inseparable from his official career. Maxwell contributed numerous papers to the Society's journal on Malay proverbs, Perak manuscripts, the Sakai and other aboriginal peoples, shamanism, and Malay land tenure, and he edited three Malay fairy tales transcribed from oral rhapsodists. He served as Honorary Secretary of the Society from 1883 to 1887, a period during which, the author argues, "the Society owed its existence in a great measure to the energy of its Secretary" amid scanty membership support (p. 3). Beyond the Society's pages, Maxwell authored a *Manual of the Malay Language* and collected a library of Malay manuscripts bequeathed to the Royal Asiatic Society.

The obituary closes with a reflection on the fragility of the Society's institutional life, drawing on Logan's earlier warning that a journal managed by a single individual is vulnerable to that individual's removal. The author contends that Maxwell's influence extended well beyond his published works, shaping the scholarly direction of Malay studies in the Straits and earning consistent respect from Dutch scholars, and warns that maintaining the standard he set will require "increased effort on the part of members" (p. 5).

### Key Findings

- Maxwell served as Honorary Secretary of MBRAS from 1883 to 1887 and was elected Vice President in 1893 (p. 3).
- He was created a C.M.G. for services connected with the Nisero affair and later a K.C.M.G. for distinguished service in the Ashanti Expedition (p. 2).
- His *Manual of the Malay Language* is identified as the work through which he "is likely to have exercised his widest influence" on learners of the language (p. 4).
- Maxwell's library of Malay manuscripts was bequeathed to the Royal Asiatic Society (p. 4).
- The author notes that Maxwell's work on Malay Proverbs "led to the publication of at least one other valuable contribution on the same subject, from the pen of Mr. H. Clifford" (p. 4).
- Maxwell's unpublished fourth Malay fairy tale was subsequently placed on record and published by the Society through Clifford (p. 5).

### Conclusion

The author's definitive takeaway is that Maxwell was the indispensable linchpin of MBRAS during its formative decades, and that the Society's survival and scholarly standard were inextricable from his personal energy and expertise. The obituary functions as both eulogy and institutional warning: without Maxwell, the Society might not have endured, and without renewed collective commitment from its membership, the standard he established is at risk of decline.

## Context

- The author, identified only by the initials C. W. S. K., was a contemporary Straits Settlements official writing in the immediate aftermath of Maxwell's death; the tone is that of a fellow administrator and Society member rather than an independent historian.
- The piece reflects the colonial administrative framing of the period, presenting Maxwell's legislative and district-administrative achievements as personal triumphs over "determined opposition" rather than as products of broader institutional or political dynamics.
