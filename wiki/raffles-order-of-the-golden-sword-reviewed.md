# Raffles’ Order of the Golden Sword reviewed

Lee Kam Hing and Ahmat Adam (1990) resolve a decades-old mystery surrounding the Acehnese Order of the Golden Sword conferred on Sir Stamford Raffles, using a recently discovered Malay letter from Sultan Jauhar Alam Syah dated 27 April 1811 to establish that the decoration was a straightforward diplomatic gesture of goodwill during the Napoleonic Wars, not the product of any clandestine arrangement.

## Summary

The article builds on C. A. Gibson-Hill's 1956 article, which had identified the Order of the Golden Sword as a puzzle: Raffles included it in his 1817 coat of arms, yet none of his biographers mentioned the award, and no documentary record of its conferral had been located. Gibson-Hill had speculated on the likely date and circumstances but could not confirm them. The authors' intervention rests on the discovery of a Malay manuscript in Jawi script, found in a British attic and translated by Ahmat Adam, in which Sultan Jauhar Alam Syah explicitly states he is conferring the title *Sri Paduka Orangkaya Berpedang Emas* ("His Excellency the Nobleman with the Golden Sword") on Raffles. The letter was delivered by the envoy Tengku Nakhoda Abdul Rahman when Raffles was in Melaka preparing for the Java expedition, and a copy was enclosed as No. 7 in Raffles' despatch to Lord Minto of 11 June 1811, now held in the Raffles-Minto Collection at the India Office Library (pp. 77–78).

The authors then reconstruct the political context that made the award meaningful to both parties. The Sultan had been aggrieved since 1808, when Penang authorities seized and sold his ship the *Hydroos* as enemy property during the war with Denmark, and he had been under suspicion of harbouring French naval interests in Acehnese ports. Raffles' letter of early 1811, informing the Sultan of the impending British expedition against Java and seeking Acehnese cooperation, must have appeared to Jauhar Alam Syah as a signal that the British were willing to overlook past grievances and restore Aceh's standing. The decoration was thus a reciprocating gesture befitting the status of the Governor-General's agent, likely exchanged for presents of gunpowder and firearms (pp. 79–80).

The final section addresses why Raffles singled out this particular award for his coat of arms when he had received more prestigious decorations from Javanese rulers. The authors argue three converging reasons: it was the first royal decoration he ever received (his British knighthood came only in 1816); he appreciated Aceh's distinguished history as the first Asian power to sign a treaty with the English East India Company in 1602; and, most significantly, including the award was a public demonstration of his political alignment with Jauhar Alam Syah against the Syed Hussein faction, which was backed by Penang merchants and officials who favoured a weaker, more commercially open Aceh (pp. 82–85).

### Key Findings

- The Sultan's letter of 27 April 1811 (Jawi script, 1 folio, 47 cm × 57.6 cm) explicitly confers the title *Sri Paduka Orangkaya Berpedang Emas* on Raffles and was delivered by Tengku Nakhoda Abdul Rahman at Melaka (pp. 77, 86–88).
- Raffles' despatch to Lord Minto of 11 June 1811, with the Sultan's letter as Enclosure No. 7, is preserved in the Raffles-Minto Collection, Vol. 6, India Office Library, London (p. 78).
- The *Hydroos* was seized by Penang in 1808 as Danish-registered enemy property; the cargo was later returned but the ship itself was sold, and the Sultan's appeals to Penang and Bengal went unanswered (p. 80).
- The Letters Patent of 2 October 1817 granting Raffles' coat of arms describe the decoration as "a mark of the High Regard of the said King and in testimony of the good understanding which had been happily established between that Prince and the British Government" (p. 82).
- The three Panglima Sagis declared the overthrow of Jauhar Alam Syah in 1814 and invited Syed Hussein of Penang to the throne; Syed Hussein had previously obtained loans of $50,000 (July 1815) and $30,000 (August 1816) from the Penang government (pp. 82–83).
- Raffles' 1819 mission to Aceh, on which he served as senior commissioner, resolved the succession in favour of Jauhar Alam Syah and resulted in the signing of the long-advocated treaty between Aceh and the British (p. 85).

### Conclusion

The authors conclude that the Order of the Golden Sword was a legitimate diplomatic exchange that should have been public knowledge in Bengal and Penang from 1811, and that Raffles' decision to immortalise it in his armorial bearings was a deliberate political statement aligning himself with Jauhar Alam Syah's legitimacy against the Syed Hussein faction — a position that ultimately determined the outcome of the 1819 Acehnese succession crisis and secured the treaty Raffles had long advocated.

## Context

- Primary archival sources: Raffles-Minto Collection (India Office Library, London), Straits Settlements Factory Records (India Office Library), and the newly discovered Malay manuscript translated by Ahmat Adam.
- Historiographically, the article closes a gap left by Gibson-Hill (1956) and provides the first documentary confirmation of the award's conferral, while also situating Raffles' Acehnese policy within the broader tension between his strategic vision and the commercial interests of Penang-based merchants.
