# Indian and Malay beliefs

R.O. Winstedt, writing in 1921 for the Journal of the Siam Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, marshals comparative evidence from Vedic, Javanese, and Malay sources to argue that a substantial body of Malay magical practice, wedding ritual, and popular belief is of Indian origin rather than indigenous or Islamic. Building on M. Winternitz's 1900 review of W. W. Skeat's *Malay Beliefs* and adding his own parallels, Winstedt presents a cumulative case for the deep penetration of Hindu-Vedic culture into the Malay archipelago.

## Summary

Winstedt's central argument proceeds through a series of specific identifications. The most striking is the equation of the Malay Spectre Huntsman with Batara Guru (Siva), who in Vedic times was Rudra, the storm-god followed by hosts of spirits and leader of lost souls—the same figure as the German Wodan/Odin of the wild huntsman legend. This identification, Winstedt notes, corroborates the hypothesised Indo-Germanic storm-god as a common source (p. 88–89). The Malay magician's practice of naming the source of a spirit before exorcism, and the use of *hong* (= *om*) in charms, are shown to correspond precisely with Vedic *mantra* practice as described by Havell (p. 89).

The article then extends to domestic and ceremonial life. Winternitz's parallels for Malay betrothal verses (the girl as a calf, the wooer searching for a lost bird) are matched against Estonian, Finnish, and Sardinian examples, while the mimic combat for the bride, the throwing of rice, the *confarreatio*, and the tinsel crown of the bridegroom are all attested in ancient India (p. 90). Occupational taboos—regard for the physician and sailor as degrading, distrust of one-eyed and hairy persons—mirror the code of Manu, and dietary prohibitions (funeral food, sheep's milk, sitting posture) match the *Dharmasūtras* of Apastamba and Gautama (pp. 90–91).

Winstedt closes with briefer observations: the moon-deer (from Sanskrit *harinanka*), the henna dance with lighted candles (likely Persian-Indian in origin), the structural parallel between Mahāmeru and Olympus, and the identity of the language of signs used in Malay intrigue with that practised in Kandy (pp. 91–93).

### Key Findings

- The Malay Spectre Huntsman is identified with Batara Guru (Siva/Rudra), the Vedic storm-god, paralleling the German Wodan/Odin and supporting the Indo-Germanic storm-god hypothesis (p. 88–89).
- Malay magical charms correspond in structure and function to the Vedic *mantra*: the syllable *om* (rendered *hong*) opens the charm, a single incorrect syllable voids it, and the formula must be kept secret (p. 89).
- Tabu vocabularies in Malay magic (substituting euphemistic terms for forbidden words) are directly paralleled in the *Satapatha Brāhmaṇa*, the *Mantrapāṭha*, and the *Dharmasūtras* of Gautama and Apastamba (pp. 89–90).
- Malay wedding customs—mimic combat for the bride, rice-throwing, *confarreatio*, and the tinsel crown of the poorest groom—match ceremonies attested in ancient India, Kashmir, and modern Bengal (p. 90).
- Occupational and bodily taboos (physician and sailor degrading; distrust of one-eyed men and persons with thick body hair) persist in Malay custom and correspond to the code of Manu (p. 91).
- The Malay objection to younger siblings marrying before elder, termed *langkah batang*, matches the Brahmanical prohibition recorded in Gautama's *Dharmasūtra* (p. 91).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that the cumulative weight of these parallels—spanning magic, marriage, diet, occupation, and cosmology—demonstrates that Indian (specifically Vedic-Hindu) cultural transmission into the Malay world was deep and enduring, reaching well beyond the surface layer of Sanskrit loanwords into the structure of popular belief itself. The article does not claim these survivals are of early date in every case, but insists that their Indian provenance is established by the specificity of the correspondences.

## Context

- The article was prompted by Dr. O. Schrieke of the Dutch Indies Native Affairs department, who directed Winstedt to Winternitz's review; the *Wiener Zeitschrift* was not held in any Peninsular library and had to be borrowed from the Batavian Society (p. 88).
- Winstedt had previously published "Hindu Survivals in Malay Custom" in the *Journal of the Federated Malay States Museums* (Vol. IX, Part I), of which this article is a companion and extension (p. 88).
