# Indian and Malay beliefs

R.O. Winstedt, a senior colonial administrator and scholar of Malay culture, published this article in 1921 in the *Journal of the Siam Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 83, pp. 88–93). Building on a review by M. Winternitz of W.G. Skeat's *Malay Beliefs*, Winstedt marshals comparative evidence from Vedic Sanskrit texts, Javanese legends, and European folklore to argue that a substantial body of Malay animistic and magical practice is of Indian origin, transmitted through Hindu-Brahmanic channels rather than arising independently or deriving from Islam.

## Summary

The article's central concern is the provenance of Malay folk beliefs and magical practices. Winstedt takes as his starting point Winternitz's 1900 review of Skeat's *Malay Beliefs* in the *Wiener Zeitschrift für die Kunde des Morgenlandes*, which had already drawn parallels between Malay and Indian ritual. Winstedt extends this comparative programme by introducing additional textual evidence from the Vedas, Dharmaśāstra literature, and Javanese sources, and by drawing on European folklore scholarship (Grimm, von Schröder) to establish a broader Indo-Germanic framework.

The argument proceeds through several thematic clusters. First, the identification of the Malay Spectre Huntsman with Batara Guru (Śiva/Rudra) is shown to corroborate the hypothesised Indo-Germanic storm-god archetype shared with Odin/Wodan. Second, the structure of Malay charms—secret, syllable-precise, efficacious only when correctly intoned—is matched point-for-point against the Vedic *mantra* as described by Havell. Third, a series of specific taboos observed by Malay Brahman-descended communities (avoidance of funeral food, prohibition on sitting above a teacher, distrust of one-eyed men and hairy persons, the *langkah batang* objection to younger siblings marrying before elder) are traced to the *Apastamba Dharmaśāstra*, *Gautama Dharmaśāstra*, and the *Manusmṛti*. Fourth, wedding customs (mimic combat for the bride, rice-throwing, confarreatio, the bridegroom's temporary elevation to princely status) are shown to be pan-Indo-Germanic with specific Indian attestations.

Throughout, Winstedt's method is one of cumulative parallelism: each Malay practice is matched to an Indian textual source and, where possible, to a European or other non-Indian cognate, thereby isolating the Indian layer from indigenous or Islamic contributions.

### Key Findings

- The Malay Spectre Huntsman is identified with Batara Guru (Śiva/Rudra), corroborating the Indo-Germanic storm-god hypothesis linking Rudra and Odin/Wodan as leaders of lost souls followed by hosts of spirits (pp. 88–89).
- Malay charms correspond structurally to the Vedic *mantra*: secret, syllable-precise, and efficacious only when correctly intoned; the syllable *hong* in Malay charms is identified with Sanskrit *oṃ* (p. 89).
- Taboo vocabularies in Malay magic (e.g., *bhagala* for *kapala* "head," *manidhanus* for *indradhanus* "rainbow") are attested in the *Satapatha Brāhmaṇa* VI, 1, 1, 2 and the *Mantrapāṭha* (pp. 89–90).
- The Malay notion of a mousedeer in the moon derives from the Indian "hare in the moon" motif, found in Sanskrit epithets *śāśin*, *mārkaṅka*, *haraṅka* (p. 90).
- The *langkah batang* taboo—universal Malay dislike of younger children marrying before elder—corresponds directly to the *Gautama Dharmaśāstra* prohibition on feeding a younger brother married before his elder (p. 91).
- The henna dance with lighted candles, attested in Wilkinson's *Incidents of Malay Life* (2nd ed., p. 58), is linked to a similar Persian candlestick dance (Hales, *From Persian Uplands*, p. 121) and argued to have arrived with Indian marriage ceremonies (p. 91).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that Malay animistic and magical practice contains a deep stratum of Indian origin, transmitted through Hindu-Brahmanic channels, which is distinguishable from both indigenous animism and Islamic influence. The cumulative weight of textual parallels—Vedic, Dharmaśāstric, Javanese, and European—supports the conclusion that these beliefs were not independently invented by a "primitive people" but were inherited, in many cases with remarkable fidelity, from Indian sources.

## Context

- The article draws on Winternitz's 1900 review in *Wiener Zeitschrift für die Kunde des Morgenlandes* XIV (pp. 243–264), Metzger's *Globus* Bd. 44 (1883), and Skeat's *Malay Beliefs* as its primary comparative corpus, supplemented by Vedic and Dharmaśāstric texts in the *Sacred Books of the East* series.
- Winstedt was a senior colonial administrator (later Adviser for Native Affairs) and a prolific contributor to MBRAS publications; the article extends his earlier paper "Hindu Survivals in Malay Custom" (*J. Federated Malay States Museums*, Vol. IX, Part I) and represents a mature phase of his comparative-folklore methodology.
