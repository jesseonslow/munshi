# The Peliosanthes of the Malay Peninsula

H. N. Ridley, then Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, published this monograph in 1898 to provide the first comprehensive treatment of the genus *Peliosanthes* in the Malay Peninsula, describing seven species (four of them new to science) and establishing a diagnostic key based on the position of the ovary relative to the staminal ring. The article situates the genus within the Liliaceae (subgroup Ophiopogoneae) and documents its distribution across the Peninsula, India, Siam, Cochin China, and Java.

## Summary

Ridley's monograph addresses a taxonomic gap in the flora of the Malay Peninsula by providing the first full account of *Peliosanthes* in the region. He begins by resolving the genus's systematic placement—acknowledging that it has been variously assigned to Haemodoraceae or Liliaceae due to the half-inferior position of the ovary—and settles it among the aberrant Liliaceae, allied to the Convallarieae. The genus description highlights two structurally distinctive features: the fleshy staminal ring (formed by connate filaments) and the unusual fruit development, in which a single seed (rarely two or three) pushes through the young capsule and matures externally with a conspicuous fleshy blue testa adapted for bird dispersal.

The core of the article is a species-by-species treatment of seven taxa, three of which Ridley describes as new: *P. viridis* (common in dense wet jungle around Singapore), *P. lurida* (from rocky outcrops at Penara Bukit, Penang, with the largest flowers of any Peninsular species), and *P. grandifolia* (noted for its very large coriaceous leaves and yellow flowers, locality uncertain but cultivated at the Singapore Botanic Gardens). A fourth new species, *P. stellaris*, is distinguished by its small star-shaped green flowers with nearly free stamens and a distinctly inferior ovary. Ridley also revisits *P. Teta* (the first species cultivated in England, 1810), *P. violacea* (with almost black globular flowers), and *P. albida* (a tall white-flowered species from the Thaiping Hills, unique in having a half-inferior ovary).

Throughout, Ridley draws on his own field collections from Penang Hill, Singapore, Perak, Pahang, and Province Wellesley, supplemented by herbarium specimens at the Botanic Gardens, Singapore. He notes that the Tahan River in Pahang yielded numerous specimens but that most were in fruit at the time of collection, leaving several undescribed forms in the herbarium.

### Key Findings

- Seven species of *Peliosanthes* are recorded for the Malay Peninsula, compared with seven in India, two or three in Siam and Cochin China, and one in Java (p. 91).
- Three new species are described: *P. viridis* (Singapore, common in dense wet jungle), *P. lurida* (Penara Bukit, Penang; flowers half an inch across, the largest of any Peninsular species), and *P. grandifolia* (leaves over a foot long and six inches wide; flowers pale waxy yellow) (pp. 95–97).
- *P. stellaris* is described as a new species from Penang Hill, Province Wellesley (Tasek Gelugur), and the Tahan River, Pahang; it flowers in February and is "very common on Penang Hill" (p. 98).
- *P. albida* is recorded from the Thaiping Hills, Perak, at altitudes of 1,500 to 4,500 feet, and from Penang Hill at 2,000 feet; it is unique among the Peninsular species in having a half-inferior ovary (p. 96).
- The flowers and rachis of most species contain indigo, turning blue when bruised (p. 93).
- *P. Teta* was the first species of the genus cultivated in England, as early as 1810 (p. 94).

### Conclusion

Ridley establishes that the Malay Peninsula harbours a significant proportion of the world's *Peliosanthes* diversity—seven of the roughly seventeen known species—and that the genus's structural peculiarities (the staminal ring and the exserted blue seed) represent a coherent adaptive syndrome for bird-mediated dispersal in shaded jungle habitats. He closes by noting that the Tahan River region in Pahang appears to be a particularly rich locality, with numerous specimens in the Singapore herbarium still awaiting formal description.

## Context

- Ridley was Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens (1887–1913) and the article reflects his role as the principal botanical authority for the Straits Settlements and the Malay states at the time; his field collections from Penang, Perak, Pahang, and Province Wellesley form the empirical backbone of the work.
- The monograph contributes to the broader colonial-era programme of flora documentation for British Malaya, building on earlier works such as Hooker's *Flora of British India* and Baker's treatment in the *Journal of the Linnean Society*, while extending the known range of the genus into the Peninsula proper.
