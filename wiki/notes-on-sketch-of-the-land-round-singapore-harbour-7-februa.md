# Notes on ‘Sketch of the Land round Singapore Harbour, 7 February 1819’. M. Langdon and Kwa Chong Guan

Nathan Porath's 2011 article applies René Girard's mimetic theory to the *Hikayat Patani*, arguing that this 1839 manuscript functions as a political charter that simultaneously affirms Patani's cosmic similarity with Ayutthaya and fractures its resemblances with Johor. The study reinterprets Patani's two major rebellions against Siam not as proto-nationalist secessionist acts but as episodes of mimetic rivalry within an Indic hierarchical order of kingdom-hood.

## Summary

Porath reads the first book of the *Hikayat Patani*—the portion Bradley identifies as originally oral—as a text structured around two opposing mimetic operations. Toward Ayutthaya, the narrative affirms similarity even in the midst of rebellion: the founding king's ancestors were on a mission to Ayutthaya, the sultans bore Thai titles before conversion, and Sultan Mudhaffar Syah's famous declaration that "the king is not different from us" is read as an explicit mimetic identification rather than a diplomatic pleasantry. Toward Johor, the text performs the opposite operation at every juncture, from the deflation of the Johor raja at the court of Queen Raja Ungu to the final episode in which the envoy Raja Lela is made to confess ignorance of Malay conduct rules, prompting the Johor servant's quip that "it is Johor in the Patani way." The underlying logic, Porath argues, is that Patani's prestige in the Malay-speaking world was derived in part from its mimetic relationship with the superior Indic kingdom of Ayutthaya, and the text works to preserve that distinction from the culturally similar but politically rival Johor.

The article's most significant intervention concerns the first rebellion. Porath re-reads the Malay phrase *dari pada sebuah negeri baiklah dua* ("rather than one country, two is better") not as a statement of Patani's desire for independence from Siam, but as an allusion to the Ayutthaya–Burmese war of the 1560s, when the Burmese king Bayinnaung demanded that the two kingdoms become "one great golden country." In this reading, Sultan Mudhaffar Syah went to Ayutthaya as an ally to preserve its independence from Pegu, and only when he perceived the weakness of King Cakkraphat and his son did he attempt usurpation—a move Porath classifies as a failed Indic succession claim rather than an act of secession. The second rebellion, under Queen Raja Ungu (who rejected her Thai title and assumed the Islamic title Paduka Syah Alam), is interpreted as a wholesale rejection of Ayutthaya's authority following the usurpation of King Prasat Thong, whose legitimacy the Patani court refused to recognise.

The article also addresses the political economy of the kingdom's internal decline. The devolution of power from the Patani centre to the *bendahara* of Sai, and later the appropriation of the throne by Kelantanese dynasts, is framed as a process by which older coastal polities managed and re-appropriated the newer political entity of Patani. The *hikayat*'s portrayal of the Kelantan period as an "age of damnation" in which "the old rules and customs kept on changing continually" is read as a cultural as well as political critique.

### Key Findings

- The *Hikayat Patani* manuscript of 1839 comprises six books composed at different periods between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries; the first book is distinguished by its oral composition and mimetic narrative structure (pp. 46–47).
- Sultan Mudhaffar Syah's attack on the Ayutthaya palace (c. 1564) is confirmed by the *Luang Prasoert* chronicle, which records the sultan riding one of King Cakkraphat's white elephants in the royal plaza, and by van Vliet's account, which identifies the motive as the perceived weakness of the Siamese monarchy under Burmese pressure (pp. 55–56).
- King Prasat Thong mobilised an army of 60,000 men against Patani in the 1630s and requested armed ships from the Dutch in Batavia, which arrived too late to affect the outcome (p. 61).
- Van Vliet's 1644 dispatch to Batavia reported that war was imminent between Patani and Johor and that the Johor raja's mother and relatives in Patani were assumed massacred; a second dispatch in October of the same year reported a Patani envoy had arrived in Johor to settle differences (p. 54).
- Bradley's revision dates the installation of the first Kelantanese dynasty to 1649–1651, with Ayutthayan support, rather than the 1688 date given by Teeuw and Wyatt (p. 52).
- The standard conversion date of 1457 (first proposed by Che Man) is rejected; early sixteenth-century Portuguese visitors found Patani already an established sultanate, but no firm date for the raja's conversion can be established (pp. 48–49).

### Conclusion

Porath's definitive takeaway is that the *Hikayat Patani* should not be read through a modernist ethnicist lens that would interpret its narrative as an assertion of Patani's separateness from Siam or its affinity with other Malay kingdoms. Instead, the text reveals that Patani's kingdomhood was constituted through its mimetic relationship with Ayutthaya—a shared Indic cosmos inherited from its Buddhist past—and that the text's ultimate political message is that the viability of the Patani *kerajaan* depended on the maintenance of hierarchical order and peace with that northern kingdom. The fracturing of resemblances with Johor, by contrast, served to preserve Patani's internal political differentiation within the Malay-speaking world.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Teeuw and Wyatt (1970) edition of the *Hikayat Patani*, the *Luang Prasoert* chronicle, van Vliet's *Siam* (Baker et al. 2005), and Bradley's (2009) historical contextualisation, with supplementary reference to the *Desawarnana* (1365) and the *Sejarah Melayu*.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in applying Girardian mimetic theory and Harrison's concept of "fracturing resemblances" to Malay *hikayat* literature, offering an alternative to both ethnicist and purely political-institutional readings of the text.
