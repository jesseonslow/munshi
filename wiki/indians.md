# Indians

The Indian community in the Malay Peninsula and Borneo constitutes one of the region's most historically layered and internally diverse populations, whose presence spans from the earliest recorded contacts between South Asian traders and the Malay world in the first century A.D. through the colonial plantation economy of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries to the present day [[1, pp. 1–2]](#ref-1). The community is not a monolith: it encompasses Tamil, Malayalee, Ceylonese, Punjabi, and Bengali sub-groups, each with distinct occupational histories, religious affiliations, and social structures [[2, p. 54]](#ref-2), [[3, p. 107]](#ref-3). Its economic footprint has ranged from the Chettiar moneylending networks that financed tin mining and rubber estates [[4, p. 61]](#ref-4) to the rubber plantation labour that formed the demographic backbone of the Indian population in Malaya [[5, p. 87]](#ref-5). Culturally, Indian influence on the Malay world extends from the Sanskrit inscriptions of the Sri Vijaya period to the living traditions of temple worship, traditional medicine, and social reform that persist into the modern era [[1, pp. 6–8]](#ref-1), [[3, pp. 77–109]](#ref-3), [[2, pp. 54–67]](#ref-2).

## Historical Depth and Cultural Transmission

The earliest documented Indian presence in the Malay world is commercial rather than demographic. Wilkinson, writing in 1935, established that Indian traders reached the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra by the first century A.D., as recorded in the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* and Ptolemy's geography, and that they brought with them learned men who converted local populations to Hinduism or Buddhism and introduced Sanskrit vocabulary into daily life [[1, pp. 1–2]](#ref-1). Dutch scholars have argued against the hypothesis of large-scale Indian migration to Java, noting that had Indians settled in numbers they would have flooded local speech with Prakrit words, which they did not [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1). Instead, the transmission was one of ideas, script, and religious practice: the Pallava script appears in inscriptions at Kedah, in the north of Province Wellesley, and at Selensing in Perak, dating from approximately 400 A.D. [[1, p. 2]](#ref-1).

The most consequential political event in this long history, according to Wilkinson, was the arrival of the Shailendra dynasty in the middle years of the eighth century, whose "coming" constituted a single transformative moment in pre-modern Malay political history [[1, pp. 8–9]](#ref-1). The Shailendra brought the *nagari* script, Sanskrit as an official language, and Mahayana Buddhism with a strong Tantric tinge [[1, p. 9]](#ref-1). Their descendants ruled the maritime empire of Sri Vijaya, which by the ninth century controlled the camphor trade at Barus, ruled Kedah, and levied tolls on all merchants passing through the Malacca Straits, the Straits of Sunda, and the land route over the Isthmus of Kra [[1, pp. 10–11]](#ref-1). Arab sources from the early tenth century describe the Maharaja of Zabaj as the wealthiest ruler in the Indies, who symbolically threw gold nuggets into a palace lagoon as a debt to the Ocean [[1, pp. 10–11]](#ref-1).

The Chola invasion of c. 1024–1028, recorded in Rajendra-cola's Tanjore inscription of 1030 A.D., captured Kedah, Sri Vijaya, *Melayu*, Tumasik (Singapore), and the Dindings, and destroyed Gangga-negara and Lenggiu permanently [[1, pp. 13–14]](#ref-1). The subsequent transfer of the capital from Palembang to *Melayu* (Jambi) is the event by which the term *Melayu* became the national name for the entire Malay world, a shift confirmed by Marco Polo's 1292 reference to the "Melayu country" with no mention of Palembang [[1, p. 14]](#ref-1).

In West Borneo, Harrisson's 1949 survey demonstrated that Indian influence was largely indirect, late, and locally modified rather than the product of large-scale settlement [[6, p. 33]](#ref-6). The material record—approximately 115 gold objects, numerous stone and ceramic artefacts, and roughly 50 golden legends—points to a final, fading wave of Indianised culture arriving via Majapahit Java in the mid-fourteenth century, after which Islam gradually absorbed and transformed what remained [[6, pp. 33–110]](#ref-6). The Limbang hoard, with its inscribed ring bearing *Nagari* characters read as "Araksara," provides the only datable epigraphic evidence from West Borneo and anchors the hoard to the thirteenth–fourteenth century [[6, pp. 57–58]](#ref-6).

## Economic Structures and Labour

The colonial period introduced a fundamentally new phase of Indian presence, characterised by large-scale labour migration and the emergence of distinct economic niches. The rubber plantation system created a demographic structure in which the Indian population was overwhelmingly concentrated in the estates, with the small sub-managerial middle class caste apart from the Tamil labouring masses and ethnically distinct, largely Malayalee or Ceylonese [[2, p. 54]](#ref-2). Arasaratnam observed that there was no educational pathway from the proletariat to an elite, and that the English-educated urban middle class which gradually grew had no social connection to plantation workers [[2, p. 54]](#ref-2).

Within this structure, the Nattukottai Chettiar moneylenders occupied a position of extraordinary economic significance. Kratoska documents how a small caste group from Pudukkottai District in South India built a network of branch offices staffed by agents working under three-year contracts with powers of attorney from parent firms in India [[4, p. 61]](#ref-4). The firms operated as Hindu joint family businesses, drawing capital from proprietors, inter-firm deposits, and Western banks, and were bound together by caste-defined social relationships and the communal functions of Murugan temples, which served as venues for information exchange and interest-rate setting [[4, p. 62]](#ref-4). Their lending extended well beyond smallholder agriculture to include financing for Chinese tin mines, commercial trading ventures, and rubber estates, with prominent entrepreneurs such as Yap Ah Loy and Loke Yew among their clients [[4, p. 62]](#ref-4). In 1930, Chettiar outstanding loans in the Federated Malay States amounted to $125 million (Straits dollars), with total capital across British Malaya estimated at slightly over $160 million; by comparison, banknote circulation in 1938 was $105 million [[4, p. 69]](#ref-4).

At the other end of the economic spectrum, the plantation labourer's experience was one of acute vulnerability. During the Japanese Occupation, the Syonan Rubber Syndicate, formed by 18 Japanese companies by the end of 1942, reduced estate labourers' working days to ten to fifteen per month and cut wages by 30 to 40 per cent below pre-war rates [[5, p. 83]](#ref-5). By 1943, the controlled price of one bag of rice was $50, while black-market prices ranged between $100 and $150; a British Intelligence Report stated that "tens and thousands of Indian rubber estate coolies were thrown out of work and many starved to death" [[5, p. 84]](#ref-5). The Indian population of Malayan estates fell by seven per cent during the occupation [[5, p. 85]](#ref-5).

The Indian population in Malaya grew by nearly 80 per cent between 1947 and 1957, driven by internal growth rather than migration, with the largest increases in Singapore; two pieces of legislation passed in 1953 subsequently plateaued Indian immigration [[7, p. 141]](#ref-7).

## Social Reform, Political Organisation, and Community Life

The social reform movements among Malayan Indians between 1930 and 1955 were driven not by the English-educated elite, as in India, but by the Tamil-educated intelligentsia and small business entrepreneurs who operated in a structural void created by the absence of any connected leadership class within the plantation economy [[2, pp. 54–55]](#ref-2). Arasaratnam identified this as a significant structural difference from the reform trajectories in India and Ceylon [[2, p. 55]](#ref-2). The principal reform domains included caste discrimination, particularly the treatment of depressed castes in housing, employment, and temple access; the temperance movement; socio-religious reform targeting practices such as *kavadi*-bearing with self-mortification, blood sacrifice, and fire-walking; and the intractable question of marriage law [[2, pp. 55–65]](#ref-2).

The Mahamariamman Temple at Penang was thrown open to all Hindus in 1935, the first of the major Agamic temples in Malaya to admit depressed castes [[2, p. 55]](#ref-2). The temperance movement took on a fundamentally different character before and after the war: pre-war it was a middle-class imposition on labourers, but post-war it became a worker-led direct action campaign that merged with the trade union movement and gave working-class youths their first experience of autonomous collective action [[2, pp. 57–58]](#ref-2). The police fired at Bedong in February 1947 during a temperance picketing campaign; this incident partly prompted the Federal Legislative Council to establish an Estates Toddy Shops Committee in March 1947 [[2, pp. 57–58]](#ref-2).

In the political sphere, Khoo Kay Kim traced the origins of the "Indian Association Movement" from the 1890s, establishing that the first Indian Association in peninsular Malaysia was founded in Penang in 1892, not 1906 as commonly believed [[8, pp. 3–4]](#ref-8). The Kinta Indian Association, formally registered on 28 April 1911, proved the most resilient of the early bodies; by 1920 it had accumulated $2,000 to purchase an acre of land near Sungai Pari, and Malayan Political Intelligence Bureau reports from 1922 identified Ipoh as the main centre of Indian nationalist activity in the peninsula [[8, pp. 18–21]](#ref-8).

The Rani of Jhansi Regiment, formed by Subhash Chandra Bose in Japanese-occupied Malaya in 1943, represents a distinct chapter in the political history of the Indian community. Datta's analysis challenges the celebratory narratives of elite veterans by demonstrating that approximately 80 per cent of recruits were from the rubber estates, the majority illiterate and second-generation residents, who joined not out of patriotic conviction but to secure regular meals, protection from *kirani* and Japanese soldiers, and a degree of bodily autonomy unavailable on the estates [[5, pp. 87]](#ref-5). After the war, arrangements for boarding school education and post-war support reached only elite recruits; estate women returned to plantation life with no provisions [[5, p. 99]](#ref-5).

In the domestic sphere, Devasahayam's study of cross-ethnic child adoption reveals how diasporic Indian families—predominantly Tamil—received girl children, almost always of Chinese birth, into their households from the late 1920s through the 1960s [[7, pp. 137–51]](#ref-7). This practice represented a fundamental revisioning of what it meant to be "Indian" in the diaspora: cultural constraints that made adoption rare and stigmatised in South Asia—public admission of infertility, son preference, caste endogamy—lost their force in the plural ethnic environment of the Straits Settlements and the Federation [[7, p. 137]](#ref-7). The Catholic Church, particularly convents in Singapore and Selangor, functioned as an informal adoption broker [[7, p. 144]](#ref-7).

The religious and medical traditions of the community maintained a distinct material culture. Colley's 1978 survey documents how plants were simultaneously medicinal agents and objects of religious veneration: the Margosa tree is associated with the goddess Sri Maha Mariamman and believed to check the spread of smallpox; *Tulasi* (sacred basil) represents the wife of Vishnu and was "probably deified in consequence of its medicinal properties" [[3, p. 103]](#ref-3). The interweaving of the pharmacological and the metaphysical is presented as a defining characteristic of the tradition [[3, pp. 100–105]](#ref-3).

At the level of individual elite biography, Sivachandralingam Sundara Raja's study of Thamboosamy Pillai (1850–1902) demonstrates how a Tamil-origin elite occupied a central position in Kuala Lumpur's colonial society from the 1870s, functioning as a critical sociopolitical intermediary between British administrators, the Chinese and Malay elite, and the Indian community [[9, pp. 101–117]](#ref-9). Pillai served as Acting Treasurer of Selangor, was the first Asian member of the Selangor Club, and in partnership with Loke Yew introduced electric pumping to Malayan mining in 1894 [[9, pp. 103–108]](#ref-9). His philanthropy extended to the Victoria Institution, the Mariaman Temple, and the Batu Caves temple complex, where he contributed an estimated $150,000 toward construction [[9, pp. 111–114]](#ref-9).

The Roman Catholic Church established a distinct rural mission strategy among Indian communities, exemplified by the St. Joseph's Mission near Bagan Serai, Perak, founded by the French priest Michael Maria Fee in the 1880s as an agricultural settlement exclusively for Indian Catholics [[10, p. 74]](#ref-10). By January 1889, the settlement comprised at least 101 families with a total population of 430, a school serving 170 students, and approximately 450 acres of cleared land for padi cultivation [[10, p. 74]](#ref-10).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on the Indian community reveals a clear evolution in scholarly priorities across nearly a century of publication. The earliest contributions, such as Wilkinson's 1935 survey of early Indian influence, were framed within the colonial administrative tradition and concerned themselves with the deep historical roots of Indian cultural transmission to the Malay world [[1, pp. 1–16]](#ref-1). Harrisson's 1949 study of gold and Indian influences in West Borneo extended this archaeological and epigraphic approach to a region previously dismissed as culturally peripheral, establishing the south-west corner of the island as the primary zone of Indianised material culture [[6, pp. 33–110]](#ref-6).

The post-war period saw a shift toward the social and political history of the Indian community in the colonial and immediate post-independence era. Arasaratnam's 1967 study of social reform was among the first to apply M. N. Srinivas's concepts of Sanskritisation and Dravidianisation to the Malayan Indian context, and it challenged the prevailing assumption that the English-educated middle class was the natural leader of all Indian reform [[2, pp. 54–55]](#ref-2). Khoo Kay Kim's 1992 article on the Indian Association Movement similarly redirected attention from plantation labour to the English-educated professional and clerical classes, establishing a direct genealogical link between the 1906 associations and the later Central Indian Association of Malaya [[8, pp. 3–24]](#ref-8).

More recent scholarship has moved further into the private and subaltern spheres. Devasahayam's 2020 study of cross-ethnic adoption extends the literature on ethnic boundary permeability by documenting a specific domestic practice that reveals how diasporic communities negotiated and redefined cultural identity from below [[7, pp. 137–51]](#ref-7). Datta's 2015 article on the Rani of Jhansi Regiment applies Michel-Rolph Trouillot's framework of manufactured silences to challenge the celebratory narratives of elite veterans, foregrounding class, caste, and language as structuring forces within the regiment [[5, pp. 77–103]](#ref-5). Kratoska's 2013 study of Chettiar moneylending fills a gap identified in the literature: while Chettiar lending in Burma has been extensively studied, no detailed account of Chettiar loan operations in Malaya had previously been published [[4, p. 61]](#ref-4). Sivachandralingam Sundara Raja's 2021 article on Thamboosamy Pillai engages with the historiographical debate over inclusivity in Malaysian national history, positioning itself against the tendency of "inclusive" scholarship to privilege non-elite perspectives at the expense of elite colonial actors [[9, pp. 101–102]](#ref-9).

The methodological range of the Society's literature on this subject is correspondingly broad, encompassing epigraphic and archaeological analysis, colonial administrative records, oral history, legislative proceedings, and ethnographic observation. The primary archival collections drawn upon include the National Archives of Singapore, the Malaysian National Archives in Kuala Lumpur, the British National Archives at Kew, the Sarawak Museum, and the Franks Collection in the British Museum [[7, p. 143]](#ref-7), [[4, p. 62]](#ref-4), [[6, p. 48]](#ref-6), [[5, p. 87]](#ref-5), [[8, p. 3]](#ref-8). This diversity of source base reflects the multiplicity of the Indian community itself—its economic, religious, linguistic, and regional diversity—and the correspondingly varied approaches required to document it.
