# Seven months in the tin country, Perak (Malacca Peninsula), 1881. . Dyer

J. Errington de la Croix, a French mining engineer, spent seven months in the state of Perak in 1880–81 and recorded his observations in a detailed account originally published in French in 1885; Colin Dyer's 2020 English translation for the *JMBRAS* makes this a valuable primary source on the tin-mining economy, colonial administration, and indigenous peoples of early-Protectorate Malaya.

## Summary

De la Croix arrived at Telok Kartang on 27 August 1880 after a five-month sojourn in Aceh, where he had been detained by Dutch authorities following the assassination of two French scientists (pp. 143–144). He was received at Thaipeng by Major Swinburne and later at Kwala Kangsa by the Resident, Hugh Low, who provided him with surveying equipment, elephants, and letters of introduction to local chiefs (pp. 145–148). Over the following months he surveyed the Larout, Kourow, Pluss, Kerbow, Kinta, and Batang Padang districts, recording river courses, mountain elevations, and mining operations that did not yet appear on any English map (pp. 152–163).

The account is rich in ethnographic and economic detail. De la Croix describes the Sakaye ("orang utan") people of Kerbow in considerable depth, noting their distinct language, blowpipe weaponry, bark clothing, and their subjugation by neighbouring Malay chiefs who treated them as slaves (pp. 157–159). He records the tin industry's explosive growth: from thirty mining sites two years earlier to 155, with production rising from 2,054 tons in 1876 to 7,149 tons in 1882 (p. 146). His geological observations include two isolated limestone monoliths—Gounong Pondoq (500 m) and Gounong Kourow (300 m)—transformed into white marble veined with red, standing alone amid granitoid country (pp. 147, 153).

The closing paragraphs survey the Protectorate's rapid modernisation between 1881 and 1885: the completion of Port Weld and its railway to Thaipeng, the opening of Tamil coolie immigration to Malaya, the establishment of surveying and meteorological services, and the advance of British influence toward the northern frontier at Raman (pp. 163–165).

### Key Findings

- Tin production in Perak rose from 2,054 tons (1876) to 7,149 tons (1882) and 10,272 tons (1884), driven by Chinese alluvial mining at 155 sites (pp. 146, 163).
- The Kong Loon Kongsi mine at Thaipeng employed nearly 1,200 workers, had already yielded 200,000 dollars in net profits, and was the first operation to install European steam engines (p. 146).
- De la Croix encountered approximately twenty Sakaye individuals at Kampong Tchabang on the Kerbow River, recording their distinct dialect, blowpipe-and-dart armament, and inability to count beyond three (pp. 158–159).
- The Malay agricultural system of cyclical village abandonment—clearing, burning, two years of cultivation, then five years of jungle regrowth—explains the numerous deserted kampongs encountered throughout the interior (p. 162).
- The Kourow River valley, approximately 16 km long and 8 km wide, was entirely unmapped before de la Croix's survey, which he conducted using a 500-metre baseline and a graduated-circle compass (pp. 152–153).
- The British annexed Pangkor Island and the adjacent Dinding coast in 1874–75 as a strategic deep-water port to rival Penang (p. 160).

### Conclusion

De la Croix's account provides a comprehensive snapshot of Perak at the moment of its colonial transformation, documenting simultaneously the indigenous social structures, the Chinese mining economy in its ascendancy, and the British administrative apparatus that was rapidly remaking the state's infrastructure and political geography. His closing projection—that British domination would extend "from Cape Romania to the territory of Tenasserim"—proved substantially correct within two decades.

## Context

- The original French text appeared in the *Bulletin de la société de géographie* (7th series, no. 10, 1885, pp. 394–432), with accompanying maps published in 1883; the JMBRAS translation (2020) includes those maps (pp. 143, 165–167).
- The article was written by a French mining engineer travelling with the explorer-diplomat Xavier Brau de Saint Pol Lias, reflecting France's colonial interest in the Malay Peninsula during the period of British consolidation (p. 143).
