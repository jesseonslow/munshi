# Clementi and the Colonial Office: Tensions over Decentralisation Policy in the Malay States, 1930–34

## Abstract

Cecil Clementi was Governor of the Straits Settlements and High Commissioner for the Malay States from 1930 to 1934. This article looks at the tensions that arose between Clementi and the Colonial Office over his implementation of a decentralisation policy for the Malay States during this period. It provides important context to existing histories that have reflected the negative narrative of Clementi’s character that emerged amongst his critics and some administrative colleagues during his tenure. These histories have apportioned the greater responsibility to Clementi for creating the strains and eventual breakdowns of relationships with Colonial Office officials, particularly with Philip Cunliffe-Lister, Secretary of State for the Colonies, from 1931 onwards. This article offers a fresh understanding of Clementi’s actions by exploring the key influences on his thinking, and the broader circumstances, both in the United Kingdom and Malaya, affecting his interactions with the Colonial Office.

## Summary

This article examines the breakdown in relations between High Commissioner Cecil Clementi and the Colonial Office over his decentralisation policy for the Malay States during 1930–34. Urwin challenges the established historiographical narrative—advanced by Heussler and Smith—that attributes the conflict primarily to Clementi's autocratic temperament and dogmatic approach. Instead, he argues that a confluence of structural and political factors shaped the outcome: Clementi's formative experience of Chinese nationalism in Hong Kong, his anticipation of a closing window of opportunity for constitutional change, the troubled passage of the India Reform Bill through Parliament, and the personal dynamics of his relationships with successive Secretaries of State. Drawing on Clementi's own papers at the Bodleian Libraries and the Colonial Office and Malaya Migrated Archives at The National Archives, the article reconstructs the sequence of decisions, miscommunications, and political pressures that led to Clementi's departure from Malaya.

The core argument is that Clementi's belief he held an assurance of sympathetic support from Lord Passfield—based on informal weekend discussions in early 1931—led him to implement his proposals with a speed and decisiveness that foreclosed wider constitutional debate. When Passfield was replaced by Cunliffe-Lister, the new Secretary of State faced a dilemma: the FMS Sultans' expectations had already been raised, yet the CO now held reservations about the policy. Cunliffe-Lister's response was to slow and dilute Clementi's plans rather than reverse them, a decision influenced by the parallel constitutional crisis over India. The Wilson Report of 1933 provided the political solution, publicly endorsing decentralisation within the FMS while shelving the broader pan-Malayan federation that had been Clementi's central vision.

### Key Findings

- The 1931 census revealed a Chinese population of 1,709,392 exceeding the Malay population of 1,644,173, a fact that informed Clementi's suspicion of Chinese nationalism and his insistence on Malay sovereignty as a political buffer (p. 49).
- Clementi's Durbar address of 18 August 1931, published in *The Times* on 20 August, announced the decentralisation plan without prior CO authorisation; Passfield's secret telegram of 25 August cautioned that he had "not committed myself, still less my colleagues, to any particular measures" (pp. 39–40).
- The Wilson Report (Cmd. 4276, March 1933) concluded that "from a political and financial point of view, there is a strong case for adopting a policy of decentralization" but placed pan-Malayan federation on the backburner, stating that day was "a long way off before it will be practical or desirable" (pp. 45–46).
- Cunliffe-Lister's telegram of 10 February 1932 excoriated Clementi for stating publicly that the new policy was "politically essential" and "a debt of honour upon British Administrators," which he could only accept as an unauthorised personal opinion (p. 43).
- Clementi's proposal for a pan-Malayan Customs Union was formally killed when the Straits Settlements Legislative Council's Committee Report deemed it opposed to the Colony's interests due to the impact on entrepot free-trade revenue; the *Malaya Tribune* declared it "dead, and dead beyond resurrection" (p. 45).
- Clementi's handwritten note of his 26 April 1934 meeting with Cunliffe-Lister documents the Secretary of State pressing him to resign, though no final decision was made; his subsequent diagnosis of diabetes in early May 1934 effectively determined his retirement from colonial service at age 59 (p. 47).

### Conclusion

Urwin's definitive takeaway is that Clementi's decentralisation policy failed not because of personal failings alone but because of a structural convergence: his fast-footed implementation foreclosed debate, the CO's initial non-objection was mistaken for endorsement, the India Reform Bill created a political environment in which parallel constitutional change in Malaya was untenable, and the Unfederated States' Sultans remained unwilling to join a wider federation. The Malay States consequently remained in their separate FMS and Unfederated structures until the Malayan Union of 1948, which Urwin identifies as an important stepping-stone towards modern Malaysia's federal constitutional monarchy.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: The National Archives, Kew (CO, FCO, and FO series); Oxford Bodleian Libraries, Papers of Sir Cecil Clementi (MSS. Ind. Ocn. s. 352); UK Parliamentary Papers including Hansards and Cmd. 4276.
- Historiographical contribution: The article revises the character-based explanations offered by Heussler (1981) and Smith (1995) by situating Clementi's actions within broader political and institutional dynamics, particularly the India Reform Bill and the personal dynamics of the Passfield-to-Cunliffe-Lister transition in the Secretary of State's office.
