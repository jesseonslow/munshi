# Managerial influences in a Johore village

K.O.L. Burridge's 1957 ethnographic study examines the distribution of power and influence in a long-established village on the west coast of Johore, arguing that traditional kin-based and religious structures have successfully absorbed newer political institutions—particularly the Local Council and national political parties—rather than being displaced by them. The article demonstrates how the concept of *pandai* (political acumen expressed as equivocation and status-quo maintenance) operates as the governing logic of village management, and how the entry of Chinese political representation through the M.C.A. represents the most significant challenge to this equilibrium.

## Summary

Burridge's central concern is to identify and evaluate the sources of power and influence that enable village actors to persuade others toward particular courses of action. The village, with approximately 370 inhabitants (250 Malay, the remainder Chinese and one Indian family), is organised around agnatic descent from a Bugis Prince P who settled there in the early nineteenth century, bringing with him a claimed agnatic line from the royal house of Malacca. The dominant agnatic group commands loyalty through a combination of Islamic hierarchy, kin solidarity (*saudara*), and the traditional expectation that the community present a united front. The Penghulu of the mukim, who resides in the village, is a younger brother of the senior agnate—a division of authority that creates a potential cleavage but which in practice results in co-operation, the younger brother deriving his authority from the state-wide political structure while the elder commands day-to-day village life through kin relationships.

The article's most sustained argument concerns the concept of *pandai* in its political sense: the deliberate avoidance of positive decision-making, the playing of rivals against each other, and the maintenance of the status quo as a positive value. Burridge traces this to the historical position of Malays between more powerful political entities, and shows how it is reinforced by kin obligations (positive decisions risk estranging part of one's support network), Islamic norms (which provide a structure of ideals but lack independent enforcement mechanisms), and the proverbial culture that relieves individuals of the burden of novel decision-making. The Local Council, formed in 1953 under the Local Councils Ordinance, is shown to have "slid neatly into the existing managerial structure": its Malay members are all agnates or closely allied to the dominant group, and the Chairman (the headmaster) owes his position to the agnates' good offices.

The article's final section identifies the forces that might disrupt this equilibrium: the growing influence of Malay women (particularly through the schoolmistress, whose cosmopolitan home life represents a new model for village girls), the emerging political consciousness of the Chinese through the M.C.A., and the potential of the national political parties to introduce new dynamics. Burridge notes that the Chinese members of the Local Council operate almost entirely outside the system of Malay political and kin values, and that the pig-rearing dispute (in which the Chinese successfully invoked M.C.A. connections to override Malay religious objections) illustrates the tension between Islamic norms and Chinese economic interests.

### Key Findings

- The village contains 41 Malay households and 20 Chinese households; the Penghulu is the tenth to hold the office in the village or cross-river area, all descended from the Bugis Prince P (p. 96, p. 104).
- The Local Council, established in 1953 under the Local Councils Ordinance (Federation of Malaya Publication No. 36 of 1952), comprises five elected Malays, two Chinese nominated by the District Officer, and the Penghulu as ex officio observer (p. 97).
- Of the seven wealthiest Malays in the village, five take little or no part in management; two of these attempted and failed election to the Local Council, demonstrating that wealth alone does not confer political influence (p. 108).
- The Naib-Kathi (Imam) performs wedding ceremonies, registers divorces, and acts as an arbitrator; the post carries no salary and cannot support a household on its perquisites alone (p. 98).
- A dispute over pig-rearing (a religious issue for Malays, an economic livelihood for Chinese) was resolved in favour of the Chinese when they recruited the aid of wealthy M.C.A. members from the nearby town, though the Malays remained bitter (p. 114, n. 46).
- The Penghulu's office is described as "in a state of flux" and "under review" at the time of writing, with Penghulus generally on the defensive against inroads by the central administration (pp. 95–96, p. 113).

### Conclusion

Burridge's definitive takeaway is that the traditional Malay political structure—rooted in agnation, Islamic hierarchy, and kin solidarity—possesses a remarkable capacity for absorption: it has incorporated the Local Council, the national political parties, and even the presence of Chinese political actors without fundamental reorganisation. The governing principle is *pandai* as status-quo maintenance, and the system's coherence depends on the interconsistency of kin values, religious norms, and the avoidance of positive decision. The most significant threats to this equilibrium are identified as the emerging political agency of Malay women and the growing assertiveness of the Chinese political community, both of which operate outside the traditional framework of kin and religious obligation.

## Context

- Burridge was a British colonial administrator in Johore at the time of writing; the article draws on his direct administrative experience and fieldwork in the village, and he references his own earlier work on rural administration in Johore (p. 94, n. 8).
- The study contributes to the early post-independence ethnographic literature on Malay village politics, complementing Burridge's companion piece "The Malay Composition of a Village in Johore" (JMBRAS 29(3), 1956) and engaging with the historiography of Johore's political system as modernised by Sultan Abu Bakar (r. 1862–95).
