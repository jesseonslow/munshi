# Boo Chih Fu and the first Malaysian Communist Party split

Marc Opper's 2019 article examines the first major internal split within the Malayan Communist Party (MCP), centring on the figure of Boo Chih Fu, a Hainanese cadre whose political line and subsequent purge in 1936 set in motion a chain of events that fundamentally weakened the Party's leadership cadre for the rest of the colonial period. Published in *JMBRAS* Vol. 92(1), the piece reconstructs the factional dynamics of the early MCP using a substantial body of Chinese-language archival and memoir sources alongside British colonial records, arguing that the 1936 purge was not merely a routine disciplinary action but a decisive power consolidation by Lai Teck and the Hainanese faction that left the Party structurally vulnerable as it entered the Emergency.

## Summary

The article addresses a long-standing gap in the historiography of the MCP: the circumstances and consequences of the Party's first internal split, which has been poorly understood in English-language scholarship. Opper reconstructs the political context in which Boo Chih Fu operated, showing that his advocacy for a patient, long-term strategy of winning over the ethnic Chinese elite through underground work was mischaracterised by his opponents as "rightist opportunist deviation." This labelling provided the pretext for his murder and the broader purge of non-Hainanese cadres from the Central Committee.

The narrative traces how the purge enabled Lai Teck to reconstitute the Party's leadership at the Fifth Enlarged Plenum in September 1936, installing himself as deputy secretary and surrounding himself with junior members whose positions depended on his patronage. Opper argues that this reconstitution was the critical turning point: it removed the very cadres who had been pushing for greater organisational activity and replaced them with loyalists lacking the political experience and strategic vision of their predecessors. The long-term consequence was that by the time Lai Teck disappeared in 1947, no veteran China-trained communists remained in the MCP's upper ranks, leaving the Party ill-prepared for the demands of the 1948 Emergency.

Opper's argument is informed by a wide range of primary sources, including MCP internal documents, British Special Branch assessments, Chinese provincial and county-level party history compilations, and memoirs of former MCP members. The article situates the 1936 split within the broader context of the Chinese Communist Party's own internal struggles during the same period, drawing on sources from the Dongjiang base area and the Dabu and Puning counties of Guangdong to illuminate the training and ideological formation of the MCP's founding cadre.

### Key Findings

- The Fifth Enlarged Plenum of the MCP was convened on 1–8 September 1936 in Muar, Johore, immediately following the purge of Boo Chih Fu and other cadres (p. 59).
- At the Plenum, Ts'ai Pai-yun (operating under the name Chua Bun Seng) was appointed Party secretary and Lai Teck was appointed deputy secretary; the remainder of the Central Committee was selected by Lai Teck from relatively junior members (p. 59).
- The Plenum issued a manifesto that denounced by name seven current Party members and five likely former Central Committee members (p. 59).
- Boo Chih Fu's political line advocated greater underground activity designed as a "long game" to win over members of the ethnic Chinese elite in Malaya, rather than immediate large-scale strikes or a putsch (p. 59).
- By the time Lai Teck disappeared in 1947, no veteran China-trained communists remained in the upper ranks of the MCP, a condition that significantly hampered the Party as it entered the Emergency in 1948 (p. 59).
- The British Special Branch's *Basic Paper on the Malayan Communist Party* (1950) concluded that the murders "deprived the Party at the time of good leaders, since those who were killed were the very men who were complaining of ineptitude and were agitating for greater activity" (p. 59).

### Conclusion

Opper's definitive takeaway is that the 1936 purge, while appearing at the time to restore Party unity under Lai Teck, was in fact a catastrophic loss of institutional memory and strategic capacity. The removal of experienced, China-trained cadres in favour of politically loyal but less capable junior members created a structural deficit that the MCP never recovered, leaving it poorly equipped to meet the military and political challenges of the post-war period and the Emergency.

## Context

- The article draws extensively on Chinese-language primary sources including Guangdong Provincial Archive materials, Dabu County and Puning City party history compilations, and memoirs of former MCP members (Fong Chong Pik, Luo Ming, Quek Jin Teck), alongside British colonial sources (Shanghai Municipal Police Files, Special Branch documents) and Japanese-language scholarship (Hara Fujio).
- The study's principal historiographical contribution is the integration of mainland Chinese archival sources on the Dongjiang base area and the training of the MCP's founding cadre with the existing English-language literature on the MCP, thereby providing a more complete account of the 1936 split than was previously available in Western-language scholarship.
