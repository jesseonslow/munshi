# Tom Harrisson

## Early Life and Formative Years

Tom Harrisson (1912–1976) was a British anthropologist, archaeologist, ornithologist, and conservationist whose career in Borneo transformed the region's natural and cultural heritage. He served as Government Ethnologist and Curator of the Sarawak Museum from 1945 to 1966, a tenure during which he expanded the institution into one of the finest museums and libraries in Southeast Asia [[1, p. 147]](#ref-1). His work spanned prehistoric archaeology, ornithology, ethnography, and conservation, and he was recognised with the title Dato in Sarawak [[1, p. 147]](#ref-1).

Harrisson's scientific interests were evident from an early age. As a schoolboy he participated in the 1928 *British Birds* heronry census and conducted local studies of Middlesex birds [[2, p. 143]](#ref-2). In the early 1930s he joined Oxford University expeditions to Lapland, Sarawak, and the New Hebrides, experiences that would shape his lifelong commitment to the natural history of tropical regions [[2, p. 143]](#ref-2). His first encounter with the Kelabit people of Sarawak's Fifth Division came through a 1949 BBC broadcast, which prompted Professor Neville Haile to apply for a posting to Sarawak and established a personal connection that would endure for decades [[3, p. 145]](#ref-3).

## The Sarawak Museum and Ethnographic Work

Upon his appointment in 1945, Harrisson inherited a small prewar institution and set about its systematic transformation. He oversaw the renovation of the museum building and the construction of a new office and workshop in 1953–54, and established a programme of regular scholarly publication that yielded over four hundred pages of articles annually in the Museum Journal [[1, p. 147]](#ref-1). Benedict Sandin, who served as Junior Assistant Curator under Harrisson from 1952 to 1966, recalled a demanding and sometimes bullying temperament balanced by genuine consideration for his staff, illustrated by an incident in the late 1950s when a lost collector was searched for with the help of surrounding villages and, upon his return, was granted two days' leave with money from Harrisson's own pocket [[1, pp. 147–148]](#ref-1).

Harrisson's ethnographic work was inseparable from his natural history interests. He collected extensively in the Kelabit uplands, and his particular devotion to the Kelabit people was such that he was given magistrate's powers over them [[1, p. 148]](#ref-1). His 1949 BBC broadcast on the Kelabit demonstrated his ability to communicate complex cultural material to a wide audience [[3, p. 145]](#ref-3). In one dramatic episode, Harrisson mounted a courtroom demonstration that secured the acquittal of two Kelabit men wrongly convicted of murder [[3, p. 145]](#ref-3). His last administrative act concerning Sarawak was to convert his "Widows and Orphans Pension Fund" into a Trust for the Kelabit peoples, and a letter written four days before his death described his intention to establish a scholarship trust for the same community [[3, p. 146]](#ref-3), [[1, p. 148]](#ref-1).

## Ornithology and Natural History

Harrisson's ornithological career was long and productive. The Loke Wan Tho/Tom Harrisson collection, funded by Dato Loke Wan Tho from 1951 to 1954, yielded approximately 7,300 bird skins, of which 3,418 were deposited at the Peabody Museum, Yale [[2, p. 144]](#ref-2). This collection served as a decisive stimulus for B.E. Smythies's *Birds of Borneo* [[2, p. 144]](#ref-2). Harrisson maintained a continuing interest in swiftlets and contributed to A.L. Thomson's *New dictionary of birds*, while his later ornithological publications addressed sea-birds, forest understorey birds, and the Tree Sparrow's invasion of Brunei [[2, p. 144]](#ref-2). Lord Medway, writing in 1976, observed that Harrisson's ethnological and ornithological interests were inseparable in practice [[2, p. 144]](#ref-2). His research also extended to orangutans and the rare lizard *Lanthanotus* [[3, p. 145]](#ref-3).

## Archaeology at Niah Caves

The most significant achievement of Harrisson's archaeological career was the excavation at the Great Cave at Niah in Sarawak, begun in 1954 and continued annually until 1966 [[4, p. 149]](#ref-4). The site revealed a habitation deposit extending back at least 40,000 years into the Pleistocene. The deeper strata yielded Palaeolithic chopping tools comparable to the Indian Sohan culture and a human skull dated to nearly 40,000 years ago, examined by Dr. D.R. Brothwell and identified as a boy of about fifteen with Negritoid affinities, generally accepted as the earliest positively dated record of *Homo sapiens* [[4, p. 149]](#ref-4). A spectacular find of boat-shaped wooden coffin burials with red haematite wall paintings was also made in an untouched cave at Niah [[4, p. 150]](#ref-4).

External funding for the Niah research came from the Borneo Shell Companies and the Gulbenkian Foundation of Lisbon, administered through a government-ordered committee; a burst of publications by multiple authors between 1960 and 1967 reflected this concentrated support [[5, p. 46]](#ref-5). The Brunei rebellion of 1962–63, which spilled into Sarawak, pulled Harrisson into civil service duties and effectively ended his direct fieldwork at Niah; thereafter, excavations were carried out by staff and associates, with Barbara Harrisson responsible for fieldwork until Tom's retirement in 1967 [[5, pp. 44–45]](#ref-5).

Barbara Harrisson's 1977 inventory of her husband's unpublished papers revealed a vast body of material distributed across institutional and private files. She identified three distinct categories: government reporting (including correspondence with the Borneo Shell Companies and the Gulbenkian Foundation), personal reporting (largely handwritten in exercise books at the Niah base camp between 1958 and 1963, representing reflective thinking intended as an anchor for a monograph he never completed), and site reporting (distributed across the Great Cave, Lobang Kain Hitam, and Lobang Jeragan, containing the descriptive and analytical data that would form the empirical core of any future publication) [[5, pp. 41–51]](#ref-5). She noted an apparent discrepancy of some five thousand years between Harrisson's references to the deep skull's date in 1972 and 1976, suggesting that a correction may have stemmed from new interpretive approaches he was unable to document before his death [[5, p. 50]](#ref-5).

## The Santubong Iron Industry and Early Historic Trade

In the early historic field, Harrisson discovered the Santubong site in 1952, with evidence of early iron smelting and T'ang and Sung Dynasty ceramics, and a more productive trading-post site at the Sarawak River delta in 1954, indicating a commercial link between China and India spanning roughly 700 to 1350 A.D. [[4, p. 149]](#ref-4). The Santubong sites—Jaong and Bongkissam—present a distinctive challenge to conventional categories: dense iron slag deposits coexist with sandstone boulders bearing anthropomorphic and geometric markings, and with what O'Connor and Harrisson termed a "Tantric shrine" [[6, p. 4]](#ref-6). The site of Jaong, on the right bank of the Sarawak River at the foot of Mt. Santubong, contains dense reefs of iron slag alongside thirty large sandstone boulders marked with anthropomorphic figures or geometric patterns [[6, p. 4]](#ref-6).

Harrisson's 1955 excavation at Bongkissam recovered 49,393 Chinese stoneware sherds, 66,668 earthenware sherds, and 659 glass beads from a single small section of the major iron-working site [[6, p. 5]](#ref-6). The most puzzling empirical feature, as O'Connor argued, is the profligate quantity of broken Chinese stoneware sherds found mixed with smelting waste—ceramics that were highly valued objects in local ritual and trade economies yet appear shattered and embedded in slag-rich soil on a scale that defies straightforward economic explanation [[6, p. 5]](#ref-6). O'Connor speculated that their presence may point to some form of ceremony or ritual associated with the iron-working process itself [[6, p. 5]](#ref-6). A second interpretive problem concerns the clay cylinders recovered in large numbers from the iron-rich trenches; in the original two-volume report, O'Connor and Harrisson tentatively identified these as crucibles associated with an Indian "wootz" steelmaking process, but O'Connor later conceded that informed opinion favours their identification as tuyeres [[6, p. 6]](#ref-6). Santubong functioned as a coastal trading station and iron-smelting centre from sometime after the tenth century until its collapse in the fourteenth century [[6, p. 2]](#ref-6).

## Indian Influences and Methodological Legacy

Harrisson's approach to protohistoric Southeast Asia was characterised by an instinct to read indigenous social and economic processes into the archaeological record rather than imposing a fixed stylistic template. Alastair Lamb, who first met Harrisson in 1959 while working on the same site at Chandi Bukit Batu Pahat in Kedah, recounted how Harrisson immediately identified the stone pillar bases as indicative of a mixed timber-and-brick construction analogous to Balinese temple architecture—a reading that Brian Peacock's later revised reconstruction confirmed, vindicating Harrisson's original intuition [[7, pp. 9–10]](#ref-7). Lamb contrasted this with the "antiquarian method" of H.G. Quaritch Wales, whose rigid model of direct Indian cultural transmission from South India to Angkor privileged style, iconography, and epigraphy at the expense of sustained study of small finds and material culture in their full archaeological and ethnographic context [[7, pp. 8–13]](#ref-7).

Harrisson's four-level framework for bead study—archaeological context, physical-chemical constitution, typological distribution, and anthropological or ethnographic value—anticipated modern material culture analysis [[7, p. 12]](#ref-7). His Kuching collection confirmed that the Kelabit's most valued chevron beads are of an "old" pattern (Venetian, pre-eighteenth century) also prized in West Africa and the Americas [[7, p. 13]](#ref-7). His chronology for Chinese export ceramics in Borneo deviated so far from the accepted sequence that a dual system was required to reconcile it with the wider Southeast Asian picture [[7, p. 9]](#ref-7).

## Conservation and the Survival Service Commission

Harrisson's conservation work extended well beyond Sarawak. His appointment to the IUCN Survival Service Commission in 1963 opened a new phase in this work, building on his earlier Sarawak turtle conservation scheme and his writings on orang-utans [[8, p. 71]](#ref-8). He served as Chairman of the SSC's Orang-utan Specialist Group, where he and his wife Barbara devised the Orang-utan Recovery Scheme (OURS) to rehabilitate illegally imported animals seized by customs authorities, and as first vice-chairman and later co-chairman of the Marine Turtle Group [[8, p. 71]](#ref-8). His 1969 visit to the Philippines with Charles Lindbergh was perhaps his most notable single achievement in the region: the pair established a locally based, officially supported conservation structure for the tamaraw and the monkey-eating eagle that remained intact at the time of his death [[8, p. 71]](#ref-8).

## Legacy and Documentation

Tom Harrisson died in a road accident near Bangkok in January 1976, at the age of sixty-four [[3, p. 146]](#ref-3), [[1, p. 147]](#ref-1). Haile lamented that one who had survived wartime and exploration risks was killed in such a manner [[3, p. 146]](#ref-3). His final public act was a plea for Malaysian forest preservation delivered at the invitation of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society weeks before his death [[3, p. 146]](#ref-3).

The Society's journals published a substantial body of memorial tributes in 1976 and 1977, reflecting the breadth of Harrisson's contributions. Lord Medway commemorated his ornithological career [[2]](#ref-2); Tweedie surveyed his archaeological achievements [[4]](#ref-4); Sandin recalled his twenty-one years as Government Ethnologist and Curator [[1]](#ref-1); Haile offered personal glimpses of his character [[3]](#ref-3); O'Connor reflected on the Santubong iron industry [[6]](#ref-6); Lamb assessed his methodological legacy in the context of Indian influences in early Southeast Asia [[7]](#ref-7); and Fitter traced his role in the Survival Service Commission [[8]](#ref-8). Barbara Harrisson's 1977 article provided the most detailed account of his unpublished legacy, proposing a structured, regionally-led path for the recovery and publication of the Niah materials [[5]](#ref-5).

Harrisson's major publications include *The Prehistory of Sabah* (1969–70), *The Prehistoric Iron Workings of Sarawak* (1969), *Gold and the Megalithic in Borneo* (1970), and *The Prehistory of Borneo* (1970) [[4, p. 150]](#ref-4). The primary excavation reports on Santubong were published as Harrisson and O'Connor, *Excavations of the Prehistoric Iron Industry in West Borneo* (Cornell Data Paper 72, 1969) and *Gold and Megalithic Activity in Prehistoric and Recent West Borneo* (Data Paper 77, 1970) [[6]](#ref-6). His field notebooks remain in the Sarawak Museum in nearly illegible handwriting [[1, p. 148]](#ref-1), and the Lobang Jeragan ceramic analysis, undertaken by Germaine Woodfield in Miri, remained entirely unpublished due to an unresolved argument between Woodfield and Harrisson over her interpretations [[5, p. 49]](#ref-5). The Lobang Kain Hitam files and photographic prints were transferred to the Brunei Museum in 1976–77 for a planned joint write-up, while all artefacts and original film remained in Sarawak [[5, p. 49]](#ref-5).
