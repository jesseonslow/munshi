# Penang Malay

A.W. Hamilton's 1922 article "Penang Malay" is a descriptive linguistic survey of the colloquial Malay spoken in the Northern Settlement, produced for the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society. Hamilton argues that what outsiders perceive as a distinct "Penang Malay" is in fact the Malay of Kedah modified by the cosmopolitan demands of a port town with a large South Indian population, and he systematically catalogues the phonological, morphological, and lexical divergences from the "Singapore Malay" (Johore-based) variety that a textbook-trained speaker would encounter.

## Summary

Hamilton's central concern is practical: a speaker of "correct" textbook Malay arriving in Penang finds himself unable to follow everyday conversation, and the article is structured to bridge that gap. He identifies six broad categories of difference between Penang and Singapore Malay—harshness of pronunciation, the alteration of final *l* to *i*, clipping of common words, peculiar idioms and constructions, words confined to Kedah, and the inclusion of Indian-origin vocabulary—and treats each in turn with copious examples drawn from observed speech.

The phonological discussion is the most detailed. Hamilton documents how final *r* is pronounced with a "steely ring" that makes it nearly indistinguishable from final *k*, so that *ayér* (water) sounds like *ayák* (sieve) and *dengar* (to hear) like *dengák*. Words ending in *-ir* shift to *-iak* (*pikir* → *pikiak*), and final *-s* is shortened to a sound approximating *-ih* (*beras* → *beraih*). The substitution of *i* for final *l* is treated as "one of the most puzzling features," with a full list of roughly sixty common words affected, including *kapal* → *kapai*, *mahal* → *mahai*, and *katil* → *kate* (where the penultimate *i* shifts to *e* and the *l* is elided entirely).

The idiomatic section reveals a grammar that departs significantly from the standard. The verb *dok* (from *duduk*, "to sit") functions as a progressive marker, replacing *sedang* or *dalam* ("I was eating" = *saya dok makan nasi*). A prefix *pe-* creates causative or inchoative verbs (*pebaik* "to mend," *pekechi* "to make smaller," *pehabis* "to finish absolutely"). The word *dan* means "to have time to" (*ta' dan* = "no time to"), and when reduplicated (*dan-dan*) means "straightaway." The interrogative *awat* (a contraction of *apa buat*) replaces *mengapa* as the universal "why." Plural is formed by the suffix *pa* attached to pronouns (*sepa* = "we," *depa* = "they").

The lexical tables that occupy the final third of the article are the most substantial component. Hamilton provides a running comparison of Penang and Singapore terms for the same referent, revealing that Penang Malay retains a large body of archaic or regionally specific vocabulary—*chak* for *pipit* (sparrow), *golok* for *parang* (chopper), *kopiak* for *songkok* (cap), *nyior* for *kelapa* (coconut)—while simultaneously absorbing a significant stratum of Tamil and Hindustani loanwords (*chamcha* for spoon, *cha* for tea, *lawak* for joking, *poni* for a small vessel, *ponu* for bride). Proper names are almost entirely Arabic, shortened to a single syllable (Ismail → *Me'*, Ibrahim → *Em*, Khadijah → *Jah*), a practice Hamilton notes is prevalent throughout the peninsula.

### Key Findings

- The final *l*-to-*i* shift affects approximately sixty common nouns and verbs; where the penultimate letter is *i*, the *l* is elided and the *i* becomes *e* (e.g., *katil* → *kate*, *bogil* → *boge*). (pp. 68–70)
- The progressive marker *dok* (from *duduk*) replaces *sedang*, *dalam*, and *tengah* in Penang speech, as in *saya dok makan nasi* ("I was eating rice") and *kapai dok mai* ("the ship is coming"). (p. 73)
- The prefix *pe-* forms causatives and inchoatives: *pebaik* (to mend), *pekechi* (to make smaller), *pehabis* (to finish absolutely), *pelekát* (to light a fire). (pp. 73–74)
- The word *dan* means "to have time to" (*ta' dan* = "no time to"); reduplicated *dan-dan* means "straightaway, immediately." (p. 74)
- The plural suffix *pa* attaches to pronouns: *sepa* (we), *hangpa* (you all), *depa* (they). (p. 76)
- The lexical comparison tables document over 200 Penang-specific terms with their Singapore equivalents, including a substantial set of Tamil and Hindustani borrowings (*chamcha*, *cha*, *lawak*, *poni*, *ponu*, *mampelam*, *korma*, *hargari*). (pp. 77–96)

### Conclusion

Hamilton's definitive takeaway is that "Penang Malay" is not a separate language or even a true dialect in the linguistic sense, but the Malay of Kedah as it has been reshaped by contact with a large Madras Presidency Indian population and the practical needs of a cosmopolitan port. The core grammar remains recognisably Malay; the differences are concentrated in pronunciation, a handful of productive morphological patterns, and a lexical layer of Indian origin that has in some cases displaced native Malay equivalents entirely.

## Context

- The article draws entirely on Hamilton's direct observation of colloquial speech in Penang; no archival or literary sources are cited.
- The study is a product of the early Straits Branch tradition of practical linguistics for colonial administrators and residents, and its comparative framework (Penang vs. Singapore) reflects the administrative division of the Straits Settlements rather than a purely linguistic one.
