# Groeneveldt’s notes on the Malay archipelago and Malacca. W.G. Maxwell

W. George Maxwell published this short philological note in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in 1909, offering a series of corrections and identifications to W.P. Groeneveldt's 1887 *Notes on the Malay Archipelago and Malacca*. Working from Chinese dynastic histories and the *Ying-yai Sheng-lan*, Maxwell argues that several of Groeneveldt's renderings of Malay and Javanese terms through Chinese ideographs can be improved or corrected through closer attention to dialectal pronunciation and comparative Malay vocabulary.

## Summary

Maxwell's article is a focused exercise in historical philology, addressing six specific passages in Groeneveldt's translation of Chinese sources on the Malay world. His central method is to compare the Chinese ideographs Groeneveldt transcribed against known Malay and Javanese words, using dialectal pronunciation (Mandarin, Hylam, Pekingese, Fukien) as the bridge between the two linguistic systems. The article is explicitly framed as speculative—"a few miscellaneous ideas, which can only be considered as guesses"—but each suggestion is grounded in concrete linguistic evidence.

The most substantial arguments concern the identification of a Hindu title in a Yuan dynasty account of Java (1292), where Maxwell reconstructs "Mud-li-ah" as *mudliyar*, a known rank among South Indian and Ceylonese Hindus, thereby demonstrating the use of this title in Java nearly a century before the Malacca Sultanate's rise. On the question of place names in Cheng Ho's itinerary, Maxwell challenges Groeneveldt's identification of one entry as Hormuz (Ormuz), arguing on geographical grounds that a Persian Gulf location would not appear between Kelantan and Perak in a list of Southeast Asian ports.

The article also engages with a contemporary scholarly dispute: Dr. Muller had suggested in the *T'oung Pa* that a Chinese rendering of Pahang should instead be read as "Panggang," after a local tribe. Maxwell rejects this, arguing that the *orang pangan* (aboriginal inhabitants) could not have given their name to the Pahang River, and that the ideographs in question are still used by Chinese speakers to represent "Pahang" in the present day.

### Key Findings

- The Sung dynasty account of Java (960–1279) records Malay words for pearl (*mutiara*), ivory, incense, and rhinoceros; Maxwell identifies the incense term as a corruption of *kemennien* (gum benjamin) and the rhinoceros term as possibly derived from *impit*, the onomatopoeic word for the rhinoceros' call used by the Besisi of Selangor (pp. 105–107).
- In Shih-pi's Yuan dynasty account of Java (A.D. 1292), the unidentified title "Muli" or "Buli" is reconstructed as *mudliyar*, a Hindu title of rank, establishing its use in Java by that date (pp. 107–108).
- In Cheng Ho's list of thirty-five places visited (A.D. 1403–1435), the entry Groeneveldt rendered as "Hormus" (Ormuz) is argued to be a local Malay or archipelagic place, possibly *Pulau Mausu*, associated with Borneo pirates (pp. 108–109).
- The *kobang* in the *Ying-yai Sheng-lan* weight system (A.D. 1416) is identified as the *kupang*, originally a weight (four to a *jampal*) that later became the ten-cent coin (p. 108).
- The superior kind of damar described as "clear and transparent and resembles amber" in the *Ying-yai Sheng-lan* is given a Chinese rendering that bears no resemblance to the Malay *mata-kuching*, remaining unidentified (p. 109).
- Maxwell defends Groeneveldt's original identification of a place as Pahang against Dr. Muller's "Panggang" hypothesis, noting that the ideographs are still used by Chinese speakers for Pahang today (pp. 109–110).

### Conclusion

Maxwell's definitive contribution is methodological rather than a single breakthrough: he demonstrates that careful attention to dialectal pronunciation and the mechanics of Chinese transcription can resolve ambiguities that Groeneveldt, working without modern comparative tools, could not. The article stands as a useful corrective to the 1887 *Notes* and a model of how philological precision can sharpen our reading of early Chinese sources on the Malay world.

## Context

- The article draws on Groeneveldt's 1887 *Miscellaneous Papers relating to Indo-China and the Indian Archipelago* (reprinted by Trubner & Co. for the Society), the *T'oung Pa* (vols. IV and VII), and Skeat and Bragden's *Pagan Tribes* (vol. II).
- Historiographically, the piece sits within the early twentieth-century Straits Branch tradition of using Chinese primary sources to reconstruct Malay and Indonesian history, a field in which Groeneveldt's *Notes* remained the foundational reference.
