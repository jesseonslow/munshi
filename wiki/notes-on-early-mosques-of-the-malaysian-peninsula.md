# Notes on early mosques of the Malaysian Peninsula

Allan Bruce, writing from the University of Southern Queensland in 1996, offers a synthetic architectural-typological survey of early mosques across the Malaysian Peninsula, arguing that mosque building in the region dates to the seventh century, that a Hindu-derived congregational type evolved in Java and was carried to fifteenth-century Melaka, and that the earliest surviving mosques are eighteenth-century Melakan structures whose builders were almost certainly Chinese.

## Summary

Bruce's central aim is to draw together the fragmentary literature on early Southeast Asian mosque architecture and to establish a historical framework linking the region's earliest Muslim communities to identifiable building types. He traces the probable origins of a Muslim presence in the Peninsula through seventh-century Chinese records of Persian traders in the China Sea, the flight of Umayyad refugees after 750, and the mass slaughter of roughly 120,000 Muslims in Guangzhou in 878, whose survivors he places in Palembang and Kedah (pp. 72–73). The earliest hard evidence he cites is a tombstone dated 291 AH (903 AD) from Kedah, but he reasons that coastal trading communities with mosques must have existed centuries earlier, given the Prophet's instruction that forty adult male believers should erect a place of worship (p. 73).

The article then turns to typology. Bruce identifies Masjid Agung Demak (usually dated 1478) as the oldest surviving congregational mosque in Southeast Asia and argues that its tiered pyramidal roof, courtyard layout, split gate, and masonry base derive from earlier Hindu-Buddhist religious architecture rather than from any Chinese pagoda model (pp. 75–77). He contends that this Javanese type was transmitted to fifteenth-century Melaka and spread along the west coast, while on the east coast an indigenous style based on Thai Buddhist *wat* architecture and local domestic building evolved independently, as exemplified by Bougas's study of Surau Aur at Patani (pp. 77–78). Masjid Kampong Laut at Nilam Puri, widely claimed as Malaysia's oldest mosque, Bruce argues is instead a fusion of the two traditions and cannot plausibly be five centuries old given the perishability of its timber construction (p. 78).

The earliest extant mosques, Bruce concludes, are those of eighteenth-century Melaka built under Dutch administration, of which he lists fourteen in the region alone. Their distinctive features—a proportionately deeper and steeper top roof tier crowned with an elaborate multi-level finial, sinuous raised ridge mouldings (*som-som*), and disproportionately large separate minarets—he attributes to Chinese builders, drawing on Yatim's identification of a pagoda prototype and on the known expansion of Melaka's southern Chinese community under the Dutch (pp. 79–80).

### Key Findings

- The earliest hard evidence of a Muslim presence in the Peninsula is a tombstone dated 291 AH (903 AD) found at Kedah (p. 73).
- The 878 AD massacre of an estimated 120,000 Muslims, Christians, and Jews in Guangzhou by Emperor Hizong is identified as the catalyst for Kedah's emergence in 879 as a major entrepôt (p. 73).
- Masjid Agung Demak, Java, usually dated 1478 AD, has a 20-metre-square prayer hall, a three-tiered shingle-tiled roof, and is the oldest surviving congregational mosque in Southeast Asia (pp. 75–76).
- Albuquerque's 1513 conquest of Melaka reportedly used stones from mosque ruins to build a fortress with 4.5-metre-thick walls (p. 77).
- The earliest extant Malaysian mosques are those of eighteenth-century Melaka: Masjid Peringgit (c. 1720), Masjid Trengkera and Masjid Kampong Hulu (both 1728), with six-storey minarets at the latter two (pp. 78–79).
- By 1500, Melaka, Ayutthya, and Demak each had populations of between 50,000 and 100,000 (p. 75).

### Conclusion

Bruce's definitive takeaway is that the architectural history of early Malaysian mosques cannot be understood through a single lineage: a Hindu-derived Javanese congregational type reached Melaka in the fifteenth century and spread westward, an indigenous Thai-Buddhist-influenced type developed on the east coast, and the two fused in structures like Kampong Laut, while the earliest surviving buildings are Chinese-built Melakan mosques of the 1720s that represent a stylistic continuation of the fifteenth-century Melakan tradition.

## Context

- The article is a synthetic typological study drawing on a wide range of secondary and tertiary sources (architectural surveys, colonial records, Chinese annals, Arabic chronicles) rather than on a single archival collection; its historiographical contribution lies in explicitly linking the architectural evidence to a proposed sequence of cultural transmission from Java through Melaka to both coasts of the Peninsula.
