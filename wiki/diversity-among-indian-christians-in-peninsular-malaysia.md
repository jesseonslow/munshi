# Diversity among Indian Christians in Peninsular Malaysia

J.R. Daniel's 1992 article examines the internal diversity of the Indian Christian community in Peninsular Malaysia, arguing that what appears to the dominant Malay and Chinese populations as a homogenous minority is in fact stratified along linguistic, denominational, and caste lines. Drawing on fieldwork conducted between 1985 and 1989, primarily in Buntong, Ipoh, Daniel maps the sociological contours of a community of roughly 100,000 people and traces how caste persistence and linguistic identity interact with Christian ecclesial structures in a plural society.

## Summary

The article's central concern is the tension between the perceived homogeneity of Indian Christians and their actual internal fragmentation. Daniel organizes the analysis around three axes: linguistic affiliation (Tamil versus Malayali), denominational leaning (Roman Catholic, Methodist, Anglican, Lutheran, Syrian Orthodox, Pentecostal, and Assembly of God), and caste attachment (predominantly a Tamil phenomenon). He argues that the church functions as an agent of socialization and social solidarity in an alien society, and that the "Indianness" of the church—its vernacular worship, its social fellowship, and its cultural continuity—serves as a major integrative force for a people navigating a multi-ethnic Malaysian environment (pp. 72–73). A recurring theme is the generational language crisis: first-generation migrants clustered in vernacular churches out of insecurity, but second- and third-generation members educated in English or Bahasa Malaysia are losing fluency in Tamil or Malayalam, creating a retention problem that has prompted experiments with bilingual sermons and Bahasa Malaysia Masses (pp. 72–73).

The denominational survey provides a detailed account of each church's founding history, organizational structure, and membership profile. The Roman Catholic Church dominates with three-quarters of all Indian Christians, having established a presence from 1786 onward and extending its work into plantation areas through initiatives such as Fr. Michael Maria Fee's agricultural settlement at Bagan Serai in the 1880s (pp. 74–75). The Methodist, Anglican, and Lutheran churches followed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, each developing distinct patterns of Indian engagement. The Syrian Orthodox churches (Mar Thoma and Jacobite) are exclusively Malayali in composition, reflecting the linguistic homogeneity of the Kerala diaspora (pp. 78–80).

The caste section is the article's most analytically distinctive component. Daniel argues that caste among Indian Christians is "highly diluted" compared to their Hindu counterparts, with an "unconscious move towards a state of castelessness" driven by Christian egalitarianism and the absence of a predominantly Hindu social environment (p. 83). Yet caste associations such as the Nalliar Association (Nadar) and KRISWAM (Christian Udayars) demonstrate that caste persists as a vehicle for socio-economic mutual aid—marriage brokering, educational funding, and funeral expense provision—rather than as a system of ritual purity or pollution (pp. 82–83). The Malayali Christians, being a smaller sub-ethnic group, have largely transcended caste in favour of linguistic solidarity, with Jacobites and Mar Thomites intermarrying freely (p. 82).

### Key Findings

- Total Indian Christian population estimated at approximately 100,000 (1984): 80,000 Tamil (80%), 15,000 Malayali (15%), 5,000 others (5%) (p. 71).
- Roman Catholics constitute 81,650 individuals (75.4% of Indian Christians) per the 1989 Official Church Directory; the Methodist Tamil Church had 5,981 members in 1980 across 26 centres (pp. 74, 77).
- The St. Joseph's Mission near Bagan Serai, established by Fr. Fee in the 1880s, recorded 101 families, a population of 430, 148 baptisms, 43 marriages, and 450 acres of cleared land by January 1889 (p. 75).
- The Nalliar Association (Nadar caste) has 600 members, 60% of whom are Christians; it functions as a marriage broker, education funder, and funeral expense provider, with Hindus and Christians intermarrying (Hindus typically converting) (p. 82).
- KRISWAM (Kristhudayar Socio-Economic and Welfare Association), registered in 1986, has approximately 118 members drawn from a community of around 5,000 Christian Udayars from Tiruchirapalli and Salem (p. 82).
- The Mar Thoma Church comprises approximately 600 families (3,000 members) across 18 parishes; the Jacobite Church has approximately 170 families (~1,000 members) served by only two travelling priests (pp. 79–80).

### Conclusion

Daniel's definitive historical takeaway is that diversity and unity coexist among Indian Christians in Peninsular Malaysia. While linguistic, denominational, and caste differences produce internal stratification, there is no inter-denominational animosity or competition for members. A common racial, religious, and cultural identity forges inter-group cohesion, and the caste system—though present in diluted form among Tamil Christians—is in a state of gradual dissolution driven by Christian egalitarianism, the absence of a Hindu social matrix, and the practical pressures of socio-economic advancement in a plural Malaysian society (pp. 83–84).

## Context

- Fieldwork was conducted between 1985 and 1989, primarily in Buntong, Ipoh, with supplementary interviews and observations across Peninsular Malaysia; the study draws on denominational annual reports, the *Official Church Directory and Order of Service* (1989), and direct interviews with clergy and caste association leaders (p. 86).
- The article contributes to the historiography of Indian diaspora religious communities in Southeast Asia by providing one of the first systematic sociological surveys of Indian Christian internal diversity in Malaysia, bridging ecclesiastical records with ethnographic observation.
