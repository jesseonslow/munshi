# Dusun and other “house horns”

This 1951 issue of the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 24, No. 1) contains two short contributions: G.E. Marrison's re-examination of a fourteenth-century Malay tombstone inscription from Aceh, arguing it employs Sanskrit Upajati metre rather than the modern Malay *sha'ir* form, and I.H.N. Evans's ethnographic note on "house-horns" — projecting rafter extensions on Dusun houses in North Borneo — which the source text truncates before completion.

## Summary

Marrison's article challenges the identification of the Minye Tujoh tombstone inscription (first published by Stutterheim in 1936) as a prototype of the modern Malay *sha'ir*. While Stutterheim and Winstedt had accepted the four-line, end-rhymed stanza as evidence of an early *sha'ir*, Marrison demonstrates that the lines conform to the fixed eleven-syllable Upajati measure of Sanskrit prosody, also used in Old Javanese verse (p. 163). This distinction matters because the *sha'ir* has variable line length and typically four stresses per line, whereas the Upajati is metrically rigid. Using the metre as a restorative tool, Marrison proposes emendations to three uncertain lines: reading "vara" (a Javanese title for a royal consort) in place of the doubtful "varda" in line A.4; reconstructing B.2 as "Târuk kayû tasiḥ tanah samûhâ" by interpreting a disputed sign as "ya" forming "kayu" (wood); and tentatively reading B.1 as "Gutra Bhasa pihak Kedah Pase ma," where "ma" functions as an enclitic conjunction linking Kedah and Pasai (pp. 163–164).

The restored inscription reads as a eulogy for a queen consort of the Bhasa clan, dated to 781 Hijri (1380 CE), who "possessed Kedah and Pasai" with "all their fields and woods, seas and lands" (p. 165). Marrison argues that the use of Sanskrit metre in a fourteenth-century Malay text is best explained as a survival of a pre-Islamic Sanskritizing poetic tradition, possibly reinforced by the Majapahit invasion of Pasai in 1377, three years before the tomb's date (p. 165).

Evans's article, which begins on the same page, describes the "house-horns" of Dusun houses in the Tempasuk (Kota Belud) District of North Borneo — upward projections of rafters left untrimmed at either end of the structure. Evans reports that his inquiries into their significance were consistently met with the response that they had no particular meaning, though he expresses a "strong suspicion" that they once carried protective value (p. 165). The text is truncated at this point.

### Key Findings

- The Minye Tujoh tombstone inscription (1380 CE / 781 Hijri) conforms to the Sanskrit Upajati metre (eleven syllables per line), not the variable-length Malay *sha'ir* form previously assumed by Stutterheim (1936) and Winstedt (p. 163).
- Marrison proposes reading "vara" (Javanese title for royal consort) in line A.4, yielding the meaning "The faithful queen consort was received into the mercy of God" (p. 163).
- The inscription identifies the deceased as belonging to the "Bhasa clan" and as possessing both Kedah and Pasai, suggesting a political entity spanning both territories in the late fourteenth century (p. 165).
- The Majapahit invasion of Pasai in 1377 is cited as a possible vector for the transmission of Javanese metrical forms to Sumatran Malay (p. 165).
- Dusun "house-horns" in the Kota Belud District are simply untrimmed rafter projections of wood or bamboo, left as equal lengths projecting above the roof-beam (p. 165).

### Conclusion

Marrison's central contribution is the demonstration that a fourteenth-century Malay verse text employs a fixed Sanskrit prosodic metre, adding a new data point to the history of Malay literary forms and suggesting that a Sanskritized Malay verse tradition preceded the Islamic revision of Malay poetic canons. The finding also provides epigraphists with a metrical key for resolving the inscription's damaged lines.

## Context

- Marrison's article draws on Stutterheim's 1936 publication in *Acta Orientalia* and the Old Javanese text *Smaradahana* (ed. Poerbatjaraka, 1931) as comparative metrical evidence; no new archival material is introduced.
- The article contributes to the historiography of early Malay literature by challenging the then-standard identification of the Minye Tujoh inscription as the earliest *sha'ir*, a position held by both Stutterheim and Winstedt.
