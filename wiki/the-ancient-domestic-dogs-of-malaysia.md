# The ancient domestic dogs of Malaysia

Lord Medway's 1977 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* synthesises archaeological, historical, and zoological evidence to trace the continuity of a distinctive small, barkless, dun-coloured domestic dog from Neolithic Borneo through the historic period to the mid-twentieth century, arguing that this type—long assumed to have vanished—survived in isolated communities until recently diluted by crossbreeding with imported breeds.

## Summary

Medway's central problem is one of continuity: Tom Harrisson had previously noted that the only dogs found in Bornean archaeological deposits were a small form resembling Japanese Neolithic types, and that this type "has vanished from the Bornean and Southeast Asian scene without a trace." Medway challenges this conclusion by assembling all known skeletal material from Sarawak caves—Niah, Lubang Kudih, Lubang Sirih, and the Bau district—and comparing it metrically with historic museum specimens and living dogs he examined in the 1960s. The argument proceeds through a careful metrical comparison of mandibular and dental measurements across time periods, showing that the ancient type did not disappear but was progressively outbred.

The most significant archaeological site is Lubang Kudih, a sepulchral cave used in the late fifteenth century, where 24 individual dogs were represented exclusively by lower jaws. Medway interprets this pattern—by analogy with the pig remains from the same site—as evidence that dog meat was ceremonially consumed at funerary feasts, making the Kudih people a sophisticated settled culture for which the dog was a supplementary food animal alongside domestic pigs. The Kudih dogs were notably smaller than the Niah specimens, with teeth so crowded that crowns overlapped and in some individuals the second premolar had failed to develop entirely—features Medway reads as extreme conditions produced by domestication.

For the historic and recent period, Medway draws on nineteenth-century travel accounts (Beccari, Lumholtz, St. John), early twentieth-century photographs, mounted specimens in the Taiping museum, and his own fieldwork among the Semai and Semelai of Pahang in the 1960s. He also references the Telomian dogs sent to the Jackson Laboratory in Maine in 1963, whose genetic uniformity confirmed the existence of a genuine breed. The linguistic evidence cited from Dentan—that all Senoi dialects retain a non-Malay cognate root for "dog" while borrowing Malay terms for other domestic animals—provides independent support for the great antiquity of dog domestication among the Orang Asli.

### Key Findings

- Lubang Kudih (late 15th century A.D.) yielded 24 individual dogs represented solely by lower jaws, alongside 94 pigs, indicating ceremonial consumption of dog meat at funerary feasts (pp. 17–18).
- The Kudih dogs exhibited extreme dental crowding with overlapping crowns of P⁴ and M¹, and in two specimens the second premolar was congenitally absent—traits Medway interprets as products of prolonged domestication (p. 18).
- Niah West Mouth specimens (Neolithic, c. 4000–250 B.C.) were metrically larger than the Kudih dogs, with lower-jaw measurements falling in the upper range or above Kudih homologues, suggesting the Kudih type was a later, more specialised derivative (p. 24).
- The Taiping museum "Sakai dogs" (turn of the 20th century) measured 66½–73 cm head-and-body length with shoulder heights of 26–31½ cm, confirming the small size attested in Senoi oral tradition (Table 2, p. 21).
- The Telomian dogs, examined at the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine) in 1963–67, proved genetically uniform across first- and second-generation crosses, confirming the existence of a discrete breed rather than a random mongrel population (p. 23).
- The breed is classified within the "pariah group" of dogs originally distributed from the Indochinese peninsula to the Middle East, with close relatives documented in Java, Sumatra, and Thailand (p. 25).

### Conclusion

Medway's definitive conclusion is that the small, barkless, yellowish-dun dog still occasionally encountered in interior settlements of Borneo and the Malayan States in the 1960s is the direct surviving descendant of a type first domesticated in the region during the Neolithic. The breed, he argues, "deserves respect" and calls for its rehabilitation through deliberate breeding efforts before it is lost entirely to mongrelisation.

## Context

- Primary material derives from Sarawak Museum excavation collections (Niah, Lubang Kudih, Lubang Sirih), the British Museum (Natural History) osteology collection (including Everett's 1878–79 Paku material and the skeleton of 'Manggis', reg. no. 71.753), and the Taiping museum mounted specimens.
- The article contributes to the historiography of Southeast Asian animal domestication by providing the first comprehensive metrical comparison of ancient and modern Malaysian dogs, and by establishing the Kudih site as evidence of ritual dog consumption in a fifteenth-century Bornean culture.
