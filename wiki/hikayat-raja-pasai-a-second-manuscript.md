# Hikayat Raja Pasai: a second manuscript

A.J. Sturrock, a British colonial administrator serving in Kelantan, published this comparative dialectological note in 1912, documenting the vocabulary, pronunciation, and grammatical peculiarities of the Kelantan Malay dialect and contrasting them with those of Perak and Central Pahang. The article's overarching purpose is to record, in a preliminary and non-exhaustive manner, the distinctive features of Kelantan Malay—particularly its heavy Siamese lexical influence and its eccentric phonological patterns—so as to stimulate further fieldwork by other observers.

## Summary

Sturrock organises his observations around three axes of dialectal variation: vocabulary, pronunciation, and distinct usages of word and phrase. On vocabulary, he notes that Perak and Pahang are relatively close to one another, while Kelantan diverges sharply from both. The Kelantan dialect is characterised by a substantial layer of Siamese borrowings—words such as *jenaiah* (criminal), *utun* (appeal), *kweng* (a title for a *penghulu*), *guam* (court case), *wat* (temple), *sam* (court-house), and *semayan* (clerk)—as well as the Chinese negative *bo*, which is in common colloquial use (pp. 2–3). Sturrock compiles an extensive, admittedly unsystematic list of Kelantan-specific lexical items covering legal, domestic, and everyday registers, noting that the dialect is "the least pure of all the Malay dialects I know" (p. 2).

On pronunciation, Sturrock ranks Central Pahang as the most "normal" in that it approximates the written standard most closely, with only minor slurring of final consonants. Kelantan pronunciation, by contrast, is "eccentric in a different direction" (p. 4): it systematically suppresses *n* and *m* before other consonants (*Kelatan* for *Kelantan*, *tēpoh* for *tēmpoh*), nasalises final *-an* into *-en* and *-ang* into *-eng*, diphthongises final *-a* into *-aw*, and renders final stops (*-t, -k, -p*) practically indeterminate in speech (pp. 4–5). He also documents a set of clipped terminal particles unique to Kelantan—*-gak*, *-teh*, *-dik*, and the enigmatic *keneh*—whose precise semantic function remains unexplained even to native speakers (pp. 6–7).

Sturrock is explicit that his notes are neither exhaustive nor systematic; they are compiled "just as they occurred to me or were brought to my notice" (p. 4). His stated aim is to provoke further documentation by other European residents in the Northern States, arguing that only through accumulated observation can the persistent difficulties of spelling and form in Kelantan Malay be resolved (p. 7).

### Key Findings

- The Kelantan dialect contains a significant Siamese lexical stratum; Sturrock identifies *jenaiah*, *utun*, *kweng*, *guam*, *wat*, *sam*, and *semayan* as confirmed Siamese borrowings, and suspects *senarai*, *gong*, and *gok* are also of Siamese origin (p. 3).
- The Chinese negative *bo* is "in quite common use" in Kelantan Malay (p. 3).
- Kelantan systematically suppresses *n* before another consonant (*jantan* → *jatan*, *bangku* → *baku*) and *m* in similar position (*tēmpoh* → *tēpoh*, *tampar* → *tapar*), with a compensatory over-insertion of *n* and *ng* in writing (e.g., *bengkas* for *bekas*) (p. 5).
- Final *-a* in Kelantan is pronounced as *-aw* (e.g., *manaw* for *mana*, *apaw* for *apa*), and final *-ah* as *-awh* (e.g., *timawh*, *lelawh*) (p. 5).
- The terminal particle *-gak* is "perhaps the most frequently used" of the Kelantan clipped affixes, functioning as an intensive with "little real signification or none at all" (p. 6).
- The expression *keneh* (as in *Balah 'tu keneh?*) is used both interrogatively and to express agreement, and no satisfactory etymology or semantic analysis could be obtained from informants (p. 6).

### Conclusion

Sturrock's definitive takeaway is that the Kelantan dialect is sufficiently distinct from both Perak and Pahang—and sufficiently opaque in its phonology and particle system—to resist easy classification or standardisation. He frames his contribution not as a definitive grammar but as a provocation: the publication is intended to "induce others to set down and publish the result of their observations," on the grounds that only collaborative, cumulative fieldwork can resolve the persistent uncertainties of form and spelling that characterise the dialect (p. 7).

## Context

- The author was a British colonial administrator in Kelantan; the article reflects the administrative and ethnographic interests of the Straits Settlements and Federated Malay States civil service in the early twentieth century, and its comparative framework (Perak as the "leader of fashion," p. 1) mirrors the hierarchical prestige structure of the Malay states as perceived by colonial officials.
- The article draws entirely on the author's personal observation and conversation with native speakers in Kelantan; no archival or literary sources are consulted.
