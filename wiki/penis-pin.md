# Penis pin

## The *Palang*: A Penile Bar Device of Borneo and the Philippines

The *palang*—a cross-piece driven horizontally through the glans of the penis—constitutes one of the most distinctive and geographically concentrated forms of male genital modification documented in Southeast Asia. Its known distribution spans West Borneo, the southern Philippines, and, in related bead-insertion forms, parts of the Malay Peninsula, Java, Celebes, and Siam [[1, p. 162]](#ref-1), [[2, p. 90]](#ref-2). The device is not a single uniform artefact but a family of objects varying in material, form, and cultural context, with over thirty varieties recorded in the sixteenth century alone, each bearing a distinct sacred name [[1, p. 171]](#ref-1). Despite its considerable antiquity and regional persistence, the *palang* was either deliberately suppressed or simply overlooked by colonial administrators and ethnographers, leaving a literature that is "light" and often "misleading, presented as semi-erotic" [[1, p. 164]](#ref-1).

## Scope and Historical Definition

The earliest European field observation of the device in Borneo appears in Robert Burns' 1847 notes, which describe the Kayan *utang* as "a round pin (frequently two or three) of wood, bone, brass or gold, about an eighth of an inch in diameter passed horizontally through the gland," adopted at puberty or before marriage, without which "marriage does not take place" [[1, p. 164]](#ref-1). The term *utang* is the Kayan designation for the same object that Iban speakers call *palang*; the two names refer to the identical practice as carried by different ethnic groups in the Baram and upper Rajang river systems [[1, pp. 166–167]](#ref-1).

The device's documented history extends well before the colonial period. Pigafetta's 1521 account of Cebu records that males "have pierced their member near the head from one side to the other with a device of gold or of tin as large as a goose quill," with star- or nail-head terminals, and that the women "want it this way" [[1, p. 168]](#ref-1). Francis Pretty's 1587 account from Capul in the Visayas records that the device was "driven through their privities when they be yong," was removable, and was instituted at the women's request as a remedy against sodomy; Pretty's party physically removed one such nail from a ten-year-old boy [[1, p. 169]](#ref-1). The Boxer Codex, compiled circa 1590, describes a wheel-type *palang* with round spurs, made of lead, brass, or gold, and notes that over thirty varieties existed, each with a different sacred name; the Spaniards punished wearers with beatings, yet the practice continued [[1, p. 171]](#ref-1). Antonio de Morga (1609) records that the devices were called *sagras* and that "there are very few (left), because now that they are becoming Christians, care is taken to get rid of (such)" [[1, p. 170]](#ref-1).

## Distribution, Adoption, and Cultural Dynamics

The *palang* did not originate independently among the Kayans but represents a continuation of an older tradition carried by ancestral populations into the Bornean interior, with parallel and possibly connected usage among the Bisaya peoples of the southern Philippines [[1, p. 166]](#ref-1). The Ibans, who became the most numerically prominent *palang` carriers in Sarawak during the twentieth century, uniformly deny any claim to invention and attribute the practice to Kayan and Kenyah influence following their north-westward expansion and the pacification of the highland groups [[1, pp. 166–167]](#ref-1). Medical reports from Sarawak doctors between 1956 and 1963 confirm that "quite a high proportion of elderly Iban males do have *palang* now" and that numerically the Ibans were the most frequent carriers in West Borneo in the preceding decade [[1, p. 166]](#ref-1).

The Kelabit case illustrates the device's more recent diffusion among groups that previously lacked it. The *palang* was unknown to the Kelabit until approximately 1925, when a visiting Kenyah youth's *palang* caused a fatal haemorrhage in a Kelabit woman; this event catalysed awareness and subsequent adoption among a small aristocratic minority by 1945 [[1, p. 167]](#ref-1). The discontinuous distribution of the practice is itself a subject of scholarly interest: Appell notes its absence in Sabah despite proximity to the Southern Philippines where it is found, and contrasts the circumcised subjects visible in early photographs with the non-circumcised interior tribes of Sabah for whom uncircumcision is a cultural marker [[3, p. 205]](#ref-3).

## Material Culture and Medical Complications

The *palang* is not a single object type but a range of forms. The Peabody Museum at Harvard University holds a *palang* and its associated perforator instrument from the Borneo collections, traced to the 1896–1898 Furness-Hiller Pacific expedition, likely collected by Furness and Charles Hose during the 1897–1898 exploration of the Baram and Tinjar River headwaters [[3, p. 203]](#ref-3). Harrisson W. Smith, an MIT electrical engineering professor who visited Sarawak in 1911–1912, donated 954 photographs to the Peabody, including three of the *palang* in situ; his accompanying description records the knobs being pulled apart to reveal the shank passing through the flesh [[3, p. 204]](#ref-3). An aging photograph of a *palang* in situ, taken by a member of the Furness-Hiller expedition in 1897 or 1898, is probably the earliest known photograph of the object [[3, p. 204]](#ref-3).

The perforation instrument itself is documented in the British Museum collection. A 1954 letter from Cranstone at the British Museum confirms that H. Ling Roth deposited four Kayan *utang* specimens in 1895 [[4, p. 172]](#ref-4). A pre-1890 Kayan tool called *katiputin* from the Belaga-Baloi area, consisting of two hardwood sticks and a twisted iron rod, was used to perforate the glans penis horizontally during the operation [[4, p. 173]](#ref-4).

Secondary medical complications are a recognised consequence of long-term *palang* use. Harrisson documents a calcinated urethral stone, or "penolith," weighing 26 grams, removed from an Iban man in the Balleh area, which had formed around a 21 mm iron *palang* bar left in situ [[4, pp. 173–174]](#ref-4). Such complications are widespread among older men in the *palang* zone but rarely reported to outside doctors due to cultural reticence [[4, p. 174]](#ref-4). Appell observes that the photographs suggest urethral bisecting by the perforation is common, though Furness's drawing implies it may not be a necessary consequence [[3, p. 205]](#ref-3).

## Research and Documentation

The scholarly investigation of the *palang* within the Society's journal literature is characterised by a progression from isolated specimen notes to systematic cultural-historical reconstruction. The earliest contribution in the record is Harrisson's 1964 article, which represents a decisive intervention: a systematic recovery of scattered early European eyewitness testimony demonstrating that the *palang* was a widespread, technically diverse practice across the Borneo–Philippines cultural zone long before the colonial period, challenging the Victorian-era tendency to treat it as an isolated "Kayan mutilation" curiosity [[1, p. 164]](#ref-1). Harrisson triangulates three kinds of evidence—early European field observations, indigenous oral traditions collected in Sarawak and the Philippines, and material culture including Iban *ikat* textiles and the Boxer Codex marginal drawing—to reconstruct a pre-Spanish cultural continuum linking Bornean and Visayan populations through shared sexual technology [[1, p. 162]](#ref-1).

His 1966 follow-up note extends the material record with the British Museum specimens, the *katiputin* perforator, and the penolith case, while also documenting the practical medical consequences of the practice [[4]](#ref-4). Appell's 1968 note shifts the analytical focus to the Peabody Museum holdings, raising three points arising from the visual evidence: the apparent universality of circumcision among photographed subjects, the question of whether urethral bisecting is a necessary consequence of the perforation, and the effect of *palang* use on fecundity, noting that the non-*palang*-using Rungus appear to produce more offspring per mating than the Iban [[3, p. 205]](#ref-3).

Harrisson's 1975 note extends the geographical and chronological frame further, drawing on Dr. J. V. G. Mills's 1970 Hakluyt Society edition of Ma Huan's *Ying-yai Sheng-lan* (1433), which records that in Siam men of the upper classes had tin or gold beads inserted into the skin surrounding the *membrum virile*, with specialist operators performing the procedure as a profession [[2, p. 89]](#ref-2). Harrisson argues this constitutes a clear parallel between Buddhist Thai and animist Dayak practices, even though the mainland form lacks the actual bar device characteristic of the Borneo *palang*, and suggests the bar may have developed from bead insertions or vice versa [[2, p. 90]](#ref-2). He also speculates, with explicit acknowledgment of the limits of his linguistic competence, on a possible terminological and symbolic connection between the Bornean *palang* and the Yuan-dynasty Tantric ritual term *pa-lang* ("eight males") recorded in Chinese dynastic histories, a link that Dr. van Gulik himself could not resolve [[1, pp. 172–173]](#ref-1). The known geographical distribution of such practices—Borneo, the Philippines, Java, Celebes, and Thailand—remains, as Harrisson notes, in need of further study of diffusion routes, particularly via the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra [[2, p. 90]](#ref-2).
