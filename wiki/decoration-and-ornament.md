Gold ornaments and decorative objects constitute one of the most tangible records of cultural contact and craft tradition across the Malay Peninsula and Borneo. From the gold rings and beads of the Limbang hoard to the *tāli* (a South Indian married-women's pendant) excavated at Kota Cina, and from the flexible armlets of the Fort Canning find to the foliated patterns of Perak silver-work, the decorative arts of the region reveal a complex interplay of Indian, Javanese, Chinese, and local influences spanning at least a millennium. These objects served not merely as personal adornment but as markers of status, religious identity, and commercial exchange, and their distribution across the archipelago provides critical evidence for the routes and chronology of cultural transmission in South-east Asia [[5, p. 48]](#ref-5), [[1, p. 117]](#ref-1), [[3, pp. 1–2]](#ref-3).

### Gold Ornaments and Personal Adornment

The most extensively documented category of decorative object in the region is gold personal ornament. Tom Harrisson, Curator of the Sarawak Museum, traced records of approximately 115 gold objects in West Borneo, excluding the Sambas hoard, of which about 60 were examined in original form, 16 in replica, and 40 known only by written reference with no surviving specimen [[5, p. 48]](#ref-5). The objects fall into several distinct types: rings (14 recorded), beads (30), "bead rings" (24), cap badges (16), unspecified ornaments (15 or more), ear ornaments (4 or more), nose ornaments (2), coat fasteners (3), necklaces and chains (3), a coin, and a gold bar [[5, pp. 52–53]](#ref-5).

The Limbang hoard, revealed by a landslide around 1904 on the slope behind the Government Offices at Limbang, is the most informative single find. It comprised eight rings, two chains, four ear or nose ornaments, two or three coat fasteners, a gold bar or buckle, gold beads, and a fragment of a Persian coin struck in 960 A.D.—the earliest positively datable object from the area [[5, pp. 52–53]](#ref-5). Among the rings, the inscribed ring bears Nagari script read as "Araksara," dated by Professor Nilakanta Sastri to the 12th or 13th century and by Dr Barnett of the British Museum and Professor Coedes to the 14th or 15th century; the reading is interpreted as a personal name or office designation [[5, pp. 57–58]](#ref-5). The collared gem ring from the same hoard is "very nearly identical" to a ring in the Franks Collection (British Museum nos. 2441–4), listed as "Java" with no date, and the fish ring closely resembles the goose ring from the Fort Canning hoard [[5, pp. 58–59]](#ref-5).

The Fort Canning find, excavated in July 1928 during construction of the Service Reservoir in Singapore, comprised a pair of flexible gold armlets bearing conventionalised Javanese *kala* heads, a ring with an incised goose motif, six small diamond-set rings identified as ear-rings, an elliptical ruby-and-diamond ornament, and a jewelled clasp [[3, pp. 1–2]](#ref-3). Dr P. V. van Stein Callenfels, Inspector of Antiquities of the Netherlands Indies, attributed the armlets to the Majapahit period at its best, that is, the middle of the 14th century A.D. [[3, p. 1]](#ref-3). Winstedt concluded that the ornaments date from the Javanese Majapahit campaign against Palembang, which captured Singapore around 1360 A.D., and that they represent the most significant archaeological remains in British Malaya apart from a few inscribed stone monuments [[3, p. 2]](#ref-3).

In the Malay Peninsula, a gold *tāli* excavated in 1976 at Kota Cina, a site with well-documented South Indian connections, provides a further data point. The *tāli* is a flat, coin-like disc 18 mm in diameter, made from two pieces of gold foil joined by a suspension loop and a small clasp, recovered at a depth of 0.60 m [[1, p. 117]](#ref-1). Michael L. Bates of the American Numismatic Society confirmed it as an imitation of a Fatimid or Ayyubid gold dinar dating to A.D. 1098–1200, with illegible Kufic inscriptions that are merely decorative lines intended to convey the impression of script [[1, p. 117]](#ref-1). McKinnon notes that the *tāli* is a distinctly Hindu ornament of the *bottu* type common in Andhra, Kannada, and Maharashtra, and that Bates' dating preference for the middle of the 12th century helps refine the span of occupation at the site [[1, p. 117]](#ref-1).

### Material Culture and Craft Traditions

Beyond gold, the decorative traditions of the region encompass a range of media and techniques. In West Borneo, goldsmiths are centred in Kuching and fall into two groups: Chinese making trinkets for local use, and Malay women weaving gold-thread sarongs [[5, p. 64]](#ref-5). Some of the gold-thread sarongs still made in Kuching are of fine quality, with a considerable nomenclature and ritual connected with them, though the thread has in historic times always been imported via Malaya [[5, p. 64]](#ref-5). There is also a keen contemporary demand for gold to cover or gild teeth, found throughout the island, particularly in the far interior; dentists at smaller northern trading stations do much of their business sheathing teeth, generally at about $8.00 a tooth [[5, p. 65]](#ref-5).

Crawfurd, writing in 1820, observed that gold "must have been the first of the metals of which the use was acquired," and that "its sole application has been to trinkets and ornaments. In the art of manufacturing these, like other rude people, they far surpass their other efforts in the mechanic arts" [[5, p. 65]](#ref-5). Raffles gave special credit to Sumatran filigree, which is not infrequently found on the handles of *keris* in the possession of leading Malay families in West Borneo [[5, p. 65]](#ref-5). The first Rajah Brooke recorded inspecting gold and silver ornaments of Dayak manufacture at a *sebayam* house in the Batang Lupar area, describing them as "neatly worked flowery patterns, consisting chiefly of bracelets and ear-rings" [[5, p. 65]](#ref-5).

In the broader decorative repertoire, Winstedt's 1941 study of Malaysian art motifs identifies several characteristic forms with deep historical roots. The *sundang*, a broad sword "almost the only Malaysian cutting instrument with both sides sharpened," is distinguished from the *keris* by its size and massiveness and its cockatoo-headed handle [[4, p. 238]](#ref-4). Winstedt traces the straight-bladed broad sword to a European or Indian provenance reaching the archipelago in the early Christian era, subsequently adapted locally [[4, p. 238]](#ref-4). Parrot-headed sword hilts appear on Byzantine porphyry reliefs outside the Basilica of St Mark's, Venice, predating the Majapahit era [[4, p. 239]](#ref-4). A Sumba cloth motif showing a long-necked bird riding a stag or ibex is connected to a spouted pottery jug excavated at Syalck near Kashan in Central Persia, possibly dating to the third or fourth millennium B.C. [[4, p. 240]](#ref-4). Most significantly for the Peninsula, foliated patterns in Malay silver-work are identical to carvings on the panels of Borobudur, and Dr Quaritch Wales has located an early Indian prototype of the Minangkabau brass "house" in Kedah, together with miniature weapons matching Ajanta frescoes and Borobudur bas-reliefs [[4, pp. 240–241]](#ref-4).

In the realm of religious and monumental decoration, the Buddhist sculpture of the Isthmus provides a major corpus. The composite character of images from Kedah, Songkhla, and the Kinta valley—showing persistent Amaravati or Ceylonese features grafted onto Gupta or Post-Gupta models—demonstrates that a local school was operating under Indian tutelage, not merely receiving imports [[2, p. 12]](#ref-2). The Takuapa *Viṣṇu*, a six-foot mitred image of Pallava style, is dated by Dupont to the 6th century and represents the prototype for the long-robed *Viṣṇu* series found across the Isthmus, Dvaravati, and Chen-la [[2, pp. 16–19]](#ref-2).

### Origins, Attribution, and Cultural Exchange

The attribution of decorative objects to specific cultural sources remains one of the most challenging problems in the field. Harrisson emphasises that the finding of an object at a particular point does not automatically date the presence of the associated culture at that location, because objects are often far more mobile than the people who made them [[5, p. 63]](#ref-5). A *T'ang* jar from China may be travelling from village to village through the far interior, or may have lain buried for centuries before being accidentally found and mobilised again [[5, p. 63]](#ref-5). This qualification is particularly relevant to small precious objects such as beads, coins, rings, and statuettes [[5, p. 63]](#ref-5).

The majority of the gold objects from West Borneo appear to belong to an Indo-Javanese style, but there are other elements as well [[5, p. 62]](#ref-5). The ear and nose ornaments from Limbang could be accepted as directly Indian, and are so accepted by Indian friends of Harrisson in Sarawak [[5, p. 69]](#ref-5). The "Javanese" influences and parallels are in a broad sense "basically Indian," with Indonesian elements varying in intensity, sometimes stamping out a craft or belief with major emphasis, sometimes only etching the faint outline of another impact [[5, p. 68]](#ref-5). Only two items can be given Chinese parallels, both rings, and even these are uncertain [[5, p. 69]](#ref-5). Everett and Hewitt, who insisted that gold had been worked in Sarawak by Chinese for many centuries, reluctantly concluded that the gold objects from the area "certainly do not possess a typical Chinese *facies*" [[5, p. 69]](#ref-5).

The earliest positively datable object is the 10th-century Persian coin from Limbang, but the main run of objects indicates a comparatively late dating, especially about the 14th century [[5, p. 70]](#ref-5). The only really strong signs are of the last wave of Indian influence, the Majapahit period [[5, p. 70]](#ref-5). Harrisson concludes that there is at present no reason to suppose that there were any large-scale settlements of directly Indian nature in West Borneo, and that the cultural background which produced these objects was complicated by many different groups and influences at work [[5, p. 94]](#ref-5).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature reveals a clear evolution in the investigation of decorative and ornamental objects. Early contributions were largely descriptive and incidental: Shelford's 1905 illustrated catalogue of the Sarawak Museum ethnographical collection provided the first systematic listing of gold and other ornaments in the institution's holdings [[2]](#ref-2). Winstedt's 1928 note on the Fort Canning gold ornaments established a precedent for the detailed publication of individual finds with art-historical attribution [[3]](#ref-3). The 1930s and 1940s saw the rise of comparative and diffusionist approaches, exemplified by Winstedt's 1941 study of Malaysian art motifs, which drew wide-ranging parallels from European museum collections and travel literature [[4]](#ref-4).

The landmark contribution remains Harrisson's 1949 survey, which was the first systematic study of gold and Indian-influenced objects in West Borneo, correcting the prevailing assumption—exemplified by Coomaraswamy's 1947 survey, which confined itself exclusively to East Borneo—that the western half of the island was archaeologically insignificant [[5, p. 49]](#ref-5). Harrisson established the south-west corner as the primary zone of Indianised material culture in the island and provided the baseline distribution data against which all subsequent Borneo archaeology has been measured [[5, p. 48]](#ref-5). His work also highlighted the severe limitations of the record: the loss of objects through the Chinese rebellion of 1857, the Japanese occupation, and the general absence of proper publication meant that a large proportion of known objects could no longer be examined [[5, pp. 34]](#ref-5).

In the Malay Peninsula, Quaritch Wales's 1970 reconsideration of his earlier archaeological fieldwork in Kedah and the Isthmus applied the art-historical method of composite style analysis to demonstrate that a local Peninsular school of Buddhist sculpture was operating under Indian tutelage, thereby resolving the apparent contradiction that had led Lamb to dismiss the Peninsula's early Indianisation [[2, p. 12]](#ref-2). McKinnon's 1980 note on the Kota Cina *tāli* illustrates the continuing value of brief, focused publications on individual objects, which can refine the chronology of a site and confirm the presence of specific cultural connections [[1, p. 117]](#ref-1). The overall trajectory of the Society's literature on decoration and ornament thus moves from catalogue description, through comparative speculation, to increasingly rigorous art-historical and archaeological analysis, though the fundamental problem of object mobility and the fragility of the physical record remain unresolved [[5, p. 63]](#ref-5).
