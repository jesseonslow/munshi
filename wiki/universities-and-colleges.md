# Universities and colleges

Tertiary and post-secondary institutions in the Malay Peninsula and Borneo trace their origins to early nineteenth-century missionary schools and evolved, through a protracted period of colonial neglect and intermittent reform, into the national universities of the post-independence era. The arc of this development spans from the Anglo-Chinese College in Malacca (1818–1843), the first Protestant school in Asia, through the contested founding of the University of Malaya in 1949, to its separation in 1962 into two independent institutions. The history of these institutions is inseparable from the political economies of colonial administration, the aspirations of local communities for accessible higher learning, and the shifting geopolitical anxieties that shaped educational policy across the region [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3).

## Early Missionary Colleges

The Anglo-Chinese College, founded in 1818 in Malacca by the British missionaries Robert Morrison and William Milne, stands as the first Protestant school established in Asia [[3]](#ref-3). Situated approximately 300 feet west of the Tranquerah Gate on the right bank of the Malacca River, facing the strait, the College occupied a walled compound that included a two-storey main building completed by Milne in August 1820, measuring 90 feet by 34 feet with a 16½-foot verandah, constructed of local *merbau* wood with imported Chinese pavement tiles at a cost of under 7,500 Spanish dollars [[3, p. 77]](#ref-3). The campus also comprised flanking rows of printing offices and residential quarters, a tropical garden, and a dispensary [[3]](#ref-3). By 1823 the College library held 2,850 volumes of Chinese books, and by 1835 a further 1,575 non-Chinese volumes, totalling at least 4,425 volumes—a collection that compared favourably with contemporary American college libraries such as Dickinson College and the University of North Carolina, each of which held approximately 3,000 volumes in 1839 [[3, pp. 80]](#ref-3). The College's press produced significant publications, most notably Milne's *Dialogues between Two Friends* (1819), which by 1906 had reached an estimated several hundred thousand to perhaps two million copies in print, making it "probably the most frequently reprinted Chinese novel of any kind during the century" [[3, p. 81]](#ref-3). Following the London Missionary Society's decision to relocate the institution to Hong Kong, the Malacca campus was sold at auction on 28 April 1843 to the Dutch merchant J. B. Westerhout for 2,120 Spanish dollars [[3, p. 74]](#ref-3).

A second, abortive attempt to establish an Anglo-Chinese College in Singapore in the years immediately following the First World War illustrates how colonial political anxieties could override educational demand. In 1914, Reverend J. S. Nagle, principal of the Anglo-Chinese School, was commissioned by Bishop William F. Oldham to plan a post-secondary institution [[1]](#ref-1). By 1920 the project had attracted $400,000 in contributions from the local community, demonstrating substantial public demand for post-secondary education [[1, p. 100]](#ref-1). The colonial government, however, opposed the scheme. Acting Director of Education R. O. Winstedt recommended that "no further encouragement be extended to the project," resting his case on unsubstantiated claims about American bias in the Mission's teaching and a speculative fear that an American college would become the "highest educational institution in a British Colony in the Far East" [[1]](#ref-1). The *Malayan Bulletin of Political Intelligence* compounded these suspicions, recording in October 1922 that American missionaries' teaching was "not thought to be doing them much good from a British point of view" [[1, p. 107]](#ref-1). The Colonial Office in London ultimately concurred with the Governor's advice, citing the threat of "throat-cutting competition" between the two proposed colleges [[1]](#ref-1).

## Colonial Higher Education and the Path to a University

The broader pattern of colonial educational policy in the Straits Settlements was dominated by parsimony and a reluctance to commit public funds to higher education [[1]](#ref-1). The Queen's Scholarships had been discontinued in 1912 and were not reintroduced until 1923, by which point they functioned as a "sop to public pressure" after Raffles College still had no immediate prospect of opening [[1, p. 108]](#ref-1). The government's own competing scheme, Raffles College, conceived as a centenary memorial for Singapore's founding in 1919, was delayed by bureaucratic inertia, an unnecessary architectural competition, and Governor Sir Laurence Guillemard's decision to divert the donated 40-acre "Mount Rosie" site for use as a military residence, securing a Legislative Council vote of $29,500 for repairs to the existing building and $2,500 for furniture [[1, pp. 101–102]](#ref-1). When Raffles College finally opened in 1928, it did so as a teacher-training institution that "failed utterly to satisfy the aspirations for higher education held by so many of the community" [[2, p. 2]](#ref-2).

The Straits Settlements Medical School, established in 1905 largely through the pressure of prominent Straits Chinese leaders, particularly Tan Jiak Kim, represented the earliest sustained attempt at post-secondary education in the colony. It began with just twenty-three students in buildings formerly part of a female lunatic asylum [[2, p. 1]](#ref-2). By 1947 it had grown to 265 students and 11 chairs, and had been recognised by the Medical Council of Great Britain since 1916 [[2, p. 1]](#ref-2).

The Japanese occupation, despite its brutality, broke the colonial inertia that had stifled educational progress. By insisting on a common language and emphasising technical education, the Japanese "stirred the stagnant waters" and stimulated national thought considerably, making any resumption of colonial inertia suspect [[2, p. 3]](#ref-2). The post-war Carr-Saunders Report of 1947 recommended merging the King Edward VII College of Medicine and Raffles College into a full university, and the University of Malaya was established on 8 October 1949 with three faculties and 645 students [[2, p. 4]](#ref-2).

## The University of Malaya: Community and Research

The University of Malaya began in 1949 with 645 students—395 in Medicine, 168 in Arts, and 82 in Science—and by 1953/54 total enrolment had grown to 954, with a gender ratio of four men to every woman [[2, pp. 4–5]](#ref-2). In 1954/55 the undergraduate population comprised 61 per cent Chinese, 13 per cent Indian, 11 per cent Ceylonese, 10 per cent Malay, and 3 per cent Eurasian, all of Malayan or Singaporean origin [[2, p. 6]](#ref-2). K. G. Tregonning, who joined the faculty in 1953 as Raffles Professor of History, described the institution as unique in possessing a fully residential, multi-racial, multi-ethnic student body drawn entirely from Malaya and Singapore, and documented the remarkable quality of the early student cohort, many of whom went on to become prominent national leaders and scholars [[2, pp. 5–10]](#ref-2).

The university also functioned as a centre of field research. The University of Malaya Archaeological Society conducted systematic surveys across Kedah in the late 1950s. In July 1956, a team of eleven members, including Dr P. E. de Josselin de Jong, surveyed the Kuala Muda district, cataloguing eight sites ranging from prehistoric shell mounds at *kampong* (village) Guak Kepah to megalithic and Hindu-Buddhist stone monuments at Neo Bah Estate and Bukit Batu Lintang [[4]](#ref-4). A recurring thread in the survey was the local attribution of these features to the legendary figure Raja Bersiong—a flagstaff, a fort, a well, a tombstone—suggesting that a single legendary figure had been superimposed on a heterogeneous archaeological landscape [[4, pp. 210–212]](#ref-4). The following year, in May 1958, a second survey under Wang Gungwu examined the Sungai Bujang corridor and the *kampongs* surrounding Merbok, functioning less as a discovery exercise than as a salvage documentation effort, capturing what remained of a landscape being reshaped by estate agriculture, road construction, and local subsistence practices [[5]](#ref-5). The surveys documented the vulnerability of archaeological evidence: villagers had stripped exposed brickwork for building material, the Japanese had levelled at least one major site during the occupation, and rubber replantation had buried or overgrown others [[5]](#ref-5).

The critical institutional failure of the period, Tregonning contends, was the University Executive's inability to appreciate the pace of political change. After the 1955 elections and the Razak Report, a rushed migration of first-year Arts students to Kuala Lumpur preceded any clear planning. The Aitken Commission of 1957 recommended a two-division university under central control, a structure Tregonning considers short-sighted, effectively making the Malayan division "a little brother of Singapore" [[2, p. 12]](#ref-2). The separation in 1962—Kuala Lumpur becoming the University of Malaya and Singapore the University of Singapore—was the logical correction to this flawed arrangement and marked "a watershed in the history of tertiary education in Malaya" [[2, p. 13]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's journal literature investigated the history of universities and colleges in Malaya through three distinct analytical lenses. The first, exemplified by H. E. Wilson's 1972 article, drew on confidential despatches between the Governor and the Colonial Office, the *Malayan Bulletin of Political Intelligence*, and Legislative Council proceedings to reconstruct the internal reasoning of colonial administrators and demonstrate how geopolitical anxieties about American influence shaped decisions with direct consequences for educational access [[1]](#ref-1). The second, represented by Tregonning's 1990 retrospective, combined institutional history with personal observation from his fifteen years at the university to trace the structural development of tertiary education from 1905 to 1962, arguing that British colonial laissez-faire attitudes perpetuated racial divisions and squandered opportunities for using the university as a unifying national institution [[2]](#ref-2). The third, embodied in the field reports of the University of Malaya Archaeological Society published in the early 1960s, demonstrated the university's role as a research institution and documented the collaborative model of Malayan archaeology in which the university provided the field team while the Kedah Government and the Federation of Malaya Museums Department offered logistical and administrative support [[5]](#ref-5), [[4]](#ref-4).

A further dimension emerges from the broader scholarly ecosystem in which these institutions operated. Arndt Graf's 2007 survey of German-language scholarship on Malay culture notes that the University of Malaya and its successor institutions, including Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia and Universiti Sains Malaysia, became increasingly central to the international study of Malay language and literature from the 1990s onward, with Malaysian scholars assuming a greater role in shaping the field and in re-examining foreign academic approaches to the Malay world [[6]](#ref-6). This shift in academic agency—from European to Malaysian institutions—mirrors the broader trajectory of the universities themselves, from colonial instruments of limited purpose to centres of national and international scholarly production.
