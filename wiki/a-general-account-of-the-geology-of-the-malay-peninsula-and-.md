# A general account of the geology of the Malay Peninsula and the surrounding countries, including Burma, the Shan States, Yunnan, Indo-China, Siam, Sumatra, Java, Borneo and other islands of the Dutch East Indies

E.S. Willbourn, Assistant Geologist of the Federated Malay States, published this comprehensive synthetic account of the geology of the Malay Peninsula and surrounding regions in 1922. Writing at a moment when colonial geological surveys were maturing into coherent regional frameworks, Willbourn argues that the area's structure was established by at least three major folding episodes, with the Mesozoic (Hercynian) and Tertiary movements exerting the most widespread influence, and that the resulting stratigraphic sequence spans from Pre-Cambrian basement to Recent alluvial deposits.

## Summary

The article is organized primarily by stratigraphical period, from Pre-Cambrian through Recent, and covers an enormous geographical area: the Malay Peninsula, Burma, the Shan States, Yunnan, Indo-China, Siam, Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and the smaller islands of the Dutch East Indies. Willbourn draws on the published work of Dutch geologists (particularly those of the Geological Survey of the Dutch East Indies), the Geological Survey of India, and the FMS Geological Survey, synthesizing their findings into a unified narrative. The structural framework is built around Suess's concept of "coulisses"—successive parallel mountain folds radiating from the Himalayan swell through the Shan States, the Malay Peninsula, and into Java and beyond. Willbourn identifies several major coulisses, including the Naga-Arakan-Andaman-Nicobar-Barissan fold, the Main Range fold, and the Annam cordillera, and traces their economic significance, particularly the association of the Main Range fold with the intrusion of Mesozoic granite and the world's richest tin deposits (pp. 238–239).

A central argument concerns the Permo-Carboniferous limestone, which Willbourn shows to be of very widespread occurrence across Indo-China, the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, and the eastern Archipelago, yet to express itself in markedly different forms depending on subsequent geological history. In Yunnan and the Shan States it produces karst topography with solution caves and subsidence depressions; in the Malay Peninsula, metamorphism by the Main Range granite converted it into coarsely crystalline marble that is impervious to water, producing vertical cliffs up to 2,000 feet and guano-bearing caves rather than karst (pp. 243–244). Willbourn also addresses the long-debated age of the "oudeschiefer" (old schist) formation of the Dutch East Indies, arguing against its pre-Cambrian assignment and correlating it with the Rhaetic to Jurassic shallow-water series of the Malay Peninsula (pp. 239, 248–249).

The Tertiary section is the most economically detailed, covering the petroleum-bearing Pegu system of Burma, the coal fields of the Irrawaddy basin, the Tertiary coals of the Malay Peninsula (Rantau Panjang, Enggor, Perlis), and the coal and volcanic geology of Sumatra, Java, and Borneo. Willbourn notes that the possibility of petroleum in the Malay Peninsula depends on the existence of extensive marine Tertiary beds east of the Main Range, but concedes there is no evidence for them (p. 254). The article closes with a discussion of Recent deposits, alluvial tin and ruby gravels, and evidence for a former land connection between the Peninsula and the Archipelago before a rise in sea level (p. 256).

### Key Findings

- The Pegu system of Burma (the main petroleum-bearing formation) attains a thickness of 12,000 feet, overlain by the Irrawaddy system of fluviatile deposits at 20,000 feet (p. 251).
- The Nantahin-Peluswa coal area near the Upper Chindwin River, covering 25 square miles, is calculated to contain 210 million tons of workable coal; the Boekit Asam field in South Sumatra holds an estimated 40 million tons with seams 6–7, 3–6, and 5–6 metres thick; and the Eocene field on Poeloe Laoet (Borneo) is estimated at 80 million tons (pp. 252–255).
- The Plateau Limestone of the Shan States and Yunnan exceeds 5,000 feet in thickness and is remarkably homogeneous, sandy to the touch but not siliceous in composition (p. 242).
- The Rantau Panjang coal (upper Miocene, ~20% moisture) is being profitably worked for tin mines and railways; the Enggor discovery of over 90 feet of calcareous shale beneath Tertiary sands suggests the deposits are marine rather than lacustrine (p. 253).
- The Letkobin mine in Shwebo district produced 10,000–15,000 tons annually for thirteen years before exhaustion in 1904; the Bantam Eocene coal field in Java holds approximately 2 million tons but is uneconomic due to intense folding (pp. 251, 255).
- The Kinta Valley boulder clays, source of the greatest part of the valley's tin deposits, are demonstrably older than the Mesozoic granite, as shown by the presence of tourmaline patches and quartz derived from veins that traversed the clays before their jumbling by solution of underlying limestone (pp. 247–248).

### Conclusion

Willbourn's definitive historical takeaway is that the geological evolution of the region is a story of successive folding, intrusion, and denudation, in which the Mesozoic granite intrusion stands as the single most consequential event for the area's economic history—producing the tin mineralization that underpinned the colonial economy of the Malay Peninsula—while the Tertiary period deposited the petroleum and coal resources that would drive the next phase of industrial development. The article implicitly frames geological knowledge as a prerequisite for rational resource exploitation in the colonial context.

## Context

- Willbourn held the position of Assistant Geologist, Federated Malay States, a colonial administrative appointment; the article functions as a synthesis of FMS, Dutch, and Indian Geological Survey work for a readership of colonial administrators and scientific colleagues.
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in its early integration of the Dutch East Indies geological literature (particularly the "oudeschiefer" debate) with the Burmese and Peninsular records into a single stratigraphical framework, predating later comprehensive treatments of Southeast Asian geology.
