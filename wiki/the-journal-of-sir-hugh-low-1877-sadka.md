# The journal of Sir Hugh Low, 1877. . Sadka

Winstedt and de Josselin de Jong published this edition and translation of a Malay *Undang-Undang* (customary law) text from Sungai Ujong in Negeri Sembilan in 1954, presenting it as a specimen of the Minangkabau-influenced legal tradition of the region. The work is based on two early-twentieth-century manuscripts and offers the first substantial published rendering of a complete Sungai Ujong customary law digest, revealing a sophisticated body of indigenous jurisprudence that operates in parallel with, and is explicitly subordinated to, Islamic canon law.

## Summary

The article presents a full translation of a 113-section *Undang-Undang* text, accompanied by a brief linguistic introduction and the original Malay (Jawi and Romanized) text. The editors note that the language of the Jawi manuscript is closely akin to Minangkabau in pronunciation, word-structure, grammar, and vocabulary, with features such as the use of *ba-* where standard Malay would employ *di-* or *ter-*, and a lexicon of terms (e.g., *chemo*, *chilok*, *gawal*) largely unknown to standard Malay speakers (pp. 3–4). The text itself is structured as a comprehensive legal and moral code: it opens with a cosmological declaration that "every man is a king in his own place," establishing a functional hierarchy in which each social role—commoner, chief, councillor, captain, child, woman, religious leader, even animals—holds sovereign authority within its proper domain (§ 2). It then proceeds to define the six categories of legal authority (ancient custom, created custom, inherited lore, decisions of common accord, ancient lore awaiting ratification, and decisions to be reached by later deliberation), the criminal law (the Eight and Twelve Laws), evidentiary procedure, fines, and the moral qualifications of various office-holders.

The text's most distinctive feature is its explicit articulation of the relationship between *adat* (custom) and *shara'* (Islamic law). The editors' introduction and the text's own passages make clear that custom is treated as a valid source of law only insofar as it does not contravene the Quran and Prophetic tradition; the text repeatedly invokes the hadith *Kullu 'l-baladi qiyamuhu bi 'adatihi* ("Each state is founded on wonted custom") while simultaneously subordinating custom to divine law (§§ 4, 6, 66, 71). The legendary founding narrative attributes the codification to three brothers—Datok Katumanggungan, Datok Parapateh Sabatang, and Datok Sri Maharaja—who, after a dispute among their descendants was resolved through a miraculous intervention (a serpent carrying a child to a teak tree), agreed to promulgate three codes: the code of Allah's Book, the code of the jurists, and the code employing the oath (§ 64). This tripartite structure is then sworn before the Ruler and made binding for all future generations (§ 65).

The text also contains extensive material on social philosophy, including classifications of human intelligence (the "three spans"), the qualities required of kings, chiefs, captains, scholars, and youth, and a catalogue of "One Hundred Evil Customs" to be avoided (§§ 48–62, 72–74). The final sections describe the legendary prerogatives of the Koto-Piliang moiety, the boundary agreement with the Sultan of Jambi sealed by the slaughter of two buffaloes, and the cultural practices attributed to Datok Sri 'diraja, including games, music, and material culture that "have spread to the isle of Sumatra" and beyond to Aceh and Mekka (§§ 80, 84, 106).

### Key Findings

- The edition is based on two manuscripts: a Jawi text (R.A.S. Maxwell 118) and a Romanized version (Maxwell 118A) produced in 1904 at Malacca; the Romanized version contains hiatuses where the Jawi text is corrupt (p. 3).
- The standard measures fixed by the three founding datoks are: 1 *chupak* = 12 *tael*; 1 *gantang* = 48 *bungkal*; 1 *bungkal* = 16 *mayam*; 1 *mas* = 4 *kupang*; 1 *kupang* = 4 *kenderi* (§ 63, § 96).
- Fines for theft are graded by the value of the stolen property: a gentleman who steals a person or property is fined 1 tael, 1 paha, 5 rial, 5 kupang, 1 piak, 4 kenderi; a non-gentleman faces whipping, seven days' imprisonment, and a fine of 10 mas, 2½ mas, 5 kupang, 5 busok, 1 kupang, 1 piak, 4 kenderi; theft of a buffalo carries a fine of 12½ mas, 5 kupang, 5 busok, 1 kupang, 1 piak, 4 kenderi in addition to restitution (§§ 32–33).
- The "Eight Laws" (criminal categories) are: sedition and rebellion, incest and fornication, robbery and violence, purloining and stealing; the "Twelve Laws" enumerate specific circumstances of proof including pursuit, chase, torn hair, wounds, binding, and arrest *in flagrante delicto* (§§ 22–24).
- The text specifies 25 items of *chemo* (circumstantial) evidence and 25 "signs of guilt" (*tanda jahat*), the first six of which lead to formal accusation (*tudoh*) and the remainder to incrimination (*chemo*), with atonement determined by the judge (§§ 25–26, 38–39).
- The boundary between Minangkabau and the Sultanate of Jambi was fixed by the Ruler and the Sultan of Jambi through the slaughter of two buffaloes (one facing upstream, one downstream) with long swords, followed by a buried oath: any Minangkabau who changes the agreement "shall be consumed by the Magic Steel of my ancestors," and any river-reach inhabitant who changes it "shall be consumed by the Magic Poison of my ancestors" (§ 84).

### Conclusion

Winstedt and de Josselin de Jong present this text not as a critical edition but as a "specimen" of the Malay *Undang-Undang* tradition, acknowledging that several passages remain unsolved and that the language has undergone strong Malay influence in the Romanized version. Their definitive historical takeaway is implicit in the text's own structure: the Sungai Ujong customary law code represents a living, self-conscious legal system that derives its legitimacy from a tripartite foundation—divine revelation, juristic reasoning, and ancestral consensus—and that explicitly positions itself as subordinate to Islamic law while maintaining its own autonomous procedural logic. The text demonstrates that Minangkabau-influenced customary law in Negeri Sembilan was not a primitive or pre-Islamic residue but a sophisticated, internally coherent body of jurisprudence with its own evidentiary standards, graduated penalties, and philosophical underpinnings.

## Context

- The primary archival sources are the R.A.S. Maxwell 118 (Jawi) and Maxwell 118A (Romanized, 1904) manuscripts, held in the Royal Asiatic Society collection; the Jawi manuscript "only came to light later" than the Romanized version (p. 3).
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in providing the first published full translation of a Sungai Ujong *Undang-Undang* text, filling a gap noted by the editors: "Little has ever been published on the dialect of Sungai Ujong, where this text comes from (or on any Negri Sembilan dialect)" (p. 3). It also preserves material on the Koto-Piliang/Bodi-Chaniago moiety system and the Minangkabau-Jambi boundary that is not found in other published sources.
