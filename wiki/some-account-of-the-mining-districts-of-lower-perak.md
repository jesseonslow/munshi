# Some account of the mining districts of lower Perak

J. Errington de la Croix, a French mining engineer (*Ingenieur de Mines*) on a government-sponsored scientific mission to Malaya, published this geological and mining survey of Lower Perak's tin-fields in 1881. The article provides a district-by-district account of the alluvial tin deposits, their geological settings, and the state of mining operations as of early 1881, arguing that the region's mineral wealth was vastly underexploited and that infrastructure investment would unlock decades of profitable production.

## Summary

De la Croix's account is structured as a field survey moving through the principal mining districts of Lower Perak—Ulu Kinta, Sungei Trap (including the Papan valley), Sungei Raya (Gopeng), Sungei Kampar, Chendarian, and the Tapa fields. His central analytical framework is geological: he identifies the granitic ranges as the source of tin-bearing material, traces its alluvial dispersal through limestone, white ferruginous clay, and talc-schist formations, and uses this stratigraphy to assess the depth, thickness, and richness of wash deposits in each locality. The limestone of the Kinta region, in particular, is noted as a significant carrier of drifted tin-ore in its fissures, a feature visible at Klian Gunong and the hills south of Gopeng.

The article's practical concern is the gap between the region's geological potential and the rudimentary methods of extraction employed by both Chinese and Malay miners. De la Croix repeatedly identifies inadequate drainage as the critical bottleneck: mines are abandoned at half their depth because neither community can organise systematic water removal, leaving ore permanently lost. He contrasts this with the infrastructure improvements already underway—river clearing on the Kinta to Kota Baru, cart-roads linking Gopeng and Papan to the Kinta, and a proposed navigable channel on the Batang Padang river—and argues that government investment in drainage and transport would be amply repaid over the long term.

The Chendarian district receives particular emphasis as the most promising future centre of production. De la Croix notes that large blocks of solid oxide found in the wash indicate exceptionally thick source lodes, and that the tin-field likely extends the full length of Bujang Malacca. He recommends that the Batang Padang river be made navigable to a steam-launch and a cart-road built to connect the district to the Kinta, which he considers the "great artery of the country."

### Key Findings

- The Klian Johan mine at Sungei Papan was the deepest in the State at 50 feet; thirteen mines in the Papan valley employed 500 men, and eleven furnaces returned an average of 40 pikuls of metal per day with a fuel ratio of one pikul of charcoal per pikul of ore (p. 2).
- The Gopeng district (Sungei Raya) had 15–16 Kongsis employing 700–800 coolies, with a weekly tin produce of 120 pikuls for the week ending 6 February 1881; surface "Lampong Works" in the area yielded 70 katties of metal per pikul of ore, a 70 per cent recovery rate (pp. 2–3).
- In the Chendarian district, the wash lay at a depth of 30 feet and was only 2–3 feet thick, yet furnished better results than any other part of the State; a single Malay-owned mine was let to a Kongsi of 50 Chinamen for one-tenth of the produce (p. 4).
- Gold was found in payable quantities only in the Tapa fields: at Klian Baru it averaged 260 grains per pikul of ore, and at Changkat Janka the lower wash layer contained 60 grains per pikul (pp. 5–6).
- The total area of the eight Lower Perak mining districts was estimated at 1,200 square miles (768,000 acres), of which one acre in one hundred constituted actual alluvial mining ground—7,680 acres of "surface utile" sufficient to employ 25,000 miners profitably for the next hundred years (p. 7).
- On the Sungei Kinta near Ipoh, a native river-washer could collect 15 katties of ore per day, valued at two dollars (p. 1).

### Conclusion

De la Croix's definitive assessment is that Lower Perak's tin-mining was still in a state of infancy: a few small centres had been exhausted, but they represented a trifling fraction of the total available ground. The binding constraint was not geological but infrastructural—specifically the absence of drainage systems and transport links. He concludes that with government investment in river navigation, cart-roads, and district drainage, the region could sustain a large mining population for a century, making it one of the most promising tin-producing areas in the world.

## Context

- De la Croix was serving as a French government mining engineer on a scientific mission to Malaya, which frames the article as an external technical assessment rather than a colonial administrative report; his recommendations for government action are directed at the Perak state authorities.
- The article includes a geological cross-section (p. 9) from the Senggan Range to Batang Padang, providing a visual stratigraphic reference that was unusual for journal publications of this period.
