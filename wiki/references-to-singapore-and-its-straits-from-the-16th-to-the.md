# References to Singapore and its Straits from the 16th to the Early 19th Century

Peter Borschberg and Benjamin J. Q. Khoo's 2021 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 94, No. 2) surveys European printed references to Singapore and its straits from the 16th through the early 19th century, arguing that the island was far from historically invisible before Raffles' arrival in 1819. The authors demonstrate that a substantial body of European knowledge about Singapore accumulated over three centuries was subsequently suppressed or ignored because it conflicted with the British imperial narrative of Singapore as a "new" settlement.

## Summary

The article is structured around three interlocking questions: what specific information about Singapore could a resourceful early modern European reader actually find in print; what mental image or impression would such a reader form from the collective body of available knowledge; and why this accumulated knowledge was deliberately set aside after 1819 (p. 89). The authors' review of hundreds of European printed sources—spanning Portuguese, Dutch, French, English, German, Italian, and Spanish publications—reveals that Singapore appeared across a remarkable range of genres, from short compendia entries and geographical treatises to poetry, fiction, and military histories (p. 89). The appendix alone catalogues dozens of pre-1819 works, including Camões' *Os Lusíadas*, Defoe's *Captain Singleton*, Valentijn's multi-volume *Ouden Nieuw Oost-Indien*, and numerous VOC and Portuguese administrative and travel texts.

The authors identify four recurring thematic clusters in how Singapore was represented: as a place of death and disaster (shipwrecks, piracy, disease); as a geographic marker or waypoint; as a site of historical antiquity (references to the old kingdom of Singapura); and as a locus of geostrategic control or military concentration (p. 89). These themes, they argue, reveal a more complex and continuous European engagement with the island than the standard colonial narrative permits. The article thus positions itself as a corrective to what the authors call the "1819 and all that" framework, which has dominated Singapore historiography and public memory alike (p. 90).

A further dimension of the argument concerns the politics of knowledge itself. The authors contend that the post-1819 erasure of pre-colonial references was not accidental but functional: a Singapore with a deep, documented European past complicated the founding myth of a blank-slate settlement established by British benevolence and enterprise (p. 90). They call for a rethinking of how colonial knowledge "formed and circulated," insisting that pre-1800 European texts "cannot be dismissed simply as narratives of discovery, conquest, and dominance" but instead "reflect an exchange of knowledge that involved Southeast Asian inputs and contributions" (p. 90).

### Key Findings

- The authors' review encompasses hundreds of European printed sources from the 16th to early 19th century, spanning at least seven languages (Portuguese, Dutch, French, English, German, Italian, Spanish) and multiple genres including poetry, fiction, compendia, encyclopaedias, and official dispatches (pp. 89–90).
- Four dominant thematic frameworks structure European references to Singapore: (1) place of death and disaster; (2) geographic marker; (3) site of historical antiquity; (4) locus of geostrategic control or military force concentration (p. 89).
- The appendix lists approximately 50+ distinct pre-1819 European publications that reference Singapore or its straits, with dates ranging from the 1530s (Rithaymer, 1538) to the 1810s (Marsden, 1811; Wilkes, 1816) (pp. 92–95).
- The authors assert that British colonial agents were "wrong to treat Singapore as a place forgotten by time before 1819," and that the deliberate post-1819 suppression of earlier references served a specific political and imperial purpose (p. 89).
- The article identifies "serious and insidious flaws in the colonial narrative" that "continue to haunt Singapore historiography today," positioning the study as part of a broader revisionist movement challenging the "1819 and all that" paradigm (p. 90).
- A companion article on compendia references published c. 1511–1819 is noted as forthcoming in the June 2022 issue of *JMBRAS*, indicating this article is part of a larger research programme (p. 95, n. 1).

### Conclusion

Borschberg and Khoo's definitive takeaway is that the standard narrative of Singapore as a historically blank slate before 1819 is "factually inaccurate and does a great disservice to the contemporary history landscape in Singapore" (p. 90). The accumulated European knowledge of the island over three centuries was not lost through ignorance but actively suppressed because it was incompatible with the imperial founding story. The authors call for innovative analytical frameworks that move beyond "entrenched British imperial historiography and its long echoes," and they suggest that ongoing digitization of scattered global sources will "fuel revisionist historical accounts" not only for Singapore but for the wider region (p. 90).

## Context

- The study draws on a wide corpus of early modern European printed materials (Portuguese, Dutch, French, English, German, Italian, Spanish) now increasingly accessible through digitization, rather than relying on a single archival repository.
- Historiographically, the article extends Borschberg's and Khoo's earlier collaborative work on Singapore before 1819 (notably their 2018 *JMBRAS* article "Singapore as a Port City, c. 1290–1819") and contributes to the broader revisionist project associated with Kwa Chong Guan's *1819 & Before: Singapore's Pasts* (2021), to which Borschberg also contributed a chapter on Portuguese and Dutch records.
