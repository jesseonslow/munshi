# The fishing boats operated from Singapore Island

C.A. Gibson-Hill, a long-serving ornithologist and ethnographer attached to the Singapore government, published this systematic typology of fishing craft in 1950, drawing on the Fisheries Department's licensing records and his own field observations to document the full range of boats operating from Singapore Island in the immediate post-war period. The article's central argument is that the island's sheltered, silt-heavy waters and extensive mangrove coastline have produced a fleet dominated by small, lightly built craft rather than the larger sailing vessels typical of open-coast Malaya, with the kelong stake-net platform serving as the single most productive fishing unit in the region.

## Summary

Gibson-Hill organises his survey around the three principal categories of licensed fishing craft—powered boats, keeled small boats (kolek), and Chinese keelless boats—and adds a brief note on the Perahu Payang, a large Malay sailing boat that migrates seasonally from the Trengganu coast. His method is taxonomic rather than narrative: each boat type is described in terms of hull form, construction technique, rig, propulsion, and typical operational use, with attention to the ethnic and professional boundaries that separate Malay-built from Chinese-built craft. The licensing returns of the Fisheries Department provide the quantitative backbone, while Gibson-Hill's own measurements, sketches, and photographs supply the morphological detail that distinguishes one type from another.

A recurring theme is the degree of cultural transfer visible in the boats themselves. The Johore Kolek, the representative Malay craft, retains traditional wood-peg fastening and single-piece ribs, yet its builders in the Singapore area have begun adopting the Chinese diagonal-nail technique, and its crews increasingly use the long-shafted Chinese oar in place of the short Malay paddle. Conversely, the Chinese-built Kolek Chiau and Kolek Selat, while sharing the general double-ended, shallow-keeled form, differ from their Malay counterpart in construction sequence, rib assembly, and finish. Gibson-Hill treats these variations not as mere curiosities but as evidence of a living process of technical exchange between the two communities working the same waters.

The article also serves as a preliminary study for what Gibson-Hill describes as an official survey of the Singapore fisheries and fishing industry, and its appendix tabulates the full range of fishing gear licensed in 1948–49, from kelongs and drift nets to portable traps and beach seines, thereby situating the boats within a broader picture of fishing effort and gear diversity.

### Key Findings

- On 31 December 1949, 213 power-driven vessels held valid fishing-boat licences; of these, 186 were licensed solely for carrying fish to market, while only 43 were registered for work inside the 30-mile limit (p. 148).
- The total licensed fleet grew from 1,530 boats in 1947 to 1,722 in 1949, with the Kolek (549–574), Kolek Chiau (368–439), and Sampan/Kolek Selat (280–382) together accounting for roughly three-quarters of all entries (p. 148).
- In 1949, 247 kelongs were licensed (139 in depths exceeding 3 fathoms, 108 in shallower water), making the kelong the single most productive fishing unit in the Singapore area (p. 170).
- A 20-foot Kolek Chiau cost approximately $6.50 per foot to build in the Beach Road yards, while a 20-foot Kolek Selat cost roughly $120 and an 18-foot example could be built by two men in 6–7 days (pp. 154, 157).
- In 1949 there were 4,387 registered fishermen working from Singapore Island: 3,073 Chinese, 1,301 Malay, 10 Indian, and 3 of other nationalities (p. 170).
- The Perahu Payang, a 33–45 foot Malay sailing boat carrying a crew of 15–20 men, operates off Singapore only during the north-east monsoon, making its headquarters at Siglap (pp. 168–169).

### Conclusion

Gibson-Hill's definitive takeaway is that the fishing fleet of Singapore Island is a product of its specific coastal environment—sheltered, silt-laden, and fringed with mangroves—which favours small, stable, shallow-draught craft over the larger sailing boats of open-coast Malaya. The kelong, rather than any single boat type, is the engine of local fish production, and the boats themselves are best understood as a set of specialised, lightly built workhorses adapted to inshore and near-shore operations, with the powered launch serving primarily as a carrier rather than a fishing vessel.

## Context

- Gibson-Hill was a colonial-era government officer (ornithologist and ethnographer) based in Singapore, and this article was explicitly prepared as a preliminary study for an official fisheries survey, giving it the character of an administrative-technical document as much as a scholarly one.
- The primary data sources are the Fisheries Department's licensing returns for 1947–49 and Gibson-Hill's own field measurements and photographs; the article draws on a small body of prior literature (Waters 1946, Worcester 1947–48, Hornell 1946) for comparative context on Chinese and Malay boat types.
