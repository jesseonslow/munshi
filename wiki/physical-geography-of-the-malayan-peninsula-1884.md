# Physical geography of the Malayan Peninsula (1884

J.E. Tenison-Woods, a British geologist and colonial official, published this account in *Nature* on 18 December 1884, summarising the results of ten months of fieldwork in the State of Perak. The letter provides one of the earliest systematic descriptions of the peninsula's physical geography, integrating geology, mineralogy, botany, and hydrology into a coherent picture of the region's mountain architecture and tin-bearing formations. It was reprinted in JMBRAS Vol. 93 (2020) as a historical document of colonial-era scientific exploration.

## Summary

Tenison-Woods describes the geology of Perak as comprising three principal formations: an immense granite mass rising into sharp, nearly meridional ridges; a Palaeozoic formation of slates, mottled sandstones, and clays found most abundantly at the foot of the ranges; and limestone in detached outliers of precipitous character, probably also of Palaeozoic age (pp. 137, 142). The tin deposits are exclusively stream tin in the form of small abraded cassiterite crystals of a distinctive blackish-gray or brown aspect, found in one or two strata from one to five feet thick in the lowest levels of the drift, generally covered by ten to thirty feet of tin-free material (p. 138). The matrix lies in the upper part of the granite at its junction with the Palaeozoic clays, and the workings are concentrated on the western slopes where denudation and wash have been greater due to prevailing rainfall patterns (pp. 138, 141).

The mountain system of the western peninsula consists of detached groups of parallel ridges, each roughly twenty-five miles long and fourteen to sixteen miles broad, separated by narrow passes such as Gapis Pass at approximately 400 feet elevation (pp. 139–140). Tenison-Woods catalogues these groups from south to north: the Dindings Islands, Gunong Bubu (5,600 ft), Mount Ijau (4,400 ft), the Kurau Group, Mount Inas (over 4,000 ft), Kedah Peak (over 4,000 ft), and the Kinta Ranges (3,750 ft), before turning to the Main Range, where he identifies a peak over 8,000 feet and Gunong Robinson at over 7,000 feet, and notes unconfirmed reports of a summit exceeding 12,000 feet (pp. 141–142). He observes that the Perak River flows southward for over 180 miles, collecting the Plus and Kinta from the east, and that the entire system prevents any drainage from the central range reaching the west coast directly (p. 141).

A notable botanical observation concerns the presence of Australian genera (*Melaleuca*, *Leptospermum*, *Podocarpus*, *Leucopogon*) on the highest granite ridges above 5,000 feet, a phenomenon he had not previously seen recorded in the Malay peninsula and which he speculates may represent relics of an ancient flora once extending across the Eastern Archipelago (p. 138). He also records only two known occurrences of recent volcanic rocks—basaltic dykes in the Kinta Valley and near the Karau River—preventing proper examination due to dense jungle and surface weathering (p. 138).

### Key Findings

- Tin in Perak is exclusively stream tin (cassiterite), found in strata 1–5 feet thick at the lowest drift levels, with the matrix in upper granite at its junction with Palaeozoic clays; the deposits are "practically inexhaustible" (pp. 137–138).
- The western mountain groups are detached, parallel, granitic ridges running nearly meridionally, each covering approximately 400 square miles, separated by elevated passes that block river outlets (pp. 139–140).
- Australian plant genera (*Melaleuca*, *Leptospermum*, *Podocarpus*, *Leucopogon*) occur exclusively on granite ridges above 5,000 feet, with no representation on lower slopes even 100 feet below the summit (p. 138).
- The Main Range contains a peak estimated at over 8,000 feet and Gunong Robinson at over 7,000 feet; an unverified report of a summit exceeding 12,000 feet south of Arung Pura remains unconfirmed (pp. 141–142).
- Only two instances of recent volcanic rocks (basaltic dykes) are known in the peninsula: one in the Kinta Valley and one near the Karau River east-south-east of Penang (p. 138).
- The mining industry is almost exclusively in Chinese hands, with Malays described as "not good miners"; gold occurs in small scaly flakes in sparing quantity at only one or two localities (p. 138).

### Conclusion

Tenison-Woods presents Perak as a landscape of extraordinary geological regularity—detached granitic massifs of uniform dimensions and orientation, overlain by Palaeozoic clays and limestone outliers, with tin concentrated in predictable western-slope alluvial traps. His account underscores both the richness of the deposits and the extreme difficulty of further exploration, attributing the Main Range's "mysterious unexplored recesses" to the combined effects of dense jungle, steep ridges, and perpetual cloud cover, with only a few Sakai as inhabitants (p. 142).

## Context

- The letter was written from Penang on 8 September 1884, during the period of intensified British colonial administration in Perak following the Pangkor Treaty of 1874, which placed the state under a British Resident.
- The reprint in JMBRAS Vol. 93 (2020) situates this text within the Society's ongoing project to recover and contextualise colonial-era scientific contributions to the natural history of the region.
