# Physical geography of the Malayan Peninsula (1884

## Abstract

From the late 1920s into 1960s, child adoptions within the diasporic Indian community in Malaya and Singapore revealed a revisioning of what it meant to be “Indian”. Cultural constraints that limited adoption in India lost their impact, and diasporic Indians received girl children, mostly from the Chinese community, into their families, shifting away from a preference for sons. In welcoming these adopted girls into their lives and treating them as family members, they contributed to a reformulation of ethnic group identity.

## Summary

This article examines cross-ethnic child adoption in pre-independence Malaya and Singapore, specifically the practice of Indian families adopting Chinese daughters from the late 1920s through the 1960s. The central argument is that this practice reveals a fundamental reconfiguration of what it meant to be "Indian" in the diaspora: cultural constraints that made adoption stigmatized in South Asia—infertility shame, caste obligations, son preference—lost their force in the plural ethnic environment of colonial Malaya, where these norms were attenuated by the conditions of migration and settlement. The author draws on oral history interviews with eleven adopted women and seven of their adult children, supplemented by testimony from a nun at Cheras Convent in Selangor and a Chinese geomancer, to reconstruct the social logic behind these adoptions and the circumstances that produced them.

The article situates these adoptions within the broader demographic and economic transformations of the 1920s–1950s, when colonial policies shifted from importing single male labourers to encouraging family-based settlement. Poverty, high fertility rates, and the low cultural value accorded to Chinese daughters created a pool of abandoned girls, while Indian families—constrained by childlessness, a desire for gender balance, and the absence of the dowry system—found in Chinese girls a viable option that also circumvented caste considerations entirely. The Catholic Church, particularly convents in Singapore and Selangor, functioned as informal adoption brokers during this period, receiving children from families who could not afford to raise them and matching them with adoptive couples.

### Key Findings

- In 1956, 301 women reported to the Singapore Family Planning Association that they had given up one or more children for adoption; of these, only six were Malay and two Indian, the remainder Chinese (p. 145).
- Singapore's Adoption of Children Ordinance was passed in 1939 but its implementation was delayed by the Japanese Occupation; a Children and Young Persons Ordinance followed in 1948, and a Federation of Malaya adoption law was passed in 1952 (p. 142).
- Between 1947 and 1957, the Indian population in Malaya and Singapore grew by nearly 80 per cent, driven by internal growth rather than migration (p. 141).
- Lower-income families in the period lived on an average income of less than $150 a month, making each additional child a significant financial burden (p. 145).
- Chinese girls born in the Year of the Tiger were particularly likely to be abandoned if a death or illness occurred in the family at the time of birth, per Chinese horoscope beliefs (p. 146).
- India's Emigration Act of 1922 led to the appointment of an Indian Agent in Malaya to improve the gender balance among migrants; by the 1930s, Tamil women constituted around 30 per cent of arrivals from India (pp. 140–141).

### Conclusion

Devasahayam argues that cross-ethnic adoption in the Indian diaspora demonstrates that diasporic Indians were "interpretative subjects" of their settlement, actively transmuting cultural practices from their homeland in response to the plural ethnic environment of Malaya and Singapore. The practice reveals that ethnic identity is not fixed but is continuously reconfigured through interaction, with diasporic communities borrowing and adapting practices from their new environment while fitting them into traditional structures.

## Context

- Primary sources include oral history taped interviews held at the National Archives of Singapore (Sister Damien Murphy, 1983; Daisy Vaithilingam, 1993; Ann Wee, 2008), student theses from the University of Malaya and National University of Singapore, and the author's own fieldwork interviews conducted over a seven-year period (2004–2011) with additional fieldwork in 2020.
- The article contributes to the historiography of ethnic identity formation in colonial Malaya by demonstrating how adoption practices reveal the fluidity of cultural boundaries, complementing existing scholarship by Heng Hong Ngoh (1957), Ann Wee, and Daphne Pereira on cross-cultural adoption in the region.
