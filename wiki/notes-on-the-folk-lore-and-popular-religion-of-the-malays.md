# Notes on the folk-lore and popular religion of the Malays

C. Otto Blagden, a colonial administrator in Malacca, published this ethnographic paper in 1896, drawing on his personal observations of rural Malay communities to argue that the popular religious practices of country Malays are fundamentally pre-Islamic in character despite their nominal adherence to Muhammadan ritual. The article, read before the Straits Philosophical Society, documents the persistence of animistic beliefs, spirit-worship, and the institutional role of the *Pawang* as a village functionary operating entirely outside the orthodox Islamic framework.

## Summary

Blagden's central thesis is that rural Malays are "only superficially Muhammadan" (p. 1). While they perform the five daily prayers, attend Friday mosque services, and observe the fast of Ramadhan, they simultaneously maintain a parallel system of beliefs and practices that he traces to a "heathen origin" (p. 1). The most prominent feature of this parallel system is the *kramat*—a holy place found in virtually every village, theoretically the grave of a saint or village founder, but in many cases a site of ancient nature-worship with no grave at all. Blagden documents specific examples, including a *kramat* on Bukit Nyalas consisting of granite boulders overhanging a cliff, identified as the dwelling-place of a *jin islam* who presides over water and rain, and another consisting of a tree whose swelling trunk was believed to grow or shrink with the agricultural fortune of the district (pp. 2–3).

The article then turns to the taxonomy of spirits (*hantu*), distinguishing between wild jungle spirits and "domesticated" familiar spirits attached to individuals or families. The *Pawang*—a hereditary village functionary whom Blagden identifies as the legitimate successor to the primitive "medicine-man" or "village sorcerer"—serves as the accredited intermediary between the human and spirit worlds (p. 5). His authority extends across all agricultural operations, fishing, mineral prospecting, and cases of sickness, and he is entitled by custom to specific fees from each householder after a good harvest (p. 6). His ordinances, known as *pantang*, include prohibitions on working in the rice-field on certain lunar days, the use of the sickle for cutting padi, and the practice of threshing by beating against a box—rules that Blagden interprets as preserving older agricultural forms against innovation (pp. 6–7).

The paper culminates in a detailed first-hand account of an earth-spirit propitiation ceremony at the village of Sebatu, observed by Blagden in October. The ritual involved a wooden altar oriented to the cardinal points, the burial of a black goat's skin and entrails with a large iron nail, the sprinkling of *tepong tawar*, the throwing of *ketupats* across the field, and a man circling the altar while spitting rice into the ground in imitation of violent nausea (pp. 8–10). Blagden notes the ceremony's "curious analogies with the sacrificial ceremonies of some of the wild aboriginal tribes of Central India" and observes its persistence within twenty miles of Malacca, where Islam had been established for approximately six centuries (p. 11).

### Key Findings

- *Kramats* are ubiquitous in Malacca territory, with "hardly a village" lacking two or three in its immediate neighbourhood; many are not graves at all but ancient sites of nature-worship, such as rock formations or singular trees (pp. 1–2).
- The *Pawang* is a hereditary village functionary entirely outside the Muhammadan religious establishment, entitled to five gantang of padi, one gantang of rice, and two chupaks of *emping* from each householder after a good harvest, and 7½ cents after treating a sick person (p. 6).
- The *pantang* system prescribes a fixed customary price for padi sold within the village community, a practice Blagden interprets as a relic of communal economic rights predating competitive market exchange (p. 7).
- The earth-spirit ceremony at Sebatu included the burial of a goat's skin with a large iron nail beneath the altar, the head oriented eastward, and the chanting of the *kalimah* (Muhammadan creed) three times by the assembled villagers—demonstrating the syncretic layering of Islamic formulae over animistic ritual (pp. 9–10).
- More strictly Muhammadan Malays of Arab descent privately disapproved of *kramat* ceremonies and spirit propitiation, viewing them as "polytheistic idolatry," but their influence in country districts was minimal and the practices were sustained by "immemorial custom" (p. 11).
- During epidemics, small elaborately constructed vessels were sent out to sea to carry off malignant spirits responsible for disease, a practice Blagden witnessed in the village of Sempang (p. 11).

### Conclusion

Blagden concludes that these observances, though "in themselves trivial," are "interesting relics of an old-world superstition" that will only yield "gradually to the spread of education" (p. 11). His definitive historical takeaway is that the coexistence of orthodox Islamic practice and pre-Islamic animistic ritual is not a contradiction to the Malay peasant but a seamless, customary whole, and that the persistence of these practices within a community where Islam had been established for six centuries testifies to the deeper root of custom than any book-learning in an agricultural population.

## Context

- Blagden was serving as a colonial magistrate in the Malacca district, which gave him direct access to rural villages and the authority to be called upon for disputes involving *Pawang* regalia and *pantang* transgressions (pp. 5–7).
- The article builds on W. E. Maxwell's earlier 1881 paper in the JSBRAS, which had first articulated the "thoroughly non-Muhammadan character" of common Malay beliefs (p. 1).
