# Malays abroad

Malays Abroad

The presence of Malay-speaking communities beyond the conventional boundaries of the Malay Peninsula and the Indonesian archipelago constitutes a significant but historically under-documented dimension of the Malay World. From the early seventeenth century, the Dutch colonial administration introduced Malay and other Indonesian groups to Sri Lanka as political exiles, company servants, and soldiers, establishing a community whose literary and cultural output has only recently been recognised as part of the broader Malay literary tradition [[1, pp. 103–104]](#ref-1). In the Indochinese Peninsula, a man of Malay origin known as Tuen Phaow operated as a military and political figure between 1786 and 1820, attested in Vietnamese, Cam, and Khmer sources, exemplifying the mobility and political opportunism characteristic of precolonial mainland Southeast Asian politics [[2, p. 1]](#ref-2), [[3, p. 44]](#ref-3). These cases, together with the wider pattern of Malay settlement in Sri Lanka, demonstrate that the Malay World extended well beyond its physical periphery, sustained by linguistic, religious, and cultural ties that persisted across considerable distances.

### Origins and Settlement Patterns

The earliest documented Malay presence in Sri Lanka dates to the middle of the seventeenth century, when the Dutch colonial government introduced political exiles, company servants, and soldiers to the island [[1, p. 104]](#ref-1). Captain H. M. Said, writing in 1926, argued for an even earlier Malay presence, tracing it to the Portuguese conquest of Malacca in 1511 and the subsequent campaign of Alphonso de Sosa against the Laksamana in 1523, which resulted in the capture of large numbers of slaves, some of whom were likely brought to Ceylon and settled at what became known as "Slave Island" in Colombo [[4, p. 267]](#ref-4). Said further identified a Javanese component in the community, linking it to the 1722 banishment of forty-four Javanese princes and chiefs to Ceylon and the Cape of Good Hope, as recorded in Crawfurd's *Indian Archipelago* [[4, p. 266]](#ref-4). The intermarriage of these Malay and Javanese groups, and their shared pride in the Malay identity, explains the mixed physical features and naming conventions observed among Ceylon Malays [[4, p. 267]](#ref-4).

The military dimension of Malay settlement in Sri Lanka was substantial. An appended note to Said's article summarising a 1924 lecture by E. Reimers, Government Archivist of Ceylon, documents Malay military service in the island from the thirteenth century through the disbandment of the Malay regiment in 1873, including their role in the defence of Colombo in 1795–96 and the execution of three Malay officers in 1803 who refused to desert the British [[4, p. 268]](#ref-4). The Ceylon Malay Regiment, which grew from the Dutch-era garrison forces, was incorporated into the Ceylon Rifle Regiment in 1827 and became the single largest employer of the Malay community, so much so that the regiment had emerged as somewhat an embodiment of a larger kinship organisation [[1, pp. 105–108]](#ref-1). The heterogeneous population of Amboinese, Bandanese, Javanese, Balinese, and Malay soldiers, recruited primarily from the communal settlements of Batavia, gradually developed into a new Indonesian national group with a simplified Malay language as their native tongue, as Vlekke observed [[1, pp. 105–106]](#ref-1).

In the Indochinese Peninsula, the pattern of Malay presence was markedly different. Tuen Phaow, identified as Malay through the use of the title *Tuan* and references to *Makah* (potentially Kelantan or Aceh), though his specific country of origin remains unconfirmed in primary sources, established himself in the highlands of southern Panduranga [[3, p. 2]](#ref-3). His army, as identified in Cam narratives, was composed of Churu, Raglai, Koho, Rhade, lowland Cam, and Malays of Cambodia (*kur jawa*), the latter wearing distinctive black tunics, large trousers, and brown turbans dyed with a mixture of leaves and pond mud [[3, pp. 7–8]](#ref-3). This indicates a pre-existing Malay presence in the region, distinct from Tuen Phaow's own origins, and suggests that Malay communities had established footholds in mainland Southeast Asia well before the colonial period.

### Literary and Cultural Production

The discovery of more than a hundred Malay written texts, mostly in manuscript form, among the small community of Malays in Sri Lanka has revealed that the island's Malay community belonged to a fairly literate society [[1, p. 104]](#ref-1). The literary material, comprising *Hikayats*, *Syairs*, religious *Kitabs*, and works of divinity and magic, depicts a community in which much of the literature derived from the great traditions of Malay literature popular throughout the Malay World, while some texts were original writings penned by members of the community [[1, p. 104]](#ref-1). The Sri Lankan Malay literary tradition was characterised by the same anonymity that enveloped other traditional Malay literary writings, with occasional references to copyists and owners of manuscripts in the colophons [[1, p. 104]](#ref-1).

Baba Ounus Saldin (1832–1906) stands as the most important literary personality of the Sri Lankan Malay community. His pioneering work in Malay journalism and lithographic printing establishes the island as a significant, previously overlooked centre of traditional Malay literary activity [[1, p. 103]](#ref-1). His first major undertaking was the printing of a fortnightly Malay newspaper, *Alamat Langkapuri*, which began publication in the middle of June 1869 and can now be considered the first Malay newspaper in the Malay World to be released in Jawi script, predating *Jawi Peranakan* of Singapore (1876) [[1, p. 122]](#ref-1). This finding revises William Roff's earlier contention that *Jawi Peranakan* was the first periodical of any kind published in the Malay language [[1, p. 125]](#ref-1). Saldin's second newspaper, *Wajah Selong* (1895), maintained a network of agents in Singapore, Malacca, Penang, Siam, and Java, and was listed in a Singapore bookseller's catalogue as the only Jawi-language newspaper available for sale during the 1896–1900 slump in Malay periodical publishing in the Peninsula [[1, pp. 124–125]](#ref-1).

Saldin's only original poetic work, *Syair Faid al Abad* (1904), comprises approximately 323 stanzas across 48 pages, covering Sri Lankan political history from Sinhalese rule through the Kandyan rebellion of 1818 and the Ceylon Rifle Regiment's dispatch to Hong Kong, alongside moral and religious themes [[1, p. 126]](#ref-1). The work symbolised a culmination of traditional literary activities in Sri Lanka, written at the dying stage of the Malay literary tradition in the island, when the number of people who could read and write Jawi had declined drastically [[1, p. 126]](#ref-1). Saldin also contributed to the teaching of the local Malay language through two grammar booklets published in 1891, *Mirat al Ghulam* and *Assirul Jawi fi'ta'lim al Nahw al Jawi*, the latter consisting of 32 pages containing concise lessons on tenses, number, pronouns, suffixes, and prefixes [[1, p. 127]](#ref-1).

In the Indochinese Peninsula, Tuen Phaow's construction of royal legitimacy drew explicitly on Malay kingship traditions. He self-awarded Sanskrit-derived titles, built a palace, demanded prostration, issued edicts proclaiming supernatural prowess, and grounded his authority in an orthodox Islamic framework distinct from the syncretic Islam practised by the Bani communities of Panduranga [[3, p. 44]](#ref-3). Weber argues that these performances relied on the Malay political currency of *nama* (reputation) and *daulat* (sacral power), and were not merely cosmetic but essential to the attraction of followers across ethnic lines [[3, p. 44]](#ref-3). Tuen Phaow rooted his authority in Islam, referencing *Ali* (Po Athur Ali) and enforcing practices such as Ramadan fasting to distinguish his orthodox faith from local unorthodox traditions [[2, pp. 9–10]](#ref-2).

### Political and Economic Engagement

The economic activities of the Sri Lankan Malay community were shaped by their military origins. Saldin's own career illustrates the transition from military to civilian life. He paid a penalty of eight British pounds to purchase his premature discharge from the Ceylon Rifle Regiment, against a daily corporal's pay of nine pence, and subsequently earned a salary of twelve sterling pounds per month with Venn & Co. by April 1863, compared to three and a half pounds for a *Subedar* (Captain) in the regiment [[1, pp. 108–109]](#ref-1). He went into the lithographic printing industry, importing a more advanced press from Europe at a cost of 250 Rupees on 2 July 1878, and established his own business house to import consumer items from abroad [[1, p. 110]](#ref-1). His firm grew to be a significant commercial establishment owned by a Malay at the turn of the century, and his son Muhammad Khalid inherited part of the business under the name M.K. Saldin & Company, rising to become the first Malay State Councillor in 1924 [[1, p. 110]](#ref-1).

The community's social life was organised around the mosque and the military institution. The main mosque for the Malay section of *Kampung Kertel* (Slave Island) in Colombo was situated in the *Bai Kandi* area, on land originally donated in 1780 by one Pandan Balie, a well-to-do person of Bali origin [[1, p. 113]](#ref-1). The Slave Island mosque dispute, triggered by the contested appointment of *Khatib* Thaiban in 1869, persisted for nearly twenty years until reconciliation in 1886, an event reported as far as Penang [[1, pp. 114–115]](#ref-1). Saldin played a leading role in resolving the dispute and in establishing the Malay Pensioners' Fund in 1877, as well as in converting the Java Lane Mosque into a fully-fledged *Jum'a* Mosque, officially known as *Masjid al Jamia* since the 1880s [[1, pp. 115–117]](#ref-1).

In Cambodia, Tuen Phaow's political career reached its zenith when he was appointed *Chavea Ta-la-ha* (first minister) in 1819, a position equivalent to viceroy or regent during King Ang Chan's reign, and was placed at the head of 5,000 Cambodian men for the construction of the Vinh Te Canal, which was supervised by 5,000 workers and 500 Vietnamese soldiers [[3, pp. 16–17]](#ref-3). He functioned as a key intermediary in the Vietnamese imperial court's efforts to extend its influence over Cambodia, facilitating Vietnamese expansion and acting as a proxy to suppress anti-Vietnamese factions within the Khmer court [[2, p. 17]](#ref-2). His tenure ended abruptly in 1820 following the suppression of the Ke revolt in Prey Veng, when he was accused of using troops against the people and of massacring Buddhist monks and civilians indiscriminately during the pacification [[3, pp. 17–18]](#ref-3).

## Research and Documentation

The study of Malays abroad has evolved from brief archival notes to sustained biographical and historiographical investigations. Captain H. M. Said's 1926 note in the *JMBRAS* represented an early attempt to trace the origins of the Ceylon Malay community, drawing on Crawfurd's *Indian Archipelago* and the Ceylon Malay tradition of Tuan Pengiran's burial at Fir Shahib Makam in Colombo [[4, p. 266]](#ref-4). The appended summary of E. Reimers' 1924 lecture provided the first systematic account of Malay military service in Ceylon from the thirteenth century to 1873 [[4, p. 268]](#ref-4). These early contributions established the basic chronological framework but left the cultural and literary dimensions largely unexplored.

B.A. Hussainmiya's 1991 biographical study of Baba Ounus Saldin marked a significant shift in the field. Drawing primarily on Saldin's own *Kitab Segala Perhingatan*, a 58-page family journal written in Jawi script, supplemented by regimental records, contemporary newspapers, and the *Register of Printed Books in Ceylon*, Hussainmiya established Sri Lanka as a recognised node in the network of traditional Malay literary production [[1, p. 103]](#ref-1). The study's principal historiographical contribution was the correction of William Roff's claim that *Jawi Peranakan* was the first Malay-language periodical, and the demonstration that the Sri Lankan Malay community had developed a strong Malay cultural identity that required constant reinforcement through a two-way relationship with the Malay world in the East [[1, pp. 105]](#ref-1). The primary sources identified include the *KSP* (microfilmed at Universiti Malaya and Arkib Negara Malaysia), Sri Lanka National Archives (regimental and municipal records), Ceylon General Orders, Ferguson's *Ceylon Handbook and Directory*, and the *Register of Printed Books in Ceylon* [[1, p. 134]](#ref-1).

Nicolas Weber's two articles in 2021 extended the geographical scope of the subject to the Indochinese Peninsula, synthesising three previously studied-in-isolation source traditions—Vietnamese official records (*Đại Nam Thực Lục*, *Gia Định Thành Thông Chí*, *Quốc Triều Chánh Biên*, *Minh Mệnh Chính Yếu*), Cam versified narratives (*ariya*) preserved in EFEO and Société Asiatique manuscripts, and the Khmer chronicle *Sastra Voat Kroch*—into a single biographical narrative [[3, p. 44]](#ref-3). Weber's central argument is that Tuen Phaow's case deconstructs the rigid ethnic boundaries assumed by nationalist historiographies, exemplifying the patterns of mobility, political opportunism, and shifting alliances that characterised precolonial mainland Southeast Asian politics [[3, p. 44]](#ref-3). The article builds on Inrasara's Cam-language publications and Weber's own 2003 French translation of the Cam narratives, representing a methodological advance in the triangulation of multilingual archival sources [[3, p. 44]](#ref-3).

The cumulative effect of these studies is to establish that the Malay World, as a cultural and linguistic sphere, cannot be confined to the Malay Peninsula and the Indonesian archipelago. Sri Lanka and the Indochinese Peninsula each represent distinct but significant extensions of Malay cultural, political, and literary activity, sustained by communities whose members maintained conscious ties to the broader Malay world through language, religion, and literary production.
