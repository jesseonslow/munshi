# Kelantan silverwork

W. Linehan's 1951 article examines six tin objects in the Perak Museum known as *gunong-gunong* ("mountains"), arguing that these modest bridal ornaments are material survivals of the ancient Hindu-Buddhist mountain-temple cult associated with Mount Meru and royal legitimacy in the Indianized kingdoms of Southeast Asia.

## Summary

Linehan begins with a detailed physical description of six tin specimens held in the Perak Museum, ranging from 15.2 to 38.1 cm in length, with bases of fairly pure tin that have flaked above. Their provenances span Chenderiang in Batang Padang, Gunong Kechil in Dindings (now Lumut), and Kuantan in Pahang, with two of the Dindings specimens having been excavated in 1922 alongside two model tin elephants and a round tin ingot. The author then systematically catalogues nine distinct uses of the term *gunong-gunong* in Malay culture, from throne backrests and bridal dais decorations to the mountain piece in Kelantan shadow-play and the triangular marks on keris damask, establishing that in every instance a tree forms the principal decorative motif.

The core argument draws on comparative evidence from Javanese and Indonesian sources to situate these objects within a broader religious and political framework. Linehan invokes van der Hoop's discussion of the *gunungan* in the Javanese shadow-play as a symbol of totality, Coedes's account of the royal linga placed on a pyramid at the axis of the world in Hinduized kingdoms, and Winstedt's analysis of palace circumambulation ceremonies as echoes of the Mount Meru cult. The *pancha-persada* tiered platform used in Malay lustration ceremonies is identified as another survival of the same mountain-temple tradition. Linehan is careful to note that the tin objects themselves need not be of great age; it is the belief system they embody that is ancient.

A speculative but intriguing observation is that the objects occur in pairs—one pyramidal, one dome-shaped—suggesting a male-female symbolic pairing. Local informant Daing Abdul Hamid of Bruas confirmed their use as decorations for the bridal dais and bed, noting that in former days they were made of tin but are now sometimes made of wood, often topped with a peacock figure symbolizing royalty.

### Key Findings

- Six tin *gunong-gunong* objects in the Perak Museum measure between 15.2 and 38.1 cm in length; bases are of fairly pure tin with upper portions flaked (pp. 99–100).
- Two specimens from Gunong Kechil, Dindings, were excavated in 1922 and presented by Mrs. W. S. Cookson, found in association with two model tin elephants and one round tin ingot (p. 99).
- The term *gunong-gunong* applies to at least nine distinct object types or features in Malay culture, all sharing a tree as the principal decorative element (pp. 99–100).
- Kuantan, provenance of one specimen, is identified with *Gangga Nagara* in the Malay Annals, a state conquered by a Raja Shulan from Nagapatam, interpreted as a Chola raid in the 11th century (p. 102).
- The *pancha-persada* tiered platform used in Malay royal lustration ceremonies is identified as a survival of the mountain-temple tradition (p. 102).
- Four of the six objects were found in pairs, each pair comprising one pyramidal and one dome-shaped specimen, possibly representing male and female elements (p. 102).

### Conclusion

Linehan's definitive takeaway is that the tin *gunong-gunong*, however modest in material and however recent in manufacture, are tangible expressions of a belief system of great antiquity—the veneration of mountains or mountain-temples brought to Malaya by Indian or Indianized colonists and associated with the Sailendra dynasty, "the king of the mountains." The objects connect the domestic sphere of Malay marriage customs to the cosmological and political symbolism of Mount Meru that underpinned kingship across the Indianized kingdoms of the region.

## Context

- The article draws on the Perak Museum collection and engages with contemporary scholarship by Winstedt (*Kingship and Enthronement in Malaya*, 1947), Coedes, and van der Hoop, situating itself within the mid-century effort to trace Hindu-Buddhist continuities in Malay material culture.
- A companion article in the same issue, *Ancient Tin Ingots in the Perak Museum*, is referenced for discussion of the linga and of hillock significance in aboriginal marriage customs, indicating a coordinated programme of research on Perak's tin heritage.
