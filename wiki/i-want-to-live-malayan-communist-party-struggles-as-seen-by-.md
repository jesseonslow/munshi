# 'I Want to Live': Malayan Communist Party Struggles as Seen by Female Defectors

## Abstract

The voices of women defectors from the Malayan Communist Party (MCP) rarely feature in the MCP’s grand narrative of its struggle for liberation in Malaya or in accounts prepared by party veterans, and only become visible through the prism of government propaganda. Using published memoirs, interview material from the Oral History Centre at Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM), and personal interviews with two former women cadres of the MCP, this article focuses on the views of women who defected before the signing of the 1989 Haadyai Peace Accord. It provides the perspective of ordinary members on the MCP’s ideas regarding war and revolution, and the position of women.

## Summary

This article addresses a significant gap in the historiography of the Malayan Communist Party (MCP): the near-total absence of ordinary members' voices, particularly those of women who defected before the 1989 Haadyai Peace Accord, from the party's grand narrative of liberation. Mahani Musa argues that the MCP's collective memory, as preserved in elite memoirs published between 2003 and 2011, presents a homogenised picture of heroic sacrifice and mass support that conceals internal dissent, leadership failures, and the divergent motivations of rank-and-file members. By recovering the accounts of female defectors, the article demonstrates that the party's self-representation as a vehicle for national liberation and female emancipation was contradicted by the lived experiences of many of its own members.

The core argument is that not all female guerrillas shared the elite perspective on revolutionary struggle. Defectors from both the First Emergency (1948–60) and the Second Emergency (1968–89) identified common reasons for leaving: the futility of the armed struggle after years of jungle life, chronic food scarcity, the psychological toll of separation from children given for adoption, and—among Malay women—a growing conviction that the MCP was incongruent with Islamic practice. Musa further shows that kinship and affectual ties were crucial to women's initial participation in the movement, but these bonds did not translate into a shared revolutionary vision; in several cases, women followed the defection decisions of husbands or lovers, revealing that patriarchal structures persisted even within a party that claimed to liberate women from patriarchy.

The article draws on a range of primary sources to construct this counter-narrative: published memoirs by both elite leaders (Chin Peng, Abdullah C. D., Suriani Abdullah, Shamsiah Fakeh, Ah Yan) and defectors (the 1983 memoir by 'Xiulan', Lam Swee's *My Accusation*), the Oral History Centre collection at Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia containing interviews with three former 10th Regiment female cadres, and personal interviews conducted in Thailand in 2009. The memoir by 'Xiulan' is particularly significant as it provides detailed internal critique of the rectification campaigns, the southern expeditions, and the conduct of party leadership—material that is conspicuously absent from elite accounts and that reveals the party's internal fractures in a way that government propaganda never could.

### Key Findings

- As many as 377 communists surrendered to police between 1948 and 1950, with the most significant early case being Wahi Annuar, commander of the 10th Regiment, who surrendered at Mentakab police station on 21 February 1950 (p. 11).
- MCP guerrilla strength declined from approximately 8,000 in 1951 to about 3,000 by 1955, following the relocation to south Thailand and the failure of the Baling Talks (p. 15).
- During the rectification campaign of late 1969 to early 1970, 35 members of the 12th Regiment were massacred, 200 were exposed and criticised, and 70 were sacked from the party (p. 17).
- The first southern expedition (1971) comprised 37 members; of the 12 in Lingqing's group who reached Tanjong Malim, only 4 survived the journey, with deaths caused by hunger, exhaustion, and the administration of sleeping pills by the expedition leader's wife (pp. 21–22).
- The MCP collected monthly subscriptions from villagers in south Thailand at rates of 3½ baht per acre for old rubber trees and 4 baht per acre for young productive trees, and in many cases resorted to coercion when voluntary donations failed (p. 16).
- The 8th Regiment had 140 members when the MCP Central Committee ordered the execution of all recruits who had joined after 1962; senior regimental cadres disobeyed the order and fabricated reports of compliance (p. 19).

### Conclusion

Musa's definitive historical takeaway is that the voices of female defectors, though marginalised in the MCP's collective memory, reveal that the party's claims of widespread mass support and its role as a channel for female emancipation were fundamentally at odds with the experiences of ordinary members. The defector accounts demonstrate that the MCP's revolutionary project failed to sustain the loyalty of its own rank and file, and that the affectual and kinship bonds which drew women into the movement ultimately proved stronger than ideological commitment when confronted with the realities of jungle warfare, leadership corruption, and internal violence.

## Context

- Primary archival collections explored include the Oral History Centre at Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (interviews with three former 10th Regiment female cadres), the Malayan People's Army 10th Regiment Archives (1959–1989) held at the International Institute of Social History, Amsterdam, and personal interviews conducted in Betong and Yala, Thailand, in 2009.
- The article contributes to the historiographical project of re-narrating Malaysian communist history from below, challenging the elite-dominated MCP narrative by centring the perspectives of defectors whose accounts had been either marginalised or dismissed as government propaganda.
