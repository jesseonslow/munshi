# Joining the communist underground: the conversion of English-educated radicals to communism in Singapore

Yeo Kim Wah's 1994 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* traces how a small cadre of English-educated Malayan intellectuals—chiefly former leaders of the Malayan Democratic Union (MDU)—were absorbed into the Malayan Communist Party (MCP) underground in Singapore between June 1948 and January 1951. The central argument is that these radicals were not covert communists operating through the MDU during 1945–48 but genuine anti-colonial nationalists whose deep commitment to an independent united Malaya, compounded by Emergency repression and the inspirational momentum of the Chinese revolution, drove them into the communist fold through the Anti-British League (ABL).

## Summary

The article reconstructs the organizational architecture of the MCP in Singapore during the Emergency, from the Singapore Town Committee (STC) at the top through District Committees, functional committees (Students', Working, Propaganda, United Front), and down to the ABL and armed units such as the Shock Troopers. It then follows the conversion process in detail: after the MDU dissolved in June 1948, the STC's United Front Committee, under Ah Har, established an English-speaking section of the ABL specifically to recruit and groom English-educated intelligentsia for eventual MCP membership. Through the intermediary Wong Siong Nien, Eu Chooi Yip was the first former MDU radical to cross the threshold, followed by P.V. Sarma, Dr Joseph K.M. Tan, Lim Chan Yong, and finally Lim Kean Chye at the close of 1949. The article devotes substantial attention to the recruitment of university students at the University of Malaya, where Dr Tan and Lim Chan Yong brought fourteen undergraduates under their political wing, with the *Malayan Orchid* serving as the movement's chief student organ.

A significant portion of the article is devoted to resolving the historiographical question of whether the English-educated radicals were "covert communists" during the MDU period. Yeo marshals testimony from both former MCP cadres (Ah Chin, Ho Seng) and the radicals themselves (Eu Chooi Yip, P.V. Sarma, Lim Chan Yong) to demonstrate that they had not attained the requisite level of Marxist theoretical knowledge, commitment, or disciplined political behaviour to qualify as communists before the MDU's closure. Their conversion required an additional psychological impetus that came only after the Emergency sealed the fate of constitutional politics and the Chinese revolution appeared to be sweeping across Asia. The article also examines the "London dimension"—the activities of Lim Hong Bee and the British Communist Party among Malayan students in Britain—concluding that their influence on the Singapore ABL was tenuous and indirect, and that the movement was essentially indigenous.

The motivations section challenges the British colonial explanation that ABL members were driven by "a psychological wish for self-expression" or "a desire for exhibitionism." Yeo argues instead that the radicals were moved by a powerful anti-colonial conviction, reinforced by the Asian revolution and the Chinese revolution specifically, and by personal ties and political camaraderie. He notes that the radicals made no clear distinction between democratic socialists and communists, and that their obsession with national independence and a "socialist millennium" overrode all other considerations, including the long-term implications of allying with the MCP.

### Key Findings

- Between June 1948 and November 1949, the police arrested 490 persons in Singapore (compared to over 10,000 in the Federation); by December 1949, 107 remained detained and 263 had been banished (pp. 50–51).
- The ABL numbered approximately 2,000 members in June 1950, the bulk of whom belonged to the Chinese-speaking ABL; the English-speaking ABL comprised 36 activists, of whom 11 had achieved full MCP membership by January 1951 (pp. 45–46).
- The Shock Troopers, the MCP's most feared armed unit in Singapore, consisted of 25 to 30 members described as "utterly ruthless, fanatical communists" (p. 32).
- On 28 April 1950, a member of the Shock Troopers threw two hand grenades at Governor Franklin Gimson outside the Happy World Stadium; only one exploded partially, and Gimson escaped with a slight bruise (p. 42).
- Dr Joseph K.M. Tan and Lim Chan Yong recruited 14 students at the University of Malaya; all except one (Seow Li Cheng) were eventually absorbed into the communist underground (p. 43).
- Of the 541 Malayan students in Britain in 1950, 337 were in London, of whom 182 were from Singapore; the Lim Hong Bee group's influence on this population was assessed as limited and indirect (p. 57).

### Conclusion

Yeo's definitive historical takeaway is that the English-educated radicals paid the heaviest possible price for their revolutionary commitment: by joining the MCP underground, they forfeited any opportunity of emerging as national leaders in their own right. They were "the child of a new Asia" fired by a spontaneous spirit of independence, yet in the subterranean conditions of the Emergency they could not deploy their superior intellect to the full and were destined to play second fiddle to experienced revolutionaries of lesser calibre who controlled the arms and the muscles of the armed struggle. By January 1951 they had been almost completely rounded up, and thereafter the hardcore either lived in exile or continued underground, while most others abandoned communism and withdrew from politics entirely.

## Context

- The article draws on British Colonial Office files (CO series), Special Branch and Police Intelligence Journal reports, and the author's own interviews with former MCP cadres (Ah Chin, Ho Seng, Eu Chooi Yip, P.V. Sarma, Lim Chan Yong, C.V. Devan Nair, Abdullah Majid) conducted between 1986 and 1993, as well as oral history transcripts of James Puthucheary and Gerald de Cruz.
- Historiographically, the study challenges the British colonial narrative that ABL members were "deceived and dragooned" into cooperation with the communists, and provides rare insider perspectives from former MCP cadres on the organizational mechanics and ideological logic of the conversion process. It also corrects Dennis Bloodworth's and Cheah Boon Kheng's characterizations of the English-educated radicals as covert communists during the MDU period.
