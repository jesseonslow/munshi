# The journals of William Scott, 1794–1805. Transcribed and annotated M. Langdon

## Abstract

A series of handwritten journals kept by William Scott between 1794 and 1805 provides a rare first-hand glimpse into life in the early settlement of Penang, or Prince of Wales Island as it was then officially known. The journals cover a number of several significant historical events, such as French aggression in the Straits, the taking of Malacca and the Moluccas, the abortive ‘Manilla expedition’, as well as visits to Malay and Thai officials and to Calcutta—all from the perspective of a young man in his 20s with respectable connections in Penang.

The originals of this journal were housed in the National Archives of Scotland, with the reference MS. 2896. On 1 April 2011, that archive merged with the General Register Office for Scotland to become the National Records of Scotland (NRS). Despite best efforts the NRS has, to date, been unable to locate the originals. The following transcript is therefore from a microform copy of the journal archived by Monash University’s Sir Louis Matheson Library, Melbourne, Australia, with the reference Microfilm 6001, R239.

## Summary

This article presents a transcribed and annotated selection from the handwritten journals of William Scott, a young Scottish merchant who arrived in Penang (then Prince of Wales Island) in October 1795 at the age of 22 and remained there until 1798, returning in 1800 and staying until 1804. The journals offer a rare first-hand perspective on the early settlement from the viewpoint of a man with respectable mercantile connections—his elder cousin James Scott was a business partner of Francis Light and the preeminent trader in the colony. The transcription covers Scott's sea voyage from Scotland to Penang aboard the *Lord Macartney*, his social and recreational life on the island, and his observations of several major geopolitical events: the French squadron's passage through the Straits of Malacca, the British seizure of Malacca and the Moluccas from the Dutch, and the abortive Manila expedition of 1797.

The journals are particularly valuable for their detailed descriptions of the island's geography and natural environment—excursions to Gibraltar Hill (Bukit Cendana), the waterfall, Flagstaff Hill (Penang Hill), the mainland river Pra, and a circumnavigation of the island by galley. They also document the founding of James Town in 1796, the social rituals of the small European community (Sunday parties, St. Andrew's Day dinners, housewarmings), and the health challenges of the tropical climate. Scott's later voyages to Calcutta and his observations of the Andaman Islands and Junksalong (Phuket) extend the geographical scope beyond Penang itself.

The original manuscripts were held at the National Archives of Scotland (MS. 2896) but have been lost following the 2011 merger into the National Records of Scotland. The transcription is therefore based on a microform copy held at Monash University's Sir Louis Matheson Library in Melbourne.

### Key Findings

- The French frigates *Cybelle* and *Prudente* passed Malacca on 20 July 1795, having captured the *Sea Nymph*, the *Java* cutter (after a two-hour engagement in which the cutter's captain and 14 men were killed), a Portuguese ship, the *Fort Louis* snow, and the Bombay ship *Laz Bux* (p. 112).
- The British expedition against Malacca sailed on 7 August 1795 with the frigates *Orpheus* and *Resistance*, transports *Carnatic* and *Ewar*, and supporting vessels; the Dutch ceded the settlement with "slight resistance" on 18 August (pp. 113–114).
- The Manila expedition assembled between 6,000 and 7,000 men aboard approximately 50 square-rigged vessels in Penang harbour in September 1797, but was cancelled when H.M. Ship *Hobart* arrived with orders to stop it, reportedly due to fears of a Tippu Sultan attack on India (pp. 124–126).
- The first tiger ever recorded on Penang Island was killed on 7 May 1797, having carried off a Portuguese man and his gun; it was described as "a beautiful Royal Tiger" (p. 124).
- Flagstaff Hill (Penang Hill) had a perpendicular height of approximately one mile, with a thermometer reading of 66½°F at the summit compared to 85–90°F at the Fort below; a bungalow and semaphore station had been established there by Francis Light from mid-1793 (pp. 110–111).
- The settlement of James Town was founded in February 1796 at Soongy Cluan, approximately twelve miles south of George Town, in a valley about 15 miles in circumference with extensive Chinese pepper plantations (p. 118).

### Conclusion

Langdon positions Scott's journals as an invaluable primary source that balances and complements official accounts of the period, adding granular detail on shipping movements, social life, and natural history that is absent from administrative records. The author acknowledges a key limitation: Scott's journals lack sustained description of the township itself and its non-European inhabitants, though they compensate with vivid accounts of the island's landscape, its social rituals, and the broader maritime and military context of the early 1790s and 1800s.

## Context

- The transcription is drawn from a microfilm copy (Microfilm 6001, R239) at Monash University's Sir Louis Matheson Library, as the original manuscripts (NRS MS. 2896) have been lost.
- The study contributes to the historiography of early Penang by providing a continuous private narrative spanning the critical period from Light's death (October 1795) through the establishment of the island as a strategic naval and commercial base, filling gaps left by the sparse official correspondence of the era.
