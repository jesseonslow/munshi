# Bird notes. F.N. Chasen and C.B. Kloss. Records of the Raffles Museum, No. 13

Dato' Sedia Raja Abdullah published "The Leading Saints in Rembau" in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* in December 1925 (Vol. III, No. 3, pp. 101–104). Writing from within the Rembau district of the Straits Settlements, the author undertakes to reconstruct the biographical traditions of the principal *Keramat* (saints) of the area, arguing that while later generations have introduced inventions into the oral records kept by local *Pawang* (spiritual healers), the accounts he presents—drawn from a *Pawang* regarded as a high authority—are as near to authentic as the secretive culture of old-school Malay religious knowledge permits.

## Summary

The article addresses a fundamental problem in the study of Malay *keramat* traditions: the tension between the deliberate secrecy of learned Malays, who treated all branches of religious and secular knowledge (*'Ilmu Akhirat* and *'Ilmu Dunia*) as esoteric, and the inevitable accretion of later inventions as younger generations sought to fill the gaps. Abdullah acknowledges that conflicting traditions attach even to the most revered saints, and positions his source—a *Pawang* well-versed in local life-sketches—as the most reliable available authority (p. 101).

He organises the saints into two categories. The first, the "living but invisible" *Keramat*, are believed to exist in a state of perpetual life, testified by dream appearances, ancient trees, stone relics, and sacred animals at their abodes. The genealogical narrative traces descent from the supreme *Keramat* To' Anjong at Pagar Ruyong, through his son Patawalau (who married a Johore princess and walked across the sea) and his seven children, who settled at various localities. The second category, *Keramat Hampiran*, comprises ordinary persons to whom sanctity and supernatural powers were conferred by the invisible class; Abdullah profiles To' Hijau (born with a green complexion, founder of Kampong Chembong), To' Anggut (whose beard reached his knees), and To' Lumpoh of Sepri (p. 102–103).

The article also documents the ritual economy surrounding these saints: annual village processions before the rice-planting season involving goat and buffalo sacrifice, public feasts, and Arabic prayers by the village *Lebai*; the placement of saffron rice and lemon juice on stone-converted relics; and the *Pawang*'s role in diagnosing illness as the work of specific demons tied to particular landscapes, with recovery contingent on paying vows at the relevant saint's abode (p. 102).

### Key Findings

- The supreme *Keramat* of the Rembau tradition is To' Anjong at Pagar Ruyong, who married Siti Fatimah and fathered Patawalau and Puteri; Patawalau married the Johore princess Puteri Sa-Kuntum Bunga and the couple walked across the sea to Gunong Dato' (Gunong Rembau) (p. 101).
- Patawalau and his wife produced seven children—To' Paroi, To' Dalong, To' Gayah, To' Sigai, To' Lendek, To' Dioh, and To' Manggam—who settled respectively at Paroi, Batang Malaka, Seri Menanti, Gunong Pasir, Padang Muar (Johore), Batang Malaka, and Mampong (Rembau) (p. 102).
- Physical relics still visible at the time of writing include praying-mats converted to granite at Palong and Penajis (Sepri), and three wells likewise converted to granite at Chembong, on which saffron rice and lemon juice are placed for vow-payment ceremonies (p. 102).
- To' Hijau, founder of Kampong Chembong, was born with a green complexion; his right side was entirely green (skin, blood, flesh, bones, nails, hair), and his saliva and bath-water possessed remedial virtues; a white tiger guards the village (p. 103).
- To' Anggut crafted the drum at Masjid Tanah in the forest of To' Hijau; the completed drum could not be lifted by the whole village until To' Anggut ordered forty-four virgins to carry it, and it sounded of its own accord every Thursday night until it collapsed from age (p. 103).
- To' Pawang Idris of Sepri, who received the *Pinggan Jambar* (a plate inscribed with the *Shahada*) as a personal gift from To' Saeh Serban Kuning, died approximately twelve years before publication (c. 1913); the plate reportedly sounded of its own accord every Thursday night when smoked with incense, and was regarded as a supremely effective amulet against demons and poison (p. 103).

### Conclusion

Abdullah's definitive historical takeaway is explicitly modernist and reformist: the overwhelming influence of these saints on the Malay population constitutes "a lamentable obstacle to their economic, spiritual and moral advancement," and the persistence of pre-Islamic "Days of Ignorance" relics is attributable to ignorance of fundamental Islamic teachings and indifference to secular education. He closes on a cautiously optimistic note, observing that among the rising generation these primaeval beliefs are "slowly but surely disappearing" (p. 104).

## Context

- Dato' Sedia Raja Abdullah writes as an insider—a titled local authority in Rembau who is both a custodian of the traditions he documents and a critical observer of their social effects. His dual position as traditionalist and reformer gives the article a distinctive tension: the biographical narratives are presented with evident respect, yet the concluding judgment is firmly aligned with colonial-era modernisation discourse.
- The article draws on the oral testimony of a named *Pawang* (To' Pawang Idris of Sepri, deceased c. 1913) and cross-references Dr. R. J. Winstedt's earlier account of Lebai Janggut/To' Anggut in *J.R.A.S., M.B.* Vol. II, Part III (p. 277), situating the study within the small network of colonial-era scholars and local informants documenting Malay religious life in the 1920s.
