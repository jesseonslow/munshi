# The origins of an irrigation policy in Malaya: a review of developments prior to the establishment of the Drainage and Irrigation Department

D.E. Short and James C. Jackson, writing in 1971 for the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, trace the half-century of administrative indecision, competing economic priorities, and institutional fragmentation that preceded the establishment of the Drainage and Irrigation Department on 1 January 1932. Their overarching thesis is that the creation of this department was not the product of a coherent policy trajectory but rather the belated resolution of a structural problem: the inability of the colonial state to reconcile the demands of a lucrative extractive economy with the agricultural needs of a rice-dependent population.

## Summary

The article begins by establishing that pre-modern Malay water-control technology was rudimentary—relying on temporary brushwood dams, earth bunds, and bamboo waterwheels (*kinchir*)—and that the introduction of sophisticated irrigation engineering was essentially a colonial achievement. The authors argue that this was not simply a matter of technological transfer but was shaped by the specific political economy of the protectorate system, in which revenue from tin and rubber consistently took priority over rural development. The 1893 *Report on the Rice Supply of the Colony and Native States* is identified as the first major landmark of official interest, yet it produced no comprehensive policy and in some respects undermined the local initiative that had been the primary engine of water-control development up to that point.

The Krian Irrigation Scheme (1899–1906) occupies the analytical centre of the article. Short and Jackson use it to illustrate the gap between engineering ambition and administrative capacity: the scheme's cost ballooned from an initial estimate of $285,000 to $1.6 million, and the engineers who successively reviewed it (Vincent, O'Shaughnessy, Anderson, Murray) disagreed fundamentally on whether storage was necessary, whether double-cropping should be planned for, and how the distribution system should be designed. O'Shaughnessy, who resigned in a breach-of-contract dispute after estimating the full scheme at $850,000, proved the most farsighted in anticipating the need for storage and a separate distribution system. The authors draw on the *Collected Reports on Krian 1892–1930* and Perak *Annual Reports* to reconstruct this process of technical and political negotiation in detail.

The second half of the article examines the period 1909–1931 across the northern padi plains (Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan) and the Federated Malay States. In the north, the extension of British influence brought the peninsula's most important rice-growing regions under colonial administration, but financial constraints (Kedah's revenue still dominated by opium in 1909; Kelantan running a deficit) and the absence of qualified engineering staff limited progress. In the FMS, the formation of the Irrigation Branch in 1913 was quickly subsumed into a broader Hydraulic Branch in 1921, whose priorities shifted toward flood mitigation and river conservancy in the mining districts rather than padi irrigation. The Depression of 1929 brought all new work to a halt, and it was the acute "rice problem"—imports exceeding half a million tons annually at a cost of $50 million—that finally compelled the High Commissioner to appoint the Rice Cultivation Committee, whose 1931 report recommended the creation of a dedicated federal department.

### Key Findings

- The Krian Irrigation Scheme, completed in 1906, cost $1.6 million (over five times the original $285,000 estimate) and served approximately 52,000 acres of padi land at an average cost of £2-6s-8d per acre, comparable to the Bari Doab works in the Punjab (pp. 92–93).
- By 1930, Malaya's rice imports stood consistently in excess of ½ million tons annually at a cost of $50 million, while home production was only a quarter of total consumption and the FMS alone produced less than 50,000 tons of milled rice per year (p. 102).
- Kedah's average padi acreage rose from 144,978 acres (1918–20) to 196,345 acres (1930–32) following approximately $1 million worth of water-control works; in Kelantan, acreage actually declined over the same period due to financial paralysis and less favourable topography (p. 98).
- The Pulau Tawar scheme in Pahang, completed in 1923 at a cost of $72,894, proved a "disastrous failure," with the works completely out of action between 1926 and 1934 and much of the commanded land going out of cultivation (p. 101).
- Between 1929 and 1932, no new drainage or irrigation work was undertaken anywhere in the peninsula, and annual expenditure on such works in the northwestern states fell to less than $44,000 (p. 97).
- The 1893 Rice Report was prompted in part by the fact that Selangor alone had imported $M11½ million worth of rice in 1890 (p. 83).

### Conclusion

Short and Jackson conclude that the establishment of the Drainage and Irrigation Department in 1932 was "a constructive beginning to a new era of irrigation in Malaya after half a century of indecision." The department did not solve all technical or political problems, but it represented the first time that water control for padi cultivation was treated as a matter of federal policy rather than a residual function of local public works or the private initiative of individual district officers.

## Context

- The study draws heavily on colonial *Annual Reports* (Perak, Kedah, Kelantan, Perlis, Selangor, FMS Public Works Department, FMS Department of Agriculture), the *Collected Reports on Krian 1892–1930*, and the 1931 *Report of the Rice Cultivation Committee*, supplemented by the 1893 *Rice Report* and various *Perak Government Gazette* entries.
- Historiographically, the article fills a gap identified by the authors in the emerging literature on Malayan rice cultivation (citing Zaharah Haji Mahmud, R.D. Hill, Robert Ho, and J. Rutherford) by providing the first systematic account of the institutional and policy history of government irrigation, rather than focusing on individual schemes or agronomic systems.
