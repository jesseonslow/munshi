# The Hikayat Abdullah; an annotated translation. A.H. Hill

A. M. S. published this biographical memoir of Captain Francis Light in 1895, on the centenary of Light's death, to recover the personal and administrative record of the man who founded Penang in 1786. The author's overarching thesis is that Light was a man of "sound sense, probity and judgment" whose pioneer enterprise, though conducted with minimal support from the East India Company, established a settlement whose commercial and strategic merits were vindicated within a decade of his death.

## Summary

The memoir reconstructs Light's career from his first correspondence with Warren Hastings in 1771 through his death in October 1794, drawing on Light's own diary, despatches to the Governor-General, and the published volumes of Logan's Journal. The author is particularly concerned with two historiographical problems: first, the legitimacy of Light's direct negotiation with the Raja of Kedah in the absence of any Siamese consent, and second, the administrative and fiscal policies that shaped the Settlement's first decade. On the first question, the author marshals Light's 1787 despatch to Lord Cornwallis to demonstrate that the question of Siamese suzerainty was never raised in the original negotiations, and that the later controversy between Colonel Low and J. R. Logan was an anachronistic projection of 1840s politics onto an 1780s situation. On the second, the author defends Light's policy of perpetuity land grants against the restrictive five-year lease regulation imposed by Lord Teignmouth in 1794, arguing that the contemporary evidence shows Light correctly anticipated that the restrictive policy would have "instantly stop[ped] all further advance" in settlement and pepper cultivation.

The narrative is informed by Light's official diary (printed in Logan's Journal Vol. III), his despatches to Cornwallis and Macpherson, and the 1805 Court of Directors' despatch establishing the Presidency Government at Penang, which the author treats as the definitive contemporary verdict on Light's work. The author also corrects a persistent error in Balfour's *Encyclopædia of India* regarding Light's marriage, identifying his wife Martina Rozells as a Portuguese Christian of the Roman Catholic Mission rather than a Malay princess.

### Key Findings

- Light arrived at Penang on 15 July 1786 with 100 native marines, 30 lascars, 15 European artillerymen, and 5 officers; formal possession was taken on 11 August 1786 (pp. 2–4).
- The population reached approximately 10,000 by the end of 1789 and over 20,000 by 1795, of whom the Chinese numbered not much over 3,000 (p. 4).
- Light's 1791 victory at Kuala Pry saw his force of 400 men disperse a Kedah force that had swelled to over 8,000 Malays (p. 7).
- The first import duty (2 per cent *ad valorem* on tin, pepper, and betel-nut) was levied in 1801 and produced $13,076 in that year (p. 14).
- The Governor-General's regulation of 1 August 1794 limiting land grants to five-year leases was rescinded on 22 August 1796, after Light's death (pp. 14–15).
- Light's salary as Superintendent was Rs. 1,000 per month, which he found insufficient to forgo his merchant income (p. 7).

### Conclusion

The author's definitive takeaway is that Light's "infant Port" succeeded not through institutional support but through the founder's personal qualities—courage, adroitness, and a "large-minded sense of public duty"—and that the 1805 establishment of the Presidency Government confirmed what Light had argued from the outset: that Penang's harbour, soil, and central position made it "an acquisition of very great importance in a commercial and political view."

## Context

- The author writes as a contemporary Penang resident commemorating the centenary of Light's death, drawing primarily on Logan's Journal (Vols. III–V) and the unpublished Light despatch of 18 June 1787 (Logan Vol. IV, p. 634).
- The memoir contributes to the historiography of early Straits settlement by defending Light's judgment against the later Siamese-suzerainty controversy and by recovering the administrative logic behind the perpetuity land grant policy.
