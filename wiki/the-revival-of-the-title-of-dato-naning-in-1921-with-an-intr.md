# The revival of the title of Dato Naning in 1921. With an introductory note by the editor

R.J.F. Curtis, a former District Officer of Alor Gajah who was present at the 1921 proclamation ceremony as a cadet, published this first-hand account in 1980 tracing the history of the Naning chieftainship from its Dutch-era origins through its abolition by the British in 1832 and its formal revival in 1921. The article argues that the 1921 restoration of the title to Omar bin Hasan was a legally and politically calculated act by the Straits Settlements administration to resolve a succession dispute and reassert colonial authority over a community that had preserved its Minangkabau customary institutions in defiance of British abolition.

## Summary

The article addresses a specific administrative problem: how the British colonial government in Malacca could reassert legitimacy over the Naning community after a century of informal governance under the title of Penghulu Besar (later Demang) Naning, a post that had quietly preserved the old customary succession procedures without official recognition. The historical dilemma was compounded by the fact that the British had abolished the title in 1832 following the Naning Wars, yet the community's matriarchal Minangkabau succession system—rotation among three lines called *Perut*—continued to operate unawares of colonial officials. By 1918, a succession dispute between Omar bin Hasan (*Perut Satu*) and Yunus bin Nordin (*Perut Dua*) had become intractable, and Yunus's seizure of the Naning regalia, particularly the *Kris Bisa* (poisonous kris) and the seals of office, in 1921 forced the administration to act.

Curtis's narrative draws on his own eyewitness testimony of the 3 September 1921 proclamation ceremony at the District Office, Alor Gajah, as well as on the administrative correspondence between the Resident Councillor L.E.P. Wolferstan, the Attorney General in Singapore, and the Colonial Secretary. The legal strategy was to officially proclaim Omar as Dato Naning, thereby giving him an unassailable title under which he could sue Yunus in the High Court for the return of the heirlooms. Curtis also records the aftermath: Omar's death shortly after the proclamation, the appointment of his eleven-year-old nephew Othman as successor (with Dato Arshad acting), and Othman's execution by the Japanese at Bedok, Singapore, in 1942. The article closes with the post-Independence reduction of the title's status by the Malacca government and the present holder, Dato Mohamed Shah, who is described as a living authority on Naning custom.

The primary sources informing the narrative include the 1641 Dutch agreement with Naning (referenced but not reproduced), the 1829–1833 military correspondence, the 1921 Annual Report of the Straits Settlements Government (reproduced in full as Appendix B), and the genealogical list of eighteen Dato Naning holders supplied by Dato Mohamed Shah in 1979 (Appendix A).

### Key Findings

- The original 1641 Dutch agreement required Naning to pay one-tenth of its annual rice crop; this was reduced to 400 gantangs of padi in 1765, but the British in 1829 demanded the full ten percent, triggering the Naning Wars (pp. 96–97).
- The British military campaign of 1831–32 required cutting an avenue eighty yards wide from Malacca to Rembia for artillery; the force of 1,000 Indian troops took six months to cover 32 miles to Taboh Naning, suffering casualties including Ensign Walker (p. 97).
- Dato Dol Syed was pardoned in 1834 and granted a house in Malacca and a pension of 100 rupees per month; he died in 1849 (p. 97).
- The 1921 proclamation ceremony took place on 3 September 1921 at the District Office, Alor Gajah; Yunus attended wearing one white and one black shoe and placed a typewritten invitation bearing the Dato Naning seal on the officials' table before quietly taking his seat (pp. 98–99).
- Dato Othman bin Kring was officially proclaimed Dato Naning in late 1932 by Resident Councillor J.S.W. Arthur; he was executed by the Japanese at Bedok, Singapore, two weeks after the fall of Singapore in 1942 (pp. 99–100).
- After Independence in 1957, the Malacca government reduced the status of Dato Naning to a level equivalent to a Senior Penghulu (p. 100).

### Conclusion

Curtis presents the 1921 revival as a pragmatic colonial solution to a stubborn customary dispute, one that restored a century-old title as a "fitting reward for a century of loyalty to British rule" (p. 101). The definitive historical takeaway is that the Naning community's preservation of its Minangkabau matriarchal institutions through a century of British abolition demonstrates the resilience of adat against colonial administrative erasure, while the post-1957 downgrading of the title illustrates how the new Malaysian state, like its colonial predecessor, ultimately subordinated local customary authority to the bureaucratic hierarchy.

## Context

- Curtis was a cadet at the District Office, Alor Gajah, in 1921 and later served as District Officer there from August 1922; the article is therefore a first-hand administrative memoir rather than a detached scholarly study (p. 98).
- The article draws on the Straits Settlements Annual Report for 1921 and the genealogical list supplied by the incumbent Dato Naning in 1979; its historiographical contribution lies in preserving an eyewitness account of the 1921 ceremony and the subsequent succession events, much of which exists in no other published source.
