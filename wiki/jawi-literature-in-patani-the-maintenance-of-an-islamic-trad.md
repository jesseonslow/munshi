# Jawi literature in Patani: the maintenance of an Islamic tradition

Virginia Matheson and M.B. Hooker survey the continuous tradition of *Kitab Jawi*—Islamic literature written in Malay—produced by Patani scholars from the early nineteenth century to the late 1980s, arguing that this literary corpus constitutes a living intellectual *silsilah* linking Meccan scholarship to the Malay world and serving as the defining cultural marker of Malay-Muslim identity in southern Thailand (pp. 1–5).

## Summary

Matheson and Hooker set out to catalogue the works of Shaykh Daud bin Abdullah al-Fatani, the earliest known Islamic writer from the peninsular Malay world, and in doing so identified sixteen additional Patani authors whose *kitab* remained in commercial circulation in 1986–87. Their sample of 42 texts, drawn from bookshops in Patani, Penang, Kuala Lumpur, Singapore and Kota Bharu, reveals a tradition of remarkable continuity: the four "great" nineteenth-century scholars (Shaykh Daud, Shaykh Ahmad bin Muhammad Zain, Zain al-Abidin bin Muhammad, and Muhammad bin Ismail Daud) produced lengthy compendia on *fiqh* and *usul al-din* while in Mecca, and their works were subsequently summarized, commented upon, and simplified by a succession of local *tok guru* writing for *pondok* students (pp. 19–34). The content is overwhelmingly juridical and theological—12 texts on *fiqh* and 20 on *kalam* sub-categories account for 75% of the sample—while *tasawwuf* is represented by only a single work, Shaykh Daud's translation of al-Ghazali's *Minhāj al-ʿĀbidīn* (pp. 40–41).

The authors situate this literary production within the political history of Greater Patani, where successive Thai assimilation policies—from the 1902 imprisonment of the last Raja, through Phibun's Cultural Laws of 1940–42, to the 1961 *pondok* registration scheme—provoked a strengthening of Islamic education as a vehicle of cultural resistance. The *pondok* system, which the authors argue originated in Patani, functions as both the primary consumer and the engine of production for *Kitab Jawi*: *tok guru* who have studied in the Middle East compose new texts or simplified versions of older ones to match the level of their students, and the commercial publishing network (local Patani presses for contemporary works; al-Maʿarif Press in Penang for the nineteenth-century canon) is tightly bound to the educational institutions that demand the books (pp. 42–56).

A postscript extending the survey to Kota Bharu, Kelantan, confirms that outside Patani only the four great nineteenth-century authors are in circulation, while the shorter, locally produced twentieth-century texts remain confined to southern Thailand. The authors note that the Thai government's policy of replacing Malay-language *kitab* with Thai- or Arabic-language textbooks produced in Bangkok threatens to render the entire corpus obsolete within a generation (pp. 76–85).

### Key Findings

- Shaykh Daud's dated works span 1809 to 1843; his *Furūʿ al-Masā'il* (659 pp.) is the longest text in the sample, and twelve of his works were still being reprinted in 1987, typically through al-Maʿarif Press in Penang (pp. 19–25).
- Of 42 texts surveyed, 12 treat *fiqh* and 20 treat sub-categories of *kalam* (*usul al-din*, *iʿtiqad*, *sifat*, *ʿaqidah*, *tawhid*); only one work addresses *tasawwuf* and one *tafsir*, suggesting a shift away from mysticism that parallels a similar trend observed in Kelantan between 1860 and 1940 (pp. 40–41).
- Local Patani presses (Patani Press, Muslim Press, Taman Pustaka, Madrasah Dar al-Salam) produce print runs of 2,000–4,000 copies per edition; one text, *Al-Risālah al-Tawḥīdiyyah*, reached a cumulative total of 23,000 copies over ten printings by 1984, and *Taqrīb al-Ikhwān* reached its seventeenth printing (pp. 52–53).
- The number of registered *pondok* in the four southern provinces fell from an estimated 400 in 1974 to 181 in 1986, and since 1985 no new *pondok* have been permitted to open; the authors warn that the standard of religious knowledge is declining as a result (pp. 45–46).
- Copyright assertions appear only in local Patani publications and only from the 1930s onward; the nineteenth-century Meccan texts carry no copyright notice, though Shaykh Ahmad—who served as supervisor of the Meccan government press from the mid-1880s—was the first Patani author to attach reprint restrictions to his works (pp. 54–55).
- The *fatawa* of Shaykh Ahmad bin Muhammad Zain (220 pp., published 1957) display a command of Arabic sources and sophistication of argument that the authors judge "technically superior" to recent Malaysian *fatawa*, underscoring the high level of scholarship achieved in the Patani tradition (p. 30).

### Conclusion

Matheson and Hooker conclude that the Patani *Kitab Jawi* tradition is not a relic but a vigorous, self-renewing system in which the *tok guru* simultaneously preserve, simplify, and extend the classical Meccan curriculum for each new generation of *pondok* students. The tradition's survival depends entirely on the *pondok* system, which is now under structural threat from Thai secularization policies; if the *pondok* are eliminated or fully absorbed into the state education system, the authors argue, the *Kitab Jawi* will cease to function as a living teaching corpus and the intellectual link between Patani and the wider Islamic world will be severed (pp. 58–60).

## Context

- The article draws on fieldwork conducted in 1986–87 at the Centre for Southern Thailand Studies (Prince of Songkla University, Pattani campus), local *pondok*, and Jawi bookshops in Patani, Alor Setar, Kuala Lumpur, Penang, Singapore and Kota Bharu; manuscript holdings at Pusat Islam (Kuala Lumpur) and the National Museum (Jakarta) are also cited (pp. 1–3, 60).
- The authors note that twelve manuscripts of Shaykh Daud's works, described as alleged originals, had been sent from Mecca to Pusat Islam, Kuala Lumpur, and that a detailed collation of these against the printed editions would be a priority for future research (p. 85, fn. 33).
