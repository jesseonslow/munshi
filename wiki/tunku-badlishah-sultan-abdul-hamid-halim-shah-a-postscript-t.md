# Tunku Badlishah Sultan Abdul Hamid Halim Shah, a postscript to the BangkokKedah personalised relations

## Abstract

For well over a century, close personal ties underlay relations between the rulers of Kedah and Siam. In the latter part of the nineteenth century, Kedah emerged as Siam’s most favoured Malay tributary, and the state’s rulers pursued parallel agendas of socio-economic and administrative modernization. That special tie ended abruptly in 1909 when Siam signed a treaty ceding the Kedah to Great Britain, but Tunku Badlishah, a key figure in the Kedah administration, was a naturalized citizen of Siam and had close personal ties with King Vajiravudh. After the latter’s death in 1925, the relationship faded away, but Badlishah’s Thai background continued to influence his career in various ways even after he became Sultan in 1943. His reign coincided with Malaya’s push for independence, and he became a key figure in pressing for a continuing leadership role for Malaya’s sultans and a retention of traditional authority.

## Summary

This article traces the long history of personalized relations between the ruling houses of Kedah and Siam, from their crystallization in the early nineteenth century through their abrupt termination in 1909, and then follows Tunku Badlishah as the final embodiment of that tradition. Suwannathat-Pian argues that the Kedah-Siam bond was qualitatively distinct from other Siamese tributary relationships because it was built on intimate personal ties—marriages, adoptions, and sustained residence at the Bangkok court—rather than merely on the conventional asymmetry of power between suzerain and vassal. The 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty, which transferred Kedah to British jurisdiction without prior consultation with the Kedah ruling elite, severed the official channel for these relations, but Badlishah's status as an adopted son of Crown Prince Vajiravudh and a naturalized Siamese citizen meant the personal dimension persisted in a private, extra-diplomatic form until Vajiravudh's death in 1925.

The article's central concern is how Badlishah's extraordinary biographical circumstances—being raised in Bangkok, educated at King's College and Oxford, and serving in the Siamese civil service—shaped his character and political conduct as ruler of Kedah during the critical transition from colonialism to independence. Drawing on correspondence in the Thai National Archives between Sultan Abdul Hamid, King Vajiravudh, and Prince Damrong, the author reconstructs the negotiations over Badlishah's return to Kedah and the emotional register of a father's plea and an adopted father's reluctant consent. British Colonial Office papers and Tunku Abdul Rahman's memoirs then inform the account of Badlishah's confrontations with the colonial authority over the Malayan Union and with UMNO over the future role of the Malay sultans.

The author positions Badlishah's reign as a period in which the ruler's traditional authority was simultaneously under siege from the colonial state and from the emerging Malay nationalist movement, and in which Badlishah's outsider status within his own family—having spent most of his formative years in Bangkok—paradoxically equipped him with the independence of mind to defend the constitutional position of the rulers against both British and UMNO encroachment.

### Key Findings

- The 1909 Anglo-Siamese Treaty ceded Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan, and Trengganu to Great Britain in exchange for the cancellation of the 1855 Anglo-Siamese Treaty and favourable loan terms for a Siamese southern railway; all subsequent diplomatic communication between Bangkok and the four states was required to pass through the British Foreign Office (p. 123).
- Tunku Badlishah, born 17 March 1894, was sent to Bangkok at approximately age seven or eight and became the only member of the Kedah royal family to be naturalized as a Siamese citizen, receive a Thai noble title (Phra Si Surintharawiwongsa), and serve in the Siamese civil service (pp. 124–125).
- The 1923 Anglo-Kedah Treaty, negotiated under Regent Tunku Ibrahim, established that the British Adviser to Kedah did not possess the same sweeping authority over domestic affairs as the Residents of the Federated Malay States, preserving a degree of Kedah's traditional autonomy (pp. 128–129).
- In 1945, after being pressured by Sir Harold MacMichael to sign the Malayan Union agreement under threat of deposition, Sultan Badlishah immediately telegraphed the Colonial Office declaring his signature had been given under duress, and in February 1946 wrote a formal complaint asserting his treaty right under the 1923 Agreement to appeal directly to the British monarch (pp. 130–131).
- In 1948, Kedah UMNO affiliates formally requested that Sultan Badlishah be replaced by Tunku Yaakob, arguing that his appointment had been made by the Japanese, recognized only under the Malayan Union Order-in-Council, and was therefore invalid with the abrogation of that Order (p. 131).
- The Conference of Rulers, with Badlishah as a leading voice, rejected UMNO's 1948 proposal to create a Malay Deputy High Commissioner at the Federal level, a decision that deeply alienated Dato' Onn Jaafar and intensified the rivalry between the rulers and the party (p. 132).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian's definitive takeaway is that Tunku Badlishah's unique formation—raised as an adopted son of a Thai king, educated in Europe, and naturalized as a foreign subject—produced a ruler whose boldness, multilingualism, and willingness to confront both the colonial authority and the nationalist movement were direct products of his dual cultural identity. His reign represents the final chapter of the Bangkok-Kedah personalized tradition and simultaneously a pivotal moment in the constitutional history of Malay kingship, as the rulers' collective position was defended and reasserted in the years immediately preceding independence.

## Context

- Primary archival collections: The Thai National Archives (Documents Reign 6, B.3.1/4, correspondence between Sultan Abdul Hamid, King Vajiravudh, and Prince Damrong); The National Archives, London (CO 273, CO 535, CO 717, CO 537); and the Rhodes House Library, Oxford (MSS. Ind. Ocn. series).
- The article extends the author's long-standing research programme on Kedah-Siam relations (building on her 1988 monograph and 1999 JMBRAS article) by filling the gap on the post-1909 period and providing the first sustained treatment of Sultan Badlishah's reign in the JMBRAS.
