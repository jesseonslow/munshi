# Rice cultivation

Rice cultivation constitutes one of the most extensively documented agricultural practices in the scholarly literature of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo, spanning ethnographic, entomological, engineering, and economic inquiries from the late nineteenth century through the early 1970s. The practice encompasses a wide spectrum of techniques, from the rudimentary brushwood dams and bamboo waterwheels (*kinchir*) of pre-modern Malay water control [[1, pp. 78–79]](#ref-1) to the large-scale irrigation schemes of the colonial and post-independence periods [[1, pp. 92–93]](#ref-1). Across the region, rice farming is characterised by significant regional variation in technique, ritual observance, and institutional support, with the northwest and northeast peninsular plains, the inland valleys of the western states, and the Bornean highlands each developing distinct cultural configurations that persisted for centuries [[2, pp. 76–80]](#ref-2). The economic stakes of these variations became acute in the mid-twentieth century, when Malaya's rice imports exceeded half a million tons annually at a cost of $50 million, prompting a sustained governmental effort toward self-sufficiency [[1, p. 102]](#ref-1).

## Customary Practices and Ritual Frameworks

The ethnographic literature reveals that rice cultivation in both the Malay Peninsula and Borneo was deeply embedded in ritual and prohibitive systems that governed every stage of the farming cycle. In Malacca, as recorded by Inche Muhammad Ja'far in 1893 and published in 1897, the planting season was synchronised with the West wind and the fourth month of the Chinese calendar, and the commencement of work required a consultation between village elders and the *pawang* (ritual specialist), the reading of *Maulud* prayers over the "mother seed," and the burning of benzoin supplied by the *pawang* [[3, pp. 286–287]](#ref-3). The sowing itself had to occur during the dark half of the lunar month to protect the seedlings from insects, and the "Soul of the Rice" was ritually extracted from seven-jointed stalks before the main harvest could begin [[3, pp. 289]](#ref-3). A three-day prohibition (*pantang tuai*) followed the extraction, during which no further cutting was permitted [[3, p. 302]](#ref-3). The text explicitly states that the virtue of all these ordinances and prohibitions lay in their power to "protect the rice and drive away all its enemies, such as grubs, rats, swine and the like" [[3, p. 304]](#ref-3).

In the Baram District of Sarawak, E. Banks documented in 1940 a far more extensive system of *pantang* (also termed *lali*) prohibitions among the Kayan, Kenyah, Kalamantan, and Punan peoples [[4, pp. 83–84]](#ref-4). These ritual retreats required the people of a longhouse to withdraw to their homes, cease all farm work, and refuse entry to strangers, invoking the aid of spirits for the success of their crops [[4, p. 83]](#ref-4). The Kayan Uma Poh spent a total of 117 days in pantang retreat over the course of their rice farming cycle, not counting additional prohibitions for births, deaths, and other events [[4, p. 85]](#ref-4). The Kayan Uma Paku observed at least ten distinct pantangs from felling to first pounding, with individual durations ranging from four days to twenty days, and forfeits including gongs, pigs, fowls, knives, and beads per door [[4, pp. 86–87]](#ref-4). The Kenyah Leppu Tau observed a one-month pantang at the start of the cycle, with a stranger's intrusion forfeiting three pigs, five fowls, and a knife; their harvest pantang (*Pantang Lunoh*) lasted twelve days [[4, p. 91]](#ref-4).

Banks drew a sharp typological distinction between the groups: the Kayans were "far and away the most fanatical," announcing pantang dates in advance and refusing even trusted friends once the period had begun; the Kenyahs were "much more philosophical" and accommodating to chance-bound strangers; the Kalamantans were "comparatively very vague," often constructing their customs retrospectively; and the Punans were "wandering" in their observance [[4, p. 84]](#ref-4). He also noted that the Kalamantans were "slightly more sensible," tending crops by day while observing the pantang only at night [[4, p. 88]](#ref-4).

Banks situated the Bornean *lali* within a broader comparative framework by drawing an extended parallel with the *genna* ceremonies of the Sema Nagas of Assam, as recorded by J. H. Hutton in 1921. The correspondence in sequence—clearing, burning, sowing, weeding, ear formation, harvesting, and bringing home—was close, but the Naga *gennas* each lasted a single day whereas the Bornean pantangs stretched from three to thirty days [[4, p. 86]](#ref-4). Banks concluded the comparison with the observation that "the Naga is the wiser in keeping them of short duration" [[4, p. 86]](#ref-4).

## Agronomic Techniques and Regional Variation

The agronomic literature demonstrates that every stage of the farming cycle—land preparation, nursery type, transplanting, harvesting, threshing, and winnowing—exhibited significant regional variation across West Malaysia [[2, pp. 76–80]](#ref-2). Jackson challenged two prevailing assumptions in the literature: first, that the basic pattern of cultivation was essentially the same everywhere and could be described by a single "fairly representative" cycle of operations; and second, that rice farming was a static, "traditional" element in contrast to the dynamic commercial sector [[2, pp. 76–77]](#ref-2). He demonstrated that these variations correlated with the historical pathways by which wet-rice cultivation reached each area: the northwest and northeast were early hearths of lowland rice, but the techniques came from different directions (Thai versus Sumatran influences), and the inland valleys of the western states were pioneered by Minangkabau migrants from the fifteenth century onward, carrying with them a distinct set of practices that persisted for centuries [[2, pp. 77–78]](#ref-2).

In Malacca, the traditional cycle as documented by Ja'far involved a single annual planting, with the nursery plot prepared by ploughing, harrowing, levelling, and ditching, and the seedlings retained for at least forty to forty-four days, preferably up to seventy [[3, pp. 298–299]](#ref-3). The preferred fertiliser, *baja pangkal*, consisted of buffalo bones burnt with chaff until thoroughly calcined, then pounded fine, passed through a sieve, and mixed with mud [[3, p. 291]](#ref-3). Transplanting was carried out in bundles of four or five plants at intervals of a span, with female labourers engaged at a wage of four cents per hundred bundles [[3, p. 291]](#ref-3). From transplanting to full ripeness was reckoned at six months, not counting the preceding period of ploughing and nursery growth, which could extend to three months [[3, p. 301]](#ref-3).

A notable technological divergence existed in harvesting methods. In Malacca, the inland farmers preferred to clip their rice with a *tuai* (a traditional harvesting implement) and place it into baskets a handful at a time without threshing it in the field, a method considered the pious one because the "soul of the rice" was not disturbed [[3, p. 303]](#ref-3). Urban farmers near the town of Malacca, by contrast, had adopted the sickle and threshing machine for speed, and a good part of the population held the belief that since the custom of threshing had been introduced, crops had been much less abundant than in years of olden time [[3, p. 303]](#ref-3). In Kedah, J. A. Baker documented in 1940 the *pisau penggiau*, a distinctive harvesting implement consisting of a wooden handle with a long hook and an iron blade set on the opposite side, used for cutting padi stalks in conjunction with buffalo threshing (*irek*) [[5, p. 43]](#ref-5). Baker traced the etymology of *giau* to the Siamese *kiau* and identified a close parallel in the Philippine *lingcao*, suggesting a wider historical distribution of this implement type across the region [[5, p. 44]](#ref-5).

In Krian, the floating seed-bed (*semaian rakit*) and multiple transplanting system, introduced by Banjarese settlers in the third quarter of the nineteenth century, was retained tenaciously by Malay farmers even after they recognised it required more seed and gave poorer germination rates than the single-transplanting methods used by their Chinese neighbours [[2, pp. 91–92]](#ref-2). This persistence of culturally embedded technique in the face of demonstrable agronomic inferiority became a recurring theme in Jackson's analysis of regional variation.

## Pest Management and the Rice-Stem Borer

The first systematic entomological account of a major rice pest in the Malay Peninsula was published by L. Wray, Curator of the Perak Museum, in 1887, in response to a request from Dr. Leech, Collector and Magistrate of the Krian District, who had observed three successive seasons of escalating damage to padi crops from an unidentified maggot [[6, p. 73]](#ref-6). Wray identified the insect as a *Chilo* species closely allied to Riley's North American *C. oryzae*, and documented its complete life cycle, reproductive capacity, and the parasitic fly that served as its principal natural check [[6, pp. 74–75]](#ref-6).

The borer's adaptive strategy across the cropping season was clearly delineated: the first brood fed on growing shoots and leaf midribs before hollow stalks formed, while subsequent broods bored into the pith of the stalk itself, producing the characteristic "head-rot" or empty-ear damage that farmers observed [[6, pp. 76–77]](#ref-6). Wray estimated approximately six broods per year, each generation spanning roughly two months, with the cycle perpetuated between seasons through self-sown rice and lateral shoots of old plants left standing in the field under the Malay harvesting method [[6, pp. 78]](#ref-6). A single female moth laid 11 egg masses in one night and 7 the next; one mass contained 39 eggs, yielding an estimated total of up to 600 eggs per female [[6, p. 76]](#ref-6). Up to 20 caterpillars were found in a single stool of young padi growing isolated in Taiping, more than two or three miles from any field, suggesting either long-range moth flight or an alternative wild host plant [[6, p. 77]](#ref-6).

A significant portion of the report was devoted to the parasitic fly (family Muscidae, subfamily Tachinariae), which Wray identified as the most powerful natural check on the borer population. In one examination he found five live parasitic pupae and three empty cases but not a single live borer pupa, and concluded that without this parasitism, padi cultivation in the Malay manner would be "quite impossible" [[6, p. 78]](#ref-6). His recommended preventive measure—burning the straw promptly after harvest and suppressing self-sown rice between seasons—was already being circulated as a Malay-language notice by Dr. Leech [[6, p. 74]](#ref-6).

## Irrigation Policy and the Drive to Self-Sufficiency

The institutional history of rice cultivation in Malaya is inseparable from the colonial state's struggle to reconcile the demands of a lucrative extractive economy with the agricultural needs of a rice-dependent population. Short and Jackson traced the half-century of administrative indecision, competing economic priorities, and institutional fragmentation that preceded the establishment of the Drainage and Irrigation Department on 1 January 1932 [[1, pp. 78–79]](#ref-1). They argued that the introduction of sophisticated irrigation engineering was essentially a colonial achievement, shaped by the specific political economy of the protectorate system, in which revenue from tin and rubber consistently took priority over rural development [[1, pp. 78–79]](#ref-1).

The 1893 *Report on the Rice Supply of the Colony and Native States* was identified as the first major landmark of official interest, prompted in part by the fact that Selangor alone had imported $11½ million worth of rice in 1890 [[1, p. 83]](#ref-1). Yet it produced no comprehensive policy and in some respects undermined the local initiative that had been the primary engine of water-control development up to that point [[1, p. 80]](#ref-1). The Krian Irrigation Scheme (1899–1906) occupied the analytical centre of the article: the scheme's cost ballooned from an initial estimate of $285,000 to $1.6 million, and the engineers who successively reviewed it disagreed fundamentally on whether storage was necessary, whether double-cropping should be planned for, and how the distribution system should be designed [[1, pp. 92–93]](#ref-1). The scheme, completed in 1906, served approximately 52,000 acres of padi land at an average cost of £2-6s-8d per acre, comparable to the Bari Doab works in the Punjab [[1, pp. 92–93]](#ref-1).

By 1930, Malaya's rice imports stood consistently in excess of half a million tons annually at a cost of $50 million, while home production was only a quarter of total consumption and the Federated Malay States alone produced less than 50,000 tons of milled rice per year [[1, p. 102]](#ref-1). It was this acute "rice problem" that finally compelled the High Commissioner to appoint the Rice Cultivation Committee, whose 1931 report recommended the creation of a dedicated federal department [[1, p. 102]](#ref-1). In the post-independence period, the drive toward self-sufficiency accelerated: in 1969, 225,000 acres of riceland in West Malaysia were planted with an off-season crop—nearly a quarter of total rice acreage and almost five times the area so used in 1962 [[2, p. 96]](#ref-2). The Muda Irrigation Project, largely completed by the early 1970s, was designed to provide irrigation for double cropping of 261,500 acres in coastal Kedah and Perlis; the Kemubu Irrigation Project in Kelantan was planned for 47,000 acres with completion targeted for 1974 [[2, pp. 80–83]](#ref-2).

Yet the reception of these new technologies was uneven. Jackson attributed the differences not to farmer laziness or ignorance but to the specific cultural configurations of each local farming system, including dietary preferences for particular rice varieties, the integration of cattle grazing into the fallow cycle, and the disruption that a second crop imposed on the ritual calendar of the village [[2, pp. 90–91]](#ref-2). In Krian, the number of locally-owned tractors rose from one in 1965 to thirty-seven after the 1967 off-season harvest, alongside a marked increase in fertiliser use [[2, p. 91]](#ref-2). In the Trengganu River Pumping Scheme, however, double cropping ranged from only 94 to 370 acres across 1964–1966 despite an assured irrigation supply for approximately 3,600 acres, illustrating the gap between technical feasibility and cultural receptivity [[2, p. 83]](#ref-2). Kedah's double-cropped area grew from 820 acres in 1961 to 18,650 acres in 1969, while in central Pahang (Temerloh District) no off-season padi was planted at all in 1969 despite 3,700 acres being double cropped elsewhere in the state [[2, pp. 80]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on rice cultivation reveals a clear evolution in scholarly priorities across nearly a century. The earliest contributions, such as Wray's 1887 entomological report and Ja'far's 1893 account of Malaccan practice, were produced at the request of colonial administrators and reflected the practical concerns of district officers: identifying a pest, documenting a local farming cycle, or recording an implement [[6, p. 73]](#ref-6), [[3, p. 285]](#ref-3). Banks's 1940 study of Baram pantangs was similarly an administrative reference document, compiled from information provided by Native Officers and Upriver Agents at the direction of Mr. F. H. Pollard, indicating it was produced for the Sarawak government's engagement with Baram communities rather than as an independent scholarly inquiry [[4, p. 83]](#ref-4).

The historiographical shift became evident in the 1960s and 1970s. Cheng Siok-hwa's 1969 historical survey of the rice industry marked a move toward synthetic economic history [[7]](#ref-7). Short and Jackson's 1971 article on irrigation policy filled a gap identified in the emerging literature by providing the first systematic account of the institutional and policy history of government irrigation, rather than focusing on individual schemes or agronomic systems [[1, p. 78]](#ref-1). Jackson's 1972 article on West Malaysian rice cultivation represented the most significant methodological advance: by reframing regional variation in farming technique as a product of culture history rather than environmental determinism, and by demonstrating through documented case studies that "traditional" systems had always been dynamic, he provided a corrective to the static model that had dominated both colonial administrative thinking and early academic writing on Malayan agriculture [[2, pp. 76–77]](#ref-2). The key debate that emerged from this literature was whether the persistence of customary practices—whether the Bornean pantangs, the Malaccan soul-extraction rituals, or the Krian floating seed-bed—represented an obstacle to modernisation or a culturally rational configuration that any successful agricultural policy would need to accommodate rather than override [[4, p. 88]](#ref-4), [[2, pp. 90–92]](#ref-2).
