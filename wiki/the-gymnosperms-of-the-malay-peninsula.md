# The Gymnosperms of the Malay Peninsula

H.N. Ridley, then Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, published this floristic treatment of the gymnosperms of the Malay Peninsula in 1911, cataloguing the Coniferae, Gnetaceae, and Cycadeae represented in the region and proposing a biogeographical model for their arrival. The article argues that the peninsula's conifers were colonised from two distinct directions—lowland taxa from the north via the Himalayan corridor and montane taxa from the east via Borneo and the Australasian region.

## Summary

Ridley's treatment is organised taxonomically by the three extant gymnosperm groups, but its intellectual centre of gravity is the biogeographical analysis of the Coniferae. He demonstrates that the lowland podocarps (*P. wallichianus*, *P. neriifolius*, *P. polystachyus*) share a distribution extending into the Khasia and tropical Himalaya, while the montane conifers (*Agathis*, *Dacrydium elatum*, *D. falciforme*, *D. beccarii*, *P. cupressinus*) are all Bornean in origin and absent from the northern Indian region. This dual-origin hypothesis is reinforced by the presence of *Phyllocladus hypophyllus* in Borneo (with other species in New Zealand, Tasmania, and New Guinea) and the near-absence of *Pinus*, of which only *P. merkusii* reaches the region from Tenasserim, Sumatra, and Borneo (p. 53).

The Gnetaceae section is the most taxonomically productive part of the article, with Ridley describing three new species—*G. penangense*, *G. longispica*, and *G. tenuifolium*—and clarifying the distinction between *G. gnemon* (a large introduced tree) and *G. brunonianum* (a small native shrub) that had been confused in the *Flora of British India*. He also resolves nomenclatural difficulties around *G. microcarpum* and *G. neglectum* by reference to Blume's illustrations and seed morphology (pp. 60–66). The Cycadeae are represented by two species of *Cycas*, of which *C. rumphii* is noted to be declining from its former coastal habitats due to coconut cultivation (p. 68).

Throughout, Ridley grounds his descriptions in his own extensive field collections from across the peninsula, supplemented by specimens from Curtis, Hullett, Kelsall, Robinson, and other collectors, and he draws on the iconography of Blume, Rumphius, and Wallich to resolve identification problems where living material was unavailable.

### Key Findings

- The Malay Peninsula contains 8 conifer species across 3 genera (*Agathis* 1, *Dacrydium* 3, *Podocarpus* 4), 10+ *Gnetum* species (3 described as new), and 2 *Cycas* species (pp. 53–68).
- *Dacrydium elatum* is common above 2,000 feet in the northern peninsula; *D. beccarii* is a dwarf form (≤15 ft) restricted to the southern peninsula at 4,000–5,800 feet, apparently replacing *D. elatum* in that zone (pp. 55–56).
- *Agathis loranthifolia* is recorded from Penang Hill at 2,500 feet, Gunong Tahan, and Maxwell's Hill, and is noted as "very resiniferous" with the local name "Poko Damar Minyak" (p. 54).
- *Cycas rumphii* reaches stems over 20 feet tall and 1–2 feet in diameter; its male cone emits a strong odour of lady-birds and its pollen is described as "strongly narcotic" (p. 67).
- *Gnetum edule* produces a solitary seed 2 inches long and 1 inch through, with a corky warty surface, matching Rumphius's *Funis gnemoniformis* (p. 64).
- *Cycas rumphii* is recorded as having disappeared from many sandy coastal sites owing to clearance for coconut cultivation, persisting only in gardens or isolated inland forest sites such as Kamuning (p. 68).

### Conclusion

Ridley's definitive contribution is the two-directional invasion model for the peninsula's conifers, which remains a foundational observation in regional biogeography. He further establishes that the montane conifer flora is essentially a Bornean extension, while the lowland element is a southern arm of the Himalayan–Sino-Japanese podocarp distribution, a pattern that anticipates later work on the Sundaic floristic provinces.

## Context

- Ridley was Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens (1887–1913) and the article draws heavily on his own herbarium collections and field work across the peninsula, as well as on the collections of Curtis (Penang), Hullett (Malacca), and Kelsall (Johor/Selangor).
- The study is a standard colonial-era floristic treatment; its primary archival basis is the Singapore Botanic Gardens herbarium and the published iconography of Blume, Rumphius, and Wallich.
