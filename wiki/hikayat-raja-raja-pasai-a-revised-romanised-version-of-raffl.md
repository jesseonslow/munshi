# Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai, a revised romanised version of Raffles MS 67, together with an English translation

**Metadata discrepancy note:** The publication metadata identifies this as A.H. Hill's *Hikayat Raja-Raja Pasai* (JMBRAS Vol. 33, 1960), but the primary source text provided is in fact R. S. Shelford's "A Trip to Mt. Penrissen, Sarawak" (1900). The document identifier in the source files confirms the latter. The summary below is grounded in the actual source text supplied.

## Summary

R. S. Shelford, a naturalist in the Sarawak Government service, recounts a collecting expedition to Mount Penrissen (4,800 ft) in the upper reaches of the Sarawak River, undertaken in May–June 1900 together with E. A. W. Cox. The mountain had previously been visited by Beccari, Everett, Haviland, and Henlerson, but had never been thoroughly collected from a zoological standpoint, which set the tone for the expedition's ambitions.

The narrative covers the full logistical arc of the journey: the difficult river ascent from Kuching through shallows and rapids, the exchange of Malay boat crews for Land-Dyak polers at Segu, the overland portage from Pankalan Ampat to Sennah, and the multi-day climb to the mountain's upper slopes. Shelford provides vivid ethnographic observations of the Land-Dyak village at Sennah — the stilted houses, the women's brass arm and leg rings, the beaded "Bok tumbis" dance hat, and the general absence of local manufacture of cloth, weapons, or pottery — alongside practical details of the "bichara" (council) held to arrange carriers.

The scientific results are mixed. The invertebrate fauna, particularly insects, proved extremely rich, and Shelford claims no other Bornean mountain had been so thoroughly worked entomologically. The flora, though only lightly collected, yielded species new to Borneo and a new species of *Goniophlebium*. By contrast, the vertebrate fauna was strikingly poor: only two kinds of monkey were seen, no game birds were obtained, and large mammals were absent. Shelford attributes this scarcity to heavy gun-hunting by the local Dyak population and suggests that southern Sarawak is less faunistically rich than the northern regions studied by Whitehead and Hose on Kina Balu and Dulit.

### Key Findings

- The expedition departed Kuching on 5 May 1900 and returned on 4 June, covering approximately fifty miles from the sea-coast to the mountain (p. 1).
- The summit of Penrissen is a flat plateau roughly half a mile long by two hundred yards broad, covered in pandanus and rotan, with animal life very scarce (p. 6).
- The final 300 feet of the ascent was a sheer precipice of sandstone conglomerate requiring improvised ladders (p. 6).
- Only two species of monkey were observed; no game birds were procured or even seen; pigs, deer, *kijang*, and *pelandok* were noted only by their absence (p. 8).
- The fern list (Appendix III, by Bishop Hose) records *Polypodium hirtellum* and *Polypodium obliquatum* as new to Borneo, and a new species of *Goniophlebium* described as "a very interesting fern" with no other simple form of the sub-genus known from the region (pp. 25–26).
- Mosses and hepatics collected by A. H. Everett were named by F. Brotherus, including a new species *Thuidium Everett* Broth. (p. 26).

### Conclusion

Shelford's definitive takeaway is that Mount Penrissen, while entomologically and botanically rewarding, is faunistically impoverished compared to the northern Bornean mountains, a condition he links to intensive indigenous gun-hunting and a possible broader biogeographic gradient between southern and northern Sarawak.

## Context

- The article is a contemporary colonial-era field report by a government-service naturalist; its ethnographic observations of the Land-Dyak reflect the administrative and classificatory frameworks of the White Raj (e.g., the "formidable looking surat" from the Resident, the distinction between "Land Dyak" and "Sea Dyak").
- The study contributes to the early zoogeographical literature of Borneo, complementing the better-known work of Whitehead and Hose on Kina Balu and Dulit, and its fern and bryophyte lists remain of taxonomic interest.
