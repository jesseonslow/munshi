# A golden kris handle from Balingian, Sarawak

Tom Harrisson's 1966 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines a beaten-gold keris handle presented to the Sarawak Museum in 1963 by Melanau donors at Balingian, on the central west coast of Sarawak. The piece, identified by specialist consultation as East Javanese in style and possibly dating to the 13th century or earlier, is argued to represent a rare material witness to Tantric Buddhist cultural transmission into Borneo and to encode, in its associated Melanau folklore, the mythological abolition of a mourning-segregation custom.

## Summary

Harrisson's central concern is the provenance and cultural significance of a small but iconographically extraordinary object: a keris hilt in pure beaten gold depicting a fanged, bearded figure holding a human skull, with a fleur-de-lys phallic motif and elaborate pleated hair. After initial consultation at the Victoria and Albert Museum yielded only a general keris attribution, Harrisson sought expert opinion in the United States in April 1964. Claire Holt of Cornell University identified the figure as a fanged demon associated with Tantric Bhairavist cults in vogue during the reign of Kirtuma-Gura, the last king of Singhasari, murdered around 1292. Wendy Sorensen of the Asia Society, New York, placed the piece in the 13th century or possibly the 10th or late 9th century, and attributed it to the Kingdom of Madura or Kediri. Harrisson treats both identifications as provisional but convergent on an East Javanese origin well before the spread of Islam to the region (p. 175–176).

The article's second major thread is the pair of Melanau origin narratives provided by the donors, Haji Abdulrahman Sanai and Balau binte Saran. Both versions describe a widower confined for seven days in a solitary enclosure following his wife's death—a custom Harrisson links to the broader Bornean tradition of mourning segregation. In each story, a fairy figure instructs the widower to escape into the jungle and kill a stranger; upon striking the victim with his spear, the man finds the golden statue at its tip. The narratives thus mythologise the object as the agent that terminated the segregation custom (p. 176–177). Harrisson argues that the abolition of segregation did not simplify Melanau funerary practice but rather complicated it, freeing the principal mourner to participate actively in the elaborate secondary burial ceremonies for which the Melanaus are known, including the erection of *jerunei* poles accompanied by human slave sacrifice (p. 178).

The wider argument positions the handle as almost unique in Borneo: no comparable piece is known to Dato Jamil in Brunei, to keris specialists in Malaya, or in the National Museum at Kuala Lumpur, and no other manifestly Javanese ancient object has been recovered from West Borneo despite extensive excavations at Niah and the Sarawak River delta since 1947. Harrisson invokes Robert Heine-Geldern's 1937 study of Tantric Buddhist iconography in keris and parang handles across Southeast Asia to frame the piece within a broader pattern of Javanese cultural export, while cautioning against over-interpretation of single finds (p. 179–180).

### Key Findings

- The handle weighs 34 grams of pure beaten gold, stands 92 mm tall, and measures approximately 20 mm at the base, 28 mm at the waist, 15 mm at the throat, and 33 mm at the widest point of the cloak (p. 177).
- Claire Holt's identification links the fanged, skull-holding figure to Tantric Bhairavist cults associated with Kirtuma-Gura, last king of Singhasari, murdered c. 1292 AD; Wendy Sorensen dates the piece to the 13th century or possibly the 10th/late 9th century, Kingdom of Madura or Kediri (p. 175–176).
- The Melanau mourning custom required a widower to be confined in a dark, solitary enclosure for seven days without seeing the sun; the origin narratives mythologise the golden handle as the object that ended this practice (p. 176–177).
- Human slave sacrifice (sometimes several slaves) was performed in connection with the erection of *jerunei* secondary burial poles among wealthy Melanaus until recent times (p. 178).
- No comparable Javanese-style object is known from West Borneo; the National Museum at Kuala Lumpur holds no parallel piece, and no other manifestly Javanese ancient object has been recovered from the region despite excavations at Niah and the Sarawak River delta since 1947 (p. 179).
- The handle was worn suspended around the neck by important Melanau shamans in healing ceremonies; it was surrendered to the museum because no shaman was considered sufficiently powerful in the ancient tradition to continue using it (p. 178).

### Conclusion

Harrisson's definitive takeaway is that the Balingian keris handle constitutes a rare, near-unique material link between East Javanese Tantric Buddhist culture and the Melanau peoples of west Borneo, predating Islam in the region and suggesting a direct cultural transmission route that existing scholarship has insufficiently explored. The object simultaneously encodes, in its associated folklore, a pivotal transformation in Melanau funerary practice—the abolition of mourning segregation—and in its iconography, the broader Javanese export of Tantric imagery across maritime Southeast Asia.

## Context

- The study draws on the Sarawak Museum collection, handwritten expert opinions from Claire Holt (Cornell University) and Wendy Sorensen (Asia Society, New York), and the unpublished folklore of Melanau donors at Balingian.
- Historiographically, the article extends Heine-Geldern's 1937 framework of Tantric Buddhist iconography in Southeast Asian weapon hilts and contributes to the ongoing debate about the relative weight of Indian, Chinese, and indigenous influences in Bornean material culture, a question Harrisson had already addressed in his 1962 *Bijdragen* monograph.
