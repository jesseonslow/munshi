# Chedi at Wat Keo, Chaiya, Suratthani

Sir William Hunter's botanical manuscript, edited and annotated by H. N. Ridley and published in 1909, provides a detailed early-19th-century survey of the flora of Penang (Prince of Wales Island), documenting the East India Company's spice gardens at Ayer Hitam and the economic botany of pepper cultivation in the Straits Settlements. The work is significant as the first systematic record of the introduction of spice plants to Penang following Christopher Smith's 1796 expedition to the Moluccas, and it preserves the earliest known accounts of nutmeg and mangosteen fruiting on the island (pp. 49–51).

## Summary

Hunter's manuscript, presented to the Marquess Wellesley as Governor-General, offers a comprehensive natural history of Penang from the perspective of a Company surgeon visiting the island in the early 1800s. The text opens with a physical description of the island's topography, climate, and population before turning to a systematic catalogue of plants arranged according to the Linnaean sexual system. The most historically valuable sections concern the Company's spice plantations at Ayer Hitam, where nutmeg, clove, canarium, and sugar palm trees were grown from stock collected by botanist Christopher Smith in the Moluccas in 1796, and the detailed economics of pepper cultivation, which was the island's chief agricultural product (pp. 50–51, 62–65).

The pepper section is particularly rich in practical detail, describing the entire cycle from clearing and planting through to harvest and drying, including the use of *Morinda citrifolia* and *Erythrina corallodendron* as support trees, the contract system with Chinese labourers, and the two annual harvests that together span from December to August. Hunter also records the failure of several attempted manufactures—sugar, gambier, and indigo—all defeated by the high cost of labour on the island, a recurring theme that underscores the economic constraints of early colonial Penang (pp. 62–65, 72–73, 107).

Ridley's editorial introduction and bracketed identifications throughout the text serve to update Hunter's Linnaean classifications with modern nomenclature, though he acknowledges that several plants could only be guessed at from description and that Hunter's original drawings have been lost. The manuscript thus functions simultaneously as a primary historical document of early colonial agricultural policy and as a botanical record of considerable taxonomic interest (pp. 50–51).

### Key Findings

- Christopher Smith, appointed Company Botanist in 1794, sent 71,266 nutmegs and 55,264 clove plants from Amboyna in 1796, along with canarium and sugar palm, to the spice gardens at Ayer Hitam—the first Botanic Gardens in the Straits Settlements (p. 50).
- A pepper plantation of 46,000 plants at Soongey Clooan, in its eleventh year, was let for three years at 70 picols yearly, equivalent to 2½ catties per plant; the total island pepper production was estimated at 16,000–20,000 picols annually, valued at approximately 216,000 Spanish dollars at 12 dollars per picol (pp. 64–65).
- The 12-year financial projection for 100 Oorlongs (approximately 113 acres) of pepper showed a net profit of 184,038 Spanish dollars, exceeding the Superintendent's earlier estimate by 31,000 dollars when interest was properly accounted for (pp. 66–68).
- The Company's Botanic Gardens at Ayer Hitam were sold cheaply by Governor Murchison in 1834, reportedly because his wife could not obtain sufficient potatoes and vegetables from them (p. 51).
- A gambier plantation near Bato Lanshun was abandoned because labour costs exceeded the market price of imported gambier; the same obstacle defeated sugar and indigo manufactures on the island (pp. 72–73, 107).
- The population of Penang was estimated at 20,000, divided roughly into thirds: Chinese (Cantonese and Hokkien), Choolia (Tamil Muslim) traders and boatmen, and a mixed group of Europeans, Armenians, Portuguese, Malays, and Burmese, with fourteen languages in constant use in the bazaar (pp. 58–59).

### Conclusion

Hunter's manuscript stands as the foundational record of Penang's early agricultural and botanical history, documenting both the promise and the limitations of colonial economic botany in the Straits Settlements. Its central historical takeaway is that while the East India Company successfully established spice plantations that broke the Dutch monopoly, the high cost of labour on the island consistently defeated value-added manufacturing, confining Penang's economic role to primary production—above all pepper—rather than processing (pp. 62–65, 72–73).

## Context

- The primary source is Hunter's original manuscript held in the British Museum, edited and annotated by H. N. Ridley, who was then Director of the Singapore Botanic Gardens and the leading authority on Malesian botany in the early 20th century (p. 50).
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in fixing for the first time the definite locality of the Company's spice gardens at Ayer Hitam and in preserving the earliest records of nutmeg and mangosteen fruiting in Penang, events previously known only from secondary references (p. 50).
