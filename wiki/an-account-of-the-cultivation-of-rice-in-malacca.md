# An account of the cultivation of rice in Malacca

Written in 1893 by Inche Muhammad Ja’far and published in 1897 by the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, this account details the traditional agrarian practices of the Malacca territory under British colonial administration. The text serves as an ethnographic record of the annual rice cultivation cycle, emphasizing the integration of empirical agricultural techniques with indigenous spiritual beliefs and social labor structures.

## Summary

The cultivation cycle begins with strict adherence to seasonal timing and ritual consultation, typically commencing between the months of Zilkaidah and Zilhijah to coincide with the West wind season and the fourth month of the Chinese calendar (p. 286). Before physical labor commences, elders must consult with a Pawang to fix the date, followed by Maulud prayers read over the “mother seed” in the mosque, where participants bring half a quart of grain for blessing (p. 287). This spiritual preparation is deemed essential, as the text notes that disregarding these ordinances invites failure and the wrath of the rice’s enemies, such as rats and grubs (p. 304).

Agronomically, the process involves a meticulous nursery phase where seedlings are soaked and sown during the dark half of the month to protect them from insects (p. 289). The fields undergo a double ploughing and harrowing process, with the first harrowing breaking clods and the second, often using a wooden harrow after an iron one, ensuring fine soil for better growth (p. 290). Transplanting occurs around the month of Safar, utilizing a specific manure known as *baja pangkal* made from calcined buffalo bones and chaff mixed with mud to ensure stalk thickness (p. 291). The growth period from transplanting to ripening spans approximately six months, with specific stages marked by the number of joints on the stalk, such as *bunting kechil* at five joints (p. 292).

Harvesting is governed by the ritual of taking the “Soul of the Rice,” where the best stalks with seven joints are clipped first and wrapped in white cloth to resemble a swaddled child (p. 293). A three-day waiting period known as *pantang tuai* follows before the main crop is cut, and laborers are compensated either through sharecropping agreements or fixed wages, such as four cents per hundred bundles (p. 295). The text also documents a technological shift where urban cultivators near Malacca town adopt sickles and threshing for speed, while inland farmers prefer the traditional *tuai* method to preserve the rice’s spirit, believing modern methods reduce yield (p. 296).

### Key Findings

*   **Seasonal Alignment:** Planting is synchronized with the West wind season and the fourth month of the Chinese calendar to ensure soft soil and adequate water levels (p. 286).
*   **Ritual Necessity:** The Pawang’s benzoin and Maulud prayers are mandatory prerequisites for sowing, believed to protect the crop from pests and ensure virtue (p. 287, p. 304).
*   **Manure Composition:** The preferred fertilizer, *baja pangkal*, consists of buffalo bones burnt with chaff, pounded fine, and mixed with mud (p. 291).
*   **Labor Economics:** Female laborers are hired at a rate of four cents per hundred bundles for transplanting, while sharecropping agreements divide produce equally or in thirds depending on expense bearing (p. 295, p. 296).
*   **Harvest Rituals:** The “Soul of the Rice” is extracted from the seven-jointed stalks and stored in a basket wrapped in white cloth before the main harvest begins (p. 293).
*   **Technological Divergence:** A divide exists between inland farmers using the traditional *tuai* (clipping) method and urban farmers using sickles and threshing machines for efficiency (p. 296).

### Conclusion

The definitive historical takeaway is that late 19th-century Malacca rice cultivation was not merely an economic activity but a spiritual economy where agricultural success was inextricably linked to ritual compliance and respect for the rice’s soul. The text captures a transitional moment where traditional agrarian wisdom coexisted with emerging colonial modernization, highlighting the community’s belief that technological shortcuts like threshing machines could diminish the spiritual potency and physical yield of the crop.

## Context

*   **Colonial Role:** The publication reflects the British colonial administration's interest in documenting local economic practices to facilitate governance and agricultural development in the Straits Settlements.
*   **Archival Collections:** This document is part of the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (JSBRAS), a primary archive for the history of Malay society and natural history in the region.
