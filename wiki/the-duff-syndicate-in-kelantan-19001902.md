# The Duff syndicate in Kelantan, 1900–1902

Leslie Ratnasingam Robert's 1972 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* examines the formation, operations, and eventual Siamese ratification of the Duff Syndicate's concession in Kelantan between 1900 and 1902. Drawing extensively on Colonial Office and Foreign Office correspondence, Robert argues that the Syndicate functioned as a dual instrument—simultaneously advancing British imperial influence in the Northern Malay States and pursuing the commercial interests of a consortium of major London and Singapore houses—and that its negotiations exposed the fundamental contradictions in British policy toward Siam's suzerainty over Kelantan.

## Summary

Robert structures the article in two parts. Part I reconstructs the Syndicate's origins and internal architecture. R.W. Duff, a former Superintendent of Police and Prisons in Pahang who had pursued Pahang rebels into Kelantan, conceived the scheme while serving as District Officer of Kuala Langat. He retired from FMS service in early 1900 and assembled a syndicate of "large commercial houses of high repute connected with the Far East" with an initial registered capital of £10,000. Robert identifies the Syndicate's dualistic character: a public rhetoric of British patriotism and the preservation of Kelantan from foreign (particularly German) encroachment, underpinned by a private commercial motive to exploit the state's mineral wealth. The author demonstrates how the Syndicate exerted pressure on the British Government through parliamentary connections, the local press, and personal intermediaries such as Stuart Wortley and T.E. Vickers, while simultaneously maintaining a working relationship with the Raja of Kelantan that was essential to its economic viability.

Part II traces the chronological negotiations. Duff's master plan involved three possible methods of acquiring title; he chose the most aggressive—dealing directly with the Raja and then calling on the Foreign Office to uphold the resulting agreement. The Partnership Deed of 10 October 1900 granted Duff exclusive rights over a vast tract of Kelantan's interior for forty years. The subsequent two years of negotiation with the Siamese Government centred on a single intractable issue: the right of Siam to confirm or veto sub-leases and transfers. The Syndicate refused this condition on commercial grounds, arguing that no London financier would invest in a title dependent on Siamese confirmation. Robert shows how this impasse, combined with the Syndicate's extension of similar titles to the Rajas of Legeh, Reman, and Jala in mid-1901, created a diplomatic crisis that the British Government resolved only by treating the Duff concession as an "exceptional case" and securing a "Simple Ratification" in July 1902.

The primary sources—drawn from the C.O. 273 series and F.O.C.P. files—reveal the internal tensions within the British Government itself. The Colonial Office, responsible for the Secret Convention of 1897, was committed to recognising Siamese suzerainty; the Foreign Office, under Lansdowne, was more flexible. Robert uses these documents to illuminate the transitional period in British policy toward Siam, showing how the Duff affair both reflected and accelerated the shift from the 1897 Secret Convention toward the 1902 treaty that formally redefined British-Siamese relations in the Peninsula.

### Key Findings

- The Duff–Raja of Kelantan Partnership Deed, signed 10 October 1900, granted a 40-year concession over the area between the Galas and Lebir rivers (later estimated at approximately 3,000 square miles), with an initial payment of $20,000, a 4% royalty on mineral receipts, a 5% export duty, and 200 shares (later 2,000) allocated to the Raja (pp. 17, 28–30).
- The Syndicate's original six members included James and Shakespeare (metal brokers), Paterson and Simons (East India merchants), Price, Boustead and Company, Pittar, Levrson and Company (diamond merchants), J.R. Cutbertson, and Wilberforce Bryant (Bryant and May Ltd.); by 1902 it had grown to include T.E. Vickers (Vickers, Maxim & Co., capital £4,250,000), E. Pratt Barlow (John Dickson & Co., £650,000), Charles Ingram (Illustrated London News, £1,060,000), C. Sugden (Borneo Company, £300,000), A.P. Adams, and Loke Yew (pp. 7–8).
- The critical sticking point in the 1901–1902 negotiations was the Siamese demand for confirmation of all transfers and sub-leases; the Syndicate's directors declared that "English financial men would refuse to consider a business proposition which was based on a title dependent on Siam for confirmation" (p. 13).
- The concession was formally ratified at Bangkok on 10 July 1902 on the basis of a "Simple Ratification": Siam agreed to ratify unconditionally, with an honourable understanding that the Foreign Office would be consulted before any transfer or sub-lease, and that Siam would then "forthwith ratify" (p. 27).
- In mid-1901 Duff extended his "October 1900 Formula" northward, obtaining 99-year titles from the Rajas of Legeh and Reman (4% duty on gold and tin, 3% on coal and oil, 2% share to the Raja, 18-month commencement deadline), though these appear never to have been formally concluded (pp. 23, 31–33).
- The Syndicate threatened to dispose of its rights to a foreign power (Germany) if the British Government would not support its position, a bluff that the Colonial Office took seriously: "The very danger, against which we have been secretly planning, will have come into being" (p. 19).

### Conclusion

Robert's definitive takeaway is that the Duff Syndicate, by embedding itself in Kelantan through the October 1900 Agreement and retaining its extensive administrative powers (including taxation rights) through the July 1902 settlement, became a "modernizing agent" whose presence complicated subsequent state governance. The retention of powers that overlapped with uniform Siamese administration explains both the Syndicate's peculiar legal character and the protracted efforts by later state governments to negotiate the surrender of those rights. The article thus positions the 1900–1902 period as a watershed in British policy toward Siam, in which a private commercial venture inadvertently forced the British Government to clarify and ultimately redefine its relationship with both the Siamese crown and the Malay rulers of the Eastern coast.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the Colonial Office 273 series (C.O. 273/215, 217, 261, 263, 264, 274, 275, 286, 288) and Foreign Office C.P. files (F.O.C.P./XV, XVII, XVIII), supplemented by the published accounts of A. Wright and T.H. Reid (*The Malay Peninsula*, 1912) and H. Clifford's expeditionary reports.
- Historiographically, the article contributes to the understanding of the 1899–1902 transition in British-Siamese relations by demonstrating how a private commercial enterprise interacted with, and was shaped by, the competing imperatives of the Colonial Office and Foreign Office during the period between the 1897 Secret Convention and the 1902 treaty.
