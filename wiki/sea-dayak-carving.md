# Sea-Dayak carving

E. Banks published this illustrated catalogue of Sea Dayak (Iban) wood carving patterns in 1941, drawing on a collection of fragile paper drawings prepared decades earlier by the late Rev. E. W. Howell of Simanggang and held in the Sarawak Museum. The article's central thesis is that Sea Dayak male carvings are overwhelmingly naturalistic and geometric in character, with animal representations confined to a narrow set of motifs, a pattern that Banks uses to corroborate Dr. Haddon's earlier distinction between Iban male and female decorative traditions.

## Summary

The article presents fourteen plates (IX–XXII) of named carving patterns, each accompanied by a brief interpretive note. Banks organises the material thematically rather than chronologically, moving from cloud motifs (Plate IX) through geometric and mechanical patterns (Plate X), the few zoomorphic designs (Plate XI), smaller floral and botanical patterns (Plates XII–XIV), cross-hatch and meander designs (Plates XV–XVI), and finally a series of single-plate illustrations (XVII–XXII). The author's interpretive framework is modest: he traces how a basic fleur-de-lys unit transforms through truncation, inversion, duplication, and attenuation to produce the cloud sequence, and how the "sudap" pattern evolves from a cut-off form into increasingly abstract variants. He notes that the Sea Dayaks are "a very literal sort of people" whose carvings mirror the directness of their broader cultural expression (p. 220).

Banks explicitly situates his material within Haddon's 1905 observation that Iban women's textile designs freely depict humans and animals while Iban men's carvings on bamboo and wood avoid such representations, restricting themselves to flowers and natural phenomena. The few animal motifs that do appear—a fowl's gizzard, centipede legs, and several caterpillar variants—are treated as exceptions that confirm the rule. Banks contrasts this restraint with the Kayan, Kenyah, and Kelamatan traditions, where dogs, prawns, and other animals feature prominently in wood carving (p. 220). He also observes that the Hornbill and Dragon, the two animals that do appear in Sea Dayak carving, are reserved for religious festival contexts.

The article is essentially a descriptive catalogue rather than a sustained argument. Banks does not attempt to explain the symbolic or ritual significance of individual patterns beyond their literal names, and he acknowledges that many meander designs are "distinctly allegorical, with little obvious meaning save in the mind of their creator" (p. 224). The twenty carved boards from the Undup River, which duplicate the paper drawings, are mentioned but not reproduced, suggesting the author's primary concern was documenting the pattern vocabulary rather than analysing material culture in situ.

### Key Findings

- The source drawings were prepared by the late Rev. E. W. Howell of Simanggang and had been held in the Museum (Sarawak Museum) for many years prior to publication; the paper originals were too fragile for direct reproduction (p. 219).
- The Museum also held twenty carved boards, approximately two feet long and eight inches broad, from the Undup River, bearing relief patterns identical to the paper drawings (p. 219).
- Animal representations in Sea Dayak male carving are limited to the Hornbill and Dragon (used in religious festivals), a fowl's gizzard, centipede legs, and several caterpillar variants; this contrasts sharply with Kayan, Kenyah, and Kelamatan carving, which freely employs the Dog, Prawn, and other animals (p. 220).
- The "Buah Sigi" or "Ukir Bebatang" (the unwavering carving) pattern appears in at least five distinct visual forms across Plates XII, XIV, and XVI, all sharing the same name but showing "almost impossible" visual connection between them (p. 223).
- The cloud sequence (Plate IX) demonstrates a systematic transformation: from a basic fleur-de-lys unit through truncation, inversion, rightward inclination (indicating drift), duplication (indicating increase), and attenuation (indicating succession), with the final form strongly resembling the throat tattoo pattern common among Sea Dayaks (pp. 220–221).
- The "Tambit Ladong" (tying up the carrier's basket) pattern in Plate XIII, Fig. 4, is described as "a more or less exact imitation of the interlaced rotan fastening between the two free sides at the back of a normal carrying basket" (p. 224).

### Conclusion

Banks' definitive takeaway is that Sea Dayak carving constitutes a closed, self-referential system of naturalistic and geometric abstraction in which a small set of generative units (notably the fleur-de-lys and the sudap) is subjected to predictable operations of truncation, inversion, duplication, and elongation to produce the full range of named patterns. The near-total absence of human and animal forms in male carving—restricted to the ritual Hornbill and Dragon—distinguishes the Iban tradition from both the female textile tradition and the carving practices of neighbouring Bornean groups, and the author presents this as a culturally significant boundary rather than a mere aesthetic preference.

## Context

- E. Banks was writing in his capacity as a colonial-era museum or administrative official in Sarawak; the article functions as a catalogue of museum holdings rather than a field-based ethnographic study, and the interpretive commentary is light and descriptive.
- The primary source material (Howell's drawings and the Undup River boards) had been in the Sarawak Museum collection for an unspecified number of years, meaning Banks was working from archival holdings rather than fresh fieldwork.
