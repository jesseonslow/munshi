# Selesilah: book of the descent of the Rajas of Bruni

H. L. E. Luering, a German missionary and philologist based in Strassburg, published this vocabulary of the Dusun (Kadasan) language of Kimanis in 1897, drawing on fieldwork conducted in 1891 in British North Borneo. The article situates the Kadasan dialect within the broader Malayan language family and argues for its status as a conservator of archaic phonological and morphological forms lost in western Malayan languages.

## Summary

Luering's primary purpose is lexicographic: to provide a systematic English–Dusun–Malay vocabulary collected among the Kadasan-speaking communities of Kimanis. He frames this work within a larger philological argument about the position of Bornean languages in the Malayan family. He proposes a two-branch model—an Eastern branch (encompassing Borneo proper, the migratory Bajau, and the languages of Butong) and a Western branch (with modern Malay as its type)—from which a Proto-Malayan language might eventually be reconstructed (pp. 1–2). He explicitly declines to enter comparative philology in the vocabulary itself, recommending instead that readers interested in Philippine connections consult Haynes's Sulu vocabulary in the same journal (p. 2).

The introduction's most substantive linguistic claim is that Kadasan preserves fuller, more archaic forms than its cognate languages. Luering illustrates this with the word for "egg," *antahlok*, which he traces through Bajau *untelok*, Bouton *ontolo*, Southern Dayak *t-anteloh*, and the Sula-Amboyna forms (e.g., Sula *metelo*, Liang *muntiro*), arguing that the strongly sounded *h* before *l* in the Dusun form represents the original Eastern Malayan shape that all other branches have simplified (pp. 3–4). He further notes the productive prefix *a-* as an intensifier before adjectives (e.g., *afagat* beside *fagat*, "heavy") and the retention of fuller reduplicative or augmentative forms such as *sumumpah* (Malay *sumpah*), *sumakit* (Malay *sakit*), and *susuduk* (Malay *suduk*) (p. 4).

The vocabulary itself spans approximately twenty pages of three-column entries, covering a wide semantic range from basic nouns and verbs to cultural terms. The Malay column is included not as a translation aid but to demonstrate the degree of divergence between Kadasan and the archipelago's lingua franca (p. 2).

### Key Findings

- The Kadasan speakers of Kimanis identify themselves as *uhlan Kadasan* and explicitly reject the European traveller's claim of descent from early Chinese settlers on the Borneo coast (p. 1).
- The word for "egg" in Kadasan, *antahlok*, is argued to represent the original Eastern Malayan form, preserving a strongly sounded *h* before *l* that has been lost in all other Malayan branches; cognates include Bajau *untelok*, Bouton *ontolo*, Sula *metelo*, and Tagalog *itlog* (pp. 3–4).
- The intensifying prefix *a-* before adjectives is a distinctive Kadasan feature, as in *afagat* (heavy), *apodos* (pungent), *aninek* (small), and *ahok* (hair) (p. 4).
- Kadasan retains fuller morphological forms compared to Malay: *sumumpah* (to swear) vs. Malay *sumpah*; *sumakit* (sick) vs. Malay *sakit*; *susuduk* (spoon) vs. Malay *suduk* (p. 4).
- The vocabulary was collected in 1891 in Kimanis, British North Borneo, and the author notes that the language is spoken in various dialects by the so-called Dusuns of that region, with greater differentiation the more inland the tribes live (pp. 1–2).
- Luering proposes that the Bornean, Bajau, and Butong languages form one distinct group intimately related to the Philippine and Sula-Amboyna groups, all derived from a common source he terms the "Eastern branch of the Malayan family" (p. 2).

### Conclusion

Luering's definitive takeaway is that the Kadasan dialect of Kimanis constitutes one of the oldest and purest surviving forms of speech in the Malayan archipelago, preserving phonological and morphological features that have been eroded in the western territories since Borneo's early geographic separation from the Peninsula and Sumatra. The vocabulary thus serves not merely as a practical reference but as philological evidence for the deep antiquity of Bornean languages within the Malayan family.

## Context

- The author was a German missionary (Rev. H. L. E. Luering, Ph.D., Strassburg) working in British North Borneo; his linguistic interest was inseparable from his evangelistic and ethnographic role among the Kadasan communities.
- The article was published in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, the standard colonial-era venue for linguistic and ethnographic reporting from the Straits Settlements and British Borneo, and it builds directly on an earlier Dusun word-list by W. H. Treacher published in the same journal in 1880 (p. 1).
