# A list of Brunei-Malay words

H. S. Haynes published "A List of Brunei-Malay Words" in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (Vol. 34, 1900, pp. 39–48), presenting a lexicon of approximately 200 Brunei-Malay terms with English glosses. The list, collected in the field, documents a distinct Malay variety spoken in the Brunei region and serves as one of the earliest systematic lexical records of that variety in the colonial-era literature.

## Summary

The list is organised alphabetically and spans a wide range of semantic domains: maritime and fishing vocabulary (e.g., *Dapong* "a boat with outriggers," *Lambu* "the Illanun boat," *Salambo* "a fishing net"), agricultural terms (*Katawi* "a place for paddy," *Sebur* "raised divisions in the paddy fields," *Napu* "sago flour"), domestic life and material culture (*Gubong* "a dug-out canoe," *Langong* "a cooking pot," *Pungaut* "a rice spoon"), the natural world (*Bengkatang* "the Proboscis Monkey," *Lekup* "the small hornbill," *Rumbia* "the sago palm"), and the body and health (*Autik* "cataract of the eye," *Dugal* "spleen, nausea," *Jahat Nawa* "sick, unwell").

Several entries reveal contact and borrowing: *Chandas* ("Chinese chop sticks") points to Chinese influence, *Baston* ("a walking-stick") likely reflects an English or Portuguese loan, and *Brian* ("money, or goods paid for a wife") may derive from a Malay root but is glossed in a way that highlights a culturally specific institution. The list also preserves terms for supernatural and spiritual concepts — *Kalindo* ("a jungle spirit"), *Sampar* ("an evil spirit"), *Gusey* ("a sacred jar") — offering a window into the cosmological vocabulary of Brunei-Malay speakers at the turn of the century.

As a document, the list is notable for its brevity and its lack of phonological or grammatical commentary. It functions as a practical reference rather than a linguistic analysis, and its spelling conventions (e.g., *Chabok*, *Chuet*, *Peasow*) reflect the romanisation practices of the period rather than any standardised orthography.

### Key Findings

- The lexicon documents approximately 200 terms covering maritime, agricultural, domestic, zoological, botanical, and spiritual domains, confirming the breadth of Brunei-Malay as a fully functional variety distinct from standard Malay (pp. 39–48).
- Maritime and fishing vocabulary is particularly dense, with terms for specific boat types (*Dapong*, *Lambu*), nets (*Salambo*, *Selereng*, *Seut*), and fishing implements (*Sangup* "a fish spear," *Pungarusan* "a charm attached to a fishing net"), reflecting the centrality of the sea to Brunei society (pp. 40–46).
- Several entries record culturally specific institutions and practices, including *Brian* (bride-price), *Pampangan* ("stocks, place of punishment"), *Porok* ("earth eaten by the natives"), and *Tunduk* ("the scalp lock"), providing ethnographic data alongside lexical information (pp. 41, 45, 48).
- The list preserves terms for the supernatural — *Kalindo* (jungle spirit), *Sampar* (evil spirit), *Gusey* (sacred jar) — documenting a layer of vocabulary absent from standard Malay reference works of the period (pp. 42, 43, 46).
- Borrowings and contact terms are identifiable, including *Chandas* (Chinese chopsticks), *Baston* (walking-stick), and *Beli* (a stick of tobacco), evidencing multilingual contact in the Brunei region (pp. 40–41).
- The spelling system is non-standardised and reflects early 20th-century colonial romanisation, with inconsistent treatment of vowels and consonants (e.g., *Chabok*, *Peasow*, *Tislear*), limiting its utility for modern phonological reconstruction (pp. 39–48).

### Conclusion

Haynes's list is a modest but valuable early record of Brunei-Malay vocabulary, capturing a cross-section of the language's lexicon at a moment when the variety was still largely undocumented in print. Its primary significance lies in preserving terms — particularly those relating to maritime life, material culture, and the supernatural — that might otherwise have been lost, and in providing a baseline against which later descriptions of Brunei-Malay can be compared.

## Context

- The publication appeared in the *Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, the principal scholarly outlet for colonial-era linguistic and ethnographic work in the Straits Settlements and surrounding territories.
- The list was "collected" by Haynes, a term that in this period typically denotes fieldwork with native informants, though no details of the collection process, informant identity, or dialectal location within Brunei are provided in the text.
