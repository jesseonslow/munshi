# A list of Brunei-Malay words

H. B. Marshall's "A Vocabulary of Brunei Malay," annotated by J. C. Moulton, was published in the Journal of the Straits Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society in 1921 (No. 83, pp. 44–74). Compiled over several years of prospecting travel throughout the State of Brunei at the suggestion of W. H. Lee-Warner, the work presents 505 Brunei Malay words with definitions, usage notes, and occasional embedded folk verses, and is framed by Moulton's comparative analysis of the lexicon's relationship to Sarawak Malay, Malay Peninsula Malay, and Dayak.

## Summary

The article is a lexicographical record of the Brunei Malay dialect, assembled by Marshall during his geological prospecting work in the state. Its stated aim was to catalogue words peculiar to Brunei, but Moulton's prefatory notes reveal that the linguistic reality is more complex: a substantial proportion of the entries have clear parallels in neighbouring dialects, particularly Sarawak Malay, and some show probable Dayak or Malay Peninsula origins. Moulton's annotation system—marking words with 'S' for Sarawak usage, 'W' for appearance in Wilkinson's Malay Dictionary, and footnoting further parallels—allows the reader to assess at a glance how much of the vocabulary is genuinely distinctive to Brunei.

The vocabulary itself is organised alphabetically and covers a wide range of semantic fields: boat-building and maritime terminology (bidar, pakarangan, kelong, selambau, pilamas), domestic architecture (penyuchok, sudok, ragian, langgar), flora and fauna with botanical and zoological identifications supplied by I. H. Burkill and the Raffles Museum directors, measures of length and weight (panggal, kayu, babat), and cultural practices including royal protocol (pangau, kaskul, bantal suraga), Islamic observances (sawar, sungkai), and traditional medicine. Several entries are illustrated with short Brunei Malay verses—romantic, didactic, or proverbial—that provide authentic contextual usage.

The article also documents earlier contributions to Brunei Malay lexicography: H. S. Haynes's list of 295 words published in the same journal in 1900 (of which 93 overlap with Marshall's), Lee-Warner's 33-word list (24 new entries incorporated), and C. N. Maxwell's 1893 compilation from Limbang (23 new entries added). Moulton estimates that roughly 35 per cent of the 505 words remain without any identified parallel or derivation, representing the core of genuinely Brunei-specific vocabulary, while acknowledging that this figure could be reduced with further comparative work.

### Key Findings

- The vocabulary comprises 505 words, of which approximately 35 per cent (roughly 175) are identified as true Brunei words with no obvious connection to neighbouring dialects (p. 45).
- H. S. Haynes's earlier 1900 list contained 295 Brunei words; 93 of these are also given by Marshall, establishing a direct scholarly lineage within the JSBRAS (p. 45).
- C. N. Maxwell's 1893 Limbang list contributed 23 additional words not found in Haynes's compilation; Lee-Warner's recent list added 24 further new entries (p. 45).
- Brunei measures of length are documented: 40 panggal = 1 kayu (14 cents); 10 kayu = 1 babat ($1.40) (pp. 48, 65).
- The article records that only three specimens of the gusi jar—a six-handled earthenware vessel believed to have been made and buried by spirits—are said to exist in Brunei (p. 56).
- The pilamas, a method of fishing for sharks and other large fish, is noted as "formerly much practised near Brunei," indicating a now-discontinued local fishery (p. 66).

### Conclusion

Marshall's vocabulary, as annotated by Moulton, establishes that Brunei Malay is not a sharply isolated dialect but sits within a continuum of Bornean Malay varieties, with significant lexical overlap with Sarawak Malay and Dayak borrowings. The work's lasting value lies in its detailed documentation of a dialect that was already undergoing change by the early twentieth century, and in Moulton's systematic comparative framework, which identified a core of genuinely Brunei-specific terms while demonstrating that the majority of the lexicon is shared with or derivable from neighbouring linguistic traditions.

## Context

- The primary sources are Marshall's field notes from prospecting travel in Brunei, supplemented by Maxwell's 1893 Limbang list and Lee-Warner's unpublished list; botanical and zoological identifications were provided by I. H. Burkill (Singapore Botanic Gardens) and R. Hanitsch / J. C. Moulton (Raffles Museum, Singapore).
- The article contributes to the early comparative lexicography of Bornean Malay dialects, building directly on Haynes's 1900 publication in the same journal and on Wilkinson's Malay Dictionary, and provides a baseline against which later linguistic work on Brunei Malay can be measured.
