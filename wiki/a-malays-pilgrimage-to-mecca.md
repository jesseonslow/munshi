# A Malay’s pilgrimage to Mecca

Haji Abdul Majid bin Haji Zainuddin Haji published this first-person account of his 1923 pilgrimage to Mecca in 1926, offering a rare Malay-language perspective on the Haj as experienced by a colonial-era subject navigating the Hejaz under the faltering rule of King Hussein. The article functions simultaneously as a devotional travelogue, a practical guide, and a quiet critique of the commercial and superstitious apparatus surrounding the pilgrimage.

## Summary

The narrative traces the full arc of the Malay pilgrim's journey from village preparation through the sea voyage to Jeddah, the camel caravan to Mecca, the performance of the Haj rites at Arafat and Mina, the visit to Medina, and the return home. Abdul Majid writes with the eye of an observer who is both participant and analyst: he describes the sacred rites in detail while also recording the petty thefts by camel-drivers, the extortion by Bedouin tribes, the "religious secrets" dispensed by shaikhs for a fee, and the gap between the Prophet's actual teachings and the folklore sold to the pilgrim. His tone is wry rather than polemical—he notes, for instance, that the story of the Jinn pillar is "a plausible story to the superstitious" yet asks who can resist a smile when history attributes the mosque's construction to Sultan Salim rather than the Prophet.

The political context of the 1920s Hejaz permeates the account. Abdul Majid documents how King Hussein's failure to pay the annual subsidies to Bedouin tribes along the Medina route led to caravans being held for weeks—nine days at Bir Abbas in 1923, over thirty days for the third caravan that year, and in 1924 no caravan reached Medina at all. He contrasts this with the Turkish period, when subsidies were paid regularly but caravans still required armed escorts. The article thus inadvertently preserves a snapshot of the Hejaz's administrative collapse in the years immediately before Ibn Saud's conquest.

The author's most sustained reflection comes in the closing pages, where he weighs the cost—financial, physical, and mortal—against the pilgrim's unshakeable willingness to repeat the journey. He records the 15% mortality rate among Malay pilgrims registered at the British Consulate in Jeddah in 1924 and yet finds himself unable to explain the incentive beyond the simple assertion that "only a Muslim, firm in the belief of Islam, shall understand."

### Key Findings

- The financial threshold for a Malay village pilgrim to undertake the Haj was approximately $500–$600, representing often a lifetime's savings (p. 269).
- In 1924, 15% of Malay pilgrims registered at the British Consulate in Jeddah died within six months of arrival (p. 287).
- The sea voyage from Singapore or Penang to Jeddah lasted 13 to 17 days; the camel journey from Jeddah to Mecca took two nights, while the route to Medina required twelve nights across eleven halting-places (pp. 270, 278).
- In 1923, the second caravan from Mecca to Medina was stopped at Bir Abbas for nine days; the third caravan was held at the same location for over thirty days, during which rice reached $1 per tea-cupful. In 1924, no caravan reached Medina at all (p. 279).
- The Masjid-il-Haram measured approximately 640 feet by 440 feet, contained 311 pillars, seven minarets, and twenty-two doorways; the Kaabah itself was about 36 feet high, 32 feet long, and 30 feet broad (pp. 274–275).
- Mecca's permanent population was approximately 30,000, swelling to over 100,000 during the pilgrimage season; Medina's permanent population was about 20,000 (pp. 273, 280).

### Conclusion

Abdul Majid's definitive takeaway is that the pilgrimage's power lies not in its material or even its doctrinal content but in an irreducible spiritual conviction that defies rational accounting. He documents the exploitation, the superstition, the danger, and the death with unflinching clarity, yet he refuses to reduce the phenomenon to any of these factors, closing with the admission that the incentive is comprehensible only from within the faith itself.

## Context

- The author was a practising Malay Muslim who performed the Haj in 1923 and wrote as a fellow pilgrim rather than an outside observer; his account carries the authority of direct experience but also the limitations of a single journey and a single route (the "Jalan Sultani").
- The article was published in the JMBRAS, a colonial-era scholarly journal, and its inclusion reflects the Society's interest in documenting Malay religious life; the text preserves details of Hejaz administration, Bedouin politics, and pilgrimage logistics that are otherwise scarce in English-language sources from this period.
