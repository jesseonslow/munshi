# An Article I Never Published: Memoir of R. Balan, vice-president of the Malayan Communist Party

Cheah Boon Kheng's "An Article I Never Published" (2015) presents the recovered, truncated memoir of R. Balan (nom-de-guerre of R. Raja Gopal), vice-president of the Malayan Communist Party, recorded in three sessions in 1974 but never completed before Balan's death. The memoir covers Balan's life from his birth in 1921 on a Perak rubber estate through his entry into the MPAJA resistance in August 1942, his years in the jungle as a Tamil-language propaganda officer, and his reflections on the MCP's post-war strategy and internal leadership failures.

## Summary

The memoir is structured in three parts, moving from personal background to jungle experience to political reflection. Balan's account is notable for its first-person testimony on the MCP's propaganda apparatus during the Japanese Occupation, a period for which few Tamil-language insider accounts exist. He describes the Lengkap Camp as the party's propaganda headquarters, where he edited the Tamil news-sheet *Jayamani* (Victorious Bell) and contributed to the English-language *Voice of Malaya* and the Chinese-language *Ren Ta Pau* (The People's Paper) (p. 137). His mentor at the camp was Wu Tian Wong, later the top Singapore representative of the MCP, who taught him communism and Chinese characters (p. 135).

The most dramatic episode in the memoir is Balan's organization of the mass escape of approximately 150 labourers from Jong Estate on 22 August 1942, preventing their conscription to build the Burma-Thailand "Death Railway." Balan describes how the women of the estate came to his house weeping, and how he gathered the labourers outside his office and instructed them to pack and leave by 2 a.m. the following day (p. 132). He then joined the MPAJA himself on 23 August, after first transporting his mother, brother, and sister to his father in Kepala Batas. The escape nearly failed when a Japanese head of estates confronted him at Tapah railway station, drew his sword, and threatened decapitation before being restrained by an Indian clerk (p. 134).

Balan's political reflections in Part III are particularly significant. He confirms the existence of a wartime agreement between the British and the MCP whereby the former supplied arms, uniforms, and money while the latter provided manpower, and whereby the British would not re-establish colonialism upon their return (p. 138). He identifies Lai Tek (the MCP chief, known in the camp as "Mr. Wright") as responsible for this "ambiguous agreement" and for ultimately betraying the party by working first for the British, then the Japanese, and then the British again (p. 138). He also offers a structural explanation for the MCP's failure to replicate the Vietnamese revolutionary outcome: Malayan society was not homogenous, and the competing nationalisms of Chinese and Indian communities, combined with Malay fear of both, made a seizure of power impossible without Malay support (p. 138).

### Key Findings

- R. Balan (R. Raja Gopal) was born on 22 November 1921 at Nova Scotia Estate, Telok Anson, Perak, to a Ceylonese father (K. Ramalingam, a conductor-cum-clerk) and a Madras-born mother (Sinnatheiammal); the family was middle-class and never in want (p. 131).
- On 22 August 1942, Balan organized the escape of approximately 150 labourers from Jong Estate who were to be conscripted for the Burma-Thailand railway; he joined the MPAJA the following day, 23 August 1942 (pp. 132–134).
- At the Lengkap Camp, Balan was mentored by Wu Tian Wong, who later became the top Singapore representative of the MCP; Balan mastered 3,000 Chinese characters under Wu's instruction (p. 135).
- The MCP's propaganda headquarters in Perak published three periodicals—*Voice of Malaya* (English), *Ren Ta Pau* (Chinese), and *Jayamani* (Tamil)—and Balan was the sole Tamil-language translator in the operation (p. 137).
- Balan was elected vice-president of the MCP in 1955 while still in detention; he was released in 1960 after twelve years, under a restriction that he eschew politics (p. 129).
- Balan confirms a wartime British-MCP agreement in which the British supplied arms, uniforms, and money in exchange for MCP manpower, and agreed not to re-establish colonialism; he attributes the agreement's ambiguity to Lai Tek, who he states ultimately betrayed the party (p. 138).

### Conclusion

Balan's memoir offers a rare insider perspective on the MCP's multi-ethnic propaganda infrastructure during the Japanese Occupation and a candid, if incomplete, assessment of the party's strategic failures. His central argument—that Malaya's racial heterogeneity, unlike Vietnam's homogeneity, made a communist seizure of power structurally impossible without Malay support—aligns with later historiographical consensus but is here voiced from within the party itself. The memoir's most significant archival contribution is its confirmation of the British-MCP wartime arrangement and its identification of Lai Tek as the architect of that arrangement and its ultimate betrayer.

## Context

The article carries the subtitle "An Article I Never Published," reflecting its provenance: Cheah Boon Kheng, then editor of the JMBRAS, had inaugurated a memoir feature in the journal and recorded Balan's testimony in 1974, but the project was abandoned when Balan fell ill and died shortly thereafter. The manuscript was lost among Cheah's papers and only rediscovered decades later (p. 130). This delayed publication means the memoir entered the historical record approximately forty years after it was recorded, after the MCP's armed struggle had ended in 1989 and after the spate of published MCP memoirs that followed that date (p. 129). The text also contains a notable historiographical engagement: Balan explicitly endorses Spencer Chapman's *The Jungle Is Neutral* as "quite correct" in its depiction of camp life while disclaiming support for the book's overall thesis, and he acknowledges that Hanrahan's *The Communist Struggle in Malaya* is "right in criticizing the MCP for not having got more out of their terms with the British" (pp. 136, 138).
