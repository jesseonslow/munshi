# Menangkabau

The Minangkabau are a matrilineal people originating in the highlands of West Sumatra whose settlers established a distinctive socio-political and cultural system in the Malay Peninsula, most notably in the confederacy known as Negri Sembilan (the Nine States). Their presence in the Peninsula is documented from at least the fifteenth century, and their matrilineal constitution—characterised by tribal exogamy, female inheritance of ancestral property, and a graded hierarchy of elective offices—constitutes one of the most elaborate customary legal systems in the Malay world [[1, pp. 42–44]](#ref-1). Beyond Negri Sembilan, Minangkabau legal and cultural influence extended into Perak, where a patriarchal sultanate adapted elements of their criminal and civil law [[2]](#ref-2), and into the broader political dynamics of the Straits of Malacca, where Minangkabau forces played a decisive role in the 1718 conquest of Johor [[3]](#ref-3). The cultural continuum from the Sumatran highlands to the Peninsular settlements has persisted through centuries of migration, producing enduring traditions in music, ritual, and customary governance [[4]](#ref-4).

### Historical Origins and Migration

The earliest datable evidence of Minangkabau presence in the Negri Sembilan hinterland is an inscribed stone at Pengkalan Kempas in Sungai Ujong territory, bearing both Arabic and Sumatran Malay inscriptions (in old-Javanese characters derived from the Adityawarman inscriptions of the Minangkabau or Padang Highlands) and dated to 1467 A.D., the reign of Sultan Mansur Shah of Malacca [[1, p. 44]](#ref-1). The stone marks the grave of a man called Ahmad, Majanu and Shaikh, who according to the Sumatran inscription came downstream and was defeated and killed [[1, p. 44]](#ref-1). By the sixteenth century, Portuguese sources confirm a substantial Minangkabau population in the hinterland. In 1586, the Minangkabau behind Malacca took the side of the Sultan of Johor in an attack on the Portuguese, mustering 2,000 fighting-men who were driven out of their village by an expedition of 100 Portuguese and 600 natives [[1, p. 45]](#ref-1). Godinho d'Eredia, writing in 1613, describes the district of Naning as "occupied by Monancabos engaged in the trade of *betre*" (betel), who came down to the river landing to proceed by boat to the market at Malacca [[1, p. 45]](#ref-1). Barretto de Resende, in 1634, records "five or six thousands of the same Manamcabo Moors" as vassals of Portugal, cultivating extensive land and purchasing tin from the interior [[1, p. 45]](#ref-1).

The Dutch period brought both formal treaties and violent conflict. The 1641 treaty between the Dutch Governor of Malacca and the elders of Naning imposed a tithe of rice, fruit, betel and pepper, a tenth of money from land sales, and the surrender of all weapons [[1, p. 48]](#ref-1). By 1765 the Dutch had commuted the rice tithe to a nominal tribute of 400 *gantang* (quart measures) [[1, p. 64]](#ref-1). The 1759 treaty between Governor David Bulen and the heads of the nine districts of Rembau is the first authentic mention of the "nine countries" (*negeri*) of Rembau and fixed the tin monopoly price at 38 Spanish reals per *bahar* of 300 *kati* [[1, p. 57]](#ref-1).

A pivotal moment in Minangkabau political history was the 1718 conquest of Johor, then the most powerful kingdom in the Malay world. Leonard Andaya's source-critical analysis demonstrates that the enigmatic figure of Raja Kechil was most likely an ingenious Minangkabau leader with genuine connections to the Pagar Ruyong court who exploited the deep legitimacy crisis within the Bendahara dynasty to orchestrate the kingdom's collapse [[3]](#ref-3). The court at Pagar Ruyong, particularly the Putri Jamilan (the hereditary Queen Mother who exercised considerable moral and political authority in the Minangkabau world), provided Raja Kechil with the symbolic instruments—a *chap*, the sword Sapurjaba, and formal installation—that legitimised his summons of Minangkabau forces across the east coast of Sumatra [[3]](#ref-3). In his first formal letter to the Dutch on 22 January 1719, Raja Kechil styled himself "Baginda Raja Kechil who rules over Pulau Gontong and acts as Shahbandar for Baginda Sri Sultan of Pagar Ruyong," notably omitting any claim to be the son of Sultan Mahmud Shah [[3, p. 72]](#ref-3). The conquest marked the emergence of the Minangkabau as a recognised political force in the Straits of Malacca, operating with the same kind of strategic weight that the Buginese already possessed [[3]](#ref-3).

The founding of the present royal house of Negri Sembilan is attributed to Raja Melewar, a Minangkabau prince who arrived in 1773 A.D. [[1, p. 41]](#ref-1). He claimed descent from the royal house of Minangkabau and brought credentials sealed with the seals of the Sultan of Minangkabau and the rulers of Palembang, Jambi, Indragiri, Indrapura, Siak and Aceh [[1, p. 59]](#ref-1). He chose Sri Menanti in Ulu Muar as his centre, deliberately avoiding the coastal states and the tin-fields of Sungai Ujong and Jelebu, and gradually extended his sway over the smaller states that would constitute the Nine States [[1, pp. 61–62]](#ref-1).

### Matrilineal Polity and Customary Law

The Minangkabau constitution in Negri Sembilan is built upon a system of mother-right (*hak ulayat*) in which relationship is reckoned purely through the maternal line [[1, p. 77]](#ref-1). A special word, *sanak*, denotes a blood relation on the distaff side, while children of a man by different wives, or children of one's mother's brothers, belong to other tribes and are described by a different term [[1, p. 77]](#ref-1). The smallest exogamous units are the sub-tribes (*perut*, literally "womb"), generally taking territorial names; the inability of two sub-tribes to trace their descent to a common ancestress is their sanction for intermarriage [[1, p. 78]](#ref-1). On marriage, a man passes from his mother's tribe to become a lodger in his wife's house, becoming subject to her tribal chief in all matters affecting her and her family [[1, p. 78]](#ref-1).

The political hierarchy is graded from the elder of the sub-tribe (*buapa*), through the tribal chief (*lembaga*), to the territorial chief (*undang*) and finally the supreme arbiter, the *Yam-tuan* (Yang di-pertuan) [[1, pp. 87–93]](#ref-1). The *buapa* is elected by sub-tribe members and approved by the tribal chief, who can dismiss him at will [[1, p. 87]](#ref-1). The *lembaga* is elected by the elders of the sub-tribe, supported by the fully enfranchised members of the tribe, male and female, and can be removed only by their unanimous vote [[1, p. 89]](#ref-1). The *undang* is elected by the tribal chiefs in Rembau and Naning, or by a council of eight in Jelebu, of whom five represent the Malacca *waris* family and only three are *lembaga* [[1, p. 90]](#ref-1). The *Yam-tuan* is elected by the four territorial chiefs and, like the Raja at Pagar Ruyong, owns no territory and levies no taxes [[1, p. 92]](#ref-1).

Property devolves *per stirpes* to daughters or their direct descendants; if a daughter has died whose share would have been one-half, her two daughters get a quarter each [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1). The mother's house is ordinarily inherited by the youngest daughter, who undertakes in return to look after her mother in old age [[1, p. 85]](#ref-1). Ancestral property may not be sold outside the tribe except to meet certain customary debts, and the next-of-kin can bar any sale or charge [[1, p. 84]](#ref-1).

Winstedt's 1953 study of an old Minangkabau legal digest from Perak (Maxwell MS. 44, copied in 1875 but containing laws likely originating between 1700 and 1728) demonstrates how these principles were adapted to a patriarchal context [[2]](#ref-2). The digest, structured into 132 sections, prioritises the law of reason, followed by customary law, and finally Quranic law when conflicts arise [[2, p. 4]](#ref-2). Inheritance rules were modified for the patriarchal state, granting sons one-third and daughters two-thirds of hereditary land, a marked departure from strict matrilineal practice [[2, p. 1]](#ref-2). Adultery penalties often required the offenders to marry, with fines of 5 *tahil* 1 *paha* applied if the man refused, rather than the stoning prescribed by religious law [[2, p. 5]](#ref-2).

### Cultural Continuity and Expression

The cultural continuum from the Minangkabau highlands to the Peninsular settlements is vividly illustrated in the musical tradition of *bongai* in Tanjung Ipoh, Negri Sembilan. Megan Collins's ethnomusicological study demonstrates that the ornamental violin style of *bongai* draws on a shared older Minangkabau source of melodic embellishment (*gelitik*) rather than being a direct derivative of the *rabab Pasisia* tradition of West Sumatra [[4]](#ref-4). The critical *gelitik* motif shared by both traditions is an oscillation between two adjacent notes interrupted by an interpolated note approached by leap, bypassing the intermediary tone; at speed this produces a "blip" effect [[4, pp. 102–103]](#ref-4). Collins proposes that these shared motifs likely originate in the *saluang* bamboo flute tradition of the Minangkabau highlands (*darek*), which predates both the *rabab Pasisia* and the *bongai* violin [[4]](#ref-4). The instrument-by-instrument parallels include the *bansi* bamboo flute, the small gong, the *rebana* frame drum, and the *caklempong* (derived from Minangkabau *talempong*) [[4]](#ref-4). Collins documents a significant decline in the tradition: Pak Udin estimated that in the late 1930s approximately 95 per cent of people in Kuala Pilah and surrounding villages could sing *bongai* songs; by 2000 this figure had fallen to roughly 25 per cent [[4, pp. 108–109]](#ref-4).

The ritual and belief systems of the Minangkabau in Negri Sembilan, as documented by Winstedt, reveal a complex syncretism of animist, Hindu and Muslim elements. The medicine-man (*pawang*) performs lustration ceremonies to combat epidemics, recites invocations over rice-seed before sowing, and conducts a triennial mock-combat to drive evil spirits from the rice-fields into the sea [[1, pp. 97–101]](#ref-1). The shaman commands invisible spirits and possesses a tiger familiar, and the *seance* ceremony, lasting about three hours after dark, involves the shaman falling into a trance while spirits are invoked to diagnose illness [[1, pp. 102–103]](#ref-1). Sacred places, persons and animals are all known by the Arabic word *karamat*, and in Rembau alone the minor saints are numbered in hundreds [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1).

The *Syair Peri Tuan Raffles Pergi ke Minangkabau*, a 162-stanza Malay verse composed in 1818, provides a native account of Sir Stamford Raffles' second expedition into the Sumatran highlands [[5]](#ref-5). The text narrates Raffles' journey from Bencoolen to Padang and thence into the Minangkabau interior, through the Tiga Belas Kota, across the lake to Simawang, and to Pagar Ruyung, with particular attention to the political negotiations and the Treaty with Minangkabau sealed at the Balai of Pagar Ruyung on 24 July 1818 [[5, p. 73]](#ref-5). Raimy Ché-Ross's recovery of this text, previously lost to scholarship for over a century, demonstrates that the *syair* was known to and worked upon by Resident Councillor Hervey in the 1880s before being forgotten until its deposit at the Arkib Negara Malaysia in 1969 [[5, pp. 28–30]](#ref-5).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on the Minangkabau spans nearly a century and reveals shifting priorities from colonial administrative concern to anthropological and cultural analysis. Winstedt's 1934 study of Negri Sembilan remains the foundational work, drawing on the *Dagh-Register*, Malacca Consultations, Straits Settlements Records, the *Sejarah Melayu*, d'Eredia's *Description of Malacca*, and Dutch treaty collections, alongside extensive fieldwork in Rembau, Jelebu and Johol [[1]](#ref-1). Its historiographical contribution lies in demonstrating that the "Nine States" was a late and partly artificial construction, and in providing the first systematic account of Minangkabau matrilineal law as practised rather than merely theorised [[1]](#ref-1). Winstedt's 1953 article on the Perak legal digest extended the inquiry to the adaptation of Minangkabau law in a patriarchal context [[2]](#ref-2).

Andaya's 1972 article on the 1718 conquest of Johor established a source-critical method for early Malay historiography by systematically cross-referencing Malay court chronicles with contemporary European diplomatic and military records, resolving specific contradictions in the narrative and demonstrating how political context shaped the composition of each source tradition [[3]](#ref-3). His work drew on Dutch East India Company records in The Hague, the Portuguese eyewitness account of Captain Joao Tavares, the *Siak Chronicles*, and the *Tuhfat al-Nafis* [[3]](#ref-3).

More recent scholarship has turned to cultural expression and indigenous textual sources. Collins's 2002 ethnomusicological study provided the first detailed musical analysis linking *bongai* and *rabab Pasisia* playing styles, identifying the *saluang* flute as a probable common source for shared ornamental motifs [[4]](#ref-4). Ché-Ross's 2003 recovery of the *Syair* restored a previously overlooked indigenous source to the literature on Raffles' Sumatran administration [[5]](#ref-5). Labi's 1969 re-analysis of Negri Sembilan socio-political organisation, though only its bibliographic header survives in the available records, indicates continued scholarly engagement with the constitutional structures first described by Winstedt [[6]](#ref-6). The Blagden contribution of 1930 on Minangkabau custom in Malacca, while its text is not available in the present records, confirms that the Society's attention to the subject extended to the colonial port city itself [[7]](#ref-7).
