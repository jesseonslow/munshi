# Recollections of my time in Malaya (1945–1948) Part 1

J.M. Gullick, a former Malayan Civil Service officer who served in Negri Sembilan from 1945 to 1956, published this revised memoir in 2013, offering a first-hand account of the British Military Administration (BMA) and the immediate post-surrender period in the Malay Peninsula. The overarching argument is that the BMA was a deliberately limited instrument of military control—never intended to replicate civil government—and that the precarious authority it established in the weeks following the Japanese surrender was shaped by the MPAJA's ambiguous position, inter-communal violence, and the near-total collapse of Japanese administrative and economic structures.

## Summary

This memoir is a revised version of a typescript Gullick originally composed in 1969–70 for Professor Robert Heussler's history of the Malayan Civil Service. Gullick opens with a theoretical framework for understanding military government, arguing that Civil Affairs units were allocated by field commanders solely to prevent the civil population from becoming a military liability (through rioting, refugee flows, disease, or theft of supplies) and to extract labour and infrastructure for the war effort. He contends that criticism of the BMA for not operating like a peacetime civil government is "beside the point," since the system was designed from the outset for a brief transitional period of three to six months before restoration of civil authority (pp. 61–62).

The narrative then traces the practical experience of the BMA in Negri Sembilan, where Gullick served as Deputy Senior Civil Affairs Officer under James Calder. He describes the chaotic landing at Morib on 10 September 1945, the improvised promulgation of Mountbatten's proclamations at the Seremban railway station, and the tenuous hold the BMA exercised over a state in which the Japanese were still physically present, the MPAJA was asserting itself as liberator, and the police were demoralised and confined to their stations. The memoir gives particular attention to the inter-communal violence that erupted in the authority vacuum between the Japanese surrender and the establishment of British control, culminating in the Batu Kikir massacre of early November 1945, in which approximately forty Chinese women and children were killed by Malay villagers. Gullick frames this episode as a formative trauma for the Malay political class, arguing that modern histories give "insufficient attention" to the effect of such clashes on the minds of Malays who would become prominent political leaders in the independence era (p. 74).

The memoir also provides valuable detail on the institutional architecture of the BMA: the Malayan Planning Unit under Major General H.R. Hone, the Civil Affairs Staff Centre courses at Wimbledon, the composition of state-level teams, and the relationships between BMA officers, Force 136, and the MPAJA. Gullick is candid about the limitations of the training provided and the personal qualities of key figures, including Hone, Willan, Newboult, and Calder.

### Key Findings

- The BMA Negri Sembilan team landed at Morib on the night of 10 September 1945 (D+2 of the planned landing), with the convoy arriving a mile south of the designated beach area; the planned "assembly area" one mile inland never existed (pp. 67–68).
- The Batu Kikir massacre occurred in early November 1945 in a remote part of Kuala Pilah district, in which approximately 40 Chinese women and children were slaughtered by Malay villagers after Chinese men withdrew from the village; the Malays involved were later tried and convicted but sentenced to imprisonment rather than death given the provocation (p. 75).
- The Japanese "banana" currency (notes bearing a banana tree emblem) had depreciated to the point of worthlessness by the time of the surrender, and local food production had greatly diminished owing to Japanese requisitioning; a smallpox epidemic broke out in autumn 1945 but was contained by mass vaccination (p. 72).
- The Malayan Planning Unit, headed by Major General H.R. Hone, identified three reasons for separating Singapore from the Malayan Union: Singapore's predominantly Chinese population would overweight the Chinese element; its free port status was incompatible with the customs system essential to Malay state revenues; and Britain would require the Singapore naval and military base long after Malayan independence (p. 65).
- Only a quarter of the 150–200 officers who crossed the Indian Ocean in August–September 1945 to establish the BMA had attended courses at the Civil Affairs Staff Centre at Wimbledon; the remainder came direct from British military units in India (p. 63).
- In the Rembau sub-district, Inche Malek bin Yusof (later Menteri Besar of Negri Sembilan and Governor of Malacca) was seized by the MPAJA, tied across his own office desk, and threatened with a knife after Brigadier Willan reactivated the district office without consulting the local SCAO—an incident Gullick cites as illustrative of the "very tenuous hold" of British authority in those first weeks (p. 74).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive historical takeaway is that the BMA's authority in the immediate post-surrender period was far more precarious than later official narratives acknowledged, and that the inter-communal violence of 1945—particularly the Batu Kikir massacre and the associated climate of terror—planted seeds of mutual suspicion that would shape Malayan political development for decades. He argues that the MPAJA, lacking coherent objectives or directives from SEAC, "drifted into lawlessness or apathy," and that the BMA's survival depended less on military force than on the absence of any organised opposition and the willingness of local community leaders to engage in pragmatic cooperation (pp. 73–76).

## Context

- Gullick served as a mid-ranking Malayan Civil Service officer (Deputy SCAO, Negri Sembilan; later District Officer) from 1945 to 1956, and this memoir reflects the administrative perspective of a colonial official operating within the BMA's chain of command.
- The text draws on personal recollection rather than archival files (Gullick kept no diary), and its value lies in the granular detail of day-to-day military government operations and the subjective experience of inter-communal relations as perceived at district level.
