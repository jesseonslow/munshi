# With three articles by W.E. Maxwell The incidents of Malay life. {In Papers on Malay customs and beliefs

R.J. Wilkinson's *Papers on Malay Customs and Beliefs* (1957) is a comprehensive ethnographic study of the religious, spiritual, and social life of the Peninsular Malay, arguing that Malay civilization constitutes a layered palimpsest in which successive religious traditions—primordial Indonesian animism, Hinduism, and Islam—have been superimposed one upon another without displacement, producing a distinctive culture in which the old is never discarded but merely overlaid by the new.

## Summary

Wilkinson's central thesis is that the Malay does not exchange old customs for new but retains both simultaneously, so that the entire ceremonial life of the Peninsula functions as "a sort of museum of ancient customs — an ill-kept and ill-designed museum in which no exhibit is dated, labelled or explained" (p. 74). He demonstrates this principle across every domain of Malay existence: in religion, where the *tauhid* coexists with saint-worship and the propitiation of pre-Islamic nature spirits; in demonology, where Persian jinn, Hindu deities like Siva (Betara Guru) and Kala, and ancient Indonesian animistic beliefs all operate concurrently; and in social ceremony, where the brief Islamic marriage contract is embedded within an elaborate apparatus of Hindu and pre-Hindu survivals including the *bersanding* enthronement, bridal rice, and lustration rites (pp. 59–60, 74–77).

The work is structured in two major divisions. The first (Chapters I–V) examines belief systems: the nature of Malay Muhammadanism and its relationship to older creeds; the stratified demonology of the Peninsula; cosmological and historical narratives; theories of life, the soul, and the natural world; and the practices of medicine, divination, and magic. The second division (Life and Customs) documents the ceremonial treatment of individuals from birth through death, including ante-natal rites, infancy, childhood education, betrothal, marriage, adult domestic life, and funeral practice. Wilkinson draws heavily on primary Malay texts—the *Sejarah Melayu*, *Bustanu's-salatin*, *Hikayat* literature—and on the field observations of predecessors such as Snouck Hurgronje, W.W. Skeat, and Sir Frank Swettenham, while contributing his own detailed accounts of Perak ceremonial life.

A recurring analytical method is the separation of "essentials" from "accessories" in any given ceremony. Wilkinson argues that feasting, dancing, enthronements, and lustrations are common accessories that accompany every major Malay rite and must be stripped away before the true historical core can be identified (pp. 74–75). Applying this method, he concludes that Islam contributed relatively little to Malay marriage custom beyond the brief contract and the identification of the *mas kahwin* with the Islamic *mahr*; that Hinduism is traceable in the bridal rice, bridal thread, and bathing pavilion; and that the *bersanding* itself is a dramatization of ancient royalty with roots in the Palembang tradition (pp. 75–77).

### Key Findings

- The Five Pillars of Islam contain no ethical pillar; the average religious Malay is "largely a formalist" whose devotion is measured by the number of prayers recited rather than by moral conduct, and the ethical standard of a Muslim country is national rather than religious (p. 7).
- Malay demonology is composed of distinct strata: a Moslem upper layer (jinn, Persian and Arab spirits), a Hindu middle layer (Siva, Kala, Sri, Indra, Ganesha, the *raksasa* ogres), and a primeval Indonesian base layer including the Four Great Spirits of the Sea and the "Black Youth" (*jin hitam*), the latter probably a deity of the aboriginal race (pp. 13–14).
- The *semangat* (spirit of life) is not a soul in the Christian or Islamic sense but a vital force common to all animate and inanimate things; its most clearly documented survival is in the rice-soul ceremonies, where the reaper uses a specially shaped knife (*tuai*) to avoid alarming the "bird of life" in the seed-rice (pp. 25–26).
- The *berhantu* ceremony, in which a *pawang* summons spirits to diagnose or cure illness, operates on a strict hierarchy: the Sultan Muda of Perak alone may traffic with the highest state spirits (*jin kerajaan*), for a fee of $100, while common people's *pawang* deal with "common devils" such as the tiger-spirit (*hantu belian*) at proportionally lower cost (pp. 68–71).
- The 1907 Krian agricultural experiment demonstrated that Banjarese native methods yielded 366 *gantang* per acre (the best result), while European interventions—bone manure, seed selection by weight, and Ceylon seed importation—produced markedly inferior crops of 128, 164, and negligible yields respectively, confirming the adequacy of indigenous rice-cultivation technique (pp. 64–65).
- Cremation was practised by the Malacca Malays in the first half of the fifteenth century according to Chinese navigators; tree-burial survives in the Northern States for those who die a "bad" death; and the Patani tradition records that bodies of the unluckily dead were formerly exposed to dogs and vultures—evidence of a complex pre-Islamic mortuary tradition now entirely replaced by orthodox Muslim interment (pp. 75–76).

### Conclusion

Wilkinson's definitive historical takeaway is that Malay ceremonial life is best understood not as a living tradition with internal coherence but as an accumulated archive of dead customs preserved out of fear of ancestral vengeance rather than from any understanding of their meaning. The Malay "has preserved them as mere formalities: dead things for the satisfaction of his dead ancestors" (p. 74). He expresses concern that this archive is rapidly disintegrating under the pressure of modernization and European administrative indifference, and he urges that the national history crystallised in these ceremonies be recorded before it is lost entirely.

## Context

- The work was published in the *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society*, the principal scholarly periodical of MBRAS, and represents one of the last major pre-independence ethnographic syntheses of Malay culture produced by a British colonial administrator-scholar.
- Wilkinson's analysis of the Perak *berhantu* and the office of the Sultan Muda draws on evidence from the 1875 trial of Sultan Abdullah following the murder of Resident J.W.W. Birch, connecting ritual practice to the political history of British intervention in the state.
