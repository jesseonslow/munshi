# With three articles by W.E. Maxwell The incidents of Malay life. {In Papers on Malay customs and beliefs

R.J. Wilkinson's *Papers on Malay Customs and Beliefs* (1957) is a comprehensive ethnographic study of the religious, spiritual, and social life of the Malay Peninsula, written from the perspective of a British colonial administrator deeply embedded in Perak. Its overarching thesis is that Malay culture constitutes a palimpsest of successive religious strata—primordial Indonesian animism, Hinduism, and Islam—layered one upon another without the older elements ever being fully displaced, producing a distinctive syncretic system in which the Malay "cares nothing for consistency" and preserves old customs alongside new ones.

## Summary

Wilkinson's central argument is that the Malay religious and ceremonial world cannot be understood through any single doctrinal lens. He demonstrates that what appears to be Muhammadanism is in fact a composite: the orthodox Islamic framework (the Five Pillars, the lunar calendar, the marriage contract) sits atop a Hindu layer (Siva as Betara Guru, the bridal rice and thread, the bathing pavilion) which in turn overlays a primordial Indonesian animism (the *semangat* or spirit of life in all things, the four Sea Spirits, the Black Youth of the earth, the rice-soul ceremonies). The Malay, Wilkinson argues, does not replace one belief with another but adds the new to the old, so that a fisherman may invoke the Four Archangels when things go well, Sanskrit names when they go badly, and pure Indonesian terms in desperation (pp. 10–15).

The study then turns to the practical dimensions of this syncretism in daily life. Wilkinson traces the full cycle from birth through childhood, betrothal, marriage, adult economic activity, sickness, and death, showing at each stage how Islamic, Hindu, and animistic elements coexist. The birth ceremony, for instance, receives the child into three religions simultaneously—spat upon by the midwife against Indonesian disease-spirits, addressed with the Islamic *adzan*, and marked with the Hindu caste-sign on the forehead (p. 41). The wedding's *bersanding* (enthronement) is identified not as a marriage rite at all but as a dramatization of ancient kingship, with the bridegroom wearing regalia drawn from the actual State treasures of Perak (pp. 57, 77). The *berhantu* ceremony, in which the Sultan Muda of Perak convenes a ghostly court of spirits borrowed from every religion, is presented as the supreme expression of this layered cosmology (pp. 67–71).

Throughout, Wilkinson draws on a wide range of primary sources: the *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Bustanu's-salatin*, the *Hikayat Marong Mahawangsa*, Abdullah Munshi's memoirs, Snouck Hurgronje's *The Achehnese*, Skeat's *Malay Magic*, and his own extensive fieldwork in Perak and the Straits Settlements. He also incorporates contemporary administrative records, including the trial evidence from the 1875 Birch assassination case and the 1907 Krian agricultural experiments, to ground his ethnographic observations in verifiable historical and economic data.

### Key Findings

- The Perak State Council fixed a scale of *mas kahwin* (marriage settlement) in which a Sultan's daughter commanded $1,000, a major chief's daughter $250, and a peasant's daughter $31.25; the bridegroom's contribution toward wedding festivities ("money to go in smoke") was traditionally $6¼ in Perak (p. 52).
- A 1907 agricultural experiment in Krian, ordered by the Director of Agriculture, tested eight plots under different cultivation methods; the best yield was 366 *gantang* per acre (Banjarese native methods), while imported Ceylon seed produced a "complete fiasco" and bone manure yielded only 128 *gantang* per acre (pp. 64–65).
- The Sultan Muda of Perak, a spiritual sovereign who could never succeed to the secular throne, charged a fee of $100 for conducting a royal *berhantu*—divided as $25 for himself, $25 for the Raja Kechil Muda, and $50 for his suite of wizards—and was attended by a nine-staged lustration pavilion erected as a feudal obligation by the villagers of Pasir Garam (pp. 69–71).
- The *berhantu* performed at Batak Rabit on or about 24 August 1875, which was cited as evidence against Sultan Abdullah at the trial following the murder of Resident Birch, involved Raja Kechil Muda and his son Raja Ahmad, who claimed they could call up Birch's spirit for $100 and that the spirit which would kill him resided at Kuala Perak (pp. 68–69).
- A Malay peasant's diversified income—rice (approximately $25 per acre net after deductions), coconuts ($50–$80 annually from 30–40 trees at 4 cents per nut), fishing, rattan-gathering, and odd employment—totalled an estimated $15–$20 per month, making him "far too well-off" to accept regular wage-labour on an estate (pp. 65–66).
- The circumciser (*mudin*) at the Perak Court was a regular officer of State who performed his duties while "the great chiefs stand round him with drawn swords ready to slay him if he mismanages the operation" (p. 50).

### Conclusion

Wilkinson's definitive takeaway is that Malay ceremonial life is "a museum of ancient customs" maintained not out of genuine belief in their efficacy but out of a deep-seated fear of the vengeance of the dead who established them. The Malay does not discard old practices when adopting new ones; he adds the new to the old, and the result is a culture in which every significant life-event is surrounded by a dense accretion of survivals from at least three distinct religious traditions. Wilkinson warns that this system is under threat not from Islam or Western science but from the Malay's own growing self-confidence and literacy, which will lead him to "improve upon" ancient customs in ways that are "of a most deadly character" to their preservation (pp. 74, 78).

## Context

- Wilkinson was a senior British colonial officer in Perak (he served as a member of the Perak State Council and was closely involved in the administration of the Native States); the work reflects the insider knowledge of a long-term resident rather than that of an outside anthropologist, and his access to court records, trial evidence, and personal relationships with Malay dignitaries is evident throughout.
- The study draws primarily on Wilkinson's own fieldwork in Perak and the Straits Settlements, supplemented by Snouck Hurgronje's *The Achehnese*, W.W. Skeat's *Malay Magic*, the *Sejarah Melayu*, the *Bustanu's-salatin*, and contemporary administrative documents including the 1875 Birch trial records and the 1907 Krian agricultural experiment reports.
