# Tom Harrisson and Indian influences in early Southeast Asia

Alastair Lamb published this memorial and methodological assessment of Tom Harrisson in 1977, using the ongoing scholarly controversy over the reconstruction of Chandi Bukit Batu Pahat and the origins of nine-chambered reliquaries to contrast Harrisson's ethnographically grounded, naturalist approach to protohistoric Southeast Asia with H.G. Quaritch Wales' rigid model of direct Indian cultural transmission. The article argues that Harrisson's instinct to read indigenous social and economic processes into the archaeological record—rather than imposing a fixed stylistic template—points toward a more nuanced understanding of how Indian influences were locally transformed in early Southeast Asian states.

## Summary

Lamb frames his discussion around the publication of Quaritch Wales' *The Malay Peninsula in Hindu Times* (1976), a work that adheres to a theory formulated in the mid-1930s: that Indian cultural influence reached mainland Southeast Asia along a direct route from South India (Mahabalipuram) to Angkor, with local sites like those in Kedah serving as waypoints in this transmission. Wales' model privileges style, iconography, and epigraphy collated against Sanskrit texts, and his insistence on a central masonry tower for Chandi Bukit Batu Pahat is driven by the need to reconcile the structure with Pallava architecture. Lamb, who first met Harrisson in 1959 while working on the same site, recounts how Harrisson immediately identified the stone pillar bases as indicative of a mixed timber-and-brick construction analogous to Balinese temple architecture—a reading Lamb initially adopted before being swayed by the prestige of the French Angkor school into giving the structure a Khmer-style stone tower. Brian Peacock's later revised reconstruction returned to the Balinese analogy, vindicating Harrisson's original intuition.

The article then broadens to the question of the nine-chambered reliquaries, of which Lamb recovered six intact examples at Chandi Bukit Batu Pahat in 1959–60. While these objects embody religious and cosmological concepts of ultimate Indian origin, their immediate formal parallels lie in Central Java, and their distribution across Kedah, Songkhla, Pimai, Cambodia, Ceylon, Bali, and Java suggests a Southeast Asian context of production and use. Treloar's analysis of the contents dated the site to the late Sung and early Yuan period (12th–13th centuries AD), a chronology that aligns the Kedah sites with states across the Malacca Straits in Sumatra rather than with the earlier dates Wales preferred.

Lamb uses this contrast to draw a broader methodological lesson. Wales represents what he calls the "antiquarian method"—a tradition descending from Schliemann and Layard, strong in style and text but weak in the sustained study of small finds, glass beads, and material culture in their full archaeological and ethnographic context. Harrisson, by contrast, was a naturalist who understood how social systems worked in practice, as evidenced by his pioneering four-level approach to bead study (archaeological context, physical-chemical constitution, typological distribution, and living ethnographic value systems). Lamb's own subsequent research on chevron beads—showing that the types most valued by the Kelabit of Borneo are also the most valued in West Africa and the Americas, and that they derive from Venetian and Levantine manufacture—illustrates the productive potential of Harrisson's approach, even though Harrisson himself never carried the argument to its full chronological conclusion.

### Key Findings

- Chandi Bukit Batu Pahat (Wales' site no. 8, Kedah): Harrisson and Lamb's 1959 reconstruction proposed a mixed timber-and-brick structure with Balinese parallels; Wales maintained a central masonry tower to fit his Pallava model; Brian Peacock's later revision confirmed the Balinese reading (pp. 9–10).
- Nine-chambered reliquaries: six intact examples recovered by Lamb at the base of the central tower of Chandi Bukit Batu Pahat in 1959–60; closest formal parallels in Central Java; Treloar's analysis of contents dates the site to the late Sung/early Yuan period, 12th–13th centuries AD (pp. 10–11).
- E. Edwards McKinnon's Sumatran finds include objects identical to those from Pengkalan Bujang, notably small stone pegs with carved tops (possibly gaming pieces) unknown elsewhere, C14-dated to c. 1200 AD, strengthening the case for close Kedah–Sumatra connections in that period (p. 11).
- Harrisson's chronology for Chinese export ceramics in Borneo deviated so far from the accepted sequence that a dual system was required to reconcile it with the wider Southeast Asian picture (p. 9).
- The "Roman" glass beads from Johore Lama and Kota Tinggi, originally reported by Gardner and acquired through Horace Beck, comprise two distinct assemblages (14th–16th century Southeast Asian types alongside Mediterranean/Near Eastern types c. 1000 BC–200 AD) whose co-occurrence at the same site raises strong suspicion of deliberate "planting" in the Piltdown tradition (pp. 11–12).
- Harrisson's four-level framework for bead study—archaeological context, physical-chemical constitution, typological distribution, and anthropological/ethnographic value—anticipated modern material culture analysis; his Kuching collection confirmed that the Kelabit's most valued chevron beads are of an "old" pattern (Venetian, pre-18th century) also prized in West Africa and the Americas (pp. 12–13).

### Conclusion

Lamb's definitive takeaway is that both Wales and Harrisson, despite their very different methods and despite the likelihood that future Southeast Asian archaeologists will challenge their specific conclusions, made contributions that "can never be ignored." The true memorial to Harrisson lies not in any single reconstruction or chronology but in his demonstration that indigenous social and economic processes must be read into the archaeological record, and in his vision of a collaborative discipline requiring the combined strengths of the antiquarian, the ethnographer, and the systematic field archaeologist—a vision that still awaits its full realisation by a new generation of Southeast Asian-born scholars.

## Context

- The article draws on Lamb's own fieldwork at Chandi Bukit Batu Pahat and Pengkalan Bujang (Kedah, 1959–60), Harrisson's collections in the Sarawak Museum at Kuching, and the Cambridge Archaeological Museum's bead holdings; it also engages the published work of Quaritch Wales, Treloar, O'Connor, Peacock, and McKinnon.
- Historiographically, the piece marks a transitional moment in Southeast Asian archaeology: it critiques the older "direct influence" paradigm while advocating for the integration of ethnographic analogy and small-finds analysis, anticipating the more systematic, regionally grounded approaches that would develop in the 1980s and beyond.
