# Punan (Bornean tribe)

## Scope and Historical Definition

The Punan are a group of indigenous peoples of Borneo, documented primarily in the Sarawak region of the island, whose name has been applied to at least two fundamentally distinct populations: a forest-dwelling nomadic group and a settled longhouse-dwelling people of the Rejang River basin. The conflation of these two groups under a single ethnonym persisted throughout the colonial ethnographic literature and was not resolved until the late 1940s, when administrative officers and ethnographers working in Sarawak began to distinguish them explicitly [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1). The term "Punan" itself, in the case of the settled group, derives from Sungei Punan, a tributary of the Ba River, and carries no implication of kinship with the nomadic bearers of the same name [[1, pp. 31–32]](#ref-1).

The nomadic Punan, as described in the earliest available account in the Society's literature, were a small, forest-dependent people whose distribution in Sarawak was recorded between the Apoh and Tutoh rivers, in the *ulu* (upper reaches) of the Rejang, in the Bintulu District, and near Bukit Batu [[2, p. 77]](#ref-2). Their language was noted to be nearly identical across these widely separated districts, suggesting a common origin or prolonged contact [[2, p. 77]](#ref-2). The settled Punan Ba, by contrast, numbered approximately 600 individuals divided among seven longhouses on the Rejang, Kakus, and Ba rivers, and identified themselves as *orang Kajang*, placing them within the broader Kajang grouping alongside the Sekapan, Kejaman, and Lahanan [[1, pp. 31–32]](#ref-1).

## Economic and Social Organisation

The nomadic Punan were described by E. V. Andreini in 1924 as stockily built, of a peculiar white colouring, and entirely dependent on blowpipe hunting with poisoned darts, supplemented by women's sago production and jungle fruit gathering [[2, p. 77]](#ref-2). The group roamed between two large rivers following wild sago, a pattern of seasonal movement tied to the ripening of the resource [[2, p. 77]](#ref-2). Andreini noted the apparent absence of old people among those he observed, attributing it to chest troubles, and recorded the impression that the group was "fast dying out as a race" [[2, p. 77]](#ref-2).

The Punan Ba, as documented by Rodney Needham in 1955, presented a markedly different social and economic profile. They were a house-dwelling people with mortuary posts, hereditary class divisions, and rice cultivation [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1). Their society was organised into three tiers: aristocrats, commoners, and slaves, a hierarchy that was materially visible in the economy of exchange. Marriage payments varied by class: $2,000 to $3,000 for aristocrats, $900 for commoners, and $100 for slaves [[1, p. 33]](#ref-1). Aristocratic burials involved a *kelireng* (mortuary post) approximately twenty feet high, with a porcelain jar set in the carved top and a vertical opening for grave goods; in former times a slave was crushed or bound beneath the post at its erection, though at Pandan a dying aristocrat ordered a pig substituted [[1, p. 34]](#ref-1).

The spiritual cosmology of the Punan Ba recognised three categories of spirit: *beyo* (equivalent to the Kenyah *bali*), *otu* (comparable to the Kayan *toh*), and *etun* (the personal soul or ghost). They placed the supreme creator Telangan in Tuli, the land above the sky, where the dead lived a second life in longhouses, planted rice, and made canoes [[1, p. 35]](#ref-1). The nomadic Punan, by contrast, held beliefs centred on spirits inhabiting large trees, stones, rivers, and mountains, along with certain pigs and a charm wood that attracted game; the dead were simply left in their shelter and the group moved on [[2, p. 77]](#ref-2).

## Taxonomic Confusion and Correction

The history of Punan ethnography is dominated by a persistent problem of misidentification. From Burns's 1849 description of a Kayan-like Punan people on the Rejang through the confused accounts of Windt, Low, Hiller, Baring-Gould, and Bampfylde, and into the 1947 census report, the literature had failed to distinguish the settled longhouse Punan Ba from the nomadic Punan, who in many cases were in fact Penan [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1). The shared name "Punan" obscured fundamentally different peoples: one a sophisticated, house-dwelling group with mortuary posts, hereditary class divisions, and rice cultivation; the other a forest nomad with no fixed settlement [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1).

The breakthrough came with Leach's 1948 administrative report, which for the first time explicitly separated the Punan Ba from the "Nomadic Punan" and located their longhouses, and with Urquhart's 1949 letter to the *Sarawak Gazette*, which recorded the critical fact that the "Jungle Punans" spoke a completely different language from the longhouse Punan of the Batang Rejang [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1). The 1947 Sarawak census, compiled before these corrections had been published, had inextricably confused the Punan Ba with settled Penan under the catch-all category "Penan and Settled Punan" within "Other and indeterminate indigenous" [[1, p. 29]](#ref-1).

Needham's own contribution was modest by design. He was in the field primarily to delimit the Penan, and his Punan Ba material consisted of notes gathered in conversation, conducted in Malay since he did not speak the Punan Ba language, with T. R. Berasap of Pandan, supplemented by information from Dr. J. D. Freeman, R. F. Mole, and I. A. N. Urquhart [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1). He was explicit about the limitations of his account, presenting it as the "random notes of an apprentice ethnographer" that a more closely situated observer could correct and expand [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1). What he provided was a framework: the Punan Ba's own account of their origin from the Batang Kayan through the Kajang to the Ba River; their three-tier class system and its expression in marriage payments, burial forms, and social sanctions; their kinship terminology; and their spiritual cosmology [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on the Punan spans from the early 1920s through the mid-1960s, reflecting a shift from brief descriptive notices to systematic ethnographic and linguistic investigation. Andreini's 1924 note represents the earliest substantial account, a short two-page observation of a single encampment that, while limited in scope, established the basic parameters of the nomadic group's physical characteristics, subsistence, beliefs, and distribution [[2, pp. 76–77]](#ref-2). The note's most significant contribution was its recording of the Murut origin myth—that the first Punans were two lazy Muruts who fled into the jungle—which provided a comparative ethnographic datum linking the Punan to the broader highland populations of Sarawak [[2, p. 77]](#ref-2).

The 1950s marked a turning point. Needham's 1955 article on the Punan Ba functioned less as a full ethnography than as a corrective taxonomy and a provocation for further study [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1). His closing observation—that the Punan Ba were "very easily accessible" and would make "a valuable introduction to the understanding of the Kajang complex of peoples, of whom we know so very little"—framed the paper as a call to action for the small community of Sarawak ethnographers of the period [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1). The article drew on a small corpus of colonial-era travel accounts, administrative reports, the 1947 census report, and Needham's own 1952 field notes, and it anticipated the more systematic Kajang studies that would follow in the 1960s and 1970s [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1).

By the mid-1960s, the focus of research had shifted toward the linguistic and communicative practices of Borneo's nomadic peoples. Tom Harrisson's 1965 article on three "secret" communication systems among Borneo nomads and their dogs extended the investigation into the cultural and practical dimensions of nomadic life, while G. Arnold's 1958 monograph on the Nomadic Penan of the Upper Rejang (Plieran) provided a more detailed account of the population that had, in many earlier sources, been mislabelled as Punan [[3, pp. 40–82]](#ref-3), [[4, pp. 37–86]](#ref-4). R. Needham's 1954 note on kinship terms and death names at Batu Belah and Long Terawan further contributed to the growing body of comparative linguistic data for the region [[5, pp. 215–217]](#ref-5).

Taken together, these records illustrate a trajectory from initial descriptive contact through taxonomic correction to increasingly specialised linguistic and cultural analysis. The central historiographical lesson is that the ethnonym "Punan" was, for much of the colonial period, a label of convenience rather than a marker of ethnic identity, and that its resolution required the combined efforts of administrative officers, census compilers, and ethnographers working in close proximity to the communities concerned [[1, pp. 24–36]](#ref-1).
