# Birds in the Botanic gardens, Singapore

H. N. Ridley, Superintendent of the Singapore Botanic Gardens, published this descriptive natural history account in 1898, drawing on his long residence in the gardens to document the avifauna observable within and around their grounds. The article's implicit thesis is that Singapore's birdlife, though often perceived by residents as limited to the ubiquitous black-and-white robin, is in fact rich and varied, and that patient observation in the gardens' mixed habitat of shrubbery, woodland, and lakes reveals a considerable assemblage of species.

## Summary

Ridley organises his observations taxonomically, moving from raptors through owls, kingfishers, hornbills, parrots, woodpeckers, cuckoos, swifts, starlings, bulbuls, finches, sunbirds, pigeons, quails, and wading birds. Rather than presenting a formal checklist, he weaves natural history notes—nesting behaviour, diet, vocalisations, Malay names, and occasional anecdotes of captivity—into a flowing narrative. The gardens function as a microcosm of Singapore's broader ecology: species that are forest specialists (hornbills, pittas) appear only rarely, while generalists and edge-habitat birds (bulbuls, bulbuls, sunbirds, quails) are regular residents.

A recurring theme is the contrast between what casual observers notice and what sustained attention reveals. Ridley repeatedly corrects the popular impression that Singapore is bird-poor, noting that dense vegetation allows birds to conceal themselves effectively and that the best viewing occurs at dawn and dusk. He also draws attention to the absence of certain expected species—crows are notably scarce, hornbills are seldom seen despite suitable jungle, and the barn-owl is extremely rare—offering possible explanations in each case.

The article also records several instances of human–bird interaction: the Malay practice of keeping turtledoves as household talismans, the use of birdlime and noose-traps for ground pigeons and turtledoves, the ingenious quail trap described in detail, and the failed Malacca experiment of releasing caged Tiongs (Eulabes javanensis) as insectivores, which proved counterproductive because the species feeds exclusively on fruit.

### Key Findings

- The serpent-eagle (*Spizaetus limnaetus*) maintained a nest in the garden jungle for over nine years, a mass of sticks on the fork of a branch in a lofty tree (p. 74).
- A sea-eagle (*Haliaetus leucogaster*) was found to have brought a portion of cuttle-fish from the sea to the garden jungle, a distance of nearly four miles, presumably to feed its young (p. 73).
- The black-and-white robin (*Copsychus saularis*) nests in May with two eggs, bluish-grey with dark red blotches concentrated at the broad end; the Bulbul (*Pycnonotus analis*) nests in March with two eggs thickly spotted dark red (pp. 83–84).
- The sunbird (*Anthothreptes malaccensis*) constructs a pear-shaped hanging nest approximately six inches long, with a lateral entrance hole and a small eave to shed rain, containing three or four small, long-oval, light-grey eggs (p. 86).
- The Bustard Quail (*Turnix plumbipes*) lays four conical, olive-brown eggs directly on the ground among long grass or sugar cane, point to point like a plover (p. 88).
- A pair of Goose-Teal (*Nettopus coromandelianus*) appeared on the garden lakes in January 1898 and remained for several weeks, a species noted as common in India but by no means so in the Malay Peninsula (p. 89).

### Conclusion

Ridley's definitive takeaway is that Singapore's birdlife is far more abundant and diverse than its residents generally appreciate, and that the Botanic Gardens, with their mosaic of habitat types, constitute an ideal vantage point for observing this diversity. The article serves as both a corrective to popular misconception and a practical guide for the attentive observer, implicitly arguing that the value of the gardens extends well beyond their botanical collections to encompass a rich and accessible natural history.

## Context

- Ridley wrote as the long-serving Superintendent of the Singapore Botanic Gardens (a position he held from 1874 to 1907), and the article reflects his dual identity as colonial administrator and naturalist; the gardens are described as his domestic environment ("Living in the Botanic Gardens… I get an opportunity of seeing them when all is quiet," p. 73).
- The piece draws entirely on Ridley's personal observations over many years of residence, with no reference to external literature beyond a single citation to Davison's earlier note on the Goose-Teal in the *Ibis* (p. 89). It represents the tradition of colonial natural history writing in which the resident scientist documents local fauna for both practical and scholarly audiences.
