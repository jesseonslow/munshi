# Charles Brook

Charles Brooke (1829–1917) was the second White Rajah of Sarawak, reigning from 1868 until his death in 1917, and the figure who transformed the ad hoc pragmatism of his uncle James Brooke's founding rule into a fully developed system of administration that consolidated Sarawak's distant territories into a coherent state [[1, p. 95]](#ref-1). His reign, spanning nearly five decades, was characterised by a distinctive blend of paternalistic personal rule, dependence on native authority structures, and a small cadre of European officers who governed a vast riverine territory in north-west Borneo [[1, pp. 95–96]](#ref-1). Brooke's administrative philosophy, articulated in his own writings and evidenced in the machinery of government he built, rested on the conviction that successful colonial rule depended not on institutions but on the character of individual European officers and the maintenance of traditional native leadership [[1, pp. 96–97]](#ref-1). In his later years, from his English estate at Chesterton Lodge in Gloucestershire, he continued to direct Sarawak's affairs through correspondence with his Treasurer, while pursuing an ambitious but ultimately unsuccessful campaign to absorb the remaining territories of the Sultanate of Brunei [[2, pp. 4]](#ref-2).

### Administrative Philosophy and the Native Principle

Charles Brooke had definite ideas about how native states should be governed by the time he assumed the Raj in 1868, having served as an out-station officer for over ten years from 1852 to 1863, much of it in the Iban areas where he was often the sole European authority in the region [[1, p. 95]](#ref-1). This experience led him to examine the various relationships between ruler and ruled and to reject both force and the gradual liquidation of the native population as foundations for colonial rule [[1, p. 95]](#ref-1). He distinguished between what he called the "native principle," which regarded the old condition of things established by custom and race as essentially natural, and the "European principle," which used the standard of Western civilisation as a guide and condemned whatever fell short of it [[1, p. 97]](#ref-1). Brooke rule was based on the native principle: it started with things as it found them, weeded out bad practices while maintaining good ones, and developed its own institutions rather than importing ready-made ones from abroad [[1, pp. 97–98]](#ref-1).

This philosophy carried practical as well as humanitarian dimensions. Brooke argued that a successful colonial ruler had to know the language and customs of his subjects, show respect for their chiefs, and encourage their own institutions [[1, p. 96]](#ref-1). He believed that the native could not be permitted to lose pride in himself or his tribe, for if he did he would drift "as a straw before the wind," come to despise traditional mores, and ultimately require direct attention from European administration [[1, p. 97]](#ref-1). Education, so far as it involved improvement in agriculture and crafts, was to be brought about in the natural sequence of events as a consequence of mixing with other races, ensuring changes that were acceptable to the people and therefore beneficial [[1, p. 97]](#ref-1). Yet Brooke was also explicit about the limits of this approach: he confessed that it was hardest of all to judge impartially what had been done or was in progress in his "semi-barbarous country," governed in conjunction with the old native authorities by a knot of foreigners in advance of those they governed in knowledge and experience [[1, p. 98]](#ref-1).

The personal qualities of the individual European officer were, in Brooke's view, the decisive factor. He argued that the right men to deal with natives must be very carefully picked, and that such men should look upon a native as a reasonable creature and speak to him in the manner of an equal [[1, p. 96]](#ref-1). He went so far as to advocate that the colonial ruler be unmarried, since a European with a wife and family, moving in the vortex of his own civilisation, found it difficult to obtain any real knowledge of the native mind [[1, p. 96]](#ref-1). This argument led him to its logical conclusion: the creation of a mixed race of colonialists, in which an infusion of native blood would produce offspring with a constitution better able to endure the climate and an elasticity of mental and physical organisation capable of modification and enlightenment in their original clime [[1, p. 96]](#ref-1).

### Structure of Government and the Machinery of Rule

Kuching was the administrative centre of Sarawak, and activities were focused on the Rajah and ten to fourteen European officers [[1, p. 98]](#ref-1). Among these was a European Resident and his two European assistants, responsible for the administration of the First Division which surrounded Kuching. Government departments already existed by the time Charles Brooke became Rajah, their European directors making up the rest of the central administration; the principal ones were the Post Office, Police and Prisons, Treasury, Lands and Surveys, Public Works, Military, Naval and Medical [[1, p. 98]](#ref-1). Everything in Kuching was under the direct control of the Rajah, who visited most departments daily and toured barracks, steamers, engineer's workshops and any conveniently located public works [[1, p. 98]](#ref-1). His routine, as described by Runciman, saw him crossing the river at nine o'clock under a ceremonial but tattered yellow umbrella carried by a sergeant of the Sarawak Rangers, greeted at the Court-House by the Resident with four Datus, then visiting the Treasury and the Resident's Office before presiding over the judiciary [[1, p. 98]](#ref-1).

Three principal governing bodies operated in Sarawak during this period. The Supreme Council, founded by James Brooke in 1855 at the suggestion of Earl Grey, operated under an established rule that European members should never outnumber native ones [[1, p. 99]](#ref-1). Its main function was consultative, serving to add the weight of native opinion to acts of government; by the turn of the century it was considered the sole legislative authority in Sarawak [[1, p. 100]](#ref-1). The Council Negri, created by Charles Brooke in 1865, comprised thirty to fifty persons drawn from the Supreme Council, more important European and native officers, and traditional native leaders [[1, p. 100]](#ref-1). It met roughly every three years to bring together the various groups within the country and give them a sense of identity and participation in respect to the central government. Charles Brooke himself waited until the next Council Negri meeting in 1870 to be officially proclaimed Rajah following his uncle's death in 1868 [[1, p. 100]](#ref-1). The third body, the Committee of Administration, was created to manage Sarawak's affairs while the Rajah was absent; it often duplicated the membership of the Supreme Council with the exception of the Rajah, and its first utilisation was in 1873 when it governed the country for a period of eighteen months [[1, pp. 100–101]](#ref-1).

Theoretically by 1890 Sarawak was separated into four administrative divisions: the First Division encompassing the Sarawak and Sadong river systems; the Second, the Batang Lupar, Saribas and Kalaka; the Third, the Rejang, Oya, Muka, Bintulu and Matu; and the Fourth, the Baram and Trusan [[1, p. 101]](#ref-1). In practice, the terms Resident and residency were used very loosely in government documents, and the term "residency" referred merely to the more important district stations likely to have a European officer in charge [[1, p. 102]](#ref-1). The number of European officers was considerably smaller than later sources would suggest; Doering estimates approximately twenty-five to thirty in the entire Sarawak Civil Service in the 1880s, with a third based in Kuching [[1, p. 102]](#ref-1). At stations such as Betong, Pusa, Tuban, Kabong, Sarebas, Balleh and Kanowit, the senior officers and the whole junior staff were usually native [[1, p. 102]](#ref-1).

At the community level, the system of indirect rule operated through designated native officers. In coastal communities, usually Malay or Melanau, one or two influential elders were designated as *Tuah Kampongs* (village heads) by the Rajah on the recommendation of the District Officer; they settled minor disputes, collected taxes, and served as spokesmen for village interests [[1, pp. 104–105]](#ref-1). In the non-Malay up-river communities, more influential leaders were chosen as *Pengulus* (village chiefs), an office slightly more powerful than that of *Tuah Kampong*, responsible for one or more villages aside from his own, trying minor civil and criminal cases, collecting the door tax, and raising and leading military forces when needed [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1). The system was highly effective among the Kenyahs and Kayans but less so among the Ibans, where the tradition of village leadership was not as strong [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1). The Chinese communities were governed through the *Kang Chew* or Captain China, who controlled gambling, pawn-broking and some of the more profitable monopolies within strictly regulated and geographically circumscribed settlements [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1). The opium farm was at one point the government's greatest single source of revenue and remained very important in the 1880s [[1, p. 105]](#ref-1).

### The Rajah in Exile: Governance from England and the Succession Crisis

From 1902 until his death in 1917, Charles Brooke resided at Chesterton Lodge in Gloucestershire, conducting much of Sarawak's detailed governance remotely through his Treasurer, F. H. Dallas, who served from 1903 until his retirement in 1917 [[2, pp. 4]](#ref-2). The Rajah wrote to Dallas two or three times a week, and Dallas in turn provided a steady stream of intelligence from Kuching, making the exchange a de facto channel of governance during the Rajah's prolonged absences [[2, p. 4]](#ref-2). In January 1906, Brooke explicitly instructed Dallas that "if the rules that you have from me from time to time are not carried out, you are responsible, whatever order the Rajah Muda may give you," a directive that underscored the tension between the Rajah's authority and that of his son, the Rajah Muda [[2, p. 5]](#ref-2).

The correspondence documents Brooke's single-minded campaign to absorb the remaining territories of the Sultanate of Brunei and, ultimately, British North Borneo itself [[2, p. 16]](#ref-2). His acquisition of the Lawas district from the British North Borneo Company in December 1904 was explicitly framed as "a stepping stone to get Brunei" [[2, p. 16]](#ref-2). He regarded Brunei as a "garden of weeds" that needed to be "swept away" to protect the surrounding districts he had already acquired, including Trusan (1885) and Limbang (1890) [[2, p. 16]](#ref-2). Despite energetic lobbying through the press and politicians, the British government negotiated a supplementary treaty with the Sultan in December 1905 providing for a Resident, effectively closing the door on Brooke's ambitions [[2, p. 16]](#ref-2). His interest then shifted to acquiring British North Borneo from the Chartered Company, a bid that also met with official resistance [[2, pp. 16–17]](#ref-2).

Equally prominent in the letters is the domestic drama surrounding the succession. Charles had little confidence in his eldest son Vyner, whose "lugubrious" correspondence and refusal to lead a punitive expedition in 1908 provoked sharp criticism [[2, pp. 5–6]](#ref-2). Vyner's marriage to Sylvia Brett in February 1911 compounded the Rajah's fears, and by 1912 the family had fractured to the point where Charles issued a proclamation recognising his second son Bertram as "part of the Government of the State" [[2, p. 22]](#ref-2). In response, Charles established a Sarawak Advisory Council in London and a Committee of Administration in Kuching, both designed to constrain Vyner's authority after his death [[2, pp. 22–23]](#ref-2). His political views, as revealed in the correspondence, were distinctly anti-imperialist and Liberal: he deplored British treatment of Indians in South Africa, supported Irish Home Rule, and declined to attend a Straits Association dinner in London because he "should hate" hearing "something resounding about the wonderful extent of the glorious Empire" [[2, pp. 9–10]](#ref-2). A hunting accident in November 1911 cost him the sight of his left eye, ending his daily participation in the Gloucestershire Hunt, which he described as "over for ever" [[2, pp. 8–9]](#ref-2). His final letter to Dallas, written from the Hyde Park Hotel in London in January 1917, expressed the conviction that "there are only two courses open—to give it up entirely or to continue to hold it entirely," choosing the former and intending to return to Sarawak [[2, p. 24]](#ref-2).

## Research and Documentation

The historiography of Charles Brooke's reign has shifted markedly over time. Earlier accounts, including the work of Baring-Gould and Bampfylde and the descriptions of Hose and McDougall, tended to present a simplified three-tiered model of Brooke governance: native community administration at the bottom, the District Officer and divisional administration in the middle, and the Rajah at the top [[1, p. 106]](#ref-1). Doering's 1966 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* challenged this model by drawing on outstation-level evidence from the *Sarawak Gazette* (Volumes X–XIII, 1880–1883) to demonstrate that control from the centre varied proportionally with the number of days it took to communicate with Kuching, and that district administration was a matter of compromise and expediency rather than orderly hierarchical command [[1, pp. 106–107]](#ref-1). Doering also noted that the formal descriptions of community officers given by Hose and McDougall must be put in context, as the Brooke regime, secure in the belief that it was adapting itself to local traditions, was in fact entering into a compromise and encouraging the evolution of a community official with new requirements and responsibilities [[1, pp. 105–106]](#ref-1).

Reece's 2008 article, drawing on the previously underutilised Dallas correspondence held in thirteen chronologically ordered files at The National Archives, Kew (PRO 30/79/1–13), opened a new window onto the final fifteen years of Brooke's life [[2, p. 4]](#ref-2). Reece noted that the vast store of Brooke records in Rhodes House Library, Oxford, had not been used in prior biographical studies, and that the Oxford University Colonial Records Project, which began depositing Sarawak records at the Bodleian from the 1960s, was itself prompted by the Rajah's expressed interest in the idea [[2, pp. 2]](#ref-2). The correspondence reveals a dimension of Brooke's rule—remote governance through a single trusted intermediary, territorial ambition, and family conflict—that earlier administrative histories had not adequately captured. Together, these two studies represent a movement from the romantic founding narrative of James Brooke toward a more nuanced understanding of the administrative mechanics, personal dynamics, and structural limitations that defined the second White Rajah's long reign.
