# The Uses of Magic: Local Knowledge and the 'Unscientific Native' in Colonial Malaya

## Abstract

Colonial violence cannot simply be studied by looking at the instances of wars and battles fought in the age of Empire. Equally important is the working of epistemic violence and the violence that accompanies the process of learning about, framing and categorizing the colonized Other. This paper looks at one aspect of colonial Othering in particular, which is the manner in which colonial functionaries and scholars turned their attention to the local knowledge/s of those who came under colonial rule, and how in the course of collecting, codifying and categorizing these knowledges native texts, histories and narratives were systematically devalued—as ‘mythologies’, ‘legends’, or fairy-tales—and in due course relegated to a secondary register as ‘non-knowledge’ that could not be instrumentalised to serve the needs of racialized colonial-capitalism. Focusing in particular on the works of Walter William Skeat with those of his co-authors Charles Otto Blagden and A. Hillman—officials who were embedded in the machinery of British rule in Malaya—this paper will look at how their study of Malay customs and beliefs was based on Western/Eurocentric understandings of (rational, instrumental) knowledge that in turn downgraded other non-Western epistemologies and belief-systems; and by doing so contributed to the notion of the lazy/backward/unscientific native Other.

## Summary

Farish A. Noor's article interrogates the epistemic violence embedded in the colonial knowledge-production apparatus of British Malaya, focusing on how the works of W. W. Skeat, C. O. Blagden, and A. Hillman constructed and reinforced the stereotype of the "unscientific native." The central argument is that colonialism was never a consultative dialogue between knowledge-systems but an epistemically unequal process in which non-Western belief-systems were deliberately downgraded as "magic," "myths," or "legends" and relegated to a secondary register of "non-knowledge" that could not be instrumentalised to serve racialised colonial-capitalism. Noor traces how an earlier colonial trope of the Malay as inherently piratical and lawless gave way, in the later period of British rule, to a new trope of the superstitious agrarian Malay trapped by custom and tradition—a shift that was itself indicative of the consolidation of British colonial power across the peninsula.

The article draws on two primary texts to make its case: Skeat's *Malay Magic* (1900) and the later *Salam the Mouse-Deer* (1938), co-authored with A. Hillman. In *Malay Magic*, Noor identifies the foundational binary of the scientific European versus the unscientific Asiatic, showing how Skeat's framing of the Malay *pawang* as a "legitimate representative of the primitive medicine-man" and his characterisation of Malay prayer-rituals as "still at an unethical stage" (quoting Tylor) established a template for reading all Malay cultural praxis through the lens of primitivism. In *Salam the Mouse-Deer*, Noor demonstrates how the idyllic, ahistorical setting and the figure of the "fast-disappearing" old Malay pirate storyteller activated the nineteenth-century trope of native auto-genocide, coddling the realities of racialised colonial-capitalism with the warm blanket of harmless native innocence.

Noor further argues that Skeat's data-gathering was inseparable from object-framing: because the foundational premise was that the Malay mind was fundamentally unscientific, everything the Malays did—from poetic descriptions of female beauty to tin prospecting—was touched by elements of the occult. The article thus positions these colonial ethnographic works not as neutral scholarship but as instruments of knowledge-power that facilitated governance by rendering the colonised Other legible, manageable, and permanently subordinate.

### Key Findings

- Skeat's *Malay Magic* (1900) was dedicated to Sir Cecil Clementi Smith, the 13th Governor of the Straits Settlements, and its preface by Blagden explicitly justified the study of Malay beliefs as a tool for governance, arguing that "an understanding of the ideas and modes of thought of an alien people in a relatively low stage of civilisation facilitates very considerably the task of governing them" (p. 9).
- Skeat's list of "chief authorities" on page 675 of *Malay Magic* consisted almost entirely of fellow Englishmen—Marsden, Leyden, Crawfurd, Maxwell, Swettenham, Clifford—revealing a self-referential colonial knowledge circuit in which Malay sources were mediated exclusively through British intermediaries (pp. 9–10).
- In *Salam the Mouse-Deer* (1938), Skeat and Hillman described the setting as "a race of leisurely-mannered, smiling, courteous men" cultivating "small-holdings of coffee, or, when coffee failed, of rubber or rice-fields and fruit-orchards but an acre or two in extent," presenting an idyllic picture that deliberately obscured the political upheavals of the period, including the founding of the Malayan Communist Party in 1930 and the 1933 immigration restriction ordinance (pp. 5–6).
- Skeat acknowledged that the Malay *pawang* could extract "a hundred or perhaps two hundred dollars" from Chinese tin-mining *towkay* for prospecting advice, yet immediately framed this as evidence of Malay superstition rather than local geological knowledge, contrasting the Malay "nose for tin" with the European reliance on "boring tools" (p. 18).
- The article identifies a clear genealogical shift in colonial Malay stereotypes: from the "piratical" Malay of the early colonial period (associated with lawlessness and violence) to the "superstitious agrarian" Malay of the later period (associated with backwardness and passivity), a transition that Noor links to the spread and development of British colonial power across the peninsula (p. 3).
- Skeat's *Malay Magic* was reprinted by the MBRAS in 2005 with an introduction by J. M. Gullick and an appendix of Skeat's later notes, making it available as an Open Access publication on JSTOR (p. 97, n. 5).

### Conclusion

Noor's definitive historical takeaway is that the colonial ethnographic project in Malaya was never a neutral exercise in cultural documentation but a form of epistemic violence that produced the "unscientific native" as a governing category. By systematically devaluing Malay knowledge-systems as magic, folklore, or superstition, colonial scholars like Skeat did not merely describe the colonised Other—they actively constructed the very conditions of colonial domination, rendering Malay epistemologies illegible as knowledge and thereby justifying their exclusion from the instrumental logic of racialised colonial-capitalism.

## Context

- The article draws primarily on the published works of Skeat, Blagden, and Hillman (*Malay Magic*, 1900; *Pagan Races of the Malay Peninsula*, 1906; *Salam the Mouse-Deer*, 1938) as its primary textual corpus, supplemented by secondary historiographical works by Cohn, Dirks, Richards, Brantlinger, and Winzeler.
- The study contributes to the historiography of colonial knowledge-production in Southeast Asia by extending the analytical framework of epistemic violence (drawn from Dirks, Cohn, and Richards) to the specific case of Malay ethnography, and by demonstrating how the devaluation of local knowledge served the material interests of colonial-capitalism rather than mere academic curiosity.
