# Special Thai-Malaysian relations

Kobkua Suwannathat-Pian's 2002 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* argues that Thai-Malaysian relations constitute a uniquely "special" bilateral relationship in Southeast Asia, sustained across more than six centuries by geopolitical necessity, intertwined socio-cultural development, political tradition, and personal human ties that transcend the formal diplomatic and colonial ruptures of the modern era (p. 1).

## Summary

Suwannathat-Pian divides the relationship into three phases: the traditional *bunga mas, bunga perak* period (fourteenth to nineteenth centuries), the colonial or semi-modern phase (1902–1909), and the post-independence modern phase. The traditional phase was characterised by a tributary system in which Siam, as the dominant mainland power, exercised suzerainty over the Malay sultanates through a combination of diplomatic ritual and military coercion. The system was fluid, with no fixed written rules, and its terms shifted according to the relative strength of the suzerain (pp. 2–5). A defining feature of this period was the absence of any fixed boundary concept among the indigenous populations; the peoples of the upper and lower Peninsula shared similar historical and socio-cultural experiences, with differentiation based on village affiliation rather than ethnicity (pp. 2–3).

The colonial phase was shaped by the tension between Britain's "forward policy" in the Straits Settlements and its official buffer policy towards Siam. British colonial officers, particularly Sir Frank Swettenham, systematically undermined Siamese suzerainty by portraying the tributary system as archaic and by offering Malay rulers the alternative of British protection. Siam's response, under Kings Mongkut and Chulalongkorn, was to intensify personal and cultural ties with the Malay ruling elite, a strategy that proved remarkably effective in states like Kedah and Kelantan but ultimately could not withstand the political weight of the forward party in London (pp. 6–13). The 1909 cession of the four northern Malay states to Britain marked the formal end of direct Thai-Malay political relations, creating a diplomatic interregnum of nearly five decades (p. 4).

The post-independence phase was catalysed by the personal connections of Tunku Abdul Rahman, whose blood ties, education in Bangkok, and genuine affection for Thailand enabled him to rekindle the dormant special relationship during his 1956 official visit. This personal diplomacy proved decisive in resolving security concerns along the border, securing Thai cooperation against the Malayan Communist Party, and facilitating Thailand's role as mediator during the Indonesia-Malaysia Confrontation of 1963–66 (pp. 14–20).

### Key Findings

- The *bunga mas, bunga perak* tributary system was not a uniquely Siamese invention but a common diplomatic practice among dominant power centres in traditional Southeast Asia, and it carried no socio-political stigma because it was understood as an impermanent reflection of relative power (p. 5).
- In 1896, the Raja of Kelantan explicitly confirmed to the British Governor of the Straits Settlements that he was "an officer of Siam," yet a 1902 British Foreign Office despatch dismissed Siamese authority over Kelantan as "a mere formality," revealing the gap between British official rhetoric and the reality of tributary relations (p. 6).
- King Chulalongkorn's 1903 statement that "the Malays, like other communities, hate foreign interference" directly rebutted the British claim that Malay rulers preferred British to Siamese rule, and the subsequent rejection of British protection by Sultan Muhammad IV of Kelantan—after he had earlier sought it—confirmed that Malay rulers preferred traditional tributary ties to the rigid legalism of the Advisory system (pp. 10, 12).
- Tunku Abdul Rahman's 1956 visit to Bangkok was so successful that the British Ambassador Sir Berkeley Gage reported it was "entirely to the Chief Minister's credit" and that "both Malaya and Thailand owe thanks to him," with the visit culminating in a royal luncheon at Hua Hin—an honour not normally accorded to foreign heads of state (pp. 17–18).
- Thailand's mediation during the Malaysia Confrontation was underpinned by the Association of Southeast Asian States (ASA, 1960), which gave Bangkok diplomatic standing with the Philippines, and by Foreign Minister Thanat Khoman's personal credibility with all three parties involved in the dispute (pp. 19–20).
- The 1923 Kedah Treaty, negotiated on the basis of Kedah's traditional rights and privileges during its tributary period with Siam, served as a legal foundation that allowed the Kedah ruling elite to resist the more intrusive aspects of British colonial rule and preserve Malay socio-cultural sovereignty (p. 15).

### Conclusion

Suwannathat-Pian's definitive takeaway is that the "special" character of Thai-Malaysian relations lies not in any single institutional arrangement but in the cumulative weight of personal, familial, and socio-cultural bonds that have persisted through every political rupture—from the fall of Melaka, through the British colonial interregnum, to the Cold War-era Confrontation. These bonds, she argues, have consistently proven stronger than formal diplomatic structures, enabling the relationship to survive and even flourish in periods when official channels were severed or strained (pp. 1, 14).

## Context

- The article is an adapted version of a talk presented to MBRAS members in June 2001, and it draws heavily on the author's earlier monograph *Thai-Malay Relations: Traditional Intra-regional Relations from the Seventeenth to the Early Twentieth Centuries* (Oxford University Press, 1988) (p. 1, n. 1).
- The piece engages directly with British colonial archival sources (FO, CO series) held at The National Archives, Kew, and Siamese royal documents, positioning itself within the historiographical debate over the nature and legitimacy of the *bunga mas* system as interpreted by both British colonial officials and Malay rulers (pp. 5–6).
