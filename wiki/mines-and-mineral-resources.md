# Mines and mineral resources

The mineral resources of the Malay Peninsula and Borneo have been exploited for centuries, with tin, gold, antimony, iron, cinnabar, and coal forming the backbone of regional economies from the pre-colonial era through the modern petroleum age. The geological basis for this wealth lies in the Mesozoic granite intrusions that form the central spine of the Peninsula, whose weathering has produced extensive alluvial deposits of cassiterite and gold on either side of the range [[1, pp. 5–6]](#ref-1). In Borneo, the mineralisation is associated with igneous intrusions piercing ancient limestone and sedimentary sequences, yielding antimony, cinnabar, and coal [[2, p. 14]](#ref-2). The economic significance of these deposits has been profound: tin mining sustained the revenue of the Straits Settlements and the Malay States for over a century, iron ore from Johore supplied a substantial share of Japan's steel industry in the 1920s, and the discovery of offshore petroleum in the 1970s gave rise to a national corporation that became a symbol of Malaysian sovereignty [[3, p. 303]](#ref-3), [[1, pp. 20–22]](#ref-1), [[4, p. 2]](#ref-4).

### Geological Foundations and the Distribution of Mineral Wealth

The earliest systematic attempt to explain the distribution of the Peninsula's mineral deposits was made by D. D. Daly in 1878, who proposed that all the gold and tin alluvial deposits were detrital products of a single primary lode running along the central mountain spine from Cape Patani in the north to the Kesang River in the south [[5, pp. 194–198]](#ref-5). Daly, a British official conducting boundary surveys between Perak and Siamese territory, traced a line of mineral occurrences from the gold workings of Tanjong Tohor in the south, through the tin deposits of Perak and the gold fields of Kelantan, to the terminus at Cape Patani, where coal had been found in the Isthmus of Kra [[5, pp. 195–197]](#ref-5). He identified the alluvial tin deposit on the western side of the range as extending approximately 250 miles in length and ranging from one to twelve miles in width, winding north-west to Tongkah and into British Burmah [[5, p. 196]](#ref-5). His methodological template was Sir Roderick Murchison's prediction of the Australian gold fields from stratal analysis, and he argued that boring through the quartz veins piercing the granite would uncover the original lode [[5, p. 197]](#ref-5).

E. S. Willbourn's 1926 geological survey of Johore provided a more refined structural interpretation, demonstrating that the State's haphazard mountain groups were the eroded remnants of Mesozoic granite intrusions along anticlinal axes that never filled their full structural positions, leaving scattered bosses rather than continuous ranges [[1, pp. 5–6]](#ref-1). Willbourn also revised the age of the volcanic rocks of the east coast and China Sea islands, demonstrating from heat-metamorphosed volcanic rocks on Pulau Tioman and Palaeozoic granite fragments in tuff on Pulau Nanas that these formations were pre-granite in age rather than Tertiary, and reclassified them into the Pahang Volcanic Series [[1, pp. 8–12]](#ref-1).

In Borneo, A. H. Everett's 1878 survey of Sarawak established that the mineralisation was associated with intrusive igneous rocks—porphyrite, basalt, and greenstones—piercing an ancient blue limestone overlain by sandstones, conglomerates, and clay-shales [[2, p. 14]](#ref-2). Everett argued that the antimony, arsenic, and cinnabar deposits occupied fissures caused by volcanic eruption and were deposited after the cessation of volcanic action, yielding a remarkably recent date for the mineral lodes [[2, p. 14]](#ref-2). L. C. Isnard, a French civil mining engineer, contributed a brief but significant note in 1890 tracing a single gold-bearing geological uplift from the Malacca colony through Gemencheh, the Muar and Serting river valleys, and into Pahang, arguing that the Peninsula's gold deposits spanned three states rather than being confined to one [[6, p. 168]](#ref-6).

### Mining Practices, Labour, and Customary Regulation

The social and technical dimensions of mining were documented most thoroughly by A. Hale, Inspector of Mines for Kinta, in his 1885 ethnographic survey of Malay tin-mining practices in the Kinta Valley, Perak [[3, pp. 303–320]](#ref-3). Hale established that the Kinta district contained nearly five hundred registered mines, of which approximately three hundred and fifty were private Malay *kelian* (private mines), while only three were operated by European companies [[3, p. 303]](#ref-3). The remainder were new concessions granted to Malays and Chinese alike. Physical evidence of pre-Muhammadan mining included at least fifty deep pits on Lahat Hill, averaging eight feet in diameter and twenty feet deep, and a single large pit of fifty feet diameter in virgin forest, identified by local informants as a Siamese mine [[3, p. 304]](#ref-3).

Hale's central contribution was his documentation of the *pawang* (medicine-man), who functioned as both spiritual intermediary and quasi-judicial authority on Malay mines [[3, pp. 305–309]](#ref-3). The *pawang* enforced a *bahasa pantang*—a taboo vocabulary in which ordinary words for animals, objects, and even tin itself were replaced by euphemisms to avoid offending the *hantu* (spirits) believed to inhabit the mine [[3, p. 305]](#ref-3). He recorded seventeen specific rules, each carrying a fine most commonly of $12.50, which Hale noted was exactly half the maximum fine a Malay chief could impose for minor offences under customary law, and also the customary dowry for marriage to a slave or to the widow or divorced wife of a *ra'iyat* (subject) [[3, p. 309]](#ref-3). The *pawang*'s professional duties included erecting the *genggulang* altar at a mine's opening, invoking tutelary spirits, and adjudicating disputes among miners [[3, p. 306]](#ref-3).

The economic structure of Malay mining was governed by customary duties that reflected the risk and capital intensity of different methods. The *hasil kelian* (mine-owner's duty) was one-sixth of output for excavations (*tebok* and *ludang*) but one-third for hill mining (*leris*), reflecting the greater capital and risk of hill work [[3, p. 314]](#ref-3). The customary furnace rent (*chukei senduk*) was two *kati* of tin per *karong* of tin sand smelted when the furnace owner was also the mine owner, and one *kati* per *karong* when they were different persons [[3, p. 313]](#ref-3). Hale compiled an extensive technical vocabulary of Malay mining terminology, covering stratigraphy, implements, and processes from hill mining to smelting, explicitly stating that the language was "so susceptible of change" that without a record the terms "may, perhaps as the method of working alter, be entirely lost" [[3, pp. 315–320]](#ref-3).

In Johore, Willbourn reported that tin mining was entirely small-scale: the largest operation, at Bukit Pelali, produced about two hundred *pikuls* per month from workings in a valley half a mile long, while Ulu Madek's gravel-pump mine peaked at one hundred *pikuls* per month with two hundred coolies before ceasing work by 1927 [[1, pp. 18–19]](#ref-1). Pulau Aur, the most easterly island in British Malaya, yielded cassiterite in beach sand assaying 0.6 per cent metallic tin, but the deposits were too small and low-grade to be workable [[1, p. 19]](#ref-1).

In Sarawak, antimony was the staple mineral export, with output peaking at 1,788 tons valued at $86,926 in 1872 [[2, pp. 20–22]](#ref-2). The principal workings were at Busan, Jambusan, Piat, and Bidi, though the most accessible deposits were becoming exhausted by the late 1870s [[2, pp. 20–23]](#ref-2). Gold prices varied by locality—Marup gold fetched $32–$34 per *bongkal*, Paku $28, and Sadong $26, the difference reflecting silver content in natural alloy [[2, p. 17]](#ref-2).

### Commercial Concessions, State Sovereignty, and the Petroleum Era

The commercial exploitation of mineral resources was inseparable from questions of sovereignty, concession, and imperial rivalry. The Duff Syndicate affair in Kelantan, 1900–1902, exemplified the tensions between private commercial enterprise and the competing imperatives of British and Siamese authority. R. W. Duff, a former Superintendent of Police in Pahang, assembled a syndicate of major London and Singapore houses with an initial registered capital of £10,000 and secured a Partnership Deed from the Raja of Kelantan on 10 October 1900, granting a forty-year concession over the area between the Galas and Lebir rivers—later estimated at approximately 3,000 square miles—with an initial payment of $20,000, a 4 per cent royalty on mineral receipts, and a 5 per cent export duty [[7, pp. 17]](#ref-7). The critical sticking point in the subsequent negotiations with the Siamese Government was the right of Siam to confirm or veto sub-leases and transfers; the Syndicate's directors declared that "English financial men would refuse to consider a business proposition which was based on a title dependent on Siam for confirmation" [[7, p. 13]](#ref-7). The impasse was resolved only by a "Simple Ratification" at Bangkok on 10 July 1902 [[7, p. 27]](#ref-7).

In Sarawak, the Borneo Company held a monopoly over all minerals except coal, gold, and precious stones, yet had never instituted systematic prospecting beyond Sarawak Proper—a failure Everett criticised as counterproductive to the territory's development [[2, p. 28]](#ref-2). The coal fields of the northwest coast represented the most promising future asset: the Lingga seams in the Batang Lupar district had been re-opened by the Government and were supplying fair steam coal, with Dr. Stenhouse's analyses showing the coal to be nearly identical in carbon and hydrogen content to the Hartley-Newcastle coals [[2, pp. 26–27]](#ref-2).

The transition from mineral to petroleum resources marked a decisive shift in the relationship between the state and extractive industry. Shakila Yacob's 2021 study of PETRONAS demonstrates how Malaysia's national oil company, incorporated on 17 August 1974 under the Petroleum Development Act with a paid-up capital of MYR 10 million, transformed into the first Malaysian firm to join the Fortune 500 [[4, p. 2]](#ref-4). The central strategic innovation was the Production Sharing Contract, which replaced the older concession system under which foreign companies retained 80 per cent of crude production; the PSC reversed this ratio in favour of the host government [[4, p. 10]](#ref-4). Shell signed its production sharing agreement on 30 November 1976 at a 70:30 split in PETRONAS' favour, and Exxon signed on 8 December 1976 at an after-tax split of 83.5:16.5 [[4, pp. 13–15]](#ref-4). By 31 March 2020, PETRONAS' assets were valued at MYR 630 billion [[4, p. 16]](#ref-4).

The iron ore industry in Johore represented a significant industrial asset in the interwar period. The Bukit Medan haematite mine, a single isolated hill of haematite and kaolin-like clay, was producing 30,000 tons monthly at 64 per cent iron by the mid-1920s, with total output rising from 18,536 tons in 1921 to 231,000 tons in 1926 and an estimated 350,000 tons for 1927 [[1, pp. 20–22]](#ref-1). This output represented approximately 40 per cent of Japan's total iron and steel consumption [[1, pp. 20–22]](#ref-1). Willbourn's provisional theory—that the ore formed by hydrothermal alteration of a rhyolite plug, with silica removed and haematite deposited—remained unconfirmed but suggested potentially large underground reserves [[1, pp. 24–26]](#ref-1).

## Research and Documentation

The Society's literature on mines and mineral resources reveals a clear evolution in disciplinary focus and methodological approach across its history. The earliest contributions, from the 1870s and 1880s, were characterised by a combination of geological speculation and ethnographic observation. Daly's 1878 paper was explicitly a theoretical exercise in prospecting geology, drawing on Murchison's Australian precedent to propose a unifying lode structure for the Peninsula [[5, p. 197]](#ref-5). Everett's 1878 survey of Sarawak was a systematic enumeration of mineral occurrences by district, grounded in direct field experience as Resident of Bintulu, and included chemical analyses and economic assessments [[2, pp. 14–29]](#ref-2). Hale's 1885 article occupied a distinctive middle ground between technical geology and social anthropology, its primary purpose being the preservation of a technical vocabulary and customary legal framework that he judged at risk of extinction [[3, pp. 315–320]](#ref-3).

The interwar period saw a shift toward rigorous geological survey and economic assessment. Willbourn's 1926 study of Johore, synthesising five years of fieldwork by himself, G. R. Fulton, and H. E. Savage, represented the first comprehensive geological account of the State and was explicitly a product of the Government Geological Survey Department [[1, pp. 5]](#ref-1). The emphasis was on structural interpretation, resource quantification, and the identification of untested targets—such as the high-level alluvium, which Willbourn recommended for deep boring by analogy with the Kinta Valley [[1, pp. 14–15]](#ref-1).

The post-war and contemporary literature reflects the maturation of the extractive industries and the political economy of resource sovereignty. Robert's 1972 study of the Duff Syndicate drew on Colonial Office and Foreign Office correspondence to illuminate the interaction between private commercial enterprise and imperial diplomacy [[7, pp. 81–110]](#ref-7). Yacob's 2021 article on PETRONAS, drawing on US diplomatic cables, British and Australian government records, and semi-structured interviews with key political figures, addressed a historiographical gap she identified in the existing literature, which had been limited to a single doctoral thesis and a handful of anecdotal corporate histories [[4, pp. 3–4]](#ref-4). The trajectory from Daly's speculative lode theory to Yacob's analysis of production sharing contracts encapsulates the broader transformation of the region's mineral economy from itinerant alluvial mining to state-directed industrial extraction.
