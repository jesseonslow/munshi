# Selangor, 1876–82: the Bloomfield Douglas diary

J.M. Gullick's 1975 article presents selections from the newly discovered private diary of W. Bloomfield Douglas, who served as Resident of Selangor from 1876 to 1882, offering substantial additional detail on the political and economic history of the state in its formative years under British protectorate. Set against the backdrop of post-civil war reconstruction, the diary illuminates the complex distribution of power among Selangor's Malay leadership and challenges the conventional Swettenham-derived image of Sultan Abdul Samad as a passive, indifferent ruler.

## Summary

Gullick's central argument is that Sultan Abdul Samad, far from being the *roi faineant* depicted in Swettenham's writings, remained an alert and well-informed ruler who actively engaged with the affairs of his state throughout his long reign (pp. 1–2, 7–8). The diary records the Sultan's interest in revenue matters, succession, boundary disputes, state debts, judicial decisions, the grant of *kuasa* to local heads, agricultural development, and religious affairs (p. 8). Douglas, a strong-willed administrator, continued to consult the Sultan on an immense variety of matters, and the old man's characteristic reply of "Benar, benar" masked a capacity for pointed commentary that Swettenham's account obscured (pp. 8–9). Gullick argues that the Sultan was "a professional survivor" who "came to terms with the shifting realities of power over his long reign" and who "repays serious study" (p. 7).

The diary also reveals the precarious position of Tunku Kudin, the Viceroy of Selangor from 1868 to 1878. Kudin was a paradox: the victor of a bitter civil war who lacked military talent, and an unusually westernised Malay who nonetheless required a sizeable following as the recognised mark of status (pp. 19–20). His relations with the Selangor royal family were strained, his wife Tunku Arfah was embittered, and he stood "astride the gap between two worlds in Selangor without having a firm footing in each" (p. 20). His decision to retire in 1878 was driven less by Douglas's occasional incivility than by the fact that Douglas's direct dealing with the Sultan had rendered his position as Viceroy redundant (pp. 20–21).

The most dramatic episode documented is the Tunku Panglima Raja affair of April–May 1878, in which Douglas arrested the Sultan's brother-in-law on charges of bribery and complicity in a petition to restore the exiled Raja Mahdi to power. The affair triggered a Colonial Office directive that "Residents have been placed in the Native States as advisers and not as rulers" (p. 31). Gullick notes the irony that Douglas had technically followed proper procedure by taking the matter to the State Council and obtaining the Sultan's approval, yet his superiors in Singapore judged his fault to be "taking the initiative so strongly against him in the mixed Council" (p. 31). The episode also exposed Douglas's underlying anxiety that the civil war might break out again, an anxiety that shaped his often heavy-handed dealings with the Selangor Rajas (p. 25).

### Key Findings

- The Sultan's 1879 visit to Kuala Lumpur was the first by any Selangor ruler to the interior in over a century; Panglima Garang, speaking on behalf of the interior Malays, stated that "it was more than a hundred years since any Sultan had visited Kuala Lumpur" and that many had "hardly credit[ed] the existence of such a Ruler" (p. 45).
- Douglas's July 1876 journey from Kuala Lumpur through Ulu Selangor to Ulu Bernam made him the first European to traverse that route on foot; he recorded population estimates of approximately 1,000 Chinese and 1,000 Malays at Ulu Selangor, with six mines in operation (pp. 40–44).
- The interior Malay communities were of mixed cultural origin—Pahang, Menangkabau, Rawa, and Bugis—creating governance difficulties, as Douglas observed that "a Selangor man has no chance whatever in making a complaint to a Menangkabau or Pahang Penghulu against their countrymen" (p. 48).
- Tunku Kudin's frank conversation with Douglas on 24 April 1877 revealed his dissatisfaction: he stated he "did not feel inclined to remain as Viceroy even with his present salary" and feared losing a rice farm in Kedah worth $20,000 a year if he remained in Selangor (p. 24).
- The diary documents the Sultan's active interest in agriculture, including being observed "in a paddy field up to his waist in mud and water surrounded by a lot of women and children sorting out padi plants for planting" (p. 14), and his willingness to provide capital to Malay settlers in accordance with Malay custom (p. 14).
- Douglas's enforced resignation in August 1882 resulted from a combination of factors: deductions from the Sultan's allowance for Western clothing and furnishings (contravening 1880 orders), the land auction scandal involving his son-in-law Daly, and allegations by James Innes (p. 15, n. 34).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive historical takeaway is that the Douglas diary corrects a significant distortion in the received narrative of early protectorate Selangor. The Sultan was not the irresponsible hedonist or opium addict of Swettenham's imagination but a shrewd, long-lived politician who exercised real influence within the constraints of a dispersed power structure. Simultaneously, the diary exposes the fundamental tension of the protectorate system: the Resident's need to assert effective control clashed with the advisory role prescribed by the 1878 directive, and the Malay aristocracy—cut off by old habits from the agricultural expansion that peace had made possible—remained largely unable to adapt to the new order for a generation (pp. 40–41).

## Context

- The original diary manuscript, comprising four volumes of approximately 150,000 words, is in the possession of Douglas's grandson, Mr. E. Douglas Potter; one or possibly two volumes appear to be missing between the extant volumes 3 and 4 (p. 48).
- Gullick prepared the article in the United Kingdom and was unable to consult the Selangor Secretariat files in Malaysia, a limitation acknowledged in the editor's note (p. 51).
