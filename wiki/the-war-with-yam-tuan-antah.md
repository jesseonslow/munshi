# The war with Yam Tuan Antah

J.M. Gullick's 1954 article reconstructs the 1875 war in Negri Sembilan, in which Yam Tuan Antah led a Malay resistance against British-backed authority in Sungei Ujong. Gullick argues that the conflict was not a simple civil war between rival Malay factions but a broader aristocratic reaction to British intervention, which had disrupted the internal political equilibrium of the Nine States federation. The war's outcome fragmented Negri Sembilan's political structure, yet paradoxically set the stage for its eventual re-unification under a new generation of rulers who adapted to the colonial order.

## Summary

The roots of the 1875 war lay in the disintegration of Negri Sembilan's confederate structure during the nineteenth century. The historic role of the Yam Tuan as overlord had been established in the eighteenth century to coordinate defence against Bugis attacks, but with the pax Britannica eliminating external threats, the chiefs no longer needed a common leader (pp. 1–2). The development of tin-mining from the 1830s introduced a new source of conflict, as chiefs competed for control of river toll revenues, particularly along the Linggi River (p. 2). When Dato' Klana Syed Abdul Rahman of Sungei Ujong secured a separate British protection treaty in 1874, he effectively challenged the Yam Tuan's paramountcy and the autonomy of the other districts. The chiefs, fearing that British-backed Sungei Ujong would dominate the State, rallied behind Tunku Antah, who was elected Yam Tuan in a meeting the Dato' Klana refused to attend (pp. 3–4).

The military campaign unfolded in three phases. The first was the skirmish at Terachi on 28 November 1875, when a survey party under Daly was ambushed and Lieutenant Hinxman's small force was driven back from a fortified Malay position across spiked padi fields (pp. 6–7). The second phase saw Antah's forces advance to Paroi and then to the Linggi River at Ampangan, within seven miles of Seremban, before being checked by Murray's use of a brass muzzle-loader gun on 5 December (pp. 8–10). The decisive phase began with Hinxman's recapture of Paroi on 7 December, supported by Fontaine's Arab irregulars and the brass gun, which inflicted 37 British and Arab casualties against approximately 35 Malay killed (pp. 15–16). The war ended on 22 December when Colonel Clay's column, reinforced by 350 Gurkhas, took the Bukit Putus pass in a combined frontal and flanking attack. Captain Channer of the 1st Gurkhas won the Victoria Cross for single-handedly storming a Malay stockade, while Murray's flanking march via the Langkap pass into the Terachi valley from the rear completed the encirclement and caused the Malay forces to collapse (pp. 17–18).

In the aftermath, Yam Tuan Antah was restored in 1877 but only as "Yam Tuan of Sri Menanti," a truncated title covering merely the four small districts adjacent to his capital, not the whole of Negri Sembilan (p. 19). His inability to govern in a despotic fashion, compounded by the loss of his former vassalage over the major districts, left him politically isolated. He died of smallpox in October 1887, and his funeral was conducted without the attendance of any chiefs (p. 22). His twelve-year-old son, Tunku Besar Mohamed, eventually achieved what his father could not: by 1898, through a combination of personal tact and the steady guidance of Resident Martin Lister, he had secured the re-unification of Negri Sembilan under the paramountcy of the Yam Tuan (pp. 22–23).

### Key Findings

- Yam Tuan Antah mobilised a force variously estimated at 800 to 4,000 men, with the lower figure considered more reliable; the minor chiefs of the Sri Menanti states gave full support while the greater chiefs of Jelebu, Johol, Rembau and Tampin would join only after an initial success (p. 8).
- The Malay forces at Paroi were armed chiefly with flintlock muskets firing tin bullets, though some Snider rifles were present; Murray later collected 36 iron and brass swivel guns and 348 muskets from Ulu Muar, Sri Menanti and Jempol after the war (pp. 15, 19).
- Captain Channer's Victoria Cross action at Bukit Putus on 22 December 1875 involved storming a stockade occupied by 25–30 Malays who were cooking; Channer and two Gurkhas each killed an assailant at the outset, and one Gurkha was killed in the ensuing melee (pp. 17–18).
- The British force at the critical moment of the Paroi action (7 December) comprised only 35 men of the 10th Regiment, 48 police, and 85 Arab irregulars, with 83 rounds of ammunition per man available to Hinxman's garrison at Rasah before reinforcements arrived (pp. 8, 15).
- Yam Tuan Antah's revenues in 1886 amounted to only $2,600, derived from opium and spirits duties, gambling and pawnbroker monopolies, a 10% tin export duty, and court fines; better collection arrangements raised this to over $8,000 in 1887 (p. 21).
- The Maharaja of Johore corresponded with the rebel leaders in all three States (Perak, Selangor, Negri Sembilan) but was not in direct communication with the Negri Sembilan rebels until after the war was over; his policy of identifying the conflict as a "Malay civil war" in which the British were on the wrong side was "unsound in conception" (pp. 4–5).

### Conclusion

Gullick's definitive historical takeaway is that the 1875 war represented the last significant assertion of the old Negri Sembilan confederate order against colonial encroachment, and that its failure was not merely a military defeat but a structural transformation. The war destroyed the Yam Tuan's role as overlord of the whole State and fragmented Negri Sembilan into separate entities under individual British protection treaties. Yet Gullick closes with a pointed contrast between father and son: Antah's intransigence and inability to adapt sealed his dynasty's temporary decline, while his son Mohamed's intelligence and self-denying tact achieved the re-unification of the State by 1898, demonstrating that the royal house could survive the colonial era only by identifying itself with the welfare and advancement of its subjects rather than with the old prerogatives of paramountcy (pp. 22–23).

## Context

- The article was published in the JMBRAS in 1954, drawing on official British reports (Murray, Hinxman, Channer, Anson, Clay) and the author's own knowledge of the Seremban–Kuala Pilah district, which he describes in the opening passage as a route he had personally travelled (p. 1).
- Gullick notes that the Langkap pass route, used by Murray for his decisive flanking march, had been largely forgotten by 1954 but was reportedly found useful by MPAJA forces during the Japanese occupation of 1942–5 (p. 18).
