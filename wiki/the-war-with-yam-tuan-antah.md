# The war with Yam Tuan Antah

Winstedt and de Josselin de Jong published this edition of a Malay *Undang-Undang* (customary law) text from Sungai Ujong in Negeri Sembilan in 1954, presenting it as a specimen of the region's adat rather than a critical edition. The text, drawn from two early twentieth-century manuscripts, documents the full corpus of customary law governing social hierarchy, criminal justice, evidence, and moral conduct in a Minangkabau-influenced community, framed by the legendary authority of three ancestral founders.

## Summary

This publication is a translated edition of a Malay customary law text originating from Sungai Ujong, in the state of Negeri Sembilan, Malaya. The editors worked from two manuscripts: a Jawi text (R.A.S. Maxwell 118) and a Romanized version (Maxwell 118A) produced in 1904 at Malacca. The edition is based primarily on the Romanized text, with the Jawi manuscript consulted where the former is corrupt or incomplete. The editors note that the language of the text is closely akin to Minangkabau in pronunciation, word-structure, grammar, and vocabulary, and they provide a brief linguistic introduction identifying characteristic features such as the use of *ba-* where standard Malay would employ *di-* or *ter-*, and a lexicon of terms seldom found in standard Malay (pp. 3–4).

The text itself is organized into 113 sections that collectively constitute a comprehensive code of customary law. It opens with the foundational principle that "every man is a king in his own place," establishing a functional hierarchy in which each social role—commoner, chief, councillor, captain, child, woman, religious leader, craftsman—holds sovereign authority within its proper domain (§ 2). The code then delineates six categories of legal authority: ancient custom (*chupak yang asali*), created custom (*chupak buatan*), inherited lore (*kata pusaka*), decisions of common accord (*kata muafakat*), ancient lore awaiting ratification, and decisions to be reached by later deliberation (§§ 6–12). The criminal law is structured around the "Law of the Eight Cases" (sedition, incest, robbery, theft) and the "Twelve Laws" (covering pursuit, wounds, arrest *in flagrante*, and related matters), with elaborate procedures for evidence, accusation, and incrimination (§§ 21–29). The text further prescribes specific fines for theft of various goods, defines the duties of village headmen, councillors, and judges, and devotes considerable space to moral philosophy, the qualities of rulers and subjects, and the legendary founding of the legal order by Datok Katumanggungan, Datok Parapateh Sabatang, and Datok Sri Maharaja (§§ 63–65).

The editors' introduction is deliberately modest, acknowledging that several passages remain unsolved and that the text is presented as a specimen rather than a fully annotated critical edition. The translation preserves the text's characteristic use of Malay *pantun* (quatrains) as mnemonic and rhetorical devices, and the editors flag corrupt or ambiguous passages with footnotes rather than attempting to resolve them.

### Key Findings

- The text is based on two manuscripts: R.A.S. Maxwell 118 (Jawi, written a few years before 1904) and Maxwell 118A (Romanized at Malacca in 1904); the edition follows the Romanized text, with the Jawi MS used to fill gaps (p. 3).
- The "Law of the Eight Cases" comprises: sedition and rebellion (*daga, dagi*), incest and fornication (*sumbang, salah*), robbery and violence (*samun, sakal*), and purloining and stealing (*maling, churi*) (§ 22, p. 13).
- Fines for theft are graded by the value and nature of the stolen property: stealing a buffalo carries a fine of 12½ *mas*, 5 *kupang*, 5 *busok*, 1 *kupang*, 1 *piak*, 4 *kenderi* plus return of the animal; stealing rice carries 1 *tael*, 1 *paha*, 12½ *mas*, 5 *kupang*, 5 *busok*, 1 *piak*, 4 *kenderi*; stealing a fowl carries 2½ *mas*, 5 *kupang*, 5 *busok*, 1 *kupang*, 1 *piak*, 4 *kenderi* (§§ 33–35, pp. 15–16).
- Standard measures are fixed as: 1 *chupak* = 12 *tael*, 1 *gantang* = 48 *bungkal*, 1 *bungkal* = 16 *mayam*, 1 *mas* = 4 *kupang*, 4 *kupang* = 4 *kenderi* (§ 63, p. 23; § 96, p. 33).
- The code enumerates 25 items of *chemo* (circumstantial) evidence and 25 "signs of guilt" (*tanda jahat*), the first six of which lead to formal accusation (*tudoh*) and the remainder to incrimination (*chemo*), with atonement determined by the judge (§§ 25–26, 38–39, pp. 13–14, 17).
- The legendary founding narrative attributes the codification of law to three brothers—Datok Katumanggungan, Datok Parapateh Sabatang, and Datok Sri Maharaja—who resolved a dispute over the proper use of oaths in judgment, after which the Ruler swore them to maintain the code unchanged, and a tree was planted in each district as a token of the decision (§§ 64–65, pp. 23–24).

### Conclusion

Winstedt and de Josselin de Jong present this text as a window into the adat system of Sungai Ujong, demonstrating that the customary law of Negeri Sembilan was a sophisticated, internally coherent body of jurisprudence with its own evidentiary procedures, graduated penalties, and philosophical underpinnings, deeply intertwined with Islamic canon law and Minangkabau cultural traditions. The text's own claim—that custom, when strong, serves as religious law (*"Idhā kāna 'l-ʿādatu qawiyyatan fahiya sharʿun"*)—encapsulates the editors' implicit argument for the seriousness with which such documents should be treated as historical and legal sources.

## Context

- The primary sources are two manuscripts held in the Royal Asiatic Society collection (Maxwell 118 and 118A), the latter a Romanized copy made at Malacca in 1904; the Jawi original predates it by a few years.
- The study's historiographical contribution lies in making accessible a previously unpublished adat text from Negeri Sembilan, a region for which, as the editors note, "little has ever been published on the dialect" or the legal traditions (p. 3). It complements Winstedt's broader work on Malay customary law and provides a comparative datum for the Minangkabau-influenced adat systems of the western Malay Peninsula.
