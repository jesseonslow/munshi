# An introduction to the Chinese glove puppet theatre

Tan Sooi Beng (1984) provides a preliminary ethnographic study of the Chinese glove puppet theatre (*Po te hi*) as performed in Malaysia, drawing on fieldwork conducted in Penang in 1979–1980. The article documents the art form's origins, material culture, musical conventions, and economic structure while arguing that the theatre is in terminal decline due to low pay, low social status, and the penetration of Western entertainment models.

## Summary

The article is a descriptive ethnography that systematically covers the glove puppet theatre's origins in Chuan-chou, Fukien Province (approximately 300 years ago), its migration to Malaya in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, and its subsequent narrowing from amusement-park entertainment to exclusively religious temple performances. Tan documents the physical stage, puppet construction and classification into five principal role types (*seng*, *toa*, *kang lang*, *thiu*, *hoa bin*), the elaborate symbolism of facial features, wigs, beards, costumes, and head-dresses, and the symbolic logic of stage props. She then turns to the internal organisation of troupes, the dual military and civil orchestral ensembles, the tune and speech types used in performance, and the ritual sequence that opens each show on a deity's official birthday.

The underlying historical problem is one of cultural preservation. Tan's primary sources are direct observation and interviews with puppeteers, musicians, and theatre proprietors in Penang, supplemented by her MA thesis research at Wesleyan University. She frames the study explicitly as preliminary, noting the near-total absence of prior written documentation on the subject. The article thus serves both as a technical reference and as a record of a dying art form, capturing conventions that the older generation of performers could no longer fully articulate or that the younger generation had no intention of learning.

A significant dimension of the analysis is economic. Tan shows that the puppet theatre occupies the lowest rung of the Chinese performing-arts hierarchy in Malaysia: it is performed only when temples cannot afford live opera, its performers are poorly paid and must hold multiple jobs, and its proprietors must resort to one-year contracts to secure a stable roster. The live opera, by contrast, has been revitalised by successful Chinese businessmen who commission performances to appease or thank the gods, further marginalising the puppet theatre.

### Key Findings

- A glove puppet theatre performance costs M$250 per day, compared with M$1,500–2,000 per day for a live opera troupe (p. 40, n. 4).
- Individual performers in Penang are paid M$15 per night (puppeteers, singers, musicians) or M$14 per night (backstage workers); the proprietor retains the remainder of the M$250 fee for electricity, transport, and equipment upkeep (p. 50).
- Each troupe comprises 7–10 members and owns at least 40 puppets, some as many as 100; the average puppet stands 8–9 inches tall (pp. 41, 50).
- The stage is a crude construction of rough planks, atap or zinc roofing, measuring approximately 3 feet above ground, 7 feet wide, 8 feet long, and 6–7 feet high (pp. 40–41).
- Proprietors require performers to sign one-year contracts to guarantee availability, yet performers routinely hold two or three other jobs and frequently quit or move between troupes (p. 51).
- The most commonly used tune is the *chit ji tiau* (seven-word tune / *li pian tiau*); free-rhythm, highly ornamented, and melismatic tune types are "hardly used anymore" because they are "very difficult to learn" (p. 53).

### Conclusion

Tan's definitive historical takeaway is that the glove puppet theatre in Malaysia is on the verge of extinction. Although the worship of deities will almost certainly continue, the art form will not survive because it lacks public interest, government support, and a pipeline of young recruits. The live opera has effectively displaced the puppet theatre from the religious performance market, and the younger generation, unable to understand the art's intricacies and unwilling to endure its rigours for such low remuneration, has no intention of carrying it forward.

## Context

- Primary archival basis: fieldwork in Penang (1979–1980) conducted for the author's MA thesis, "The Phor Tor Festival, Penang, 1979" (Wesleyan University, Connecticut, 1980), with cooperation from the Guat Poh Hong Puppet Theatre, Beng Geok Hong Hokkien Puppet Theatre, and the Ang Kim Sai Hokkien Theatre (p. 40, n. 1).
- Historiographical contribution: the first systematic written documentation of the *Po te hi* in Malaysia; Tan notes that no research had been done on the other two types of Chinese three-dimensional puppet theatre still performed in the country—marionettes (Hokkien dialect, performed only for the Jade Emperor) and red puppets (Teochew dialect, stick-manipulated) (p. 40, n. 2).
