# The voyages of Father Pécot in the Peninsula, 1821–23

Anthony and Helen Reid's 2020 edition presents the translated and edited correspondence of Father Mathurin-Pierre Pécot (1792–1823), a French missionary of the Société des Missions Étrangères de Paris who traversed the Malay Peninsula during the Siamese conquest of Kedah (1821) and the early British colonial consolidation of Penang and Singapore. The letters, drawn from the SMEP archives in Paris, constitute a rare first-hand European account of the Peninsula's interior during a period of intense political upheaval, while also documenting the origins of Catholic vernacular publishing in Malay.

## Summary

Pécot arrived in Penang on 13 November 1821 aboard the *Angélique*, having sailed from Macao via Singapore and Melaka. His initial assignment to the struggling Catholic community there—then numbering roughly 600—was immediately complicated by the opposition of the ageing German priest Fr Rectenwald, who controlled access to the faithful and refused Pécot even wine for Mass (p. 169). Within months, Pécot had produced a Malay-language missal and catechism, *Baktian Saharian*, with the assistance of the catechist Aloysius Bretaud (de Brito), a Siamese-born layman educated at Pondicherry who knew Malay, Siamese, Latin and French (pp. 170, 175). This manuscript was ultimately published in Paris in 1828 as *Kebaktian Sahari Harian dan Sembahiang Iang didjadikan deri Parochius Romanus*, with two companion catechisms for children and adults printed in 1826 and 1827 (p. 171).

Frustrated by Rectenwald and eager to reach the interior, Pécot undertook a trans-Peninsula overland journey in April–May 1822, travelling from Kedah through Trang to Ligor (Nakhon Si Thammarat) and thence by sea to Bangkok. His account of this crossing is the article's most vivid material: a seven-day elephant trek through an "impenetrable forest" of trees exceeding 100 feet in height, punctuated by an encounter with a tiger, a ten-day bout of dysentery, and the baptism of 54 dying patients in the hospitals of Trang (pp. 178–181). He had also been received by the Siamese-appointed ruler of Kedah, Phra Pakdiborrirak, in a court audience of 200 armed guards, where he declined to prostrate himself and instead offered a French-style bow (pp. 176–178).

A second trans-Peninsula return journey in 1823, undertaken after Rectenwald's death made Penang a viable base again, proved fatal. Pécot contracted malaria ("the Ligor sickness") and died at Penang on 21 July 1823, aged 31 (p. 170). By the time of his death the Penang Catholic community had grown to approximately 1,000, partly through his own conversions and energy (p. 171).

### Key Findings

- The Siamese invasion of Kedah in late 1821, triggered by Sultan Ahmad's failure to send tribute to Bangkok, is documented from the Penang vantage point: a squadron of 150 Malay and Siamese boats appeared off the island on Christmas Day 1821, the Sultan fled to Penang where the British Governor turned his cannons to protect him, and rice prices tripled (pp. 173–174).
- Pécot's Malay missal *Baktian Saharian* (33 chapters) was the first Catholic prayer book produced in Malay for the Penang community; it included morning and night prayers, the Mass wording, hymns covering the liturgical year, Gospel readings, and a catechism, with selected Latin prayers (e.g. *Gloria in Excelsis*, *Te Deum*) provided in Malay translation (p. 175).
- The trans-Peninsula route from Kedah to Ligor required a seven-day elephant trek through primary forest with no roads or houses, using resting places built by the King of Siam for his messengers—stockaded courtyards with bamboo cabins designed to exclude tigers (p. 180).
- Pécot baptised 54 persons during his 1822 journey, including 33 children on a Chinese ship at Trang where 80 people were "covered in ulcers and for the most part at death's door" (p. 179).
- The Siamese-appointed ruler of Kedah (Phra Pakdiborrirak, a son of the Governor of Nakhon Si Thammarat) offered Pécot the position of ambassador to France, asked him to speak well of him in European reports, and promised elephants and guides for his onward journey to Siam (pp. 177–178).
- Pécot's letters reveal that English had already displaced Portuguese as the commercial *lingua franca* of the region by 1821, and that Protestant missionary schools were drawing the children of upwardly mobile families away from Catholic influence (p. 174).

### Conclusion

The Reids present Pécot as a product of the French counter-revolution whose modernity—clarity of language, rationalism, confidence in progress—was inseparable from an intolerance of the hybrid, the local, and the vernacular that limited his effectiveness among indigenous clergy and ultimately shaped both his achievements and his premature death. His legacy lies less in any lasting institutional foundation than in the opening of a field for Malay-language Catholic publishing that would be sustained by subsequent reprints through the nineteenth century.

## Context

- The letters are held in the SMEP archives in Paris (AME 887, 892) and were previously known only fragmentarily through Guillot (1997); this edition provides the first complete English translation with substantial editorial framing.
- The publication sits within the broader MBRAS archival mission to make primary sources on early modern Southeast Asian history accessible, and complements the Reids' earlier work on the vernacularization of Christianity in the region (Reid 2008, 2009, 2014, 2015).
