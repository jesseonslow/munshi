# Joseph Ducroix, a French agent of the Comintern in Singapore (1931–1932

Laurent Metzger's 1996 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* reconstructs the brief and ultimately failed mission of Joseph Ducroux, a young French Comintern agent who arrived in Singapore in April 1931 under the false identity of Serge Lefranc and was arrested within a month. Drawing on British colonial records, French police archives, Ducroux's own unpublished diary, and two Malay-language novels of the period, Metzger argues that Ducroux was a minor figure whose significance was deliberately inflated by the Singapore authorities for political and inter-imperial reasons, and that the degree of intelligence collaboration between British, French, and other colonial powers in the region has been substantially underappreciated in the historiography.

## Summary

Metzger's central intervention is to deflate the legend that had grown around Ducroux in both British police memoirs and subsequent secondary literature. The Director of Criminal Intelligence, René Onraet, and later writers such as Hanrahan and Yong had portrayed Ducroux as a mastermind tasked with reorganising the Malayan Communist Party and delivering substantial funds to the movement. Metzger demonstrates that this narrative was a construction of the Singapore authorities, who had every incentive to present the arrest of a European as a major intelligence coup. In reality, Ducroux was a 26-year-old with no prior experience in Southeast Asia, who had been instructed in Moscow to gather information about the communist organisation in the region while waiting for a visa to proceed to India, his actual long-term assignment. The reorganisation of the MCP had already been assigned to Ho Chi Minh in 1930, and the Chinese communists arrested alongside Ducroux—Fu Tai-keng and Wong Muk-han—were far more significant local figures than the Frenchman.

The article also illuminates the mechanics of inter-colonial intelligence sharing that made Ducroux's capture possible. British authorities in Paris had flagged him as early as 1925 following his arrest in Marseille; Singapore police were warned months before his arrival that the French national "Lefranc" might be Ducroux in disguise. His mail was intercepted, his Indian employee was recruited as an informant, and French authorities in Hanoi and Saigon provided corroborating intelligence, albeit with the embarrassing error of initially identifying the real Serge Lefranc rather than the impostor. The broader context included the arrest of Gu Shunzhang, head of Chinese secret services, in April 1931, and the subsequent French crackdown on the Indochinese Communist Party in May 1931, all part of a coordinated regional effort.

A distinctive feature of the article is its use of Malay-language literary sources. Metzger examines two novels by Shamsuddin Saleh—*Siasat yang Dasyat* (1936) and *Pemimpin Sulit* (1937)—which incorporate a character named Lipan, clearly based on Lefranc/Ducroux, and which depict him as a leading figure in the communist organisation across Malaya and Indonesia. While the novels contain factual errors (including the wrong arrest date), they provide tantalising evidence of Ducroux's contacts with Malay and Indonesian communists, and possibly with a French liaison agent named Marie Arnaud, whose existence is confirmed in a single Hanoi dispatch but who appears nowhere else in the record.

### Key Findings

- Ducroux arrived in Singapore on 27 April 1931 and was arrested on 1 June 1931, giving him barely one month of freedom; he was sentenced on 22 June to eighteen months' rigorous imprisonment and life banishment from Singapore (pp. 4, 8).
- He was one of fifteen persons arrested by the Director of Criminal Intelligence at that time; fourteen others were brought to trial alongside him, and a printing press was seized in the operations (pp. 5–6).
- A figure of $50,000 in gold was cited at the trial and later by Onraet as funds Ducroux was to deliver to the MCP, but Ducroux consistently denied ever handling such a sum (p. 10).
- The real Serge Norbert Lefranc, whose identity Ducroux had assumed, was born on 22 March 1903, a year before Ducroux himself (24 May 1904, Belleville-sur-Saône) (pp. 2–3).
- After serving his sentence in Outram Prison, Ducroux was deported on the *Paul Doumer* to Saigon in late 1932, where he was re-arrested by French police and sentenced to one year for using a false identity; he was finally released on 3 November 1933 (pp. 12–14).
- Ducroux's unpublished diary, written at age 65, begins with the Singapore episode and describes it as "a real turnabout in my political life," marking the end of his international communist career (p. 15).

### Conclusion

Metzger's definitive takeaway is that Ducroux was a peripheral Comintern operative whose arrest became a propaganda event of disproportionate significance, manufactured by the British colonial state to demonstrate the effectiveness of its intelligence apparatus and to assert prestige vis-à-vis its French and Dutch counterparts. The article corrects a persistent historiographical error—the inflated role attributed to Ducroux in the MCP—and redirects attention to the structural realities of inter-imperial policing in 1930s Southeast Asia, where the small number of European communists operating in the region made each arrest a matter of diplomatic as well as local importance.

## Context

- Primary sources include the CO 273 series (British National Archives), the Archives d'Outre-Mer at Aix-en-Provence (French colonial records), Ducroux's unpublished three-volume diary (accessed through his daughter in 1993), and Onraet's post-war memoir *Singapore: A Police Background* (1947).
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in its systematic correction of errors that had accumulated in the secondary literature (misspelled names, wrong dates, inflated roles) and in its demonstration that the "Ducroux affair" was less a story of individual agency than of institutional inter-colonial coordination.
