# The early rulers of Perak, Pahang and Acheh

R.O. Winstedt, a colonial-era scholar and administrator (C.M.G., D.Litt.), published this article in 1932 to demonstrate through comparative genealogy that the early rulers of Perak, Pahang, and Acheh were all branches of the Malacca royal house, and that Acheh's repeated raids on the Peninsular states were dynastic in motivation rather than purely expansionist. By cross-referencing Malay annals, Dutch archival records, and Acheh gravestone inscriptions, Winstedt argues that the interlocking pedigrees of these four polities explain a series of otherwise puzzling historical events between the fall of Malacca in 1511 and the Dutch capture of that city in 1641.

## Summary

Winstedt's central method is genealogical triangulation. He constructs and compares the royal trees of Perak, Pahang, and Acheh against the known Johore succession, showing that all three Peninsular states descended from Sultan Mahmud of Malacca (d. 1528) through different sons and daughters. For Perak, he argues that Raja Mudzafar, the elder son of Sultan Mahmud, was passed over for the throne in favour of his younger half-brother (the founder of the Johore dynasty) and consequently established himself in Perak. The existence of a Perak dynasty in the sixteenth century is confirmed by the 1533 Portuguese account of Paulo da Gama, who encountered twenty-seven lanchars sent by the Johore Sultan to aid his "brother" the Sultan of Perak, and by the fact that Acheh carried off a Perak consort and her children before 1579, one of whom subsequently ruled Acheh as Sultan Mansur Shah (1579–1585). Winstedt resolves a long-standing textual contradiction in the *Sejarah Melayu*—which names a "Sultan Mansur Shah" as ruler of Perak in a later interpolation—by identifying him as the Raja Muda (crown prince) of the contemporary Perak Sultan Mudzafar (Raja Sulong), whose son bore the same name.

For Pahang, Winstedt demonstrates that the branch was progressively weakened by internal incompetence and external pressure. The 1540 murder of the Pahang Sultan by a Bruneian envoy triggered a massacre of over four thousand. Subsequent rulers were drawn into the orbit of Acheh, Johore, and the Portuguese in turn. The critical dynastic link is that Sultan Ahmad of Pahang was carried captive to Acheh in 1618, married into the Acheh royal family, and his son Iskandar Thani succeeded Iskandar Muda as Sultan of Acheh in 1636. This explains both Acheh's claim to the Pahang throne and the 1635 reconquest. After Malacca fell in January 1641 and Iskandar Thani died in February of the same year, Acheh's succession passed to a series of women rulers, and its claim to Pahang was effectively abandoned.

The article's broader argument is that Acheh's Peninsular campaigns were not simple conquests but assertions of dynastic right through captured royal women and their offspring. The genealogical evidence shows that Acheh's Sultans from 1579 to 1641 were, in several cases, literally the sons or grandsons of Peninsular rulers, giving them a claim to the thrones they raided. Winstedt notes that the Perak royal visitation circuit still omits the graves of the sixth, seventh, and ninth rulers, all of whom died in Acheh—a material trace of this dynastic entanglement preserved in local practice.

### Key Findings

- In 1533, Paulo da Gama found 27 lanchars off Malacca dispatched by Sultan Alaedin of Johore to assist his "brother" the Sultan of Perak, confirming a living Perak dynasty in the 1530s (p. 35).
- A Perak prince, Sultan Ahmad Taju'd-din (also identified as Mansur Shah), ruled Acheh from 1579 to 1585 after being carried there following an Acheh raid on Perak; his daughter married Raja Omar of Johore (pp. 34–35).
- The Sultan of Pahang was murdered in 1540 by Khoja Zainal, a Bruneian envoy, in an outbreak that killed more than 4,000 men and destroyed the Portuguese factory (p. 36).
- Sultan Ahmad of Pahang was carried captive to Acheh in 1618; his son Iskandar Thani succeeded Iskandar Muda as Sultan of Acheh in 1636, and Iskandar Muda re-conquered Pahang in 1635 (pp. 38–39).
- Malacca fell to the Dutch on 14 January 1641; Iskandar Thani died on 15 February 1641, after which Acheh's succession passed to a series of women rulers and its claim to Pahang was abandoned (p. 39).
- A 1682 letter from the Laksamana Paduka Tuan to the Governor-General of Batavia refers to Sultan Ibrahim Shah as "the first king of Johore and Pahang," indicating the formal unification of the two thrones under the Johore line (p. 39).

### Conclusion

Winstedt's definitive takeaway is that the genealogical interlocking of the Malacca-descended houses of Perak, Pahang, Johore, and Acheh provides the key to understanding the pattern of raids, alliances, and captivities that characterised the region between 1511 and 1641. Acheh's Peninsular campaigns were dynastic assertions rooted in the blood claims of captured royal women, and the comparative method of cross-referencing Malay, Dutch, and Acheh sources is essential to untangling the succession puzzles that single-source traditions leave unresolved.

## Context

- Winstedt was a senior colonial administrator and scholar in the Straits Settlements; his access to Dutch archival material (Dagh-Register, Bijdragen) and Malay manuscripts (including a copy in the possession of the late Sultan of Pahang) reflects his institutional position.
- The article draws on the *Sejarah Melayu* (Shellabear edition and the Raja Bongsu variant), *Bustanu's-Salatin*, *Salasilah Perak*, Dutch records compiled by Rouffaer, and Acheh gravestone inscriptions worked out by Moquette and Husain Jayadiningrat. Its historiographical contribution lies in applying a strictly comparative genealogical method to resolve contradictions between these sources, a technique that remained influential in early twentieth-century Malay historiography.
