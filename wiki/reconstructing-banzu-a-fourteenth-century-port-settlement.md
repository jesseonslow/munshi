# Reconstructing Banzu, a fourteenth century port settlement

Derek Heng Thiam Soon's 2002 article in the *Journal of the Malaysian Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* attempts a spatial reconstruction of Banzu, the fourteenth-century Malay port settlement on the north bank of the Singapore River basin, by synthesizing Chinese travel accounts, the *Sejarah Melayu*, colonial-era observations, and archaeological data from Fort Canning Hill. The overarching thesis is that Banzu can be understood as a typical example of the classical Malay trading-port polity, whose physical layout—comprising a commoner habitation plain, a hilltop palace precinct with ritual and artisan zones, and a multi-layered defence system—followed a recognizable regional pattern inherited from Srivijaya.

## Summary

The article addresses a fundamental problem in Singapore's early history: the near-total absence of surviving physical evidence for the Temasik settlement, whose perishable timber architecture has long since decomposed. Soon's methodological response is to treat the shared characteristics of Malay trading-port settlements—documented at Palembang, Kota Cina, Kedah, Malacca, and Aceh—as a legitimate comparative source, arguing that the cultural continuity from the Srivijayan period onward produced a remarkably consistent urban morphology across the region (pp. 2–4). He justifies this approach by demonstrating that the key natural features (hill, foothill plain, river drainage basin) and the categories of archaeological finds (ceramic shards, glass, metal wares, post moulds, religious architecture) recur across all known sites, regardless of period.

The reconstruction proceeds through three spatial zones. The plain area along the north bank and river basin housed the commoner population in elevated stilt houses, with probable ethnic quarters for foreign settlers managed through the Sharbandar system; however, Wang Dayuan's observation that Chinese and local populations lived side by side at Longyamen (Keppel Strait) suggests that strict ethnic segregation may have been unusual at Banzu specifically (pp. 5–9). The palace precinct on Fort Canning Hill is divided into a ritual area on the upper north and east slopes (containing the Kramat Iskandar Shah tomb and a probable multi-tiered temple near the summit), a royal residency on the south and west slopes with a *taman* (orchard) and a *pancur larangan* (royal bath) at the western foot, and an artisans and servants area on the lower east slope where glass processing and goldsmithing took place (pp. 9–12). The defence system combined an earth rampart (approximately 16 feet wide by 8–9 feet high as measured by Crawfurd in 1822), a moat fed by the Fresh Water Stream, a wooden palisade along the shoreline, and a chain boom across the river basin entrance (pp. 12–18).

A significant portion of the article is devoted to evaluating the *Sejarah Melayu* as a source. While acknowledging Wolters' argument that the Temasik section was composed by a Malaccan court writer to legitimize the Malaccan sultans' claim to Srivijayan descent, Soon contends that the physical landmarks described in the text—the inscribed boulder, the chain boom, the streets, the market—display a high degree of geographical accuracy, suggesting the composer had reliable knowledge of Banzu's physical features even if the narrative events surrounding them were fictional (pp. 4–5). The article concludes by arguing that the Temasik polity likely extended beyond the fortified perimeter, with satellite Orang Laut settlements at Keppel Strait and other coastal locations serving as outposts for trade and naval defence (pp. 18–20).

### Key Findings

- The earth rampart enclosing the northern part of the settlement measured approximately 16 feet wide by 8 or 9 feet high when observed by Crawfurd in 1822, and must have been considerably higher and steeper during the Temasik period given five centuries of tropical erosion (p. 12).
- A cache of gold ornaments unearthed in 1926 at a spot 200 feet west-southwest of the Kramat Iskandar Shah included a pair of repoussé gold armlets with Javanese Kala heads, a ring with a goose motif, six rings set with eleven Pontianak-type diamonds, and a jewelled clasp with fifteen diamonds and a pale ruby—evidence of foreign (Javanese/Majapahit) craftsmen residing on the hill (p. 7).
- The Siamese military expedition of the 1330s besieged Banzu for approximately one month before being called off by the approach of a Majapahit fleet en route to China, attesting to the effectiveness of the settlement's defence system (p. 13).
- The Temasik-period layer excavated at the Parliament House site in 1995 consisted of fine sand stained black with carbon ash, interpreted as the result of the Portuguese bombardment and burning of the settlement in 1613 (p. 17).
- The Yogjakarta kraton, used as a comparative model, was enclosed by four walls each measuring approximately one kilometre and housed 10,000 inhabitants in 1811, suggesting the Banzu palace precinct population was similarly substantial (p. 9).
- The chain boom across the river basin entrance, described in the *Sejarah Melayu*, functioned to prevent unauthorized ships from entering the settlement's water-borne sections, with foreign trading vessels required to anchor offshore and transfer cargo via smaller local vessels (p. 17).

### Conclusion

Soon's definitive takeaway is that Banzu, as the only fourteenth-century Malay settlement with substantial contemporaneous documentation of its physical characteristics, provides a credible template for understanding the typical Malay port settlement of the second millennium AD. The reconstruction, while necessarily hypothetical given the absence of surviving architectural evidence, demonstrates that the spatial organization of Banzu—its ritual-political hierarchy expressed through hilltop seclusion, its economic functions distributed across the plain and water, and its layered defence system—was consistent with the broader Srivijayan-derived urban pattern of the Malay region, and should serve as a foundational framework for future archaeological research in Singapore.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on the Fort Canning Hill archaeological excavations (Miksic, 1984), the Parliament House site excavation (1995), Crawfurd's 1822–23 colonial observations, the 1825 untitled map of Singapore Town, and Wang Dayuan's *Daoyi zhilue* (c. 1349), alongside the *Sejarah Melayu* and comparative data from Palembang, Kota Cina, Kedah, and Malacca.
- Historiographically, the article extends Wheatley's and Manguin's work on the Malay urban pattern by applying their comparative framework to a specific settlement reconstruction, and it engages directly with Wolters' source-critical arguments regarding the *Sejarah Melayu* to establish a more nuanced position on the text's evidential value for physical geography.
