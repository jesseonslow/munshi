# Communal disturbances in the Straits Settlements in 1857

Constance M. Turnbull's 1958 article examines the communal disturbances that erupted across the Straits Settlements in 1857, arguing that these seemingly trivial clashes over police enforcement of new legislation proved a more vital catalyst for the eventual transfer of the Settlements from Indian to British Colonial Office control than the longer-standing grievances over trade policy and judicial reform. Writing from the Straits Settlements Records at Raffles National Library and the contemporary press, Turnbull reconstructs how the enforcement of the Police and Conservancy Acts ignited successive outbreaks among the Chinese and Indian communities in Singapore and Penang, and how these incidents became entangled with the growing demand among the European mercantile elite for a share in government.

## Summary

Turnbull's central argument is that the disturbances of 1857 derived their historical significance not from their intrinsic importance as isolated incidents of mob violence, but from the way they exposed and intensified three structural problems that had been accumulating for years: the inadequacy of British administrative methods for governing the Chinese community, the demand of the non-official European merchants for political representation, and the fundamental question of whether the Straits Settlements should remain a dependency of the Government of India. The article traces how each disturbance—beginning with the Chinese shop-closure in Singapore in January, followed by the fatal clash between police and Indian Muslims in February, and culminating in the Penang riot in March—was magnified beyond its actual scale by a combination of police incompetence, official obstinacy, and the aggressive editorial campaigns of the local Press, particularly the *Penang Gazette* under J. R. Logan and the *Singapore Free Press*.

A recurring theme is the failure of communication between the colonial administration and the Asian communities it governed. The inability to translate English legislation into Chinese rendered the new Acts incomprehensible to the population they affected, allowing rumours to distort their provisions and provoke alarm. Turnbull shows that where the Governor did engage directly with Chinese leaders—as Blundell did in Singapore in January—the trouble subsided immediately; where he refused to listen, as in Penang, the conflict escalated into a prolonged political crisis. The article also highlights the paradox of Blundell's governance: a man of genuine integrity and firm conviction who, through his lack of diplomatic skill and his obstinate adherence to his own judgments, alienated virtually every section of the community and thereby accelerated the movement for constitutional change.

The political dimension of the article is as important as the communal one. Turnbull demonstrates how the disturbances provided the emotional fuel for the mercantile community's long-standing desire for representative institutions, and how the imposition of press censorship following the Indian Mutiny—legislation that was meaningless in the Straits but symbolically devastating—pushed the most influential merchants beyond mere criticism into active agitation for transfer to the Colonial Office. The article thus positions 1857 as the pivotal year in which the question of the Straits' constitutional status moved from abstract debate to concrete political action.

### Key Findings

- The Police Act (Act XIII) and Conservancy Act (Act XIV) of 1856, passed by the Legislative Council of India on 13 June 1856, came into force in the Straits Settlements on 1 January 1857, triggering the Chinese shop-closure in Singapore that lasted from 1 to 5 January (pp. 94–97).
- The Pennefather incident of 5 February 1857 resulted in one Indian Muslim killed, a second mortally wounded, and eleven others hospitalised; Pennefather was acquitted on 28 April after an eight-day trial, though the Governor-General of India upheld his dismissal from the Police Force (pp. 101–108).
- The Penang riot of 14 March 1857, sparked by the police demolition of a wayang stage, left one Chinese dead, two Indians wounded, and one sepoy knocked down; two sepoy muskets were seized by the mob and not recovered until found in a European merchant's godown in early July (pp. 112–127).
- The Commission of Enquiry into the Penang disturbances opened on 13 July 1857, comprising Police Magistrate Thomas Braddell, Justice of the Peace F. S. Brown, and P. E. Mathieu as the Chinese nominee; the official members refused to issue a joint report, and Mathieu published his findings independently (pp. 127–129).
- Act XV of 1857, imposing press censorship throughout India following the Mutiny, was automatically extended to the Straits Settlements on 13 June 1857, provoking a public meeting in Singapore on 28 July and a petition to the British Parliament for exemption (pp. 141–142).
- Thomas Dunman was appointed the first Commissioner of Police for Singapore on 1 June 1857, a position he held until 1871, during which the secret societies, though not formally suppressed, ceased to threaten the general peace (p. 137, n. 127).

### Conclusion

Turnbull's definitive takeaway is that the communal disturbances of 1857 were the essential precondition for the constitutional transformation of the Straits Settlements: without the anger and resentment they generated against the Governor and the Indian administration, the subsequent catalysts of press censorship and the Calcutta petition for Crown rule would not have found a receptive audience. The disturbances thus served as the vital link between long-standing structural grievances and the concrete political action that culminated in the transfer of the Straits Settlements from the Government of India to the Colonial Office in 1867.

## Context

- Turnbull's research draws primarily on the Straits Settlements Records (R, S, W, V, U, and DD series) held at Raffles National Library, Singapore, supplemented by contemporary newspaper reports from the *Straits Times*, *Singapore Free Press*, and *Penang Gazette*.
- The article's historiographical contribution lies in shifting the explanatory focus for the 1867 transfer away from the better-documented commercial and legislative grievances toward the underexplored communal disturbances of 1857, which Turnbull identifies as the more immediate and emotionally potent driver of political change.
