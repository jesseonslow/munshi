# The master attendants (harbour masters) at Singapore, 1819–67

R. S. Shelford's "A Trip to Mt. Penrissen, Sarawak" (1900) is a first-person narrative of a zoological collecting expedition to a 4,800-foot mountain in the upper Sarawak River region, undertaken in May–June 1900 by Shelford and E. A. W. Cox of the Sarawak Government service. The article serves as both a travelogue of the journey and a preliminary faunal report, with the central argument that Penrissen's invertebrate fauna was exceptionally rich while its vertebrate fauna was strikingly depleted by intensive native hunting.

## Summary

The expedition departed Kuching on 5 May 1900 with a staff of Dyak collectors, Malay and Chinese boys, and a Chinese cook, travelling in three boats up the Sarawak River. The narrative details the practical difficulties of navigating shallow, rapid-filled upper reaches, the exchange of heavy boats for lighter ones crewed by Land-Dyak polers, and the eventual overland approach from the village of Sennah to the mountain. Along the way, Shelford provides ethnographic observations of the Sennah (Land Dyak) people, including descriptions of their stilted village architecture, women's brass ring ornaments, the "Bok tumbis" beadwork hat worn during harvest dances, and their economic dependence on Malay and Chinese traders for cloth, weapons, and pottery.

On the mountain itself, the party was initially misled by their guides into encamping at 3,400 feet rather than the superior plateau at 4,500 feet. Cox ascended to the summit — a flat expanse of pandanus and rotan reached by improvised ladders over a sheer sandstone cliff — and sent down a specimen of the trogon *Harpectes dulitensis* and an eighteen-inch earthworm. The party then split, with Cox moving to Mt. Prang (3,900 feet) to investigate curious insect larvae (*Vermileo* sp.) while Shelford continued collecting on the lower plateau. The return journey to Kuching was completed on 4 June.

The faunal results were mixed. Mammals and birds were extremely scarce — only two kinds of monkey were seen, and no game birds at all — a condition Shelford attributes to the fact that 60 per cent of the local Dyak population were armed with guns and conducted massive foraging parties before every harvest feast. Reptiles and amphibians were moderately abundant, three fish species were captured using *Tuba* (poison), and the invertebrate fauna was described as "extremely rich," with Shelford confident that no Bornean mountain had been more thoroughly worked entomologically. A botanical appendix by Bishop Hose lists twelve fern species (including two new to Borneo) and several mosses and hepatics.

### Key Findings

- Mt. Penrissen stands at 4,800 feet, five miles from the "ulu" (head) of the left-hand branch of the Sarawak River, approximately fifty miles from the sea-coast (p. 1).
- The summit is a flat plateau roughly half a mile long by two hundred yards broad, covered in pandanus and rotan, reached by a final 300-foot sheer precipice of sandstone conglomerate requiring improvised ladders (p. 6).
- Only two species of monkey were observed; pigs, deer, *kijang*, and *pelandok* were "noticeable only by their absence," and not a single game bird was procured or seen (p. 8).
- Approximately 60 per cent of the Dyak population in the district were armed with guns, and huge foraging parties kept up "a constant fusillade on anything above the size of a thrush" before every harvest feast (p. 8).
- Bishop Hose's fern appendix records *Polypodium hirtellum* and *Polypodium obliquatum* as new to Borneo, previously known only from Java, Perak, Ceylon, the Philippines, and China (p. 25).
- A new moss species, *Thuidium Everett* Broth. n. sp., was described from specimens collected by A. H. Everett on the mountain (p. 26).

### Conclusion

Shelford's definitive takeaway is that Penrissen, while entomologically the best-worked mountain in Borneo at that time, was faunistically impoverished in its vertebrate assemblage due to sustained indigenous hunting pressure, and that the southern end of Sarawak was likely less rich in fauna than the northern regions where Whitehead and Hose had worked on Kina Balu and Dulit. He announced plans to return in October with collectors dedicated entirely to botany, signalling that the mountain's plant life held the greater untapped scientific value.

## Context

- Shelford was a naturalist operating within the Sarawak Government service under the White Rajah administration; the expedition was facilitated by a "formidable looking surat" (official letter) from the Resident of Sarawak, which smoothed arrangements for carriers with the Sennah village (p. 2).
- The article draws on the collections of the Sarawak Museum and the natural history resources of the Bishop of Singapore and Sarawak (Charles Hose), reflecting the collaborative colonial scientific network of the period.
