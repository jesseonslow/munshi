# Economic change and the emergence of the Straits Chinese in nineteenth-century Penang

Neil Khor Jin Keong (2006) traces the formation of a distinctive Straits Chinese identity in nineteenth-century Penang through the interlinked biographies of three Chinese families—the Kohs, the Khoos, and the Lims—arguing that this identity emerged from the fusion of Peranakan Chinese heritage, Hokkien immigrant culture, and British colonial modernity, shaped decisively by economic change and the rise and fall of secret societies.

## Summary

The article addresses a specific historiographical problem: why the Straits Chinese identity that crystallized in Penang differed markedly from that in Singapore, where the numerically dominant Baba Chinese maintained a more clearly Peranakan cultural profile. Khor's core argument is that Penang's unique demographic circumstances—the overwhelming influx of Hokkien immigrants who outnumbers and culturally absorbed the earlier Peranakan settlers—produced a hybrid "Penang Hokkien" culture that became the substrate of Straits Chinese identity in the colony. This identity was neither purely Chinese nor purely Western but a self-conscious amalgam that its bearers actively constructed across successive generations.

The narrative is organized around six generational stages, using the three family genealogies as a chronological model. The first generation (Koh Lay Huan, who arrived with Francis Light's 1786 landing party) operated as intermediaries between the British and the Chinese community, leveraging revenue farming and secret society enforcement to accumulate capital. The second and third generations witnessed the critical cultural fusion: Hokkien immigrants like Khoo Teng Pang married into Peranakan families, learned Baba Malay, and combined secret society muscle with Peranakan social connections to dominate Penang's economy. The fourth generation saw the colonial state's systematic dismantling of the secret societies and the rise of English education as the new engine of social mobility, exemplified by Koh Seang Tat's entry into municipal politics. The fifth and sixth generations, represented by Lim Hin Leong and his English-educated sons, show the final consolidation of a Straits Chinese identity that was Malayan-centred, Westernized in public life, and Peranakan in private domestic culture.

Primary sources anchor the narrative at each stage: C. S. Wong's *A Gallery of Chinese Kapitans* (1963) provides the foundational biographical data; Logan's extracts from Light's journal document the founding period; Vaughan (1879) and Anson (1920) supply colonial-era observations of Chinese customs; and the *Penang Gazette* records municipal elections and administrative decisions. Khor also draws on oral history interviews with Dato' Seri Khoo Keat Siew and Dato' P. G. Lim to fill gaps in the clan and family records.

### Key Findings

- The Chinese population in Penang had a sex ratio of approximately 50 males to 1 female, creating a bachelor society in which secret societies functioned as the primary mechanism of social control and economic enforcement (p. 62).
- In 1852, the British extended citizenship by naturalization throughout the Empire; by 1857 this was restricted to those born in the Straits Settlements, and the term "Straits-born" became the marker of political and social distinction (pp. 64–65).
- Koh Seang Tat became the first elected Straits Chinese Municipal Commissioner in 1887, coming in third with 136 votes behind Dr Brown (141) and Cheah Chen Eok (145) (p. 72, n. 13).
- The colonial government declared secret societies illegal in 1890 by amending the 1869 Dangerous Societies Suppression Act, which had previously merely required registration; the Chinese Protectorate (1877) and the Settlements-wide police force (1872) had progressively sapped secret society influence in the preceding decades (pp. 63–64).
- The Bean Wah Lee Oil Mill, established by Koh Leap Cheng and partners in the 1880s, cost $400,000 and was then the largest oil mill in Penang, illustrating the scale of capital the Penang Hokkiens could pool for industrial investment (n. 17).
- In 1914, prominent Straits Chinese families including the Kohs, Khoos, and Lims established the Hu Yew Seah reading club to promote Chinese culture and Mandarin, a response to perceived dilution of their Chinese identity by English education (p. 78).

### Conclusion

Khor's definitive takeaway is that Straits Chinese identity was not a passive by-product of colonial modernization but an active, aspirational self-construction. The Straits Chinese were "not passive recipients of colonial influence but actively sought to define their own cultural identity," which reflected "an idealized image of themselves: an image which was Chinese, but wealthy, sophisticated, Westernized, and cosmopolitan" (p. 78). Culture, in Khor's framing, was not merely a derivation of socio-economic forces but included deliberate choices about which elements of Peranakan, Hokkien, and Western culture to adopt, discard, or combine.

## Context

- The study draws primarily on C. S. Wong's *A Gallery of Chinese Kapitans* (1963), colonial administrative records (Straits Settlements Factory Records, *Penang Gazette*), and oral history interviews with living clan and family members, rather than on a single archival repository.
- Historiographically, the article provides a Penang-specific counterpoint to the better-documented Singapore narrative of Straits Chinese formation, demonstrating that the "Straits-born" category meant something fundamentally different in a Hokkien-dominated colony than in a Baba-dominated one.
